# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 8

## Topic
Understanding Point of View

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the point of view (first person, third person limited, third person omniscient) of a narrative passage
- Explain how the narrator's perspective shapes what the reader knows and does not know
- Analyze how point of view affects the reader's understanding of characters and events
- Describe the limitations and advantages of different narrative perspectives

## Prerequisites
- Understanding that a narrator tells the story and that different narrators have different access to information (RL.5.6)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lessons 1-2, RL.6.1)
- Ability to analyze how structural elements contribute to a text (Lessons 6-7, RL.6.5)
- Experience reading texts narrated from different perspectives

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Index cards (6-8 per student, for sorting activity)
- T-chart template (What the narrator reveals / What the narrator hides)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about narrative voice and introduce the concept that point of view shapes meaning.

**Activity: Who Is Telling This Story?**

Say: "Every story has a narrator — someone who tells the events to the reader. But the narrator is not always the same kind of voice. Think about it: if your best friend tells you about a party, you get one version. If someone who was watching from across the room tells you about the same party, you get a different version. Same events, different perspective."

Write on the board:
- **First person** — The narrator is a character in the story. Uses "I" and "me." Can only share their own thoughts and observations.
- **Third person limited** — The narrator is outside the story but follows one character closely. Uses "he," "she," or "they." Reveals that character's thoughts but not others'.
- **Third person omniscient** — The narrator is outside the story and knows everything — every character's thoughts, feelings, and secrets. Uses "he," "she," or "they."

Say: "I am going to read three sentences. On your index card, write 1P, 3L, or 3O for the point of view."

Read aloud:
1. "I watched the door close and felt my heart drop." (1P — first person)
2. "Amir watched the door close, his heart sinking, though he could not explain why." (3L — third person limited)
3. "Amir watched the door close, his heart sinking. On the other side of the door, Lena was already crying, though Amir would never know that." (3O — third person omniscient)

Say: "In Grade 5, you learned to describe how a narrator's point of view influences how events are described. In Grade 6, the standard goes deeper: you need to explain how the author develops the point of view. That means understanding not just WHAT the perspective is, but HOW it shapes the entire reading experience."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to analyze how point of view controls the flow of information and shapes the reader's experience of a text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Point of view:** The perspective from which a story is told — who the narrator is and what they can know, see, and reveal.
- **Narrator:** The voice that tells the story. May be a character (first person) or an outside observer (third person).
- **First person:** A narrative perspective where the narrator is a character in the story, using "I" and limited to their own perceptions.
- **Third person limited:** A narrative perspective where the narrator focuses on one character's thoughts and experiences, using "he/she/they."
- **Third person omniscient:** A narrative perspective where the narrator has unlimited access to all characters' thoughts and feelings.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Point of view is not just a label — it is a tool that controls what the reader knows. Every choice an author makes about point of view creates specific effects."

Write on the board — **What Point of View Controls:**
1. **Access** — Which character's thoughts and feelings can the reader see?
2. **Limitation** — What is hidden from the reader because of the narrator's position?
3. **Reliability** — Can we trust what this narrator tells us? Do they know the full picture?
4. **Intimacy** — How close does the reader feel to the characters?

Say: "Let me demonstrate these with two versions of the same scene."

### Worked Examples

**Scene: A student waits for test results.**

**Version A — First Person:**

> I sat in the hallway, turning my pencil over and over between my fingers. Mr. Delgado had been grading the tests for twenty minutes, and every time I heard a page flip, my stomach twisted. I had studied all weekend — given up the basketball game, turned down Rosa's invitation to the lake — and now it came down to whatever was written in red ink on that paper.
>
> The door opened. Mr. Delgado held out a stack of papers. I took mine without looking at it. I folded it in half and slid it into my notebook. I would look later, I told myself. When nobody was watching.
>
> But I already knew, from the way Mr. Delgado's eyes did not quite meet mine, that it was not the number I wanted.

Say: "What does the reader know? What does the reader NOT know?"

Write on the board:
- **Knows:** The narrator's anxiety, their sacrifices (basketball game, lake), their physical sensations (stomach twisting), their interpretation of Mr. Delgado's eye contact.
- **Does not know:** The actual test score, whether the narrator's interpretation of Mr. Delgado's eyes is correct, what Mr. Delgado is thinking, how other students did.

**Version B — Third Person Omniscient:**

> Mateo sat in the hallway, turning his pencil over and over between his fingers. Inside the classroom, Mr. Delgado was not grading the tests — he had finished ten minutes ago. He was rereading Mateo's essay question for the third time, impressed by a connection Mateo had made that no other student in the class had attempted. He was trying to decide whether to give it full marks or one extra credit point.
>
> Mr. Delgado opened the door and held out the stack of papers. Mateo took his without looking at it. He folded it in half and slid it into his notebook, convinced from Mr. Delgado's expression that the news was bad. Mr. Delgado watched Mateo walk away and thought, "That kid has no idea how well he did."

Say: "Look at how differently the two versions shape the reader's experience. In Version A, we feel Mateo's anxiety because we are trapped inside his perspective. We believe, with him, that the test went badly. In Version B, we know something Mateo does not — that Mr. Delgado is actually impressed. This creates dramatic irony: the reader sees Mateo suffering unnecessarily."

Write on the board:

| | Version A (First Person) | Version B (Third Person Omniscient) |
|---|---|---|
| **Access** | Only Mateo's thoughts | Both Mateo's and Mr. Delgado's thoughts |
| **Limitation** | Cannot know the real test result | No limitation — the reader knows everything |
| **Reliability** | Mateo misreads Mr. Delgado's expression | The narrator reveals that Mateo is wrong |
| **Intimacy** | Very close to Mateo — we feel his anxiety | Close to both characters — we see the full picture |
| **Reader's experience** | Suspense, shared anxiety | Dramatic irony, compassion for Mateo's unnecessary worry |

Say: "Same events, completely different experience — all because of point of view. That is why this analysis matters."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying point of view and analyzing its effects with facilitator support.

Say: "Read this passage silently. Identify the point of view, then answer the questions I will give you."

**Passage: "The New Student" (Third Person Limited — focused on Eleni)**

> Eleni noticed the new boy on his first day because he sat in the desk no one ever chose — the one in the front row, directly in front of Mrs. Yamamoto's desk. Everyone knew that seat was a punishment seat, the one you got assigned to when you talked too much. But the new boy sat down there like he had chosen it.
>
> He was quiet during morning announcements. He did not look around the way new students usually did — scanning faces, trying to map the social landscape. He opened a thick book with a leather cover and read silently, his lips not moving, his posture perfectly still.
>
> At lunch, Eleni watched him from three tables away. He sat alone, eating an orange. He peeled it slowly, placing each piece of rind in a neat stack. When Marcus and Jerome walked past his table, Marcus said something Eleni could not hear. The new boy looked up, said a word or two, and Marcus laughed — not unkindly. Then Marcus and Jerome walked away, and the new boy went back to his orange.
>
> Eleni wanted to walk over. She wanted to ask his name, where he was from, what the book was about. But she stayed at her table, peeling the label off her water bottle, wondering if he was lonely or if he preferred the quiet.
>
> She could not tell. And that, she realized, was what made her curious.

Say: "First — what is the point of view of this passage? How do you know?"

Expected response: Third person limited, focused on Eleni. The narrator uses "she" and shares Eleni's thoughts and observations, but we do not have access to the new boy's thoughts. We see him only through Eleni's eyes.

Say: "Now, fill in this T-chart in your notebook."

Write on the board:
| What the narrator reveals (through Eleni) | What the narrator hides |
|---|---|
| | |

Allow 2-3 minutes, then discuss.

Expected responses:

| What the narrator reveals | What the narrator hides |
|---|---|
| Eleni's curiosity and desire to approach the new boy | The new boy's name, thoughts, and feelings |
| Eleni's observations of his behavior (sitting in the "punishment seat," reading, peeling the orange) | What Marcus said to the new boy |
| Eleni's internal conflict (wanting to walk over but staying at her table) | Whether the new boy is lonely or prefers quiet |
| Eleni's awareness that she cannot read him ("She could not tell") | How other students perceive the new boy |

Say: "How does this point of view shape the reader's experience? Why might the author have chosen third person limited instead of third person omniscient?"

Expected response: By limiting the perspective to Eleni, the author makes the reader share her curiosity and uncertainty. If we could read the new boy's thoughts, the mystery would disappear. The limited perspective forces the reader to observe him the same way Eleni does — from a distance, with incomplete information — which mirrors the real experience of encountering someone new. The author chose this point of view to make the reader feel curiosity rather than knowledge.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how point of view shapes a narrative.

**Passage: "The Argument" (First Person — Damon's perspective)**

> I told Renata the truth. That is what everyone keeps forgetting. She asked me if I thought her poem was good enough to submit to the school literary magazine, and I said what I actually thought: that the ending felt rushed and the middle stanza did not connect to the rest. I did not say it was bad. I said it needed work.
>
> But Renata did not hear it that way. She closed her notebook so fast the pages crinkled, and she said, "I did not ask you to edit it. I asked if you thought it was good."
>
> I tried to explain. "I think it could be good. It is almost there. The first stanza is really —"
>
> "Forget it," she said, and she walked out of the library without taking her backpack. I sat there for a minute, holding the strap of her bag, wondering how being honest had turned into being wrong.
>
> Later, in the hallway, I saw her laughing with Keisha. She did not look at me when I walked past. I waited three seconds to see if she would turn around. She did not.
>
> Here is what I keep thinking about: maybe I was right about the poem. Maybe the ending is rushed and the middle stanza does not connect. But maybe being right is not the same as being helpful. And maybe there is a difference between honesty and kindness that I have not figured out yet.

**Directions:** Analyze the point of view of this passage and its effects.

1. What is the point of view? How do you know? Cite specific evidence from the text.
2. How does the first-person perspective shape what the reader knows about Renata's feelings? What can we observe, and what must we infer?
3. In the final paragraph, Damon reflects: "Maybe being right is not the same as being helpful." How does the first-person point of view make this realization more powerful than it would be in third person omniscient?
4. Is Damon a fully reliable narrator? Identify one moment where his perspective might be incomplete or biased, and explain what information we might be missing.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 with cited evidence.
**Stretch:** Rewrite the second paragraph of the passage ("But Renata did not hear it that way...") from Renata's first-person perspective. Then explain what new information becomes available and what is lost.
**Challenge:** Write a paragraph arguing whether the first-person point of view in this passage makes the reader more sympathetic to Damon, more sympathetic to Renata, or equally sympathetic to both. Support your argument with specific evidence from the text.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.2) What is the theme of this passage? State it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.6) In Grade 5, you learned that a narrator's point of view influences how events are described. Give one specific example from this passage where Damon's perspective influences how he describes an event.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Lila watched her brother cross the stage to receive his diploma. She clapped harder than anyone in the auditorium, even though her hands hurt from clapping for the two hundred students who had walked before him. She knew what nobody else in this room knew: that he had almost dropped out in October, that she had found him sitting in the driveway at midnight, and that she had been the one to talk him into going back.

1. What is the point of view? What does it reveal about Lila that other characters in the scene cannot know?
2. How does the narrator's access to Lila's private knowledge affect the reader's experience of this moment?

**Expected Answers:**
1. The point of view is third person limited, focused on Lila. It reveals that Lila possesses private knowledge about her brother — "She knew what nobody else in this room knew: that he had almost dropped out in October." This information is inaccessible to the other characters, including the brother's classmates, teachers, and other family members.
2. The narrator's access to Lila's private knowledge transforms the graduation from an ordinary ceremony into an emotionally charged moment. Without Lila's perspective, this is just another student crossing a stage. With it, the reader understands the significance behind the walk — the near-dropout, the midnight conversation, Lila's role in his return. The limited perspective makes the moment intimate and reveals layers of meaning beneath the public event.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the point of view, names at least one piece of information the narrator reveals through the chosen perspective, and explains how this shapes the reader's experience.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three types of point of view with a simple sorting activity: give the student six short opening sentences and ask them to categorize each by perspective.
- Practice the T-chart (reveals / hides) with a very short passage.
- Ask: "Whose thoughts can you hear? Whose thoughts are hidden? What questions can you not answer because of the narrator's position?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9, which focuses on how authors develop point of view through specific techniques.
- Encourage the student to identify the point of view in their independent reading and write one sentence about what it reveals and one sentence about what it hides.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Point of View Shift:** The student rewrites the "New Student" passage from the new boy's first-person perspective. They then write a paragraph comparing the two versions: what is gained and lost in each?
2. **POV Detective:** The student collects the opening paragraphs of three different novels or stories and identifies the point of view of each. For each one, they write two sentences about how the perspective shapes the reader's experience.
3. **Omniscient Expansion:** The student takes the "Argument" passage and rewrites it in third person omniscient, giving the reader access to both Damon's and Renata's thoughts. They then explain how the change in perspective alters the theme.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This lesson introduces a new standard area (RL.6.6).** Students have encountered point of view in Grade 5 (RL.5.6), but Grade 6 requires them to explain HOW the author develops the narrator's perspective, not just identify it. This lesson focuses on identification and basic analysis; Lesson 9 goes deeper into development techniques.
- **Reliability is a powerful concept at this level.** First-person narrators are inherently limited, and students should begin to notice that narrators can be wrong, biased, or self-serving. The Damon passage is specifically designed to introduce this idea.
- **"What the narrator hides" is the key insight.** Students often focus on what a text tells them. Push them to think about what the text CANNOT tell them because of the point of view. This absence shapes meaning as much as presence does.
- **Pacing note:** The Direct Instruction section compares two versions of the same scene, which is essential for understanding but takes time. If pressed, abbreviate the Guided Practice discussion and let students finish the T-chart as part of Independent Practice.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The point of view is first person, narrated by Damon. Evidence: the narrator uses "I" throughout ("I told Renata the truth," "I tried to explain," "I keep thinking about"). The reader has access only to Damon's thoughts, observations, and interpretations. We experience Renata entirely through his eyes.
2. The first-person perspective means the reader sees Renata's reaction (closing the notebook, saying "I did not ask you to edit it," walking out, laughing with Keisha) but cannot access her thoughts or feelings. We must infer her emotional state from her actions and words as Damon perceives them. For example, when Renata says "Forget it" and leaves without her backpack, we can infer she is hurt or angry, but we do not know the exact nature of her feelings — whether she is embarrassed, betrayed, or simply frustrated. The first-person perspective keeps Renata's interior life hidden, which mirrors the real-life experience of not knowing how your words affected someone.
3. In first person, Damon's realization — "maybe being right is not the same as being helpful" — reads as genuine self-reflection, a moment of growth happening in real time within the narrator's own thinking. If this were third person omniscient, the narrator could simply state this as a fact: "Damon was right about the poem but wrong about how to deliver his opinion." In first person, the realization is uncertain — "maybe" appears twice — and it comes from the same voice that made the mistake. This makes it more powerful because the reader witnesses the narrator questioning himself rather than being corrected by an outside authority.
4. Damon is not fully reliable. One moment where his perspective may be incomplete is when he describes his criticism: "I did not say it was bad. I said it needed work." Damon frames his feedback as gentle and constructive, but we only have his version of the words. We do not know his tone, whether he was aware of Renata's emotional state when she asked the question, or whether his feedback was more blunt than he remembers. Additionally, when Renata says "I did not ask you to edit it. I asked if you thought it was good," her response suggests she was looking for encouragement, not a critique — a distinction Damon seems to have missed. His narration focuses on the content of his words ("I said what I actually thought") but does not consider the context of the conversation.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.5.2) Theme: Being honest and being kind are not always the same thing, and learning to tell the truth in a way that supports rather than hurts the people you care about is a challenge that requires understanding what they need, not just what you think. (Accept reasonable variations about the difference between honesty and kindness, or about how intention does not always match impact.)
6. (RL.5.6) One example: Damon describes watching Renata in the hallway and says, "I waited three seconds to see if she would turn around. She did not." His perspective makes this feel like evidence that Renata is ignoring him on purpose. But from Renata's perspective, she may not have noticed him, or she may have been deliberately protecting herself. Damon's narration frames the moment as a rejection, but that interpretation is filtered through his sense of being unfairly treated.
