# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
How Authors Develop Point of View

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify specific techniques authors use to develop the narrator's or speaker's point of view (word choice, detail selection, tone, internal monologue, contrasting perspectives)
- Explain how an author builds and deepens a narrator's perspective over the course of a text
- Analyze how the narrator's voice — including their language, attitudes, and observations — shapes the reader's understanding of events
- Compare how two different narrators describing similar situations create distinct reading experiences

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify point of view (first person, third person limited, third person omniscient) and analyze its effects (Lesson 8, RL.6.6)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lessons 1-2, RL.6.1)
- Understanding that narrators control what information the reader receives (Lesson 8)
- Familiarity with the concept of narrator reliability and limitation

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Two-column comparison chart template
- Highlighters in two colors
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect Lesson 8's point of view identification to Lesson 9's focus on how authors actively develop a narrator's voice and perspective.

**Activity: Same Scene, Different Voice**

Say: "In Lesson 8, we learned to identify point of view and analyze what it reveals and hides. Today, we are going to focus on the HOW — the specific techniques authors use to develop a narrator's point of view. A narrator is not just a camera that records events. A narrator is a character with a personality, biases, and a way of seeing the world. The author creates that personality through deliberate choices."

Write on the board two descriptions of the same event:

**Voice A:** "The cafeteria was loud and chaotic, as usual. Kids threw napkins across tables, someone had spilled chocolate milk near the door, and the fluorescent lights buzzed like trapped insects. I ate my sandwich as fast as I could and left."

**Voice B:** "The cafeteria hummed with its usual energy — laughter bouncing off the walls, trays clattering, the warm smell of bread rolls drifting from the kitchen. I found a seat near the window and ate slowly, watching the rain streak down the glass."

Say: "Both narrators are in the same cafeteria. But one sees chaos and wants to escape. The other sees warmth and wants to linger. The author developed these different points of view through word choice, detail selection, and attitude. Today, we are going to learn to spot these techniques."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students the specific techniques authors use to develop a narrator's perspective.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Voice:** The distinctive personality, style, and attitude of a narrator, expressed through word choice, sentence structure, and observations.
- **Tone:** The narrator's attitude toward the subject — serious, playful, bitter, reflective, hopeful — conveyed through language choices.
- **Detail selection:** The specific details a narrator chooses to notice and describe, which reveal their priorities, values, and emotional state.
- **Internal monologue:** A character's private thoughts presented directly to the reader, revealing their inner perspective.
- **Contrasting perspective:** A moment where the narrator's view is placed alongside a different character's view, highlighting the narrator's particular way of seeing.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "The standard says you must explain how an author develops the point of view of the narrator or speaker. The word 'develops' is key. It does not mean 'chooses' — it means 'builds over time through specific techniques.' Here are the five main tools authors use."

Write on the board — **Five Techniques for Developing Point of View:**
1. **Word choice** — The narrator's vocabulary reveals their personality and attitude.
2. **Detail selection** — What the narrator notices (and ignores) shows their priorities.
3. **Tone** — The emotional quality of the narration reflects the narrator's feelings.
4. **Internal monologue** — The narrator's private thoughts show their reasoning and values.
5. **Contrasting perspectives** — Placing the narrator's view alongside others highlights their unique position.

Say: "Let me show you all five techniques at work in a single passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The New Apartment" (First Person — narrated by Suki)**

> The apartment smelled like fresh paint and emptiness. My mother called it "a fresh start," which is what adults say when they mean "I am sorry about what happened, but we are pretending it is a good thing." The rooms were small and square, like cells in a worksheet. My bedroom had one window that faced a brick wall.
>
> My mother walked through the rooms touching the walls, testing the faucets, opening cabinets with a kind of cheerfulness that felt rehearsed. "Look," she said, pointing to the kitchen. "There is a dishwasher!"
>
> I looked at the dishwasher. It was the color of a bandage. I did not say that.
>
> What I said was, "Great. A dishwasher."
>
> My mother's smile did not waver. She was committed to the performance, and I had to respect the effort, even if I could not join it. She had driven four hundred miles in one day, carried every box herself, and not once complained. I could give her a "Great" and mean it a little.
>
> That night, I lay on my mattress on the floor — the bed frame had not survived the move — and listened to the sounds of the new neighborhood. A dog barked three streets away. A car alarm sang its pointless song. Somewhere below us, a television played a show I could not identify.
>
> I closed my eyes and tried to hear the sound of my mother breathing in the next room. I could. And that was enough to fall asleep.

Say: "Let me analyze how the author develops Suki's point of view."

Write on the board:

**1. Word choice:** Suki describes the apartment as smelling like "fresh paint and emptiness" — she pairs a neutral detail with an emotional one, showing her tendency to see beyond surfaces. She calls the dishwasher "the color of a bandage," a metaphor that suggests she sees this new home as a wound being covered rather than healed.

**2. Detail selection:** Suki notices that her bedroom window faces a brick wall, that the rooms are "small and square, like cells in a worksheet," and that the bed frame "had not survived the move." She selects details that emphasize confinement, loss, and impermanence — revealing her emotional state through what she observes.

**3. Tone:** The tone is wry and observant — slightly sarcastic ("which is what adults say when they mean") but not cruel. Suki is hurt but self-aware, critical but also compassionate. The tone develops across the passage: it begins bitter and gradually softens.

**4. Internal monologue:** Suki's private thoughts reveal the gap between what she says and what she thinks. She describes the dishwasher as bandage-colored but says aloud "Great. A dishwasher." Her internal monologue reveals her honesty with herself and her effort to protect her mother.

**5. Contrasting perspectives:** Suki's view contrasts with her mother's rehearsed cheerfulness. The mother calls the move "a fresh start" and points out the dishwasher with enthusiasm. Suki sees through the performance but also respects it, creating a complex relationship between the two perspectives.

Say: "Notice that by the end, Suki's point of view has shifted slightly. She begins the passage cynical and ends it listening for her mother's breathing and finding comfort in it. The author develops the point of view not just through style but through change."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying the techniques authors use to develop point of view.

Say: "Read this passage silently. It is written in third person limited, focused on Emeka. After reading, identify at least three techniques the author uses to develop Emeka's perspective."

**Passage: "The Garden Plot" (Third Person Limited — focused on Emeka)**

> Emeka's assigned plot in the community garden was the one nobody wanted — the narrow strip along the north fence, shaded by the apartment building next door for most of the day. The other gardeners had plots in full sun, with dark soil that crumbled between their fingers. Emeka's soil was pale and hard, compacted from years of neglect.
>
> He did not complain. Complaining was something his father had never done, even when the factory cut his hours, even when the car needed repairs they could not afford. Emeka had inherited this silence the way other people inherit eye color or a talent for music.
>
> He worked the soil every afternoon after school. He broke it apart with a borrowed spade, mixed in compost from the kitchen scraps his mother saved in a bucket under the sink, and watered carefully, measuring each cup because the garden's shared hose reached his plot only if Mr. DiMatteo was not using it.
>
> The other gardeners' tomatoes were already fruiting by June. Emeka's seedlings were small and pale, leaning toward the narrow band of sunlight that slipped between the buildings in the late afternoon. He adjusted them daily, rotating each pot a quarter turn so every side would get its share.
>
> Mrs. Petrov, whose plot was in the sunniest corner, walked past one afternoon and glanced at his seedlings. "Shade garden," she said, shaking her head, as if the words explained everything.
>
> Emeka looked at his seedlings and then at Mrs. Petrov's lush rows. "They are growing," he said. It was not a defense. It was an observation. And it was true.

Say: "In your notebook, identify the techniques the author uses to develop Emeka's point of view. For each one, cite specific evidence and explain the effect."

Allow 3-4 minutes, then discuss.

Expected responses:

**Detail selection:** The narrator describes the soil as "pale and hard, compacted from years of neglect" and notes that Emeka measures each cup of water and rotates pots a quarter turn. These details show Emeka's meticulous care and resourcefulness — he does not have the best conditions but maximizes what he has.

**Contrasting perspectives:** Mrs. Petrov dismisses the plot as a "shade garden," shaking her head. Emeka responds with "They are growing" — a quiet, factual statement. The contrast between dismissal and patient acceptance develops Emeka's perspective as one of quiet resilience.

**Internal monologue / Background:** The narrator tells us Emeka "did not complain" and connects this to his father's silence during hardship. This develops his point of view as inherited — his stoicism is not just a personality trait but a family value.

**Tone:** The tone is observational and restrained — the narrator does not dramatize Emeka's disadvantage or make him a victim. Phrases like "It was not a defense. It was an observation. And it was true" reflect Emeka's own steady, understated way of seeing the world.

**Word choice:** The narrator describes the seedlings "leaning toward the narrow band of sunlight," which personifies the plants and mirrors Emeka's own posture toward opportunity — reaching for what is available rather than lamenting what is not.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how an author develops point of view through specific techniques.

**Passage: "The Archivist" (First Person — narrated by Noelle)**

> I am the kind of person who keeps everything. Movie ticket stubs, birthday cards, the wrapper from the first candy bar I ever bought with my own money. My desk drawer is an archaeological site — every layer is a different year. My best friend Deja says I am a hoarder. I prefer the word "archivist."
>
> The reason I keep things is not complicated: I do not trust my memory. Memories change. You remember a birthday party as perfect, and then you find a photograph and realize you were crying in half the pictures. You remember a friend as someone who was always there, and then you check the dates and realize they moved away when you were nine. Memory edits itself. Objects do not.
>
> When my grandmother moved into assisted living last spring, my parents asked me to help sort her house. Room by room, we packed boxes and decided what to keep and what to donate. In the attic, I found a shoebox labeled "1987" in my grandmother's handwriting. Inside were concert tickets, a hospital bracelet, a dried flower pressed between two pieces of wax paper, and a folded letter with no envelope.
>
> My father said, "Just toss it. She will never miss it."
>
> I put the box in the trunk of the car, under my jacket where nobody would notice. Not because I wanted to keep it for myself. But because someone had labeled it. Someone had climbed the attic stairs and placed it on a shelf and thought it mattered enough to save. And if that person could no longer protect it, then someone else should.
>
> I do not know whose hospital bracelet that is or what the concert sounded like or who wrote the letter. But I know this: those things survived because someone decided they were worth keeping. I refuse to be the person who undoes that decision.

**Directions:** Analyze how the author develops Noelle's point of view. For each question, cite specific evidence.

1. How does the author use word choice to develop Noelle's voice and personality? Identify at least two specific word choices and explain what they reveal about her.
2. How does the author use detail selection to show Noelle's values? What does she notice, and what does that tell us?
3. The passage includes a contrasting perspective: Noelle's father says "Just toss it." How does this contrast develop Noelle's point of view?
4. How does Noelle's internal monologue (her reasoning about memory, objects, and her grandmother's shoebox) develop her perspective across the passage? Trace how her thinking deepens from the opening paragraph to the final paragraph.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 with cited evidence.
**Stretch:** The final sentence is: "I refuse to be the person who undoes that decision." Analyze this sentence as both a character statement and a thematic statement. How does it serve both purposes simultaneously?
**Challenge:** Write a paragraph from the father's first-person perspective about the same scene in the attic. Use at least three of the five point-of-view development techniques to create a distinct voice that contrasts with Noelle's. Then explain your choices in a brief reflection.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.2) What is the theme of this passage? State it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.1) What inference can you draw about Noelle's relationship with her grandmother? Cite evidence that is not stated directly.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Gabriel walked into the science fair and immediately noticed the smell of vinegar — probably from the volcano projects, which were clustered near the entrance like a small, poorly planned mountain range. He counted four volcanoes. Last year, there had been seven. Progress, he thought. He walked past them quickly, looking for the table with the real experiments — the ones with data tables and control groups and hypotheses that could actually be wrong.

1. Name two techniques the author uses to develop Gabriel's point of view in this short passage.
2. What do these techniques reveal about Gabriel's personality and values?

**Expected Answers:**
1. (a) Word choice / tone: Gabriel describes the volcano projects as "a small, poorly planned mountain range" and thinks "Progress" sarcastically about there being fewer this year. The language is dry and witty, establishing a critical voice. (b) Detail selection: Gabriel looks past the volcanoes to find experiments "with data tables and control groups and hypotheses that could actually be wrong." He notices and values scientific rigor, which reveals his priorities.
2. Gabriel is intellectually rigorous, opinionated, and somewhat dismissive of projects he considers unserious. His sarcasm shows confidence in his own standards, while his interest in "hypotheses that could actually be wrong" reveals a mature understanding of what real science looks like. The techniques develop him as someone who values substance over spectacle.

**Mastery = Student identifies two specific techniques by name (word choice, detail selection, tone, internal monologue, or contrasting perspective) and explains what each reveals about the narrator's perspective or personality.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the five techniques with a matching activity: give the student five short excerpts, each demonstrating one technique, and ask them to label each one.
- Practice with the warm-up activity: rewrite a single cafeteria description using different word choices and details to create a new perspective, then identify which techniques changed.
- Provide a frame: "The author develops the narrator's point of view through [technique], as shown by the detail '___,' which reveals that the narrator ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10, which integrates all four standards.
- Encourage the student to read a chapter of their independent reading book and identify two techniques the author uses to develop the narrator's or main character's perspective.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Voice Collection:** The student finds three passages from different books that each have a strong, distinctive narrator voice. For each, they identify two techniques the author uses and explain how the techniques create the narrator's personality.
2. **Develop a Voice:** The student writes a one-paragraph description of their classroom from two different first-person narrators — one who loves school and one who does not. They must use at least three of the five techniques in each version and label them.
3. **Author Interview:** The student writes 3-4 interview questions they would ask the author of "The Archivist" about their choices in developing Noelle's voice. For each question, the student predicts what the author might say based on evidence from the text.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This lesson completes the RL.6.6 sequence.** Lesson 8 introduced point of view identification and effects; this lesson focuses on the author's techniques for developing it. Together, they prepare students for the integrated analysis in Lesson 10.
- **Voice and personality are the hooks.** Students are often drawn to narrators with strong personalities. Use this interest to teach technique — when a student says "I like this narrator," push them to explain HOW the author made the narrator likable (word choice? tone? details?).
- **Development across a passage matters.** Point of view is not static. Suki in the Worked Example begins cynical and softens. Noelle in the Independent Practice deepens her reasoning across paragraphs. Encourage students to track how perspective evolves, not just what it IS at any given moment.
- **Pacing note:** The Worked Example analysis covers all five techniques, which is intentionally thorough. In Guided Practice, students should identify at least three. Do not require all five in every analysis — the goal is recognizing and explaining techniques, not checking all boxes.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Word choice:** Noelle calls herself an "archivist" rather than accepting Deja's label of "hoarder." This word choice reveals her self-awareness and her desire to be seen as someone who preserves rather than simply accumulates. The distinction between "hoarder" and "archivist" shows she has thought carefully about how to frame her habit — she sees purpose where others see excess. Additionally, she describes her desk drawer as "an archaeological site" where "every layer is a different year," using language from history and science that elevates her collection from clutter to something worthy of study.
2. **Detail selection:** Noelle notices and describes the specific contents of her grandmother's shoebox: "concert tickets, a hospital bracelet, a dried flower pressed between two pieces of wax paper, and a folded letter with no envelope." She does not describe the shoebox as junk or clutter — she lists each item with attention, giving equal weight to each object. She also notices that the box was "labeled '1987' in my grandmother's handwriting," emphasizing the intentionality behind the saving. These detail choices reveal that Noelle values the act of preservation itself — she sees significance in the decision to keep, not just in the object kept.
3. The father's response — "Just toss it. She will never miss it" — represents the opposite perspective from Noelle's. For the father, the box has no current practical use and the grandmother will not remember it. This contrast sharpens Noelle's point of view: she does not argue against her father, but she takes the box anyway, placing it "under my jacket where nobody would notice." The contrast reveals that Noelle's commitment to preservation is strong enough to act on quietly, even when the people around her disagree. It also shows that her perspective is not just an abstract belief — it drives her behavior.
4. In the opening paragraph, Noelle's perspective is established as a personal quirk — she keeps ticket stubs and candy wrappers, and her friend calls her a hoarder. Her reasoning is somewhat casual: "I do not trust my memory." In the second paragraph, the perspective deepens philosophically — "Memory edits itself. Objects do not" — as she articulates a worldview, not just a habit. By the attic scene, her perspective is tested by a real situation with emotional weight: she must decide whether to preserve someone else's memories, not just her own. The final paragraph reaches its deepest point: "those things survived because someone decided they were worth keeping. I refuse to be the person who undoes that decision." The voice has developed from personal preference to philosophical conviction to moral commitment. The author develops the point of view through a deliberate progression from quirk to principle.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.5.2) Theme: The objects we choose to save become guardians of memories and decisions that deserve to be honored, and when the people who made those decisions can no longer protect them, it becomes someone else's responsibility to carry them forward. (Accept reasonable variations about the value of preservation, the relationship between objects and memory, or the responsibility of caring for others' stories.)
6. (RL.5.1) Noelle's relationship with her grandmother appears to be one of deep respect and emotional connection, even if the passage does not describe them interacting directly. Noelle's decision to secretly save the shoebox — especially the reasoning that "if that person could no longer protect it, then someone else should" — suggests she feels a responsibility toward her grandmother that goes beyond obligation. She treats her grandmother's past as sacred, even the parts she does not understand (she does not know whose bracelet it is or what the concert sounded like). This suggests Noelle sees her grandmother not just as a family member but as a person with a rich, independent history that deserves protection.
