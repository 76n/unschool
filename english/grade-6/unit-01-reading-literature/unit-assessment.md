# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 1: Reading Literature

## Unit Assessment

### Overview
This assessment evaluates student mastery of the four standards addressed in Unit 1:
- **RL.6.1** — Cite textual evidence to support analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the text
- **RL.6.2** — Determine a theme or central idea of a text and how it is conveyed through particular details; provide a summary of the text distinct from personal opinions or judgments
- **RL.6.5** — Analyze how a particular sentence, chapter, scene, or stanza fits into the overall structure of a text and contributes to the development of the theme, setting, or plot
- **RL.6.6** — Explain how an author develops the point of view of the narrator or speaker in a text

### Administration Notes
- **Estimated time:** 50-60 minutes
- **Format:** 5 sections with original passages, totaling approximately 25 questions
- **Question types:** Multiple choice, short answer, and extended response
- **Scoring:** Total = 60 points (see scoring guide)
- **Mastery threshold:** 70% overall (42/60) AND at least 50% in each standard
- Students may reread passages as needed
- Read directions aloud if needed for students with accommodations

## Section 1: "The Weaver" (Prose — Realistic Fiction)
*Standards assessed: RL.6.1 (Evidence/Inference), RL.6.2 (Theme)*

> Aminata's mother wove cloth on a loom that filled half the living room. The wooden frame stood as tall as the doorway, and the threads stretched across it like the strings of some enormous instrument. The shuttle flew back and forth in her mother's hands — a blur of wood and color — while Aminata sat at the kitchen table doing homework, the rhythmic clacking as familiar as a heartbeat.
>
> Every bolt of cloth her mother produced was different. She did not follow patterns. She chose colors the way a musician chooses notes — by instinct, by feeling, by whatever the morning light or the evening rain suggested. Customers from three towns away drove to their house to buy the cloth, but her mother never rushed. "I will not sell something that is not finished," she said. "Finished does not mean done weaving. Finished means the cloth knows what it is."
>
> Aminata did not understand what that meant until the spring she turned twelve. She had entered a photography competition at school — her first — and she spent weeks taking pictures of the neighborhood. She photographed the barbershop, the vacant lot, the mural on the overpass, the laundromat with its steamed windows. She took over two hundred photographs and then sat in front of her computer, trying to choose the best twelve.
>
> Every time she selected a photograph, she second-guessed it. She rearranged the twelve images over and over, changing the order, swapping one picture for another, deleting and restoring. Nothing felt right. The collection looked random, like snapshots, not like a story.
>
> One evening, her mother paused the loom and looked at the screen. She did not say anything about composition or lighting or subject matter. She pointed to three photographs — the barbershop at dawn, the vacant lot with a single dandelion, and an old woman standing at a bus stop in the rain — and said, "These three are the ones that know what they are."
>
> Aminata looked at the three photographs. She understood immediately. They were the ones that did not need explanation. They were complete — not perfect, but certain. Each one said something whole.
>
> She rebuilt the collection around those three. The final twelve photographs won second place. But the ribbon mattered less than the sentence she kept hearing in her head as she selected each image: *Does it know what it is?*

**Questions:**

**1.** (RL.6.1 — Multiple Choice — 2 points) Which of the following best states an inference supported by the text?

A. Aminata's mother is a professional weaver who sells cloth for a living.
B. Aminata's mother approaches her craft with the belief that art must reach its own completeness before it is shared.
C. Aminata's mother does not care about money and refuses to sell her cloth.
D. Aminata prefers photography to weaving because it is more modern.

**2.** (RL.6.1 — Short Answer — 3 points) The text says Aminata's mother chose colors "the way a musician chooses notes — by instinct, by feeling." What inference can you draw about the mother's creative process from this comparison? Cite additional evidence from the passage to support your inference.

**3.** (RL.6.2 — Short Answer — 3 points) What is the theme of this passage? State it as a complete sentence and explain how at least two specific details from the passage convey it.

**4.** (RL.6.2 — Short Answer — 3 points) Write an objective summary of this passage in 3-4 sentences. Your summary must be distinct from personal opinions or judgments.

**5.** (RL.6.1 — Short Answer — 3 points) What is the significance of the final sentence: "Does it know what it is?" How does this question connect the mother's weaving philosophy to Aminata's photography?

## Section 2: "The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter" (Historical Fiction — Two Scenes)
*Standards assessed: RL.6.5 (Structure), RL.6.2 (Theme Development)*

> **Scene 1 — November 1891:**
>
> The storm hit the coast at four in the morning. Clara heard the wind change before she heard the thunder — a low, rushing sound that made the lighthouse shudder on its stone foundation. She pulled on her boots and climbed the seventy-two iron stairs to the lamp room, where her father's whale oil lamp burned in the center of the great Fresnel lens.
>
> The flame was steady. It always was. Her father had taught her that the flame was the one thing that could not waver, no matter what the night brought. Ships in the dark had nothing else to steer by.
>
> But tonight, the glass on the seaward side of the lens was cracked. A line ran through it like a frozen river, splitting the light into two beams instead of one. Clara ran her finger along the crack. It must have happened during the wind — something striking the glass, a branch or a stone carried up from the cliff.
>
> She could not fix the lens. She was fourteen years old, and the replacement glass was on a supply ship that would not come for two weeks. But the light had to reach the water in one beam, not two. A split beam could confuse a ship's navigator, could send them toward the rocks instead of away from them.
>
> Clara took her father's heaviest coat, the one lined with wool, and climbed outside onto the gallery walkway. The wind hit her like a wall. She wedged herself against the railing and held the coat over the cracked section of glass, blocking the second beam. The coat flapped violently. Her arms ached.
>
> She held it there until dawn.
>
> ---
>
> **Scene 2 — The Next Morning:**
>
> When the storm passed and the gray light of morning replaced the darkness, Clara's father found her slumped against the gallery railing, the coat still pressed against the glass. Her hands were numb and her lips were blue.
>
> He carried her downstairs without speaking. He wrapped her in blankets and heated water for tea. When she could feel her fingers again, she said, "The light needed to be one beam."
>
> Her father nodded. He did not praise her. He did not scold her for going outside in the storm. He looked at the cracked glass and then at his daughter, and he said something she would remember for the rest of her life: "There are people who wait for the storm to pass. And there are people who hold the light steady."
>
> Clara drank her tea. Outside, the sea was calm. A fishing boat crossed the horizon, heading home.

**Questions:**

**6.** (RL.6.5 — Short Answer — 3 points) Analyze the scene break between Scene 1 and Scene 2. What does each scene accomplish, and why does the author choose to break between Clara's night on the gallery and her father finding her the next morning?

**7.** (RL.6.5 — Multiple Choice — 2 points) What is the structural function of the final two sentences ("Clara drank her tea. Outside, the sea was calm. A fishing boat crossed the horizon, heading home.")?

A. They introduce a new conflict that will be explored in a sequel.
B. They provide a calm resolution that contrasts with the storm in Scene 1, reinforcing that Clara's action had real consequences for real people.
C. They show that Clara's efforts were unnecessary because the storm was going to pass anyway.
D. They shift the focus to Clara's father's perspective.

**8.** (RL.6.2 — Short Answer — 3 points) How does the theme develop from Scene 1 to Scene 2? Identify what the theme is and trace how it is introduced, tested, and crystallized across the two scenes.

**9.** (RL.6.5 — Short Answer — 3 points) The passage says Clara "held it there until dawn." This sentence is the last line of Scene 1. Analyze this sentence's placement. Why does the author end Scene 1 here rather than continuing into the morning? What effect does this create?

## Section 3: "Two Trains" (Paired Passages — Poetry)
*Standards assessed: RL.6.6 (Point of View), RL.6.1 (Evidence)*

**Poem A: "Leaving"**

> The train pulls away from the platform
> and I press my forehead to the glass,
> watching the town shrink —
> first the buildings, then the water tower,
> then the church steeple,
> until the whole place fits inside
> the palm of my hand,
> and then inside a thimble,
> and then inside nothing at all.
>
> I close my eyes.
> The town is still there
> behind my eyelids,
> full-sized and loud and warm,
> the way it always was
> when I was in it.
>
> I wonder which version is real —
> the one disappearing through the window,
> or the one I carry with me,
> growing brighter the farther I go.

**Poem B: "Arriving"**

> The train slows and I see a town
> I have never seen before —
> squat buildings, a water tower,
> a church steeple rising
> like a finger pointing at the clouds.
>
> Everything looks the same as the town I left
> but none of it is mine.
> The faces on the platform
> are waiting for someone,
> but not for me.
>
> I step off the train
> and the air is different —
> colder, with a sharpness
> that catches in my throat
> like a word I do not know
> in a language I am learning.
>
> I pick up my bag.
> The platform is gray.
> The sky is wide.
> Everything is possible
> and nothing is certain.
> I walk forward.

A. The speaker does not speak the local language.

## Section 4: "The Debate" (Drama Excerpt)
*Standards assessed: RL.6.5 (Structure), RL.6.6 (Point of View)*

> SCENE: A classroom. Two students, ROSA and ISAAC, stand behind podiums. Their classmates sit in rows. MS. KIMURA, the teacher, sits at her desk with a timer.
>
> MS. KIMURA: The topic is: "Should the school replace the library's physical books with digital versions?" Rosa, you argue in favor. Isaac, you argue against. Two minutes each, then rebuttals. Rosa, begin.
>
> ROSA: (confidently) Physical books take up space. Our library has exactly twelve hundred books, but it could have access to twelve million through a digital platform. Students could search for any title instantly instead of waiting for a book to be returned. The world is digital. Our library should be too.
>
> ISAAC: (quietly, turning a page in a notebook) May I ask Rosa a question before I begin?
>
> MS. KIMURA: (checking the timer) Briefly.
>
> ISAAC: Rosa, when was the last time you went to the library?
>
> (Pause. ROSA looks at MS. KIMURA, then back at ISAAC.)
>
> ROSA: That is not relevant to the argument.
>
> ISAAC: (to the class, still quiet) I go to the library every day during lunch. Not because I have to — because it is the only room in this school that is quiet. The chairs are soft. The light is warm. There is a window next to the biography section where you can see the oak tree in the courtyard. Ms. Okonkwo, the librarian, knows my name. She saves new books she thinks I will like and leaves them on the table by the window.
>
> (He pauses. Looks down at his notebook, then up.)
>
> A digital platform does not know my name. It does not save anything for me. It does not have a window or a chair or a person who asks, "How did you like the last one?" Rosa is right that digital libraries have more titles. But a room full of books is not just a delivery system for information. It is a place. And some of us need places.
>
> (Silence. MS. KIMURA's timer goes off. She does not stop it immediately.)
>
> MS. KIMURA: (quietly) Thank you, Isaac. Rosa, your rebuttal?
>
> ROSA: (after a long pause) I... I want to change my argument. Not my position — my argument. Give me a minute.

**Questions:**

**14.** (RL.6.6 — Short Answer — 3 points) The author develops Rosa's and Isaac's points of view through different techniques. Identify the primary technique used for each character and explain how it shapes the reader's understanding of their positions.

**15.** (RL.6.5 — Short Answer — 3 points) Analyze the stage direction "(Silence. MS. KIMURA's timer goes off. She does not stop it immediately.)" What does this structural detail contribute to the scene? How does it affect the reader's experience of Isaac's speech?

**16.** (RL.6.5 — Multiple Choice — 2 points) Rosa's final line is: "I want to change my argument. Not my position — my argument. Give me a minute." What is the structural significance of ending the scene here?

A. It shows Rosa has lost the debate and is giving up.
B. It creates an unresolved ending that suggests Isaac's speech has changed how Rosa approaches the issue, leaving the reader to consider both sides.
C. It proves that physical books are better than digital books.
D. It indicates that Ms. Kimura will declare Isaac the winner.

**17.** (RL.6.6 — Short Answer — 3 points) Isaac's speech shifts from a personal narrative ("I go to the library every day during lunch") to a broader argument ("a room full of books is not just a delivery system for information. It is a place. And some of us need places"). How does the author use this shift to develop Isaac's point of view? What effect does it have on the reader?

## Section 5: "The Inheritance" (Prose — Extended Response)
*Standards assessed: All four standards (RL.6.1, RL.6.2, RL.6.5, RL.6.6)*

> My grandfather built clocks. Not the kind you buy at the store — the kind with brass gears and glass faces and pendulums that swung with a precision he calibrated by ear. He said he could hear when a clock was running correctly the way a piano tuner hears when a string is in tune.
>
> His workshop was in the barn behind the house. I spent most of my childhood summers there, sitting on a stool beside his workbench while he assembled movements — the internal mechanism that makes a clock tick. He explained every step, even when I was too young to understand. "I am not teaching you to fix clocks," he said once, tightening a mainspring with a pair of needle-nose pliers. "I am teaching you to pay attention."
>
> When I was eleven, he gave me a clock to assemble on my own. He set the parts on the bench — gears, springs, hands, a brass casing — and sat in the corner reading a newspaper. I worked for four hours. I got the gears wrong three times. Each time, I disassembled and started over, following the sequence he had shown me but adjusting for my own hands, which were smaller and less steady than his.
>
> When I finished, the clock ticked. It was not perfectly calibrated — the pendulum swung a fraction too wide, and the hands were a minute slow. But it ticked.
>
> My grandfather lowered his newspaper, listened, and said, "Good. Now take it apart and do it again."
>
> I stared at him. "It works."
>
> "It works," he agreed. "But you do not understand it yet. When you can build it without thinking about the sequence — when your hands know the order without your brain telling them — then you understand it."
>
> I took it apart. I built it again. And again. By the fifth time, my hands moved through the assembly the way his did — not fast, but steady, certain, as if the gears belonged in a specific order and my fingers had learned to find it.
>
> That clock sits on my dresser now. It still runs a minute slow. I never fixed it, because the imperfection reminds me of the first time it ticked — the surprise of it, the pride, and then the harder lesson: that getting something to work is not the same as understanding how it works.

**Questions:**

**18.** (RL.6.1 — Short Answer — 3 points) The grandfather says, "I am not teaching you to fix clocks. I am teaching you to pay attention." Cite this evidence and explain what inference you can draw about the grandfather's deeper purpose in sharing his craft with his grandchild.

**19.** (RL.6.2 — Extended Response — 5 points) Determine the theme of this passage and trace how it develops from the beginning to the end. Your response must:
- State the theme as a complete sentence
- Identify at least three specific moments where the theme is introduced, complicated, or crystallized
- Explain how each moment contributes to the theme's development
- Be written in your own words (no copied sentences from the text without quotation marks)

**20.** (RL.6.5 — Short Answer — 3 points) The passage is structured around repetition — the narrator builds the clock five times. Why does the author include this repetition rather than showing only the first assembly and the final result? How does the structural choice contribute to the theme?

**21.** (RL.6.6 — Short Answer — 3 points) The passage is narrated in first person by the grandchild, looking back on a childhood experience. How does the author develop the narrator's point of view? Identify two specific techniques and explain their effect.

**22.** (RL.6.5 — Short Answer — 3 points) Analyze the final sentence: "getting something to work is not the same as understanding how it works." Why does the author place this sentence at the very end? What does it contribute to the overall structure and theme?

### Section 2: "The Lighthouse Keeper's Daughter"

**6.** (RL.6.5 — 3 points) Scene 1 establishes the crisis (cracked lens creating a split beam), Clara's resourcefulness (using the coat to block the second beam), and the physical ordeal of holding the coat through the night. It builds tension and shows Clara's determination. Scene 2 shows the aftermath — Clara's physical cost (numb hands, blue lips), her father's response, and the calm that follows. The scene break between Clara's night on the gallery and her father's discovery the next morning skips the long hours of holding the coat. This is significant because the author does not dramatize the suffering itself — instead, the break compresses the ordeal and lets the reader imagine it, which can be more powerful than describing it. The break also shifts from action (Clara holding the coat) to reflection (the father's words about holding the light steady).

**7.** (RL.6.5 — 2 points) **B.** They provide a calm resolution that contrasts with the storm in Scene 1, reinforcing that Clara's action had real consequences for real people.

**8.** (RL.6.2 — 3 points) The theme is that there are people who endure hardship to protect others, and their quiet, unseen actions hold the world steady during its most dangerous moments. In Scene 1, the theme is introduced through Clara's problem — the cracked lens could confuse ships — and her decision to act alone despite the danger. It is tested through the physical ordeal of holding the coat through the storm all night. In Scene 2, the theme is crystallized in the father's words: "There are people who wait for the storm to pass. And there are people who hold the light steady." This sentence encapsulates Clara's identity as someone who acts rather than waits. The final image of the fishing boat heading home confirms that her action mattered — someone made it through the night because of her.

**9.** (RL.6.5 — 3 points) Ending Scene 1 with "She held it there until dawn" compresses an entire night of suffering into one sentence. The brevity is the point: by not describing the cold, the pain, the hours of darkness, the author lets the reader fill in the enormity of the ordeal. The sentence is stark and final, matching Clara's determination — there is no complaint, no wavering, just the fact that she held on. Continuing into the morning within the same scene would dilute the impact by blending the crisis and the resolution. By breaking the scene here, the author forces the reader to pause and sit with the image of Clara alone on the gallery, coat pressed against the glass, before learning what happened next.

### Section 5: "The Inheritance"

**18.** (RL.6.1 — 3 points) The grandfather's statement — "I am not teaching you to fix clocks. I am teaching you to pay attention" — suggests his deeper purpose is not vocational training but the cultivation of a way of being in the world. The inference is that the grandfather views clockwork as a vehicle for teaching patience, precision, and the discipline of close observation — skills that apply far beyond clocks. This is supported by his insistence that the narrator rebuild the clock five times: he does not stop at "it works" because his goal is not a functioning clock but a person who understands process deeply enough to work without conscious deliberation. The grandfather is passing on a philosophy, not a trade.

**19.** (RL.6.2 — 5 points) Theme: True understanding of a craft — or any skill — comes not from achieving a result but from repeating the process until it becomes part of who you are, because mastery is not about the product but about the depth of knowledge embedded in the maker.

The theme is introduced when the grandfather says, "I am not teaching you to fix clocks. I am teaching you to pay attention." This establishes that the purpose of the work is not the clock itself but the discipline it cultivates — the theme begins with the idea that a skill serves a deeper purpose.

The theme is complicated when the narrator finishes the clock and it ticks, but the grandfather says, "Now take it apart and do it again." The narrator protests — "It works" — and the grandfather agrees but insists that working is not the same as understanding. This moment challenges the narrator's (and the reader's) assumption that a functioning result equals mastery. The theme deepens: results are not the goal; understanding is.

The theme is crystallized in the final paragraph, where the narrator keeps the clock "a minute slow" because "the imperfection reminds me of the first time it ticked — the surprise of it, the pride, and then the harder lesson: that getting something to work is not the same as understanding how it works." The narrator has come to value the process and the lesson embedded in the imperfection. The clock is both a timepiece and a monument to the learning process, and the theme reaches its fullest expression: mastery lives not in the finished product but in the practitioner's journey toward deep understanding.

**20.** (RL.6.5 — 3 points) The author includes the repetition of five assemblies rather than showing only the first and last because the repetition IS the theme. The passage argues that understanding comes from doing something repeatedly until it becomes instinctive — "when your hands know the order without your brain telling them." Showing only the first assembly and the final result would demonstrate a before-and-after, but it would skip the process that gives the passage its meaning. By describing the repeated disassembly and reassembly, the author makes the reader experience the patience and persistence required. Structurally, the repetition mirrors the grandfather's teaching method: just as the narrator must repeat the process to understand it, the reader must sit through the description of repetition to grasp why it matters. The structure embodies the theme.

**21.** (RL.6.6 — 3 points) The author develops the narrator's point of view through two techniques: (1) Retrospective reflection — the narrator is looking back on a childhood experience from an older perspective. Phrases like "I spent most of my childhood summers there" and "That clock sits on my dresser now" signal temporal distance, allowing the narrator to understand lessons they could not fully grasp at the time. This technique creates a voice that is both nostalgic (the warmth of the workshop, the grandfather's presence) and analytical (understanding the grandfather's methods in hindsight). (2) Internal monologue — the narrator shares their feelings during the experience, such as the "surprise" and "pride" of the first ticking, and then the "harder lesson" that followed. This technique develops the narrator as someone who processes experiences in layers — first the emotional reaction, then the intellectual understanding — showing a mind that values reflection.

**22.** (RL.6.5 — 3 points) The author places "getting something to work is not the same as understanding how it works" at the very end because it is the passage's thesis — the sentence that all the preceding events have been building toward. Structurally, the passage progresses from action (building the clock) through conflict (the grandfather's insistence on rebuilding) to reflection (keeping the slow clock), and this final sentence distills the entire experience into one insight. Placing it last gives it the weight of a conclusion — the reader has witnessed the process and is now given the lesson. It also mirrors the grandfather's teaching method: he did not state the principle first and then demonstrate it. He let the experience happen and then allowed the understanding to emerge. The sentence's position at the end enacts the very idea it describes: understanding comes after the doing.

### Scoring Notes

**Multiple-choice questions (1, 7, 13, 16):** 2 points each. Full credit for correct answer only.

**Short-answer questions (2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 15, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22):** 3 points each, scored as follows:
- 3 points: Complete, accurate response with cited evidence and clear explanation
- 2 points: Mostly accurate response with evidence but explanation is partial or vague
- 1 point: Response shows basic understanding but lacks evidence or explanation
- 0 points: Response is inaccurate, off-topic, or missing

**Summary question (4):** 3 points, scored as follows:
- 3 points: Objective summary with key events and theme, no personal opinions
- 2 points: Summary includes key events but either misses the theme or includes minor opinion language
- 1 point: Incomplete summary or contains significant opinion language
- 0 points: Not a summary (pure opinion or unrelated content)

**Extended response (19):** 5 points, scored as follows:
- 5 points: Theme stated as complete sentence; three or more specific moments identified and explained; clear connection between moments and theme development; written in student's own words
- 4 points: Theme stated accurately; two moments identified and well-explained; mostly clear connection to theme development
- 3 points: Theme stated but may be vague or incomplete; at least one moment identified; some connection to development
- 2 points: Theme partially identified; limited evidence; weak or unclear development analysis
- 1 point: Attempt made but theme is a topic (single word) or evidence is absent
- 0 points: No attempt or completely off-topic

**General scoring principles:**
- For quoting/evidence questions, the student must use quotation marks correctly around exact text. Minor punctuation errors within the citation should not cost full credit if the quote itself is accurate.
- Accept reasonable variations in theme statements as long as the student expresses a complete thought (not just a topic word) that is supported by the text.
- For inference questions, the student must provide a logical conclusion supported by text evidence. Unsupported guesses do not earn credit.
- For structural analysis questions, the student must name the structural element, describe its function, and explain its contribution to theme, setting, or plot.
- For point of view questions, the student must identify the perspective or technique and explain its effect on the reader's experience.
