# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 6

## Topic
Analyzing How Parts Fit the Whole

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify structural elements in a text (opening scene, flashback, key sentence, pivotal paragraph)
- Analyze how a specific sentence or passage fits into the overall structure of a text
- Explain how a structural choice contributes to the development of theme, setting, or plot
- Articulate why an author placed a particular element where they did and what effect it creates

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine theme and trace its development through a text (Lessons 3-4, RL.6.2)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lessons 1-2, RL.6.1)
- Understanding of basic story structure (beginning, middle, end; conflict and resolution)
- Experience identifying key events and turning points in a narrative

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils and colored pencils
- Highlighters in two colors
- Sticky notes (for marking structural elements)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept of structural analysis by using a familiar analogy.

**Activity: Why This Piece Here?**

Say: "For the last five lessons, we have been working with what texts mean — evidence, inferences, themes. Starting today, we are going to focus on how texts are built. Think about a building. Every wall, window, and beam is placed where it is for a reason. If you moved a support beam to a different spot, the building might collapse. Stories work the same way."

Write on the board:
- **Structure** = How a text is organized — the arrangement of its parts
- **Structural analysis** = Examining WHY an author placed a particular sentence, scene, or section where they did, and what it contributes to the whole

Say: "Here is a quick example. Imagine a story about a girl who wins a race. The very first sentence is: 'Before today, Mira had never finished anything she started.' Why would the author begin with that sentence? Talk to a partner for thirty seconds."

Expected responses: The sentence creates contrast — it tells us that Mira has a history of quitting, which makes her eventual victory more meaningful. Starting with this sentence sets up the theme (perseverance) and raises a question (will she finish this time?). It shapes how we read everything that follows.

Say: "That is structural analysis. You are not just asking what the sentence means — you are asking what it DOES for the story. Today, we are going to practice that kind of thinking."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to analyze how specific sentences and passages fit into a text's overall structure and contribute to theme, setting, or plot.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Structure:** The way a text is organized and arranged — including the order of events, paragraph breaks, scene divisions, and placement of key sentences.
- **Flashback:** A scene that interrupts the present narrative to show events from the past, often used to provide background or explain a character's motivation.
- **Foreshadowing:** A hint or clue early in the text about something that will happen later, creating suspense or deeper meaning.
- **Pivotal sentence:** A key sentence in a text that shifts the tone, direction, or meaning of the narrative — a structural turning point.
- **Contribute:** To add to or help develop something — a structural element contributes to the whole by shaping how the reader experiences the story.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "The Grade 6 standard for structure says: analyze how a particular sentence, chapter, scene, or stanza fits into the overall structure of a text and contributes to the development of the theme, setting, or plot. That is a lot of words, but the core question is simple."

Write on the board:
**The Structure Question:** What does this part DO for the whole?

Say: "Every part of a text does at least one of three things."

Write on the board:
1. **Develops the theme** — This part builds, complicates, or reveals the theme.
2. **Develops the setting** — This part establishes, shifts, or deepens the world of the story.
3. **Develops the plot** — This part advances the action, creates conflict, or resolves tension.

Say: "When you analyze structure, you name the part, describe what it does, and explain why it matters. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Storm Jar"**

> *Paragraph 1:* Nadira kept a jar on her windowsill filled with small objects she had collected during storms — a twig stripped bare by wind, a stone washed smooth by floodwater, a piece of blue glass that had been part of someone's window before the hurricane two years ago.
>
> *Paragraph 2:* Her therapist, Dr. Osei, had suggested it. "When you feel the anxiety building," he said during one of their sessions, "hold one of the objects. Focus on its texture. Let it remind you that storms end."
>
> *Paragraph 3:* Nadira did not always believe storms ended. Some days, the sky outside her window was perfectly clear, and she still felt the pressure dropping inside her chest, the way it did before a real storm. She would reach for the jar and roll the smooth stone between her fingers, counting her breaths the way Dr. Osei had taught her.
>
> *Paragraph 4:* One afternoon in October, a real storm rolled in — dark clouds stacking up over the coast, the trees bending sideways. Nadira watched from the window. She expected the familiar tightness in her chest. Instead, she felt something she had not felt during a storm in two years: calm. Not the forced calm of counting breaths, but the real kind — the kind that comes from having survived before and knowing she would survive again.
>
> *Paragraph 5:* When the storm passed, Nadira opened the window and found a small white feather on the sill, damp and bent but whole. She picked it up, smoothed its edges, and dropped it into the jar. The jar was almost full now.

Say: "Let me analyze two structural elements from this passage."

Write on the board:

**Analysis 1 — Paragraph 2 (The flashback to Dr. Osei's session):**
This paragraph is a flashback — it interrupts the present to show a past conversation. It fits into the overall structure by providing essential background: without it, the reader would not understand why Nadira has the jar or what the objects mean to her. It develops the plot by explaining her coping mechanism and develops the theme by introducing the idea that "storms end" — a statement that the rest of the passage will test and ultimately prove true.

**Analysis 2 — The final sentence: "The jar was almost full now."**
This sentence closes the passage and works on two levels. Literally, the jar has accumulated many storm objects. Symbolically, the jar represents Nadira's collection of survived experiences. The sentence contributes to the theme by suggesting that each storm she endures adds to her resilience — the jar being "almost full" implies she is nearing a kind of completeness or readiness. Placing this sentence last gives it emphasis: it is the note the reader is left with.

Say: "Notice that I named the structural element, described what it does, and explained how it contributes to the theme and plot. That is the pattern we will follow."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice analyzing structural elements with facilitator support.

Say: "Read this passage silently. I have marked four structural elements with labels. After reading, we will analyze each one together."

**Passage: "The Cartographer"**

> *[Element A — Opening sentence:]* Diego's father drew maps of places that no longer existed.
>
> He worked at a large drafting table in the spare bedroom, using fine-tipped pens and a magnifying glass. The maps showed streets, parks, and buildings from towns that had been flooded by reservoir projects, demolished for highways, or abandoned after mines closed. He drew from photographs, oral histories, and his own memory.
>
> *[Element B — Middle paragraph:]* Diego used to think the maps were pointless. "Why draw a place if nobody can go there?" he asked once. His father looked up from the drafting table and said, "Because if nobody draws it, nobody remembers it. And if nobody remembers it, it never existed at all."
>
> Diego was ten when his father drew the map of Aguaverde, the village where his grandmother had been born. It had been flooded when the government built a dam in 1972. Diego's grandmother never spoke about it. But his father recreated it from fragments — a photograph of the church, a list of street names his grandmother had recited once, the memory of a fountain she had described to him as a child.
>
> *[Element C — Pivotal sentence:]* When Diego showed the finished map to his grandmother, she put on her reading glasses and traced the streets with her finger, saying the names aloud as if she were walking through the village again.
>
> *[Element D — Final paragraph:]* That night, Diego found his father still at the drafting table, starting a new map. "What place is this one?" Diego asked. His father smiled. "I do not know yet. Your grandmother just told me about a bakery she used to visit. I am drawing outward from there."

Say: "Let us analyze each element. For Element A, what does the opening sentence do for the story?"

Expected response for Element A: The opening sentence — "Diego's father drew maps of places that no longer existed" — immediately establishes the central tension of the passage: the conflict between what is lost and what can be preserved. It sets up the theme (memory and preservation) and creates curiosity (why would someone map places that are gone?). Placing this as the very first sentence signals that this idea is the heart of the story.

Say: "Good. Now discuss Element B with a partner — the middle paragraph where Diego questions his father. What does this exchange do for the structure?"

Expected response for Element B: Diego's question ("Why draw a place if nobody can go there?") represents the reader's own doubt and introduces a complication to the theme. The father's response — "Because if nobody draws it, nobody remembers it. And if nobody remembers it, it never existed at all" — directly states the philosophical foundation of the theme. Structurally, this exchange serves as the intellectual center of the passage, setting up the emotional payoff that comes later when the grandmother sees the map.

Discuss Elements C and D similarly, guiding students to see how C provides the emotional turning point and D extends the story forward, suggesting the work continues.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how structural elements contribute to a text's meaning.

**Passage: "The Last Practice"**

> *Paragraph 1:* Coach Herrera blew the whistle one final time, and the sound echoed off the empty bleachers. It was the last practice of the season — the last practice, in fact, that would ever be held on this field. The school district had voted to sell the land to a developer, and by next September, the field would be a parking lot.
>
> *Paragraph 2:* The team stood in a loose circle at midfield. Nobody was stretching. Nobody was checking the time. For once, nobody was in a hurry to leave.
>
> *Paragraph 3:* Amara remembered the first day she walked onto this field as a sixth grader, carrying her cleats in a plastic bag because she did not have a proper bag. She had been terrified. The grass was patchy and the goalposts were crooked, but it had felt like the most important place in the world.
>
> *Paragraph 4:* Coach Herrera looked at the team and opened her mouth to speak, then closed it. She bent down, scooped a handful of dirt from the center circle, and let it fall through her fingers. Then she brushed off her hands and said, "Same time tomorrow. Wherever we are."
>
> *Paragraph 5:* Nobody moved. The sun was low, the shadows long. Amara looked at the field — the worn patches in front of each goal, the rut near the corner flag where everyone planted their foot to take corners, the faded white lines that had been repainted every August since before she was born. She bent down and scooped her own handful of dirt. Then, one by one, so did everyone else.

**Directions:** Analyze the following structural elements. For each one, explain what it does for the overall text and how it contributes to the development of the theme, setting, or plot.

1. Analyze Paragraph 1. How does the opening paragraph establish the context for the rest of the passage? What tone does it set?
2. Analyze Paragraph 3 (Amara's memory). This is a flashback. Why does the author place it here, between the team's silence and the coach's action? What does it contribute?
3. Analyze Coach Herrera's action in Paragraph 4 — scooping and releasing the dirt. Why does the author include this gesture before the coach speaks? How does it shape what comes next?
4. Analyze the final sentence: "Then, one by one, so did everyone else." How does this sentence fit into the overall structure, and what does it contribute to the theme?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 with at least one cited piece of evidence per response.
**Stretch:** For question 3, compare the coach's action (scooping dirt) with her words ("Same time tomorrow. Wherever we are."). How do the gesture and the words work together? Could the passage have the same effect with only one or the other?
**Challenge:** Write a paragraph arguing that the most structurally important element in this passage is either the flashback in Paragraph 3 OR the final sentence in Paragraph 5. Explain why the element you chose is more essential to the passage's theme than the other.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.2) What is the theme of "The Last Practice"? State it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.1) What inference can you draw about Coach Herrera's emotional state in Paragraph 4? Cite evidence and explain your reasoning.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> The story opened with a description of the empty house. Then, in the second paragraph, the author wrote: "But the mailbox still had their name on it — SANTOS — in hand-painted letters that Rosa's mother had brushed on the first day they moved in, twelve years ago." The rest of the story followed Rosa as she returned to the house to collect the last of her family's belongings.

1. What does the sentence about the mailbox do for the story's structure?
2. Does it contribute to theme, setting, or plot? Explain.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The sentence about the mailbox bridges the empty present and the lived past. Structurally, it interrupts the description of emptiness with a specific, personal detail that connects the house to the family's history. It serves as a transition from the general (empty house) to the specific (Rosa's family and their twelve years there).
2. It contributes to both setting and theme. For setting, the hand-painted letters and the twelve-year timespan ground the house in a specific family history, making the emptiness feel like a loss rather than just a fact. For theme, it suggests that even when people leave a place, traces of their presence remain — the mailbox still carries the name, implying that identity persists even in absence.

**Mastery = Student identifies what the structural element does (its function) and explains how it contributes to theme, setting, or plot with a specific reason.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Simplify the analysis by asking: "What would the story lose if this sentence were removed?" If the student can articulate the loss, they understand the function.
- Practice with a familiar text: use a passage from an earlier lesson and ask the student to analyze one specific sentence's role in the structure.
- Provide a frame: "This [sentence/paragraph/scene] contributes to the [theme/setting/plot] by ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7, which extends structural analysis to chapters and scenes.
- Encourage the student to mark one "pivotal sentence" in each chapter of their independent reading book and note what it does for the story.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Structural Remix:** The student takes the passage "The Last Practice" and rewrites the opening using a different structural choice — for example, starting with the flashback in Paragraph 3 instead of the present-day scene. They then write a paragraph explaining how the change affects the reader's experience.
2. **Sentence Surgery:** The student selects three key sentences from a book they are reading and explains, for each one, what it contributes to the structure and what would be lost if it were removed.
3. **Author's Blueprint:** The student creates a visual "blueprint" of the passage — a diagram showing how each paragraph connects to the others and what each one contributes. They use arrows, labels, and color-coding to show the flow of the structure.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This is a new skill area for many students.** While theme and evidence are familiar from Grade 5, structural analysis at the sentence/scene level is a Grade 6 introduction (RL.6.5). Expect students to need more modeling and guided practice than in earlier lessons.
- **"What would the story lose?" is the most effective question.** When students struggle to articulate what a structural element does, ask them to imagine the text without it. The absence reveals the function.
- **Connect structure to author's choices.** Remind students that every structural decision — where to begin, when to flashback, which sentence to end on — is an intentional choice by the author. Structural analysis is about understanding those choices.
- **Pacing note:** The Guided Practice in this lesson involves four labeled elements, which may take longer than usual. If time is tight, focus on Elements A and C (opening and turning point), as these are the most generalizable structural analysis skills.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Paragraph 1 establishes that this is the last practice on a field that will soon be destroyed. The phrase "the sound echoed off the empty bleachers" sets a tone of finality and emptiness. By revealing immediately that "the field would be a parking lot," the author creates a sense of loss that colors everything that follows. This context transforms the ordinary action of a team practice into something significant — every detail the reader notices from this point on carries the weight of being the last time.
2. The flashback to Amara's first day on the field is placed between the team's uncharacteristic stillness (Paragraph 2) and the coach's pivotal action (Paragraph 4). Structurally, it provides an emotional bridge — it explains WHY the team is not in a hurry to leave by showing what this field means through personal memory. The detail that the "grass was patchy and the goalposts were crooked, but it had felt like the most important place in the world" reinforces the theme that a place's value comes from the experiences it holds, not its physical appearance. Placing the flashback here deepens the reader's understanding of the loss before the coach responds to it.
3. The coach's gesture of scooping and releasing dirt is placed before her words, which creates a powerful structural effect. The physical action communicates what words cannot — a tactile farewell to the field itself. By letting the dirt "fall through her fingers," the coach embodies the theme of letting go while still honoring what is being lost. The gesture gives weight and sincerity to her subsequent words ("Same time tomorrow. Wherever we are"), because the reader has already seen her express emotion physically. Without the gesture, the words might feel like a simple coaching cliche; with it, they feel like a deliberate choice to look forward rather than dwell on loss.
4. The final sentence — "Then, one by one, so did everyone else" — mirrors the coach's action across the entire team. Structurally, it serves as the passage's resolution: the team responds to the coach's gesture not with words but with the same physical act, creating a silent ritual of farewell. This contributes to the theme by showing that shared experiences create shared responses — the team does not need to discuss what the field means to them; they express it through the same instinctive gesture. The phrase "one by one" emphasizes that each person makes an individual choice to participate, giving the moment personal significance within a collective act.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.5.2) Theme: The places that shape us carry their meaning not in their physical condition but in the experiences and memories they hold, and saying goodbye to those places requires honoring what they meant to us. (Accept reasonable variations about the significance of shared spaces, or about how communities grieve losses together.)
6. (RL.5.1) Coach Herrera is deeply moved but chooses action over words. She "opened her mouth to speak, then closed it," suggesting she has strong feelings she cannot or chooses not to articulate verbally. Instead, she scoops dirt — a physical, tactile farewell. This suggests her emotional state is complex: she feels the loss as deeply as anyone but channels it into a gesture rather than a speech, demonstrating the same resilience she has likely taught her team.
