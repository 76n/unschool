# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 10

## Topic
Reading Literature Integrated Analysis

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply all four unit standards (RL.6.1, RL.6.2, RL.6.5, RL.6.6) to a single literary text
- Write an integrated analytical response that cites evidence, identifies theme, analyzes structure, and explains point of view
- Demonstrate the ability to move fluidly between different analytical lenses when examining a passage
- Synthesize multiple analytical skills into a cohesive written response

## Prerequisites
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis and inferences (Lessons 1-2, RL.6.1)
- Ability to determine theme and trace its development through specific details (Lessons 3-5, RL.6.2)
- Ability to analyze how structural elements contribute to theme, setting, or plot (Lessons 6-7, RL.6.5)
- Ability to explain how an author develops the narrator's point of view (Lessons 8-9, RL.6.6)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils and colored pencils (for annotation)
- Highlighters in four colors (one per standard)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all four standards and prepare students for integrated analysis.

**Activity: The Four Lenses**

Say: "Over the past nine lessons, you have developed four powerful analytical skills. Today, you are going to use all of them together — like putting on four different pairs of glasses and looking at the same text through each one."

Write on the board — **The Four Lenses:**
1. **RL.6.1 — Evidence:** What does the text say, and what can I infer? What specific evidence supports my analysis?
2. **RL.6.2 — Theme:** What is the theme, and how is it conveyed through particular details?
3. **RL.6.5 — Structure:** How does the arrangement of sentences, scenes, or sections contribute to the text's meaning?
4. **RL.6.6 — Point of View:** How does the author develop the narrator's or speaker's perspective?

Say: "Good literary analysis does not use just one lens. Strong readers move between these lenses, connecting them to build a complete understanding. Today, we are going to practice that integration."

Write on the board — **Color-Coding for Annotation:**
- Yellow = Evidence (RL.6.1)
- Green = Theme (RL.6.2)
- Blue = Structure (RL.6.5)
- Pink = Point of View (RL.6.6)

Say: "As you read today's passages, use these four colors to annotate. Some sentences may deserve more than one color — that is a sign that the standards are working together."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model integrated analysis by applying all four lenses to a single passage.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Integrated analysis:** Examining a text through multiple analytical lenses simultaneously, showing how evidence, theme, structure, and point of view work together.
- **Synthesize:** To combine separate ideas into a unified, coherent understanding.
- **Analytical lens:** A specific way of examining a text — each standard represents a different lens for understanding.
- **Cohesive response:** A written analysis where all parts connect to each other rather than standing as separate, unrelated observations.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In past lessons, you analyzed one skill at a time. Today, you will analyze one text through all four lenses. The key is that the lenses do not work in isolation — they connect to each other. Structure affects how theme develops. Point of view shapes which evidence the reader has access to. Evidence supports every other kind of analysis."

Say: "Watch how I model this with a passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Tide Pool"**

> I had been coming to this stretch of beach since before I could walk — my father carried me then — so I knew every rock formation the way other kids knew their backyard fences. The tide pool at the base of the northern cliff was mine. Not legally, obviously, but in the way that matters more: I had watched it change through every season, and nobody else had.
>
> The summer I was twelve, a storm reshaped the coastline. Two rocks that had sheltered the pool for as long as I could remember split apart during the night, and when I came down the path the next morning, the pool was gone. Not drained — dissolved. The rocks that had held the water in place had shifted, and the ocean poured freely through the gap. The anemones, the hermit crabs, the starfish I had named (ridiculous, I know, but I had named them) — all carried away.
>
> I sat on the wet sand and stared at the gap for a long time. My father stood behind me, close but not touching, the way he always did when he knew I needed to feel something on my own.
>
> "The pool will come back," he said eventually.
>
> "Not this one," I said. "Maybe a new one. But not this one."
>
> He did not argue. I appreciated that. Some adults try to convince you that the thing you lost is the same as the thing that replaces it. My father understood the difference.
>
> By August, the tide had carved a new pool — smaller, deeper, sheltered by the shifted rocks. Different creatures appeared: a small octopus, a colony of barnacles, a bright red crab I had never seen before. I visited it. I watched it. But I did not name anything. Not yet.
>
> Maybe someday. For now, I was still learning the difference between something new and something healed.

Say: "Let me analyze this through all four lenses."

Write on the board:

**Lens 1 — Evidence (RL.6.1):**
The narrator's claim that the old pool cannot be replaced is supported by the evidence: "Not this one. Maybe a new one. But not this one." This distinction between a new thing and the original is the intellectual center of the passage. The inference that the narrator is processing grief — not just about a tide pool but about loss in general — is supported by the father's behavior: "close but not touching, the way he always did when he knew I needed to feel something on my own."

**Lens 2 — Theme (RL.6.2):**
The theme is that loss is not erased by replacement — something new may come, but it is not the same as what was lost, and learning to accept newness without pretending it is a restoration is part of growing up. This theme is conveyed through the narrator's refusal to name the creatures in the new pool ("Not yet") and the final sentence: "I was still learning the difference between something new and something healed."

**Lens 3 — Structure (RL.6.5):**
The passage is structured around a before-and-after: the pool before the storm and the pool after. The storm itself occupies only one paragraph, compressing the moment of loss. This structural choice emphasizes that loss often happens quickly, while understanding it takes much longer — the passage devotes far more space to the narrator's reflection than to the event itself. The final sentence functions as a pivotal statement that reframes the entire passage: it is not about a tide pool, it is about the narrator's understanding of loss.

**Lens 4 — Point of View (RL.6.6):**
The author develops the first-person narrator as someone who is deeply observant, emotionally honest, and slightly self-deprecating ("ridiculous, I know, but I had named them"). The narrator's voice carries a maturity beyond their twelve years — they notice their father's posture, appreciate his restraint, and articulate a philosophical distinction between new and healed. The author develops this perspective through internal monologue ("Some adults try to convince you that the thing you lost is the same as the thing that replaces it") and contrast with the father's more hopeful statement ("The pool will come back").

Say: "Notice how all four lenses connect. The point of view shapes which evidence the reader receives. The structural choices support the theme development. The evidence grounds every claim. This is integrated analysis."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice applying all four lenses to a passage with facilitator support.

Say: "Read this passage and annotate with four colors. Then we will discuss each lens together."

**Passage: "The Tournament" (Third Person Limited — focused on Adaeze)**

> The chess clock showed two minutes and fourteen seconds on Adaeze's side. Her opponent, a boy from Westfield Academy whose name she had already forgotten, had seven minutes remaining. He was leaning back in his chair, arms crossed, watching her think.
>
> She was losing. She knew it the way experienced players know it — not from the position on the board, which still looked complicated to an observer, but from the rhythm. He had been controlling the center since the opening. She had been reacting, not creating. Every move she made answered something he had already planned for.
>
> Adaeze looked at the board and saw two paths. The first was safe: trade queens, simplify the position, play for a draw. It was the smart move. Her coach would approve. The draw would keep her tournament rating intact.
>
> The second path was a sacrifice — her knight for a pawn, opening a diagonal that could, in four moves, expose his king. If she calculated correctly. If he did not see the threat until it was too late. If everything went exactly right.
>
> If. If. If.
>
> She thought about her coach's voice: "Play the position, not the ego." She thought about her rating number, which she had worked all year to build. She thought about the boy from Westfield, leaning back in his chair as if the game were already over.
>
> Adaeze picked up her knight and placed it on the sacrificed square. The boy unfolded his arms.
>
> She pressed her clock. One minute fifty-one seconds remained.
>
> The game was no longer safe. But for the first time in twenty moves, it was hers.

Say: "Take three minutes to annotate with four colors. Then we will build an analysis together."

After annotation, discuss each lens:

**Evidence:** Guide students to cite the moment Adaeze sees "two paths" and her choice of the sacrifice. Ask: "What inference can you draw about Adaeze from her decision to sacrifice the knight? Cite evidence."

**Theme:** Ask: "What is the theme? How is it conveyed through the details of Adaeze's internal debate?" Guide students to a theme about the value of taking risks to play authentically rather than playing safely to protect what you have.

**Structure:** Ask: "What does the sentence 'If. If. If.' do structurally? Why three separate sentences rather than one?" Guide students to see how the repetition creates a pause — a moment of suspended decision — and mirrors the uncertainty of the sacrifice.

**Point of View:** Ask: "The passage is third person limited, focused on Adaeze. What does the reader NOT know because of this perspective? How does that limitation serve the story?" Guide students to notice we do not know the opponent's thoughts — his crossed arms and lean-back are interpreted through Adaeze's eyes, making her choice feel like a response to his confidence.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently produce an integrated analysis applying all four lenses.

**Passage: "The Archive" (First Person)**

> The town decided to demolish the old newspaper building on Seventh Street. It had been empty for nine years — ever since the Herald Tribune moved to a digital-only format and let the lease expire. The windows were boarded. The roof leaked. The city council said the building was a liability.
>
> I was the only person at the council meeting who objected, which made me feel ridiculous and necessary at the same time. I stood at the microphone and said what I had come to say: that the basement of that building held seventy years of archived newspapers — physical copies, bound in leather, dating back to 1952. Births, deaths, elections, floods, school plays, basketball scores. The entire recorded memory of this town, stacked on shelves in the dark.
>
> The council chair, Mr. Whitfield, nodded politely. "We appreciate your concern," he said. "But we cannot save a building to save some old papers."
>
> Some old papers. I almost laughed.
>
> They gave me two weeks to remove what I wanted. I showed up the first Saturday with my truck and a flashlight. The basement stairs creaked, and the air smelled like mildew and ink. I pulled the first bound volume from the shelf — 1952 — and opened it carefully. The pages were brittle but legible. The headline read: GROUNDBREAKING CEREMONY FOR NEW COMMUNITY POOL.
>
> I loaded seventeen bound volumes that day. Each one weighed about forty pounds. My back screamed by the fourth trip, but I kept going, because if I did not carry them out, nobody would.
>
> The next Saturday, a woman I did not recognize was waiting by the door. "I heard about the newspapers," she said. "I brought my truck too." Behind her, an old man sat in a folding chair. He waved. "My father's obituary is in the 1968 volume," he said. "I would like to hold it one more time."
>
> By the end of the month, we had rescued every volume. They are stored now in the basement of the public library, accessible to anyone who wants to look. The newspaper building was demolished on a Tuesday. I did not watch.
>
> But the papers are here. And as long as they are here, so is the town — every flood, every election, every child born, every person mourned. Some old papers. No. Some old proof that we were here.

**Directions:** Write an integrated analysis of this passage. Your response must address all four lenses. You may organize your analysis by lens or weave the lenses together — but every lens must be represented.

1. **Evidence (RL.6.1):** Cite at least two pieces of textual evidence that support inferences about the narrator's character and motivation. Explain each.
2. **Theme (RL.6.2):** Identify the theme and explain how it develops from the beginning to the end of the passage through specific details.
3. **Structure (RL.6.5):** Analyze one structural choice the author makes (e.g., the placement of the final paragraph, the scene break to the second Saturday, the opening with the demolition decision) and explain what it contributes.
4. **Point of View (RL.6.6):** Identify two techniques the author uses to develop the narrator's point of view. Cite evidence and explain the effect of each.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 separately, citing evidence for each.
**Stretch:** After answering questions 1-4, write a paragraph that connects at least two of the lenses — show how structure and point of view work together, or how evidence and theme connect.
**Challenge:** Write a single cohesive essay (8-12 sentences) that integrates all four lenses into one flowing analysis rather than treating them as separate questions. The essay should read as a unified argument about the passage, not a checklist of skills.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.2) Write an objective summary of this passage in 3-4 sentences. Remember: no personal opinions.
6. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.3) Compare the narrator's view of the newspapers with Mr. Whitfield's view. How do their perspectives differ, and what does the difference reveal?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Hana folded the origami crane slowly — beak, wings, tail — and placed it on the shelf beside the others. There were three hundred and twelve cranes now. She had started folding them the day her sister left for college, one crane each evening before bed. "When you reach a thousand," her mother had told her, "you can make a wish." Hana did not believe in wishes. But she believed in the folding — the quiet rhythm of it, the way each crease required her full attention, the way the paper transformed from flat to alive. She picked up another square of paper and began again.

1. Choose any TWO of the four lenses (evidence, theme, structure, point of view) and write a brief analysis for each.

**Expected Answers (any two of the following):**
- **Evidence:** The text says Hana "did not believe in wishes. But she believed in the folding." This evidence supports the inference that the cranes are not about the superstition — they are about the process itself, which gives Hana comfort and focus during her sister's absence.
- **Theme:** The theme is that rituals provide comfort not because of what they promise (a wish at 1,000 cranes) but because of what they require — presence, attention, and transformation. The detail that "each crease required her full attention" conveys this directly.
- **Structure:** The passage begins with a completed crane and ends with Hana starting another, creating a circular structure that mirrors the repetitive nature of her ritual. This structural choice reinforces the theme that the value is in the ongoing process, not the destination.
- **Point of View:** The third person limited perspective reveals Hana's internal distinction between wishes and folding: she does not believe in one but believes in the other. This internal contrast, only visible through the narrator's access to her thoughts, develops Hana as someone who finds meaning through practice rather than magic.

**Mastery = Student applies two lenses accurately with cited evidence and connects each lens to a meaningful observation about the passage.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Have the student choose one lens they are most comfortable with and write a strong analysis using that lens. Then add a second lens, showing how it connects to the first.
- Provide the four-lens checklist and have the student annotate the passage with colors before writing.
- Review the weakest lens with a brief reteaching example from an earlier lesson.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is prepared for the Unit Assessment.
- Encourage the student to practice integrated analysis in their independent reading by choosing one passage per week and analyzing it through all four lenses.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Integrated Analysis Portfolio Piece:** The student selects a passage from a book they are reading independently and writes a full integrated analysis (covering all four lenses) as a portfolio submission. The analysis should be 10-15 sentences.
2. **Four-Lens Book Talk:** The student prepares a brief oral presentation about a book they have read, organized around the four lenses. They share one insight for each lens, citing specific evidence from the book.
3. **Cross-Passage Connection:** The student selects two passages from earlier lessons in this unit and writes a paragraph comparing them through one lens of their choice (e.g., how both passages use structure to develop theme, or how both narrators' points of view create different effects).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This is a culminating lesson, not a new-skill lesson.** The goal is synthesis, not introduction. If students struggle with a specific lens, refer them back to the relevant lesson for review rather than re-teaching within this lesson.
- **Integration is harder than isolation.** Students may default to treating the four lenses as a checklist rather than seeing how they connect. Model the connections explicitly: "The structure supports the theme by..." or "Because of the point of view, the reader's evidence is limited to..."
- **Writing organization matters.** For the Independent Practice, the on-level tier allows students to answer by lens (organized by standard). The challenge tier asks for a single cohesive essay. Both are valid. The key assessment criterion is whether each lens is addressed meaningfully with evidence.
- **Pacing note:** This lesson is slightly longer than previous lessons (55-60 minutes recommended) to allow for the integrated analysis. If time is limited, abbreviate the Guided Practice discussion and give students more time for Independent Practice.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Evidence (RL.6.1):** The narrator says they felt "ridiculous and necessary at the same time" while objecting at the council meeting. This reveals someone who is self-aware enough to recognize how they appear to others but committed enough to act anyway. Their motivation is made explicit when they say, "if I did not carry them out, nobody would" — the narrator acts not out of ego but out of a sense that someone must. The detail that the old man wanted "to hold it one more time" provides evidence that the narrator's instinct was correct — these papers matter to real people in personal, irreplaceable ways.
2. **Theme (RL.6.2):** The theme is that a community's recorded history — its everyday events, not just its grand moments — is worth preserving, because it is the proof that the people who lived there mattered. The theme develops from the council meeting (where the papers are dismissed as "some old papers") through the narrator's solitary rescue effort, to the arrival of the woman and old man (showing the narrator is not alone in valuing the archive), to the final statement: "Some old proof that we were here." The theme deepens across the passage: at first, the narrator seems to be fighting alone against indifference; by the end, the community itself joins the effort, and the narrator articulates why it all matters.
3. **Structure (RL.6.5):** The scene break from the first Saturday (the narrator working alone) to the second Saturday (the woman and old man arriving) is a pivotal structural choice. If the passage showed only the narrator rescuing the papers, it would be a story about one person's stubbornness. By introducing new helpers in a second scene, the author transforms the narrative from individual action to communal effort. This structural shift contributes to the theme by showing that when one person acts on principle, others follow — the narrator's solo effort became a community project. The break also accelerates the pacing: "By the end of the month, we had rescued every volume" compresses weeks of work into one sentence, suggesting that once momentum built, the rest came quickly.
4. **Point of View (RL.6.6):** (a) Internal monologue: The narrator's thought "Some old papers. I almost laughed" reveals their contempt for the council's dismissal. This technique develops the narrator as someone who sees deeper value in things others overlook — a consistent trait throughout the passage. (b) Detail selection: The narrator notices specific content — "Births, deaths, elections, floods, school plays, basketball scores" — listing a mix of the monumental and the mundane. This selection reveals a perspective that values everyday community life as much as historic events, which is central to their argument for preservation.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.5.2) Summary: When the town council votes to demolish an old newspaper building, the narrator objects and is given two weeks to remove seventy years of archived newspapers from the basement. Working alone at first, the narrator begins loading the heavy bound volumes by truck. Others join the effort after hearing about it, and by the end of the month, every volume is rescued and stored at the public library. The passage conveys the theme that a community's recorded history is worth preserving because it serves as proof of the lives that created it.
6. (RL.5.3) The narrator sees the newspapers as "the entire recorded memory of this town" — irreplaceable records of births, deaths, and community events that define the town's identity. Mr. Whitfield sees them as "some old papers" — a practical obstacle to demolishing a building that is a financial liability. This difference reveals a fundamental disagreement about what is valuable: Mr. Whitfield values the present-day utility of the building and its land, while the narrator values the historical and emotional significance of what the building contains. The narrator's perspective proves to be shared by more people than Mr. Whitfield's, as evidenced by the community members who join the rescue effort.
