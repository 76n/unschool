# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 2

## Topic
Making Inferences with Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between explicit information (stated directly) and implicit information (inferred from clues)
- Draw a logical inference from a literary text and support it with cited textual evidence
- Explain the reasoning that connects the evidence to the inference
- Recognize when an inference is unsupported or contradicted by the text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis of what the text says explicitly (Lesson 1, RL.6.1)
- Understanding of how to draw inferences from a text using accurate quotes (RL.5.1)
- Familiarity with the Evidence Sandwich structure (claim, evidence, explanation)
- Experience distinguishing between what a text says and what a text suggests

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Sticky notes (3-4 per student)
- Highlighters in two colors
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about inference and review the citing evidence skill from Lesson 1.

**Activity: What Is Not Said**

Say: "Yesterday, we practiced citing textual evidence — choosing the right quote and explaining what it means. Today, we are going to push that skill further. Sometimes the most important information in a text is not stated directly. The author gives you clues, and you have to figure out the meaning on your own. That is called making an inference."

Write on the board:
- **Explicit** = stated directly in the text
- **Implicit** = suggested through clues; the reader must figure it out

Say: "Here is a quick example. Read this sentence silently."

Write on the board: *Anika set the phone down gently, as if it might shatter, and then sat very still for a long time.*

Say: "The text does not say Anika received bad news. But what clues suggest it? Talk to a partner for thirty seconds."

Expected responses: Setting the phone down "gently, as if it might shatter" suggests fragility — either the phone or Anika's emotions. Sitting "very still for a long time" suggests she is stunned or processing something heavy. Together, these clues imply she received upsetting or shocking news.

Say: "You just made an inference. You combined what the text said with what you already know about how people behave, and you drew a logical conclusion. Today, we are going to practice doing that with longer passages — and supporting every inference with cited evidence."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students the process of building text-supported inferences and distinguishing them from unsupported guesses.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Inference:** A logical conclusion drawn by combining text evidence with the reader's knowledge and reasoning. It goes beyond what is stated directly.
- **Explicit:** Information that is clearly and directly stated in the text.
- **Implicit:** Information that is suggested or hinted at but not directly stated. The reader must infer it.
- **Reasoning:** The logical thinking that connects the evidence to the inference — the "bridge" between what the text says and what you conclude.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In fifth grade, you learned to draw inferences. In sixth grade, the standard asks you to cite evidence that supports both what the text says explicitly AND inferences drawn from the text. That means every inference must be grounded in specific evidence. You cannot just say what you think — you have to show where in the text the clues are."

Write on the board — **The Inference Equation:**
**Text Evidence + Reader's Knowledge = Inference**

Say: "An inference is not a guess. A guess has no evidence. An inference always starts with something specific from the text. Let me show you the difference."

Write on the board:
- **Guess:** I think the character is probably sad because stories usually have sad parts.
- **Inference:** The character is struggling with loss. The text says she "pressed the photograph against her chest and closed her eyes," which suggests she is holding onto a memory of someone important to her.

Say: "See the difference? The guess refers to nothing in the text. The inference points to specific words and explains what they reveal. Now watch how I build an inference from a full passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Orchard"**

> Every Saturday in autumn, Joaquin's family drove forty minutes to the Harmon Valley orchard to pick apples. His mother navigated the rows of trees with a map she had drawn herself years ago, marking which varieties ripened when. His father carried the wooden crates — the same ones his own father had used, the corners rounded smooth from decades of handling.
>
> This year, Joaquin noticed something different. The eastern rows, where the Cortland apples grew, had been cut down. In their place stood a chain-link fence and a sign: FUTURE SITE OF HARMON VALLEY SHOPPING CENTER. Joaquin's mother folded her hand-drawn map and slipped it into her back pocket without saying anything.
>
> They picked apples in the remaining rows. Joaquin filled his crate and carried it to the car. On the drive home, no one spoke. His mother stared out the passenger window. His father gripped the steering wheel with both hands, the way he did when he was thinking hard about something he could not fix.
>
> At home, Joaquin set a single apple on the kitchen counter. It was a Honeycrisp, golden with red streaks, slightly imperfect. He did not eat it. He just left it there, like a small monument to the afternoon.

Say: "The text never says the family is sad or worried about losing the orchard. But we can infer it. Watch my process."

Write on the board:

**Inference:** The family is grieving the loss of the orchard and what it represents to their traditions.

**Evidence 1:** "Joaquin's mother folded her hand-drawn map and slipped it into her back pocket without saying anything." — The map represents years of personal knowledge and tradition. Folding it silently suggests she is putting away something that may no longer be useful, and her silence implies she is processing a loss she does not want to speak about.

**Evidence 2:** "His father gripped the steering wheel with both hands, the way he did when he was thinking hard about something he could not fix." — The comparison to thinking about unfixable problems reveals that the father sees this change as permanent and painful. His physical tension (gripping the wheel) externalizes an emotion he is not expressing in words.

**Evidence 3:** "He did not eat it. He just left it there, like a small monument to the afternoon." — The word "monument" is powerful. Monuments are built to remember things that are gone or in danger of being forgotten. Joaquin's instinct to preserve the apple rather than eat it suggests he understands, at some level, that this tradition may not last.

Say: "I used three pieces of evidence to build one inference. Each piece adds to the argument. Notice that nothing in the text says 'the family was sad' — I inferred it from their actions, their silence, and the details the author chose to include."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying implicit meaning and building evidence-supported inferences.

Say: "Now we are going to work through a passage together. Read it silently, and as you read, highlight any sentence that seems to hint at something the author is not saying directly."

**Passage: "The Understudy"**

> Mei Lin had memorized every line of the lead role in the school play — not because she was cast in it, but because she was the understudy. She sat in the back of the auditorium during every rehearsal, mouthing the words along with Sofia, who stood center stage under the lights with the confidence of someone who had been performing since kindergarten.
>
> The director, Mr. Okafor, sometimes glanced back at Mei Lin and gave her a small nod, as if to say, "I see you." She appreciated the nods, but they also stung, because they confirmed what she already knew: she was prepared, and it did not matter.
>
> On the Thursday before opening night, Sofia twisted her ankle during the dance number. She sat on the edge of the stage, wincing, while the school nurse wrapped her foot. Mr. Okafor walked to the back of the auditorium.
>
> "You know the part," he said. It was not a question.
>
> Mei Lin stood up. Her heart was pounding, but her voice was steady. "I know the part."
>
> She walked to center stage. The lights were hotter than she expected, and the auditorium looked enormous from this angle. She took a breath, found her mark, and began.
>
> In the front row, Sofia watched with her ankle propped on a chair. When Mei Lin delivered the monologue — the one about choosing courage over comfort — Sofia leaned forward and whispered to the girl beside her, "She is better than I was."

Say: "Here are two claims. For each one, find evidence from the text and explain your reasoning. Write your responses in your notebook."

**Claim A:** Mei Lin has been quietly preparing for an opportunity that might never come, and she has mixed feelings about her position as understudy.

**Claim B:** Despite being competitors for the same role, Mei Lin and Sofia develop a form of mutual respect by the end of the passage.

Allow 3-4 minutes for students to work, then discuss.

Expected response for Claim A: The text says Mei Lin "had memorized every line of the lead role — not because she was cast in it, but because she was the understudy." This shows deliberate, unprompted preparation — she did not need to memorize every line, but she chose to. Her mixed feelings emerge when Mr. Okafor nods at her: "She appreciated the nods, but they also stung, because they confirmed what she already knew: she was prepared, and it did not matter." The word "stung" reveals frustration, while "appreciated" shows she values being seen. Together, these suggest Mei Lin is caught between dedication and disappointment.

Expected response for Claim B: Evidence of respect from Sofia appears at the end: "She is better than I was." This is a generous acknowledgment from someone who could feel threatened or bitter. Evidence of Mei Lin's respect for Sofia appears earlier — she watches Sofia perform "with the confidence of someone who had been performing since kindergarten," a description that acknowledges Sofia's skill without jealousy. The passage does not show rivalry or resentment between them; instead, it builds toward quiet mutual recognition.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently draw inferences and support them with cited textual evidence.

**Passage: "The Lantern Festival"**

> The paper lanterns hung from wires strung between the apartment buildings, casting soft orange light across the courtyard below. Yuto stood at his window on the fourth floor, watching his neighbors gather. Mrs. Nakamura carried a pot of something steaming. The Sato children chased each other around the benches. Mr. Reyes tuned a guitar under the largest lantern, the one shaped like a carp.
>
> Yuto's mother appeared in his doorway. "Are you coming down?"
>
> He shook his head. "Maybe later."
>
> She studied him for a moment. "You have been saying 'maybe later' for three festivals now."
>
> Yuto did not answer. He pressed his forehead against the cool glass and watched Mr. Reyes begin to play. The music drifted up through the open window, clear and warm. Below, people began to sway. Mrs. Nakamura ladled soup into paper cups and handed them to anyone who passed.
>
> Yuto's mother sat down on the edge of his bed. She did not push. She just sat there, close enough that he could feel her presence without turning around.
>
> After a while, Yuto said, "What if I go down and nobody talks to me?"
>
> His mother was quiet for a moment. "What if you go down and somebody does?"
>
> Yuto watched the courtyard for another minute. Then he stepped away from the window, put on his shoes, and walked to the door. He paused with his hand on the knob. His mother did not move from the bed.
>
> "Are you coming?" he asked.
>
> "I will be right behind you," she said.

**Directions:** For each question, draw an inference based on the text. Cite specific evidence and explain your reasoning.

1. What can you infer about Yuto's emotional state and why he has been avoiding the festival? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
2. What does the mother's behavior reveal about her understanding of Yuto? How does she handle his reluctance?
3. What is the significance of Yuto asking "Are you coming?" at the end of the passage? What does this moment reveal about the shift in his feelings?
4. The text says the music was "clear and warm" and drifted "up through the open window." What might the author be suggesting about the community through this sensory detail?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4, citing at least one piece of evidence per response with explanation.
**Stretch:** For question 1, identify both what the text says explicitly and what you must infer. Label each part of your answer as "explicit" or "inferred."
**Challenge:** Write a paragraph analyzing how the author uses the physical setting — the window, the courtyard, the distance between the fourth floor and the ground — as a metaphor for Yuto's emotional distance from the community. Cite at least three pieces of evidence.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.2) What is the theme of this passage? State it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.3) Compare Yuto's mother's approach to encouraging him with a more direct approach (such as saying "You have to go — no excuses"). What is the effect of her chosen strategy?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> When the final bell rang, Kira did not rush for the door the way the other students did. She straightened the papers on her desk, capped her pen slowly, and slid her notebook into her backpack one pocket at a time. By the time she stood up, the classroom was empty except for her and the teacher, who was erasing the board.

1. What inference can you draw about Kira from this passage? Cite one piece of evidence.
2. Explain the reasoning that connects your evidence to your inference.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Inference: Kira is reluctant to leave school, possibly because she prefers being there to wherever she goes next. Evidence: "She straightened the papers on her desk, capped her pen slowly, and slid her notebook into her backpack one pocket at a time." These deliberately slow actions suggest she is stalling rather than eager to go.
2. While the other students "rushed for the door," Kira does the opposite — she slows down. The series of small, careful actions (straightening, capping, sliding) are not necessary tasks; they are ways to extend her time in the classroom. This contrast between Kira and her classmates suggests that school is a place of comfort or safety for her, and leaving it feels unwelcome.

**Mastery = Student draws a logical inference that goes beyond what is explicitly stated, cites relevant evidence with quotation marks, and provides reasoning that explains how the evidence supports the inference.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Inference Equation (Text Evidence + Reader's Knowledge = Inference) with a simplified example.
- Practice with two-sentence passages: give the student a brief description of a character's actions and ask "What can you figure out that the text does not say directly?"
- Provide a sentence frame: "The text says '___.' This suggests that ___ because ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3, which introduces determining theme through character actions and challenges.
- Encourage the student to practice inference in independent reading by writing one "What I infer" sticky note per chapter.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Inference Journal:** The student selects a passage from a book they are reading independently and writes three inferences about a character, each supported by cited evidence. They label each inference as "strong" or "possible" based on how much evidence supports it.
2. **Explicit vs. Implicit Sort:** Give the student eight statements about the "Lantern Festival" passage. Four are explicitly stated in the text; four are inferences. The student sorts them into two columns and explains how they made each determination.
3. **Writing Implicit Meaning:** The student writes a short paragraph (5-7 sentences) about a character who is feeling a specific emotion — but the emotion word itself cannot appear in the paragraph. The student relies on actions, details, and descriptions to convey the feeling implicitly.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Key distinction for Grade 6:** In Grade 5, students learned to draw inferences. In Grade 6, the emphasis shifts to citing evidence that supports those inferences. The inference itself is important, but the evidence-reasoning chain is what RL.6.1 assesses.
- **Common error — unsupported leaps:** Students may make inferences that go far beyond the text. Teach them to ask: "Does the text actually suggest this, or am I inventing a backstory?" A good inference is grounded in textual clues, not imagination.
- **Emotional sensitivity:** Some passages in this lesson touch on themes of isolation, anxiety, and loss. Be prepared to acknowledge that these are real feelings that many people experience, and that literature helps us understand them.
- **Pacing note:** The Guided Practice passage is rich and can generate extended discussion. If time is limited, focus on Claim A and briefly discuss Claim B, saving deeper analysis for students working at the Stretch or Challenge tier.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Yuto appears to be experiencing social anxiety or fear of rejection. The text says he has been saying "maybe later" for "three festivals now," revealing this is a recurring pattern, not a one-time reluctance. His question "What if I go down and nobody talks to me?" explicitly names his fear — he is afraid of being ignored or excluded. His physical position — "pressed his forehead against the cool glass" — suggests longing (he is watching, not turning away), but the glass creates a barrier between him and the community below. Together, these details suggest someone who wants to belong but is afraid of the vulnerability it requires.
2. The mother understands that pushing Yuto will not work and that he needs to make the decision himself. She "did not push" and "just sat there, close enough that he could feel her presence without turning around." This physical closeness without verbal pressure shows she is offering support, not commands. Her response to his fear — "What if you go down and somebody does?" — is a gentle reframe rather than a dismissal. She does not say his fear is silly or wrong; she simply offers an alternative possibility. Her strategy reveals patience and respect for Yuto's process.
3. When Yuto asks "Are you coming?" it reveals that he has shifted from isolation to wanting companionship. Earlier, he refused to go at all. Now he is not only going but inviting his mother to join him. This question also shows vulnerability — he is not yet confident enough to go alone. The phrase "Are you coming?" mirrors his mother's earlier question ("Are you coming down?"), creating a symmetry that suggests he has internalized her support and is ready to take the step, but with her beside him.
4. The description of the music as "clear and warm" drifting "up through the open window" suggests the community's warmth is reaching Yuto even through his self-imposed distance. The open window is significant — Yuto has not shut himself off completely. The music enters his space uninvited, just as the community's presence gently penetrates his isolation. The sensory detail implies that connection is always available, always reaching out, even to those who are not yet ready to reach back.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.5.2) Theme: Overcoming the fear of rejection requires a small act of courage, often made possible by the patient support of someone who does not force the decision but stands ready to walk alongside you. (Accept reasonable variations about courage, belonging, or the role of gentle encouragement.)
6. (RL.5.3) The mother's approach — sitting quietly, not pushing, offering a gentle counter-question — gives Yuto space to make his own choice, which makes his decision to go feel genuine and self-driven. A more direct approach ("You have to go — no excuses") might have gotten him out the door faster, but it could also have increased his anxiety or made him feel his fear was being dismissed. The mother's method respects his feelings and builds his confidence because the choice is his, not hers.
