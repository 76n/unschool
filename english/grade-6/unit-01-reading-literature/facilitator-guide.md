# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 1: Reading Literature

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview
Unit 1 builds on the literary analysis foundation established in Grade 5 by introducing sixth graders to four essential close-reading skills: citing textual evidence to support analysis, determining theme and tracing its development across a text, analyzing how structural elements contribute to a text's overall meaning, and explaining how authors develop point of view. Across ten lessons, students move from precise evidence-based citation through thematic analysis, structural interpretation, and narrative perspective, culminating in an integrated analysis lesson that weaves all four skill areas together. The unit features original fiction passages spanning realistic fiction, historical fiction, fantasy, poetry, and drama, with diverse characters and settings that reflect the experiences of 11-12 year olds.

### Prerequisite Skills (Grade 5)
Students entering this unit should be able to:
- Quote accurately from a text when explaining what the text says explicitly and when drawing inferences (RL.5.1)
- Determine the theme of a story, drama, or poem from details in the text, including how characters respond to challenges; summarize the text (RL.5.2)
- Compare and contrast two or more characters, settings, or events in a story or drama, drawing on specific details (RL.5.3)
- Describe how a narrator's or speaker's point of view influences how events are described (RL.5.6)
- Analyze how visual and multimedia elements contribute to meaning, tone, or beauty of a text (RL.5.7)

If students struggle with these prerequisite skills, use the interleaved review sections in each lesson to reinforce them before moving forward.

### Facilitation Tips
- **Evidence anchor chart:** From Lesson 1, maintain a visible chart showing how to cite textual evidence properly. Unlike Grade 5 where students learned to quote accurately, Grade 6 emphasizes citing evidence to support analysis — students should explain what the evidence means, not just copy it.
- **Think-alouds are essential:** Model your analytical thinking frequently. Sixth graders benefit from hearing how an experienced reader connects evidence to claims, traces a theme across paragraphs, or identifies how structure shapes meaning.
- **Guided independence model:** At this grade level, students should read instruction passages and work through practice more autonomously. Position yourself as a coach who checks in rather than a director who leads every step.
- **Annotation habits:** Encourage students to develop consistent annotation marks — for example, underline evidence, circle vocabulary, bracket structural transitions, and write margin notes about point of view.
- **Turn and discuss:** Before whole-group sharing, give students 60-90 seconds to discuss with a partner. This builds confidence and surfaces misconceptions early, especially for the more abstract concepts in Lessons 6-9.
- **Flexible grouping:** Use exit ticket data to form small re-teaching groups. Students who do not meet mastery on a given lesson should receive targeted support before moving on.
- **Passage preview:** Read all passages before the lesson. Some contain themes of loss, personal struggle, or identity that may benefit from a brief content introduction.
- **Cross-lesson connections:** Explicitly link concepts across lessons. For example, when analyzing structure in Lesson 6, remind students to cite evidence (Lesson 1) and connect structural choices to theme (Lessons 3-5).

### Common Misconceptions
| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| Citing evidence means just copying a sentence from the text | Citing evidence means selecting a specific quote or detail AND explaining how it supports the analysis or claim being made. The explanation is as important as the quote itself. |
| Theme is the same as the topic or subject | The topic is what the text is about (e.g., "friendship"). The theme is the message or insight the text conveys about that topic (e.g., "True friendship requires accepting someone's flaws, not just their strengths"). Theme is always a complete statement. |
| A text has only one theme | Many texts contain multiple themes. Students should be able to identify and support at least one, but recognizing that themes can coexist is an important Grade 6 skill. |
| Structure means only paragraphs and chapters | Structure includes sentence-level choices (short sentences for tension, long sentences for reflection), scene breaks, flashbacks, foreshadowing, shifts in time or setting, and the arrangement of events. Any organizational choice an author makes is part of structure. |
| Point of view means the character's opinion | Point of view in literary analysis refers to the narrative perspective — who is telling the story and what they can or cannot see, know, and reveal. It includes first person, third person limited, and third person omniscient, as well as the way the narrator's position shapes the reader's understanding. |
| First person is always the main character | A first-person narrator can be a secondary character observing the main character, an unreliable narrator, or a participant whose perspective is deliberately limited. The narrator's position shapes what information the reader receives. |

### Assessment Guidance
- **Formative:** Use the exit ticket in each lesson to gauge daily mastery. Students scoring below the stated mastery threshold should receive re-teaching before the next lesson. The exit tickets progress in complexity across the unit, so early intervention prevents compounding gaps.
- **Summative:** The unit assessment includes five sections covering all four standards with original passages, multiple-choice items, short-answer questions, and extended-response prompts. Administer it after Lesson 10. Students should score at least 70% overall (42/60 points) and demonstrate competency in each standard (at least 50% per standard).
- **Portfolio option:** If your program uses portfolios, include the student's Lesson 10 integrated analysis response and the unit assessment as evidence of growth. Students' annotated passages from Lessons 1-2 also make strong portfolio artifacts showing their evidence-gathering process.

### Differentiation at a Glance
- **Below level:** Provide pre-reading vocabulary support; allow paired reading; use the "On-level" tier in independent practice; offer sentence frames for evidence integration (e.g., "The text states, '___,' which suggests that ___ because ___"); for structural analysis lessons, provide a graphic organizer that maps the text's parts before asking students to analyze them.
- **On level:** Follow lesson as written; use the "On-level" and "Stretch" tiers; encourage independent annotation and analysis.
- **Above level:** Assign "Challenge" tier activities; encourage deeper structural and thematic analysis; ask students to compare texts across lessons for recurring themes or contrasting structural choices; invite extended written responses that synthesize multiple standards.

### Connection to Future Units
The skills developed in this unit — evidence-based citation, thematic analysis, structural analysis, and point of view — will be applied and extended in Unit 2 (Reading Informational Text), where students will cite evidence from nonfiction, analyze central ideas, and evaluate author's purpose. In Unit 3 (Writing), students will draw evidence from literary texts to support their own arguments and analyses. The structural awareness developed in Lessons 6-7 directly supports narrative writing, and the point of view skills from Lessons 8-9 prepare students to craft their own narratives with intentional perspective choices.
