# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 1

## Topic
Citing Textual Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify specific textual evidence that supports an analytical claim about a passage
- Distinguish between evidence that directly supports a claim and evidence that is loosely related but insufficient
- Integrate cited evidence into a written response using proper formatting and explanatory follow-up

## Prerequisites
- Ability to quote accurately from a text when explaining what the text says explicitly (RL.5.1)
- Understanding of how to locate key details in a narrative passage
- Familiarity with using quotation marks to set off words taken directly from a text
- Experience writing responses that refer to details from a reading passage

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Sticky notes (3-4 per student)
- Highlighters in two colors
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about text evidence and introduce the shift from quoting to citing.

**Activity: Quote or Claim?**

Say: "In fifth grade, you learned how to quote accurately from a text — pulling the exact words and putting them in quotation marks. This year, we are going to build on that skill. Citing textual evidence means more than just copying a sentence. It means choosing the right evidence and then explaining what it proves. The evidence is only half the work. The explanation is the other half."

Write on the board:
- **Quoting** = Using the exact words from the text
- **Citing evidence** = Choosing the right quote AND explaining what it means for your analysis

Say: "Here is a quick warm-up. I am going to give you a claim about a character, and two quotes from a text. You tell me which quote actually supports the claim."

Write on the board:
- **Claim:** Marcus feels conflicted about leaving his neighborhood.
- **Quote A:** "Marcus packed his suitcase and set it by the front door."
- **Quote B:** "Marcus ran his hand along the fence he had helped his father build, wondering if the new family would bother to repaint it."

Say: "Which quote supports the claim? Talk to a partner for thirty seconds, then we will share."

Expected answer: Quote B, because it shows Marcus lingering on a memory and worrying about what will happen after he leaves, which reveals inner conflict — not just the physical act of packing.

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to select evidence that directly supports analytical claims and integrate it into written responses with explanation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Textual evidence:** Specific words, phrases, sentences, or details from a text used to support an analysis or argument.
- **Cite:** To refer to a specific part of the text as proof for a claim, including identifying where it appears and what it means.
- **Analysis:** Examining the parts of a text closely to understand the meaning, purpose, or effect of the author's choices.
- **Claim:** A statement about the text that requires evidence to support it.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In fifth grade, you learned to find evidence and put it in quotation marks. In sixth grade, the expectation is higher. You are not just finding evidence — you are choosing the strongest evidence and then explaining what it reveals. Think of it as a three-part process."

Write on the board — **The Evidence Sandwich:**
1. **Claim** — State what you believe about the text.
2. **Evidence** — Cite the specific words from the text that support your claim.
3. **Explanation** — Explain HOW the evidence supports your claim. What does it reveal? Why does it matter?

Say: "Watch how I use the Evidence Sandwich with a real passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Letter"**

> Zara found the envelope tucked between the pages of her grandmother's cookbook, right between the recipe for lentil soup and the one for cardamom cake. The paper was thin and yellowed, the ink faded to a pale brown. She recognized the handwriting immediately — it was her grandmother's, the same looping letters that labeled every jar in the pantry.
>
> The letter was addressed to someone named Farid. Zara had never heard the name before. She read it slowly, sounding out words in a language she only half understood — her grandmother's first language, the one she spoke on the phone with her sisters but never taught Zara.
>
> "Dear Farid," the letter began. "I am writing to tell you that I have arrived safely. The city is louder than I imagined, and the cold is like nothing I have felt before. But I have found work, and the woman next door brings me tea in the evenings. I think I will be all right."
>
> Zara folded the letter carefully and placed it back between the recipes. She had a hundred questions, but she also had the feeling that some of them were not hers to ask — at least, not yet.

Say: "Here is a claim I want to make: Zara feels a mix of curiosity and respect for her grandmother's private history. Now I need to find the strongest evidence."

Write on the board:

**Claim:** Zara feels a mix of curiosity and respect for her grandmother's private history.

**Evidence:** The text says, "She had a hundred questions, but she also had the feeling that some of them were not hers to ask — at least, not yet."

**Explanation:** This sentence reveals Zara's internal tension. The phrase "a hundred questions" shows intense curiosity — she clearly wants to know who Farid is and what the letter means. But the phrase "not hers to ask" shows that she recognizes boundaries around her grandmother's past. The addition of "at least, not yet" suggests maturity — Zara is not dismissing her curiosity, but choosing to wait for the right moment. This evidence directly supports the claim because it shows both emotions — curiosity and respect — existing simultaneously.

Say: "Notice that my explanation is longer than my evidence. That is intentional. The explanation is where the real thinking happens. Anyone can copy a quote. The analysis is what makes your response strong."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice selecting and citing evidence with facilitator support.

Say: "Now we are going to read a new passage together. As you read, use your sticky notes to mark sentences that seem important — sentences that reveal something about the characters or situation."

**Passage: "The Tryout"**

> DeShawn arrived at the basketball court fifteen minutes early, the way his older brother Malik always had. The court looked the same — cracked concrete, chain nets, the painted lines fading into ghost outlines. But the players warming up were strangers. DeShawn recognized none of the quick passes, none of the inside jokes shouted across the half-court line.
>
> He dribbled alone near the three-point arc, working through the routine Malik had taught him: crossover, hesitation, pull-up. His body knew the moves, but his hands felt stiff, like he was performing for an audience that was not watching.
>
> When the captain — a tall kid with a whistle around his neck — called everyone to center court, DeShawn jogged over and stood at the edge of the group. The captain assigned teams. DeShawn was placed with four players he had never met.
>
> On the first play, DeShawn caught a pass at the elbow and froze. He saw the lane open, the way Malik would have driven through it without thinking. But DeShawn passed the ball instead, a safe bounce pass to the corner. The corner player scored.
>
> "Nice pass," the captain said.
>
> DeShawn nodded, but the compliment felt hollow. He had not made the pass because it was the right play. He had made it because he was afraid to drive.

Say: "Here is a claim: DeShawn is struggling to establish his own identity on the court, separate from his brother's legacy. Find two pieces of evidence that support this claim. Write each one on a sticky note."

Allow 90 seconds for students to search.

Say: "What did you find? Let us share and evaluate."

Expected responses:
- "His body knew the moves, but his hands felt stiff, like he was performing for an audience that was not watching." (Shows physical disconnection between learned skill and personal confidence.)
- "He saw the lane open, the way Malik would have driven through it without thinking. But DeShawn passed the ball instead." (Shows DeShawn measuring himself against Malik's instincts.)
- "He had not made the pass because it was the right play. He had made it because he was afraid to drive." (Shows self-awareness about his avoidance.)

Say: "Now let us build a complete response together using the Evidence Sandwich."

Guide students to write: DeShawn is struggling to establish his own identity on the court, separate from his brother's legacy. The text says, "He saw the lane open, the way Malik would have driven through it without thinking. But DeShawn passed the ball instead." This reveals that DeShawn is constantly comparing himself to Malik — he sees opportunities through Malik's eyes rather than his own. His admission that "he was afraid to drive" shows that his hesitation is not about basketball strategy but about personal confidence. He is not yet ready to play as himself rather than as Malik's younger brother.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently select evidence and build analytical responses.

**Passage: "The Mural"**

> The wall on Garfield Avenue had been blank for as long as Priya could remember — a wide, flat stretch of beige concrete between the laundromat and the bodega. People walked past it every day without looking up. It was the kind of wall that existed only to hold up the buildings on either side of it.
>
> When the city announced a public mural project and invited community members to submit designs, Priya sketched her idea on a napkin during lunch: a river of faces flowing from one end of the wall to the other — Mrs. Adeyemi from the hair salon, Mr. Chen who sold mangoes from a cart, the Gutierrez kids who played stickball in the street. Not famous faces. Neighborhood faces.
>
> She submitted her napkin sketch and forgot about it. Three weeks later, the letter arrived: her design had been selected.
>
> On the first day of painting, Priya stood on the scaffolding with a brush in her hand and her heart hammering. The wall was enormous up close. Her napkin sketch suddenly seemed childish and small. She dipped the brush and made the first stroke — the curve of Mrs. Adeyemi's cheekbone — and it looked nothing like the sketch.
>
> But Mrs. Adeyemi herself was standing below, watching. "That is me?" she called up, laughing. "You make me look like a queen."
>
> Priya smiled. The sketch on the napkin did not matter anymore. The wall was telling her what it wanted to become.

**Directions:** Read the passage carefully. For each question, state your claim, cite specific evidence from the text, and explain how the evidence supports your analysis.

1. What does the passage reveal about how Priya views her neighborhood? Cite evidence and explain.
2. How does Priya's confidence change from the beginning to the end of the passage? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
3. What is the significance of the sentence "The wall was telling her what it wanted to become"?
4. Why does the author describe the wall as existing "only to hold up the buildings on either side of it"? How does this detail contribute to the passage's meaning?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4, citing at least one piece of evidence per response with explanation.
**Stretch:** In your response to question 2, use two pieces of evidence and explain how they connect to each other — show the progression, not just two separate examples.
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph arguing whether the mural matters more to Priya or to the neighborhood. Cite at least three pieces of evidence and explain each one.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.2) What is the theme of this passage? State it as a complete sentence, not just a topic word.
6. (Grade 5 review — RL.5.3) Compare Priya's view of the wall at the beginning of the passage to her view at the end. What has changed?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Tomás sat in the front row of the auditorium, clutching his speech notes so tightly that the paper wrinkled. When his name was called, he walked to the podium and unfolded the crumpled pages. His voice shook on the first sentence, but by the third paragraph, he had stopped reading from the notes entirely. He was just talking — to the room, to the faces looking up at him, to himself.

1. What claim can you make about how Tomás changes during his speech? Cite one piece of evidence.
2. Explain how your evidence supports your claim.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Claim: Tomás overcomes his nervousness and finds genuine confidence during the speech. Evidence: "His voice shook on the first sentence, but by the third paragraph, he had stopped reading from the notes entirely. He was just talking."
2. The shift from a shaking voice to speaking without notes shows that Tomás moved from anxiety to authentic engagement. The phrase "He was just talking" suggests he stopped performing and started communicating naturally, which indicates real confidence rather than forced composure.

**Mastery = Student states a clear claim, cites a relevant quote with quotation marks, and provides at least one sentence of explanation connecting the evidence to the claim.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Evidence Sandwich structure with the student using a simplified passage.
- Provide a sentence frame: "The text states, '___,' which reveals that ___ because ___."
- Practice identifying strong vs. weak evidence by giving the student a claim and three quotes, asking them to rank the quotes from strongest to weakest support.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 2, which builds on citing evidence by teaching students to draw and support inferences.
- Encourage the student to practice the Evidence Sandwich in their independent reading responses.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Evidence Detective:** The student rereads the "Mural" passage and identifies three pieces of evidence that could support a completely different claim — for example, that the mural project changes how the neighborhood sees itself. Write a full Evidence Sandwich for this alternate claim.
2. **Strong vs. Weak Evidence:** The student writes a claim about a book they are reading independently, then finds two pieces of evidence — one strong and one weak. They write a paragraph explaining why the strong evidence is more effective than the weak evidence.
3. **Peer Analysis:** The student exchanges their Independent Practice responses with a partner. Each student highlights the claim, evidence, and explanation in their partner's work, then provides feedback on whether the explanation is specific enough.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Most common mistake at Grade 6:** Students cite evidence but skip the explanation, treating the quote as if it speaks for itself. Remind them: "The quote is your proof, but you are the lawyer. You have to explain what the proof means."
- **Transitioning from Grade 5:** Fifth graders learned to quote accurately; sixth graders must analyze. If students are still just copying quotes without analysis, use the warm-up format (claim + two quotes, choose the stronger one) as a daily five-minute exercise.
- **Evidence quality matters:** Not all evidence is equally strong. Teach students to ask: "Does this quote directly prove my claim, or does it just relate to the same topic?" This distinction is central to RL.6.1.
- **Pacing note:** If students grasp the Evidence Sandwich quickly during Guided Practice, spend extra time on Independent Practice question 4, which requires students to analyze the author's purpose — a skill that bridges to later lessons on structure and point of view.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Priya views her neighborhood as full of important, real people who deserve recognition. The text says she sketched "a river of faces flowing from one end of the wall to the other — Mrs. Adeyemi from the hair salon, Mr. Chen who sold mangoes from a cart, the Gutierrez kids who played stickball in the street." She then clarifies: "Not famous faces. Neighborhood faces." This evidence shows Priya believes the people in her community are worthy of public art — that their everyday lives are as meaningful as anything famous or grand.
2. Priya's confidence follows an arc from casual, to intimidated, to empowered. Early on, she "sketched her idea on a napkin during lunch" and "submitted her napkin sketch and forgot about it," suggesting relaxed informality. But when painting begins, "her heart was hammering" and "her napkin sketch suddenly seemed childish and small," showing self-doubt when facing the real scale of the project. By the end, however, she smiles and thinks "The wall was telling her what it wanted to become," showing she has moved past her prepared plan and is creating with confidence and flexibility.
3. The sentence "The wall was telling her what it wanted to become" means Priya has shifted from trying to control the project to collaborating with it. She started with a fixed plan (the napkin sketch) but realized the actual painting has its own energy and direction. This sentence shows that real creativity involves responding to the moment, not just executing a plan. It also suggests that the wall — and by extension the neighborhood — has its own voice that Priya is now listening to.
4. The author describes the wall as existing "only to hold up the buildings on either side of it" to establish that the wall was invisible and purposeless before the mural. This detail creates a contrast with what the wall becomes — a canvas for community identity. By starting with the wall's insignificance, the author emphasizes how art can transform overlooked spaces into places of meaning and pride. The detail also mirrors Priya's journey: just as the wall seemed unremarkable, Priya's napkin sketch seemed small, but both turned out to hold more potential than expected.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.5.2) Theme: Ordinary people and places hold extraordinary value when someone takes the time to see them and share that vision with others. (Accept reasonable variations about the transformative power of art, the importance of community representation, or how creative expression reveals hidden significance.)
6. (RL.5.3) At the beginning, Priya sees the wall as invisible and purposeless — it existed "only to hold up the buildings on either side of it" and people walked past "without looking up." By the end, the wall has become something alive and meaningful to her — "the wall was telling her what it wanted to become." The wall has transformed from an object into a collaborator, and Priya has transformed from a passerby into a creator.
