# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 10

## Topic
Varying Sentence Patterns for Style

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify how sentence length, opening, and rhythm contribute to writing style and reader engagement
- Use short sentences strategically for emphasis and impact after longer, more complex sentences
- Vary sentence openings (subject-first, adverb-first, prepositional phrase-first, participial phrase-first) to create rhythm and avoid monotony
- Revise a passage by applying multiple sentence variety techniques to improve style and reader interest

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of the four sentence types and their meaning relationships from Lesson 9
- Ability to combine, expand, and reduce sentences (Grade 5, L.5.3a)
- Knowledge of prepositional phrases, adverbs, and participial phrases (Grades 4-5)
- Familiarity with nonrestrictive elements and punctuation from Lessons 7-8

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example passages (monotonous vs. varied)
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (for marking sentence openings and lengths)
- Highlighters for passage analysis

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students hear the difference between monotonous writing and writing with sentence variety.

**Activity: Listen and Compare**

Say: "I am going to read two short passages aloud. Close your eyes and listen to how each one sounds. After I read both, tell me which one you would rather keep reading."

Read Passage A (monotonous):
"The dog sat on the porch. The dog watched the street. The dog saw a squirrel. The dog jumped up. The dog ran across the yard. The dog chased the squirrel up a tree. The dog barked. The dog wagged its tail."

Read Passage B (varied):
"The old brown dog sat on the porch, watching the street with half-closed eyes. Suddenly, a squirrel darted across the sidewalk. The dog leaped up. Racing across the yard with its ears pinned back, it chased the squirrel straight up the tallest oak tree. Victorious, the dog barked once, wagged its tail, and trotted back to the porch."

Say: "Which passage did you prefer? Why?"

Allow responses. Then say: "Passage B is more engaging because it uses different sentence lengths, different sentence openings, and different structures. It has rhythm. Today we learn how to create that rhythm in your own writing."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach three techniques for varying sentences for style: varying length, varying openings, and using strategic short sentences.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sentence variety:** The practice of using sentences of different lengths, structures, and openings to create rhythm and keep the reader engaged
- **Sentence opening:** The first word or phrase in a sentence — varying the opening avoids the "subject-verb-object" monotony
- **Cadence:** The rhythm and flow of writing, created by the pattern of long and short sentences
- **Strategic short sentence:** A deliberately short sentence placed after longer ones for emphasis, drama, or impact

### Instruction Notes

**Technique 1: Vary Sentence Length**

Say: "Writing that uses only long sentences feels exhausting. Writing that uses only short sentences feels choppy and childish. The best writing mixes both."

Write on the board:
- "After the long hike through the dense forest, over the rocky ridge, and down the steep slope to the valley below, the exhausted hikers finally reached the campsite. They collapsed."

Say: "Feel how the long first sentence creates a sense of the long, exhausting journey? Then the short sentence — 'They collapsed' — hits you with its simplicity. That contrast is powerful."

Write the guideline: **Long sentences build detail. Short sentences create impact. Use both.**

**Technique 2: Vary Sentence Openings**

Say: "Most students start every sentence the same way: Subject-Verb. 'The dog ran. The cat slept. The boy walked.' This gets boring fast. Here are four ways to start a sentence differently."

Write on the board:

| Opening Type | Example |
|-------------|---------|
| **Subject-first** (default) | The explorers discovered a hidden cave. |
| **Adverb-first** | Cautiously, the explorers discovered a hidden cave. |
| **Prepositional phrase-first** | Deep in the forest, the explorers discovered a hidden cave. |
| **Participial phrase-first** | Pushing through the thick underbrush, the explorers discovered a hidden cave. |

Say: "Each opening creates a slightly different effect. The adverb tells us how. The prepositional phrase tells us where. The participial phrase adds action and detail. Mixing these keeps your writing alive."

**Important:** Say: "When you start a sentence with an adverb, prepositional phrase, or participial phrase, you usually need a comma before the main clause."

**Technique 3: The Strategic Short Sentence**

Say: "This is one of the most powerful tools in a writer's toolkit. After building up detail and tension with longer sentences, drop in a short sentence. It lands like a drumbeat."

Write examples:
- "The gymnasium was decorated with streamers and balloons. The DJ tested the speakers. Students in their best clothes filed through the double doors, laughing and talking. Then the music started."
- "The storm had raged for three days, tearing off roofs, flooding streets, and uprooting ancient trees across the entire county. On the fourth day, the sun appeared."

Say: "Notice how 'Then the music started' and 'On the fourth day, the sun appeared' feel powerful. They are not the longest or most detailed sentences, but they carry the most emotional weight because they follow longer, more complex ones."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Revising for varied openings**
Write (monotonous): "The team arrived at the stadium. The team warmed up on the field. The team huddled together. The captain gave a speech. The game began."

Say: "Every sentence starts with 'the team' or 'the.' Let me vary the openings."

Revise: "The team arrived at the stadium well before game time. On the freshly mowed field, they stretched and warmed up under the bright lights. Huddled together near the bench, they listened as the captain gave a fiery speech. The game began."

**Example 2: Revising for varied length**
Write (all medium-length): "The library was quiet and peaceful. Students sat at the tables reading. A few people whispered near the shelves. The librarian organized new books."

Revise: "The library was quiet. Students sat hunched over books at long wooden tables, and near the shelves, a few people whispered about which novel to choose next. At the front desk, the librarian carefully organized a stack of new arrivals, placing each one in its proper place."

Say: "Notice how the one-word opening — 'The library was quiet' — sets the scene with a simple statement, and then the longer sentences fill in the details."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice applying the three variety techniques with facilitator support.

**Exercise A: Vary the Openings (items 1-4)**
Say: "Each sentence below starts with the subject. Rewrite it with the opening type I specify."

1. "The rain pounded against the windows all night long." → Rewrite with a prepositional phrase opening.
2. "The goalkeeper dove to the left and blocked the shot." → Rewrite with a participial phrase opening.
3. "The students filed quietly into the auditorium for the assembly." → Rewrite with an adverb opening.
4. "The old clock tower chimed twelve times at midnight." → Rewrite with a prepositional phrase opening.

**Exercise B: Add a Strategic Short Sentence (items 5-6)**
Say: "Each passage below is missing a short, punchy sentence. Read the passage and write the short sentence that should go at the end."

5. "The roller coaster climbed slowly up the first hill, clicking and clanking with each inch of progress. The riders gripped the safety bars. The wind picked up as they neared the top. _______________."
6. "For weeks, the students had rehearsed their lines, practiced their blocking, built the sets, and sewn the costumes. Every detail had been checked and double-checked. _______________."

**Exercise C: Revise for Variety (items 7-8)**
Say: "Revise each passage to improve sentence variety. Change at least three things: vary a sentence opening, add or adjust sentence length, and use a strategic short sentence."

7. "The fox crept through the tall grass. The fox spotted a rabbit. The fox crouched low. The fox pounced. The rabbit escaped into its burrow."
8. "The science fair was in the gymnasium. The gymnasium was full of projects. Students stood next to their displays. Judges walked around with clipboards. Everyone was nervous."

Work through items 1-2 and item 5 together. Have students complete the remaining items independently.

---

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate independent ability to vary sentence patterns for style and reader engagement.

**Part A: Identify the Technique (items 1-4)**
For each sentence or passage, identify which variety technique is being used: varied opening, varied length, or strategic short sentence.

1. "Beneath the ancient bridge, the river rushed over smooth stones."
2. "The magician waved his wand, spoke mysterious words, and pulled a long ribbon of silk from his sleeve. Then the hat was empty."
3. "Exhausted from the three-hour practice, the swimmers dragged themselves to the locker room."
4. "She studied. She planned. She practiced. On the day of the competition, she was ready."

**Part B: Revise the Passage (items 5-6)**
Revise each monotonous passage to create engaging writing with varied sentence patterns. You must change at least four sentences.

5. "The park was crowded. Families were having picnics. Children were playing on the swings. Dogs were chasing frisbees. The sun was shining. Everyone was having a good time."

6. "The astronaut climbed into the spacecraft. The astronaut buckled the harness. The astronaut checked the instruments. The countdown began. The rocket launched. The astronaut was in space."

**Part C: Original Writing (items 7-8)**

7. Write a five-sentence paragraph about a storm (real or imaginary). Use at least three different sentence openings and include one strategic short sentence.

8. Write a five-sentence paragraph about a celebration or event. Vary the sentence lengths deliberately — include at least one sentence under seven words and one sentence over twenty words.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Part A and Part B.
**Stretch:** Complete Part A, Part B, and one item from Part C.
**Challenge:** Complete all items, plus analyze a paragraph from a published author. Identify the sentence variety techniques the author uses and write a brief explanation (3-4 sentences) of how these techniques affect the reader's experience.

### Interleaved Review
9. (Grade 5 review — L.5.3a) Reduce this wordy sentence without losing its meaning: "In my own personal opinion, I believe that the school cafeteria should consider the possibility of maybe adding more vegetarian options to the menu."
10. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1e) Write a sentence using the correlative conjunction pair "not only/but also" about a school event.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Revise this passage by varying the sentence patterns: "The boat sailed across the lake. The boat hit a wave. The boat rocked to one side. Water splashed over the edge."
2. Name two techniques for creating sentence variety in your writing.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Answers vary. Example: "The small boat sailed steadily across the glassy lake. Without warning, it struck a wave. Rocking sharply to one side, the boat sent a spray of cold water over the edge." (Must show at least two variety techniques: different opening, different length, combined sentences, or strategic short sentence.)
2. Any two of: vary sentence length, vary sentence openings (adverb, prepositional phrase, participial phrase), use strategic short sentences for emphasis, mix sentence types (simple, compound, complex). Accept any two clearly stated techniques.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct. For item 1, the revised passage must show at least two variety techniques.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on one technique at a time — start with varying openings, since it is the most mechanical and easiest to practice
- Provide sentence opening cards with starters: "Beneath...", "With great care...", "Sprinting toward...", "Silently,..."
- Read passages aloud before and after revision so students can hear the difference
- Use color-coding: highlight the first word of every sentence in a passage to make repetitive openings visible

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student has completed all instructional lessons for this unit and is ready for the Unit Assessment
- Encourage the student to apply sentence variety in all their writing across subjects, especially in essays and creative pieces

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Author Study:** Choose a favorite author and select a one-page passage from one of their books. Analyze the passage for sentence variety: How many simple, compound, and complex sentences are there? How does the author vary openings? Are there any strategic short sentences? Write a one-paragraph analysis.
2. **Before-and-After Portfolio:** Take three paragraphs from your earlier writing assignments (from any class). Revise each one using the sentence variety techniques from this lesson. Create a before-and-after display showing the improvement.
3. **Rhythm Writing Challenge:** Write a short piece (half a page) about any topic. Deliberately create a rhythm pattern: long, long, short. Long, long, long, short. See how the pattern affects the mood and pacing.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students sometimes overdo variety once they learn it, producing paragraphs where every sentence starts differently and the lengths zigzag wildly. Remind them that variety should serve the writing, not dominate it. The goal is a natural flow, not a forced pattern.
- The participial phrase opening is the most prone to errors. Watch for dangling modifiers: "Running down the hall, the bell rang" (the bell was not running). Teach students to check that the subject of the main clause is the one performing the action in the participial phrase.
- Reading aloud is one of the most effective tools for this lesson. When students hear their writing, they can detect monotony and choppiness far more easily than when they read silently. Encourage them to read their revised passages aloud to a partner.
- This lesson and Lesson 9 work together: Lesson 9 focused on meaning (using structure to show relationships between ideas), while this lesson focuses on style (using structure to create rhythm and engagement). Help students see that the best writing accomplishes both simultaneously.
