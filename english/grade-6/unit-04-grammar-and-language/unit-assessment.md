## Answer Key and Scoring Guide

### Section A: Subject-Verb Agreement (L.6.1c) — 14 points

**1.** Answer: **d) The collection of seashells sits on the bookshelf.**
- (a) "boxes...is" — plural subject, singular verb (should be "are")
- (b) "leader...organize" — singular subject, plural verb (should be "organizes")
- (c) "students...answers" — plural subject, singular verb (should be "answer")

**2.** Answer: "The flowers in the garden near the old stone wall **bloom** every spring."
- Subject is "flowers" (plural). "Near the old stone wall" and "in the garden" are prepositional phrases. "Blooms" is singular and should be "bloom."

**3.** Answer: **d) Neither the wind nor the waves were strong enough to cancel the sailing lesson.**
- (a) Nearest subject is "students" (plural) → should be "were," but the answer says "was." Wait — re-reading: "Neither the teacher nor the students was" — nearest subject is "students" (plural), so verb should be "were." This is incorrect.
- (b) "Either the dogs or the cat are" — nearest subject is "cat" (singular), so verb should be "is." Incorrect.
- (c) Compound subject with "and" → always plural → should be "walk." Incorrect.
- (d) Nearest subject is "waves" (plural) → "were" is correct. Correct.

**4.** Answer: "Everyone in the three sixth-grade classes **is** excited about the upcoming field trip."
- "Everyone" is an indefinite pronoun that is always singular → "is," not "are."

**5.** Answer: **need**
- "Several" is an indefinite pronoun that is always plural → "need."

**6.** Errors and corrections (1 point each, 2 points total):
- "There **are** many reasons to join the school band." (Subject is "reasons" — plural.)
- "Each of the instruments **requires** practice." ("Each" is always singular → "requires.")
- Note: "The group of musicians perform" could be considered an error (group = singular → "performs"), but since performing at games could imply members acting together, accept either. Award full credit if student corrects the first two.

**7.** Answer: **want**
- Nearest subject to the verb is "players" (plural) → "want."

**8.** Answers vary. Award 2 points per sentence (4 points total) if:
- Sentence 1 uses a singular indefinite pronoun (everyone, someone, nobody, each, etc.) with a singular verb
- Sentence 2 uses a compound subject with "and" and a plural verb
- Both sentences are grammatically correct
- Example 1: "Nobody in the cafeteria **wants** to eat the mystery casserole."
- Example 2: "The principal and the counselor **greet** students at the door every morning."

### Section B: Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement (L.6.1c) — 14 points

**9.** Answer: **a) The birds built their nest high in the oak tree.**
- (b) "student...your" — person mismatch (third person antecedent, second person pronoun)
- (c) "team...his" — number mismatch (team is either singular "its" or plural "their," not "his")
- (d) "firefighters...his" — number mismatch (plural antecedent, singular pronoun)

**10.** This item is nuanced. "Each" is grammatically singular, so strictly: "Each of the athletes stretched carefully before the race, making sure **his or her** muscles were warm." However, singular "they/their" is widely accepted here. Award 1 point if the student either: (a) changes "their" to "his or her" with an explanation that "each" is singular, OR (b) notes that the sentence is acceptable as written because singular "they" is accepted with "each." Award 0 points only if the student makes no comment or makes the sentence worse.

**11.** Answer: "The gardeners planted new flowers along the path, and **they** watered them every evening."
- Antecedent is "gardeners" (plural) → pronoun must be "they," not "he."

**12.** Answer: **b) The jury reached its verdict after three hours of deliberation.** AND **d) The committee submitted their separate reports individually.**
- (b) The jury acts as one unit (reaching a single verdict) → "its" is correct.
- (d) The committee members act individually (each submitting a separate report) → "their" is correct.
- Award 1 point if student selects either (b) or (d) with correct reasoning. Both are valid answers.
- (a) is wrong: "orchestra...their instrument" — "instrument" should be plural if using "their," or singular possessive if treating the orchestra as a unit.
- (c) "flock...their direction" — a flock changing direction acts as one unit → should be "its."

**13.** Answer: **their**
- Nearest antecedent is "players" (plural) → "their."

**14.** Three errors (1 point each, 3 points total):
- "When a student finishes **your** test" → should be "**his or her** test" or "**their** test" (third person, not second person)
- "they should review **your** answers" → should be "**his or her** answers" or "**their** answers" (third person)
- "The class should turn in **your** tests" → should be "**their** tests" (third person; "class" is third person, not second)
- Corrected: "When a student finishes **his or her** test early, **he or she** should review **his or her** answers carefully. The class should turn in **their** tests to the basket on the front table." (Also accept consistent use of singular "they" throughout.)

**15.** Answer: **its**
- The family acts as one unit (packing together) → singular pronoun "its."

**16.** Answers vary. Award 2 points per sentence (4 points total) if:
- Sentence 1 has a clearly plural antecedent matched with a plural pronoun (they, them, their)
- Sentence 2 uses "everyone" and matches with "his or her" or "their"
- Both sentences are grammatically correct
- Example 1: "The volunteers cleaned up the park and donated **their** time every Saturday."
- Example 2: "Everyone in the class should submit **his or her** project by Friday." (Accept "their.")

### Section D: Punctuation for Nonrestrictive Elements (L.6.2a) — 10 points

**23.** Answer: "The Grand Canyon**,** which is located in Arizona**,** attracts millions of visitors each year."
- "Which is located in Arizona" is nonrestrictive — "The Grand Canyon" is already identified.

**24.** Answer: **Restrictive.** The clause "who won the essay contest" is essential because it identifies which specific student will read at the assembly. Without it, the sentence would be "The student will read her piece at the assembly," which does not specify which student. Since the clause is essential to the meaning, it is restrictive and correctly has no commas.

**25.** Answer: "The final act of the talent show **—** a stunning dance performance **—** received a standing ovation."

**26.** Answer: "The school was built in 1952 **(** over seventy years ago **)** and has been renovated twice."

**27.** Answer: **c) The Mona Lisa, which hangs in the Louvre, is one of the most famous paintings in the world.**
- (a) "that" introduces a restrictive clause — should not have commas. Also, "that" is more appropriate than "which" for restrictive clauses.
- (b) "My sister who is a doctor" — "who is a doctor" is nonrestrictive (presumably the speaker has only one sister, so the clause is extra info) — needs commas: "My sister, who is a doctor, lives in Portland."
- (d) "Students, who arrive late," — if we put commas, it means ALL students arrive late, which is likely not the intended meaning. It should be restrictive (no commas): "Students who arrive late must check in at the office."

**28.** Answer: "The science teacher**,** who gives the hardest tests**,** is also the most popular teacher in the school."
- The second comma is missing. A nonrestrictive element must be set off by commas on both sides.

**29.** Award 2 points total: 1 point for correctly writing all three versions, 1 point for stating a preference with a reasonable explanation.
- Commas: "The ancient oak tree, estimated to be over 300 years old, still produces acorns every fall."
- Parentheses: "The ancient oak tree (estimated to be over 300 years old) still produces acorns every fall."
- Dashes: "The ancient oak tree — estimated to be over 300 years old — still produces acorns every fall."
- Preference: answers vary. Parentheses work well because the age estimate is background detail (de-emphasized). Dashes work if the writer wants to draw attention to the impressive age.

**30.** Answers vary. Award 1 point if the sentence contains a nonrestrictive appositive correctly set off by commas.
- Example: "My neighbor, a retired marine biologist, gives fascinating talks about ocean conservation."

### Section E: Varying Sentence Patterns for Meaning and Style (L.6.3a) — 10 points

**31.** Award 1 point for a complex sentence showing cause-effect with a subordinating conjunction.
- Example: "Because the river rose six feet overnight, several roads near the bridge were closed."
- Also accept: "Since the river rose six feet overnight, several roads near the bridge were closed." Or: "Several roads near the bridge were closed because the river rose six feet overnight."

**32.** Award 1 point for a compound sentence showing contrast.
- Example: "The first half of the book moved slowly, but the second half was full of action and suspense."
- Also accept: "The first half of the book moved slowly; however, the second half was full of action and suspense."

**33.** The strategic short sentence is: **"They reached the summit."**
- It is effective because it follows a long, detailed sentence that builds a sense of exhaustion and difficulty. The short sentence delivers the payoff — the achievement — with maximum impact. The contrast in length creates a satisfying rhythm and emphasizes the climbers' success.
- Award 1 point for identifying the sentence and providing a reasonable explanation of its effect.

**34.** Award 3 points for a revised passage that demonstrates: varied sentence openings (at least two different types) (1 point), varied sentence lengths (1 point), and at least one strategic short sentence (1 point).
- Example: "Streamers and balloons transformed the gymnasium into a sparkling dance floor. From the DJ booth in the corner, popular songs pulsed through the crowd as students filled the floor, laughing and spinning under the colored lights. Near the walls, chaperones watched with smiles, and the refreshment table — stocked with punch and cookies — drew a steady line of hungry dancers. Three hours flew by."

**35.** (1 point each, 2 points total)
a) Prepositional phrase opening: "Above the open meadow, the hawk circled lazily." (Accept any prepositional phrase opening that maintains the original meaning.)
b) Participial phrase opening: "Gasping for air, the runners sprinted toward the finish line." (Accept any participial phrase opening that maintains the original meaning and avoids a dangling modifier.)

**36.** Award 2 points for a response that: identifies Version B as more effective (1 point) and provides specific analysis of the sentence variety techniques used (1 point).
- Example: "Version B is more effective because it uses varied sentence openings ('At noon,' 'Within minutes'), mixes long and short sentences for rhythm, and includes the strategic short sentence 'People lit candles,' which creates a striking image after the longer descriptions of the storm. The compound and complex sentences in Version B also show clear relationships between ideas, such as the cause-effect connection between the power going out and the neighborhood falling into darkness."
- Award 1 point if the student identifies Version B but provides only general or vague reasoning.
