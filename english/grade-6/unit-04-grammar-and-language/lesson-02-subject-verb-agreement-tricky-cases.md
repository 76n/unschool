# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 2

## Topic
Subject-Verb Agreement: Tricky Cases

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply subject-verb agreement rules to compound subjects joined by "and," "or," and "nor"
- Determine the correct verb form for indefinite pronoun subjects (everyone, nobody, several, few, etc.)
- Identify the subject and correct the verb in inverted sentences (sentences where the verb comes before the subject)
- Edit a passage for subject-verb agreement errors involving tricky constructions

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of basic subject-verb agreement from Lesson 1
- Ability to identify subjects and verbs, including crossing out prepositional phrases
- Familiarity with correlative conjunctions either/or and neither/nor (Grade 5, L.5.1e)
- Knowledge of singular and plural noun forms

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (blue for subjects, red for verbs)
- Indefinite pronoun reference chart (singular, plural, and both categories)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review Lesson 1 concepts and introduce the idea that some subjects are harder to classify.

**Activity: Quick Agreement Check**

Say: "Before we start today's lesson, let us make sure our basic agreement skills are sharp. Choose the correct verb for each sentence. You have ninety seconds."

Write on the board:
1. The windows in the old building (rattle / rattles) when the wind blows.
2. My sister (want / wants) to join the robotics club.
3. The instruments in the orchestra (sound / sounds) beautiful together.

Review answers quickly (1. rattle, 2. wants, 3. sound).

Say: "Good. You have the basics down. But what happens when the subject is not a simple noun? What if you have two subjects connected by 'and' or 'or'? What if the subject is a word like 'everyone' or 'nobody'? What if the subject comes after the verb? Today we tackle these tricky cases."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach three categories of tricky subject-verb agreement: compound subjects, indefinite pronouns, and inverted sentences.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Compound subject:** Two or more subjects connected by a conjunction (and, or, nor)
- **Indefinite pronoun:** A pronoun that does not refer to a specific person or thing (everyone, someone, nobody, few, several, all)
- **Inverted sentence:** A sentence in which the verb comes before the subject (e.g., "Here comes the bus.")

### Instruction Notes

**Tricky Case 1: Compound Subjects**

Say: "When two subjects are joined by 'and,' the subject is almost always plural, so it takes a plural verb."

Write: **My brother and my cousin play soccer together.** (Two people = plural)

Say: "But when two subjects are joined by 'or' or 'nor,' the verb agrees with the subject closest to the verb."

Write:
- **Either the coach or the players decide the starting lineup.** (players = plural, closest to verb)
- **Either the players or the coach decides the starting lineup.** (coach = singular, closest to verb)

Say: "This is the 'nearest subject' rule. It works the same way with neither/nor."

**Tricky Case 2: Indefinite Pronouns**

Say: "Indefinite pronouns are words like everyone, nobody, something, and few. Some are always singular, some are always plural, and some can be either."

Write on the board:

| Always Singular | Always Plural | Can Be Either |
|----------------|--------------|---------------|
| everyone, everybody | both | all |
| someone, somebody | few | most |
| anyone, anybody | many | some |
| no one, nobody | several | none |
| each, every | | any |
| either, neither | | |

Say: "If the indefinite pronoun is in the 'always singular' column, it takes a singular verb — even though it might feel plural. 'Everyone' refers to all the people, but grammatically it is singular."

Write: **Everyone in the sixth grade knows the school fight song.**

Say: "For the 'can be either' column, look at what the pronoun refers to. If it refers to something uncountable or singular, use a singular verb. If it refers to something countable and plural, use a plural verb."

Write:
- **Some of the cake is gone.** (cake = uncountable → singular)
- **Some of the cookies are gone.** (cookies = countable, plural → plural)

**Tricky Case 3: Inverted Sentences**

Say: "In most sentences, the subject comes before the verb. But sometimes the order is flipped. Sentences starting with 'here,' 'there,' or a question word often put the verb first."

Write:
- **Here comes the parade.** (Subject: parade — singular → "comes")
- **There are three reasons to celebrate.** (Subject: reasons — plural → "are")
- **Where is my backpack?** (Subject: backpack — singular → "is")

Say: "When you see an inverted sentence, mentally rearrange it to the normal order to check agreement. 'Here comes the parade' becomes 'The parade comes here.' Now you can see the subject clearly."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 (compound subject with "and"):**
Write: "The guitar and the drums creates a powerful sound."

Say: "Two subjects joined by 'and' — that is plural. 'Creates' is singular. I need 'create.'"
Correct: "The guitar and the drums **create** a powerful sound."

**Example 2 (compound subject with "or"):**
Write: "Neither the students nor the teacher were ready for the fire drill."

Say: "Nearest subject to the verb is 'teacher' — singular. So I need 'was,' not 'were.'"
Correct: "Neither the students nor the teacher **was** ready for the fire drill."

**Example 3 (indefinite pronoun):**
Write: "Several of the paintings in the gallery is for sale."

Say: "'Several' is always plural. So I need 'are,' not 'is.'"
Correct: "Several of the paintings in the gallery **are** for sale."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply tricky-case rules with facilitator support and immediate feedback.

Say: "For each sentence, identify the tricky case (compound subject, indefinite pronoun, or inverted sentence), then choose the correct verb."

1. My mom and my dad (drive / drives) me to school on rainy days. [compound — "and"]
2. Neither the librarian nor the volunteers (has / have) found the missing book. [compound — "nor," nearest = volunteers]
3. Everyone in the two science classes (need / needs) safety goggles. [indefinite — everyone = singular]
4. There (is / are) several good restaurants near the park. [inverted — subject = restaurants, plural]
5. Few of the hikers (was / were) prepared for the sudden rainstorm. [indefinite — few = always plural]
6. Either my aunt or my cousins (is / are) picking me up after practice. [compound — "or," nearest = cousins]
7. Here (come / comes) the marching band down the street. [inverted — subject = band, singular]
8. Nobody in the three classrooms (want / wants) to miss the field trip. [indefinite — nobody = singular]

Work through items 1-3 on the board together. For items 4-8, have students write their answers, then discuss with a partner before class review.

---

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate independent mastery of tricky subject-verb agreement cases.

**Directions:** For items 1-8, choose the correct verb or correct the error. For items 9-10, write original sentences.

1. Both the principal and the counselor (greet / greets) students at the door each morning.
2. Someone in the back rows (keep / keeps) whispering during the assembly.
3. There (was / were) only two seats left in the front row.
4. Neither the novel nor the short stories (appeal / appeals) to Marcus.
5. Most of the homework (is / are) due by Friday afternoon.
6. Correct the error: "Each of the musicians in the orchestra tune their instrument before the concert."
7. Correct the error: "Here is the photos from our field trip to the science museum."
8. Correct the error: "Either the goalie or the defenders was responsible for the last goal."
9. Write an original sentence using an indefinite pronoun (everyone, nobody, few, or several) as the subject with a correctly agreeing verb.
10. Write an original sentence that begins with "There" and uses a plural subject with a correctly agreeing verb.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-8.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-10.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-10, plus write a short paragraph (4-5 sentences) about a school event. Include at least one compound subject, one indefinite pronoun subject, and one inverted sentence. Make sure all subjects and verbs agree.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 5 review — L.5.3a) Combine these two sentences into one: "The thunderstorm knocked out the power." + "The thunderstorm flooded the basement."
12. (Grade 5 review — L.5.2a) Add correct punctuation to this series: "For the science project we need cardboard glue paint and markers."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Choose the correct verb: "Neither the cat nor the dogs (sleep / sleeps) on the porch at night."
2. Choose the correct verb: "Everybody in the sixth-grade classes (enjoy / enjoys) the annual talent show."
3. Correct the error: "There is many reasons to read every day."

**Expected Answers:**
1. **sleep** (Nearest subject to the verb is "dogs" — plural → "sleep.")
2. **enjoys** ("Everybody" is an indefinite pronoun that is always singular → "enjoys.")
3. "There **are** many reasons to read every day." (Subject is "reasons" — plural. Rearranged: "Many reasons are there.")

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review one tricky case at a time rather than all three together
- Provide the indefinite pronoun chart as a reference tool during practice
- For compound subjects, have the student circle the conjunction and draw an arrow to the nearest subject
- For inverted sentences, have the student physically rewrite the sentence in normal order before choosing the verb
- Reteach with simpler sentences before adding prepositional phrases

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 3 on pronoun-antecedent agreement
- Encourage the student to look for tricky agreement cases in their own writing and self-correct

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Tricky Case Scavenger Hunt:** Find two examples each of compound subjects, indefinite pronoun subjects, and inverted sentences in a book or article. Write each sentence and label the subject and verb.
2. **Error Creation Challenge:** Write six sentences with deliberate subject-verb agreement errors (two per tricky case). Exchange with a partner and race to find and correct all six.
3. **Indefinite Pronoun Poster:** Create a colorful reference poster showing all three categories of indefinite pronouns (always singular, always plural, either) with example sentences for each.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The "nearest subject" rule for or/nor compound subjects is the most confusing concept in this lesson. Use plenty of side-by-side examples showing how flipping the order changes the verb: "Either the dogs or the cat *is*..." vs. "Either the cat or the dogs *are*..."
- Students often want to treat "everyone" and "everybody" as plural because they refer to many people. Reinforce that grammatically, these words are singular: "everyone has" not "everyone have." This is a convention of standard English, not a matter of logic.
- For the "can be either" indefinite pronouns (all, most, some, none, any), the trick is to look at the object of the prepositional phrase that follows. This builds naturally on Lesson 1's strategy of examining prepositional phrases.
- If students are overwhelmed by all three tricky cases at once, consider splitting this lesson across two days, addressing compound subjects and inverted sentences on day one and indefinite pronouns on day two.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **greet** (Compound subject with "and" → always plural)
2. **keeps** ("Someone" is always singular → "keeps")
3. **were** (Inverted sentence; subject is "seats" — plural → "were")
4. **appeal** (Nearest subject to verb is "stories" — plural → "appeal")
5. **is** ("Most of the homework" — homework is uncountable/singular → "is")
6. Corrected: "Each of the musicians in the orchestra **tunes** **his or her** instrument before the concert." ("Each" is always singular → "tunes." Also accept singular "their" as colloquially standard.)
7. Corrected: "Here **are** the photos from our field trip to the science museum." (Subject is "photos" — plural → "are")
8. Corrected: "Either the goalie or the defenders **were** responsible for the last goal." (Nearest subject is "defenders" — plural → "were")
9. Answers vary. Example: "Nobody in our class **wants** to miss the pizza party on Friday."
10. Answers vary. Example: "There **are** several interesting trails near the lake that we could hike this weekend."

### Interleaved Review
11. Combined: "The thunderstorm knocked out the power and flooded the basement." (Also accept: "The thunderstorm knocked out the power and flooded the basement at the same time.")
12. Corrected: "For the science project, we need cardboard, glue, paint, and markers." (Comma after introductory phrase + commas in a series with Oxford comma)
