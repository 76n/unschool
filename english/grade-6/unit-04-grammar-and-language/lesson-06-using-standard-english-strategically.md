# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 6

## Topic
Using Standard English Strategically

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Determine when a situation calls for standard English and when informal language is appropriate
- Edit a piece of informal writing to make it suitable for a formal audience
- Revise a piece of overly formal writing to make it suitable for a casual audience
- Apply code-switching skills by rewriting the same message for two different audiences

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of language variation from Lesson 5 (dialect, register, slang, code-switching)
- Ability to identify non-standard features and rewrite in standard English
- Knowledge of audience and purpose in writing (Grade 5, W.5.4)
- Subject-verb and pronoun-antecedent agreement from Lessons 1-4

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected writing samples at different registers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- T-chart graphic organizer (Formal Situations / Informal Situations)
- Copies of editing passages for Independent Practice

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Reinforce the concept from Lesson 5 and begin thinking about strategic language choices.

**Activity: Which Register?**

Say: "I am going to describe five situations. For each one, hold up one finger if you would use informal language and two fingers if you would use standard/formal English. Be ready to explain your choice."

Read aloud:
1. Writing a thank-you letter to the principal for organizing a school field trip. (formal)
2. Leaving a voice message for your best friend about weekend plans. (informal)
3. Presenting a book report to your class. (formal)
4. Chatting with teammates during basketball practice. (informal)
5. Writing an email to a local business to request a donation for a school fundraiser. (formal)

After each one, ask a student to explain their reasoning. Highlight the key factors: audience, purpose, and setting.

Say: "Yesterday we learned to recognize differences between standard and non-standard English. Today, we put that knowledge to work. We are going to practice choosing the right register and editing real writing to match the situation."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach a framework for deciding when standard English is expected and demonstrate how to edit writing to match the appropriate register.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Audience:** The person or group who will read or hear your communication
- **Purpose:** The reason you are writing or speaking (to inform, persuade, entertain, request)
- **Register:** The level of formality in language — from very casual to very formal
- **Tone:** The writer's attitude toward the subject and audience, conveyed through word choice and style

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Making the right language choice comes down to three questions."

Write on the board:
1. **Who is my audience?** (Friend? Teacher? Stranger? Business?)
2. **What is my purpose?** (To inform? Persuade? Request? Entertain?)
3. **What is the setting?** (Text message? School essay? Job interview? Email?)

Say: "When the audience is someone in authority, the purpose is serious, and the setting is academic or professional — use standard English. When the audience is a peer, the purpose is social, and the setting is casual — informal language is fine."

Write a spectrum on the board:

**Very Casual ←——————————————→ Very Formal**
text to friend | talking with classmates | class presentation | school essay | letter to senator

Say: "Let me show you how to edit writing to shift it along this spectrum."

**Editing Strategy — Casual to Formal:**
Write on the board:
1. Replace slang and abbreviations with precise vocabulary
2. Use complete sentences (no fragments)
3. Fix any non-standard grammar (agreement, double negatives, pronoun case)
4. Remove filler words (like, basically, you know, and stuff)
5. Use a respectful, professional tone

**Editing Strategy — Formal to Casual:**
Write on the board:
1. Replace stiff or overly technical vocabulary with everyday words
2. Shorten sentences where possible
3. Use contractions (don't, can't, it's)
4. Add a warm, conversational tone
5. Include personal touches that fit the relationship

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Casual → Formal**
Write the casual version: "hey mr. thompson so basically me and my group are gonna need like two more days for our project cuz we had some issues and stuff. thx"

Say: "This is a message to a teacher. Let me apply the editing strategy."

Write the formal version: "Dear Mr. Thompson, my group and I are requesting a two-day extension on our project. We encountered some unexpected difficulties during the research phase and want to make sure our final product meets the assignment requirements. Thank you for considering our request. Sincerely, [Student Name]"

Point out each change: slang removed, complete sentences, standard grammar ("my group and I"), professional tone, proper greeting and closing.

**Example 2: Formal → Casual**
Write the formal version: "I would like to extend an invitation for you to accompany me to the cinema this Saturday afternoon at approximately 3:00 PM to view the newly released science fiction film."

Say: "This is a message to a friend. It sounds robotic and stiff. Let me make it sound natural."

Write the casual version: "Hey, want to go see that new sci-fi movie on Saturday? I was thinking the 3 o'clock showing."

Point out: shorter, contractions, everyday vocabulary, warm tone.

**Example 3: Matching register to audience**
Write: "The cafeteria food is, like, so gross today. Nobody wants to eat it."

Say: "If I am telling this to a friend at lunch, it is perfectly fine. But what if I am writing a formal complaint to the school nutrition committee?"

Write: "Several students have expressed concerns about the quality of today's lunch options. We respectfully request that the nutrition committee review the menu and consider improvements."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice editing writing to match specific audiences and purposes.

Say: "For each item, I will give you a sentence or short passage and tell you the audience. Rewrite it to match the situation."

**Exercise A: Make it formal.** (These are casual — rewrite for the given formal audience.)

1. "yo the book fair is gonna be so lit this year you gotta come check it out" → Rewrite as an announcement for the school newsletter.
2. "i don't got my homework cuz my dog like actually ate it no joke" → Rewrite as an explanation to your teacher.
3. "that history project was lowkey really hard but me and jada crushed it" → Rewrite as a reflection paragraph for your teacher.

**Exercise B: Make it casual.** (These are overly formal — rewrite for a friend.)

4. "I am writing to inform you that I shall be unable to attend the recreational gathering scheduled for this coming Saturday." → Rewrite as a text to a friend.
5. "It has come to my attention that the new student who recently transferred to our educational institution appears to be an individual of considerable friendliness." → Rewrite as something you would say to a classmate.

Work through item 1 and item 4 together. Have students complete the rest independently, then share with a partner.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to strategically choose and apply the appropriate register.

**Part A: Edit for the Audience (items 1-4)**

1. Rewrite this casual text as a formal email to your principal: "hey so a bunch of us think we should get longer lunch breaks cuz 25 min isnt enough time and we're always rushing and stuff. can you do something about it plz?"

2. Rewrite this overly formal message as a casual note to a classmate: "I wish to express my sincerest gratitude for your willingness to share your comprehensive notes from yesterday's lecture, as I was regrettably absent due to a minor illness."

3. A student wrote this paragraph for a school essay: "The American Revolution was like this huge deal where the colonies basically told Britain they were done being bossed around. The colonists were super mad about taxes and stuff, so they fought back and won their freedom." Rewrite it in standard academic English.

4. Your friend writes you this message: "I would like to formally request your opinion regarding the optimal selection of extracurricular activities for the forthcoming academic semester." Rewrite it the way a real friend would actually say it.

**Part B: Code-Switch (items 5-6)**

5. Write TWO versions of the same message — you want to tell someone about a great book you just finished reading.
   - Version A: Text message to your best friend
   - Version B: Book recommendation for the school library website

6. Write TWO versions of the same message — you want to explain why you were late to class.
   - Version A: What you would say to your friend in the hallway
   - Version B: A written note to your teacher

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Part A (items 1-4).
**Stretch:** Complete Part A and one item from Part B.
**Challenge:** Complete all items in Part A and Part B, plus write a brief reflection (3-4 sentences) explaining which direction of code-switching you find harder — casual to formal or formal to casual — and why.

### Interleaved Review
7. (Grade 5 review — L.5.3a) Combine these three choppy sentences into one smooth sentence: "The museum was large." "The museum was full of dinosaur fossils." "The museum was on the corner of Main Street."
8. (Grade 5 review — L.5.2a) Add correct punctuation to this sentence: "For the camping trip we packed a tent sleeping bags flashlights and a first aid kit."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Name two situations where standard English is expected and two where informal language is appropriate.
2. Rewrite this sentence for a school essay: "Me and my partner done a experiment about plants and it was pretty cool."

**Expected Answers:**
1. Standard English expected: school essays, emails to teachers, job interviews, college applications, formal presentations, letters to businesses. Informal appropriate: texting friends, talking at lunch, social media posts to peers, casual conversations with family. (Accept any two reasonable examples for each.)
2. "My partner and I conducted an experiment about plants, and the results were fascinating." OR "My partner and I performed an experiment on plant growth, and it produced interesting results." (Must fix: "Me and my partner" → "My partner and I"; "done" → "conducted/performed"; "pretty cool" → more formal vocabulary.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 items correct. For item 2, student must fix at least two of the three non-standard features and produce a sentence appropriate for academic writing.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three-question framework (Who? What purpose? What setting?) using concrete scenarios
- Provide a checklist for the casual-to-formal editing strategy and have the student use it step by step
- Practice with shorter sentences before moving to paragraphs
- Use a T-chart: write the informal version on the left and build the formal version word by word on the right

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 7 on commas with nonrestrictive elements
- Encourage the student to apply code-switching awareness in all their writing across subjects

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Register Remix:** Find a short paragraph from a news article or textbook. Rewrite it as a text message conversation between friends discussing the same information. Then reflect: what information was lost or changed in the translation?
2. **Audience Switcheroo:** Write a short letter about your favorite hobby. Then rewrite the letter three times — once for a friend, once for a teacher, and once as a magazine article. Compare the three versions.
3. **Real-World Code-Switching:** Listen to a podcast episode, a news broadcast, and a casual YouTube video. Take notes on the language register used in each. How does the speaker's language change based on the audience?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students may initially resist the idea that "both registers are valid." Some may have been told their informal speech is "wrong" in previous settings. Be patient and consistent in reinforcing the code-switching framework. The message is not "stop talking that way" but rather "add this way of talking to your options."
- The casual-to-formal direction is typically harder for students than formal-to-casual. Provide extra scaffolding for the editing tasks that require formalization.
- When editing the essay in item 3 (Independent Practice), students may overcorrect — making the writing so formal that it loses personality. Guide them toward a balance: academic does not mean robotic. Standard English can still be engaging and have a clear voice.
- This lesson pairs well with writing instruction. Consider connecting it to an ongoing writing assignment where students must write for a specific audience. The grammar lesson becomes immediately practical.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A:**
1. Example formal email: "Dear Principal [Name], a group of students and I would like to respectfully request that the school consider extending our lunch period. Currently, the 25-minute break does not allow enough time for students to eat comfortably without rushing. We believe a longer lunch period would benefit students' well-being and focus during afternoon classes. Thank you for your time and consideration. Sincerely, [Student Name]"

2. Example casual note: "Thanks so much for sharing your notes from yesterday! I was out sick and really needed to catch up. You're a lifesaver!"

3. Example academic version: "The American Revolution was a pivotal event in which the American colonies declared independence from British rule. The colonists, frustrated by taxation policies they considered unjust, organized a resistance that ultimately led to the creation of a new nation founded on principles of self-governance."

4. Example casual version: "Hey, what clubs or activities are you thinking about doing next semester?" (Or: "What extracurriculars do you want to sign up for next semester?")

**Part B:**
5. Answers vary. Example:
   - Version A: "just finished this amazing book about a kid who discovers a hidden city underground!! u HAVE to read it its so good"
   - Version B: "I highly recommend *The Hidden City* by [Author Name]. This adventure novel follows a young protagonist who discovers an underground civilization. The suspense builds with each chapter, and the vivid descriptions make the setting feel real. It is an excellent choice for students who enjoy science fiction and mystery."

6. Answers vary. Example:
   - Version A: "Dude, I was so late because my bus totally broke down and we had to wait forever for a new one."
   - Version B: "Dear Ms. [Name], I apologize for arriving late to class today. My bus experienced a mechanical issue, and we had to wait for a replacement vehicle. I will make sure to get the notes I missed. Thank you for your understanding."

### Interleaved Review
7. Combined: "The large museum on the corner of Main Street was full of dinosaur fossils." (Accept any smooth combination that includes all three details.)
8. Corrected: "For the camping trip, we packed a tent, sleeping bags, flashlights, and a first aid kit." (Comma after introductory phrase + commas in series with Oxford comma.)
