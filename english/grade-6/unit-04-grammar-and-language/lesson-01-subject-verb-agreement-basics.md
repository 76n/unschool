# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 1

## Topic
Subject-Verb Agreement Basics

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define subject-verb agreement and explain why it matters for clear communication
- Identify the subject and verb in a sentence and determine whether they agree in number
- Apply the basic rule: singular subjects take singular verbs; plural subjects take plural verbs
- Correct sentences with subject-verb agreement errors

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify subjects and verbs in simple and compound sentences (Grade 5)
- Understanding of singular and plural nouns (Grade 4)
- Familiarity with present-tense verb forms (Grade 5, L.5.1d)
- Experience combining and expanding sentences (Grade 5, L.5.3a)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (blue for subjects, red for verbs)
- Index cards for subject-verb matching activity

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of subjects and verbs and set the stage for agreement.

**Activity: Subject-Verb Spotlight**

Say: "I am going to put four sentences on the board. For each one, underline the subject in blue and the verb in red. Work quickly — you have two minutes."

Write on the board:
1. The soccer team practices every afternoon.
2. My neighbors grow tomatoes in their garden.
3. A flock of geese flies south each winter.
4. The students in the hallway whisper quietly.

After two minutes, review as a class. For each sentence, ask: "Who or what is doing something? That is the subject. What are they doing? That is the verb."

Say: "Today we are going to learn about an important rule that connects subjects and verbs — it is called subject-verb agreement. When you get this right, your writing sounds clear and polished. When you get it wrong, readers stumble."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the foundational rule of subject-verb agreement and demonstrate how to check for it.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Subject-verb agreement:** The grammatical rule that a verb must match its subject in number (singular or plural)
- **Singular subject:** A subject that refers to one person, place, thing, or idea (the dog, she, the idea)
- **Plural subject:** A subject that refers to more than one (the dogs, they, the ideas)
- **Number:** Whether a word is singular (one) or plural (more than one)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Here is the core rule of subject-verb agreement: a singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb. It sounds simple, but there is a tricky part — in English, singular verbs often end in -s, while plural verbs do not. This is the opposite of how nouns work."

Write on the board:
- **Singular:** The bird **sings** in the morning. (one bird, verb ends in -s)
- **Plural:** The birds **sing** in the morning. (more than one bird, verb does not end in -s)

Say: "Notice something that confuses a lot of people: we add -s to a noun to make it plural (bird → birds), but we add -s to a verb to make it singular (sing → sings). Think of it this way — the -s can only be in one place: either on the noun or on the verb, but not both."

Write the rule: **Noun -s = plural. Verb -s = singular. They take turns.**

Say: "Let me show you my three-step checking strategy."

Write on the board:
1. **Find the subject.** Ask: Who or what is doing the action?
2. **Decide: singular or plural?**
3. **Check the verb.** Does it match?

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:**
Write: "The captain of the debate team argue persuasively."

Say: "Step 1 — find the subject. I might be tempted to say 'team,' but let me look more carefully. 'Of the debate team' is a prepositional phrase — it describes the captain. The real subject is 'captain.' Step 2 — singular or plural? 'Captain' is one person, so it is singular. Step 3 — check the verb. 'Argue' does not end in -s, so it is a plural verb form. Singular subject, plural verb — they do not agree. I need to change 'argue' to 'argues.'"

Write the correction: "The captain of the debate team **argues** persuasively."

**Example 2:**
Write: "The cookies on the kitchen counter smells delicious."

Say: "Step 1 — find the subject. 'On the kitchen counter' is a prepositional phrase. Cross it out mentally. The subject is 'cookies.' Step 2 — 'cookies' is plural. Step 3 — 'smells' ends in -s, so it is a singular verb form. Plural subject, singular verb — they do not match. I need 'smell.'"

Write the correction: "The cookies on the kitchen counter **smell** delicious."

**Example 3:**
Write: "My older sister walks to school every day."

Say: "Step 1 — the subject is 'sister.' Step 2 — singular. Step 3 — 'walks' ends in -s, so it is singular. Singular and singular — they match. This sentence is correct."

**Example 4:**
Write: "The players on the basketball team run drills before each game."

Say: "Step 1 — cross out 'on the basketball team.' The subject is 'players.' Step 2 — plural. Step 3 — 'run' does not end in -s, so it is plural. Plural and plural — correct."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply the three-step strategy with facilitator support and immediate feedback.

Say: "Let us practice together. For each sentence, follow the three steps: find the subject, decide if it is singular or plural, and check the verb. If the verb is wrong, fix it. If it is correct, write 'correct.'"

1. The dogs in the park (chase / chases) squirrels every morning.
2. My best friend (ride / rides) her bike to school.
3. The books on the top shelf (belong / belongs) to the library.
4. A group of dolphins (swim / swims) near the shore at sunset.
5. The teacher with the red glasses (explain / explains) things clearly.
6. Those tall sunflowers in the garden (grow / grows) very fast.
7. The captain of the two ships (sail / sails) into the harbor at dawn.
8. My neighbors across the street (own / owns) three cats.

Work through items 1-3 together on the board, using the three-step strategy explicitly each time. For items 4-8, have students write answers independently and then compare with a partner before reviewing as a class.

Say: "What pattern are you noticing? When there is a prepositional phrase between the subject and the verb, it can trick you. Always find the true subject first."

---

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to identify and correct subject-verb agreement errors independently.

**Directions:** For items 1-8, choose the correct verb form. For items 9-10, write your own sentences.

1. The collection of rare stamps (is / are) worth thousands of dollars.
2. The athletes on the track team (practice / practices) six days a week.
3. My grandmother (bake / bakes) the best apple pie in the neighborhood.
4. The mountains beyond the valley (rise / rises) over ten thousand feet.
5. A swarm of bees (buzz / buzzes) around the old oak tree every spring.
6. The chapters in this novel (reveal / reveals) the mystery slowly.
7. The girl with the bright green sneakers (run / runs) the fastest mile in our grade.
8. Several paintings in the gallery (feature / features) landscapes from our state.
9. Write a sentence with a singular subject and a verb that agrees. Underline the subject and circle the verb.
10. Write a sentence with a plural subject separated from the verb by a prepositional phrase. Underline the subject and circle the verb.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-8.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-10.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-10, then write a short paragraph (3-4 sentences) about your favorite hobby. Make sure every subject and verb agrees. Underline each subject-verb pair.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1e) Choose the correct correlative conjunction pair: "_______ the rain _______ the cold could stop the fans from cheering at the game." (either/or, neither/nor, both/and)
12. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1d) Find and correct the verb tense error: "Yesterday, the class performed a play and receives a standing ovation from the audience."

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Choose the correct verb: "The leader of the volunteer groups (organize / organizes) the food drive every November."
2. Correct the error in this sentence: "The stars above the city shines brightly on clear nights."

**Expected Answers:**
1. **organizes** (The subject is "leader" — singular — so the verb must be singular: "organizes.")
2. "The stars above the city **shine** brightly on clear nights." (The subject is "stars" — plural — so the verb must be "shine," not "shines.")

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 items correct with correct identification of the subject.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three-step strategy with additional examples using only simple subject-verb pairs (no prepositional phrases) before reintroducing sentences with intervening phrases
- Provide a reference card: "Singular subject → verb with -s. Plural subject → verb without -s."
- Have the student physically cross out prepositional phrases with a pencil before identifying the subject
- Practice orally: read sentences aloud and have the student point to the subject before choosing the verb

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 2, which covers tricky cases like compound subjects, indefinite pronouns, and inverted sentences
- Encourage the student to check subject-verb agreement when editing their own writing in other subjects

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Agreement Detective:** Find five sentences in a book, magazine, or website. Copy them, underline the subject, and circle the verb. Confirm that each pair agrees.
2. **Error Hunt:** Write three sentences with deliberate subject-verb agreement errors. Exchange with a partner and see if they can find and correct all three.
3. **Grammar Journal:** Start a running list of "tricky subjects" — words that are hard to classify as singular or plural (news, mathematics, scissors). Add to the list throughout the unit.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common error at this level is students matching the verb to the nearest noun rather than the true subject. Emphasize the prepositional-phrase crossing-out strategy from the start, as it prevents this error reliably.
- Some students may struggle with the counterintuitive -s pattern (noun -s = plural, verb -s = singular). Use the mnemonic "the -s takes turns" and revisit it each time a student is confused.
- When students encounter sentences where the subject comes after the verb (inverted order), save detailed instruction for Lesson 2 but acknowledge the pattern if a student asks.
- Be aware that some students may speak dialects where subject-verb agreement patterns differ from standard English. Handle this with sensitivity — the goal is to add standard English as a tool, not to label home language patterns as wrong. This topic is explored fully in Lessons 5-6.
