# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4: Grammar and Language

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview

Unit 4 strengthens students' command of English grammar conventions as they transition into middle school writing and communication. Students master subject-verb and pronoun-antecedent agreement, including challenging cases with compound subjects and indefinite pronouns. They explore how language varies across contexts — learning to recognize dialect, informal registers, and slang not as errors but as purposeful choices, while building the code-switching skills to select standard English when the situation calls for it. The unit also introduces punctuation for nonrestrictive elements (commas, parentheses, and dashes) and teaches students to vary sentence patterns strategically for meaning, emphasis, and style. Throughout the unit, grammar instruction is rooted in authentic, context-rich sentences rather than isolated drills, and students practice both identifying conventions and producing their own well-crafted writing.

### Facilitation Tips

- **Model thinking aloud.** When demonstrating grammar concepts, narrate your decision-making process so students internalize the reasoning, not just the rules. For example, say "The subject is 'everyone,' which is singular, so the verb needs to be singular too — 'everyone knows.'"
- **Use color-coding consistently.** Assign specific colors throughout the unit: blue for subjects, red for verbs, green for pronouns, orange for antecedents, purple for nonrestrictive elements. This visual anchor helps students track relationships across a sentence.
- **Approach language variation with respect.** When teaching L.6.1e, frame dialect, informal language, and slang as valid forms of communication — not as "bad grammar." The goal is code-switching awareness: understanding when standard English is expected (academic essays, job applications) and when informal language is appropriate (texting friends, casual conversation).
- **Connect grammar to writing purpose.** Regularly ask students why a particular grammar choice matters for meaning or reader experience. A comma changes the meaning of a sentence; a varied sentence pattern changes the rhythm of a paragraph.
- **Use student-generated examples.** After introducing each concept, invite students to create their own examples. Peer-generated sentences often resonate more than textbook examples and increase engagement.
- **Provide sentence frames for struggling writers.** For production tasks, offer sentence starters that incorporate the target grammar structure. Gradually remove scaffolding as confidence builds.
- **Revisit previous lessons briefly.** Spend one to two minutes at the start of each lesson reviewing a concept from the prior lesson. This cumulative review prevents forgetting and builds connections across the unit.
- **Leverage interleaved review.** Each lesson includes two review items from Grade 5 standards. Use these to reinforce prior learning and help students see how earlier grammar skills connect to current ones.

### Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| Students assume that the noun closest to the verb determines agreement, even when it is part of a prepositional phrase. | Teach students to cross out prepositional phrases to find the true subject. Example: "The box of chocolates *is* [not *are*] on the table." The subject is "box," not "chocolates." |
| Students believe that collective nouns are always singular or always plural. | Explain that collective nouns (team, family, class) are singular when the group acts as one unit and plural when members act individually. Provide contrasting examples: "The team *is* winning" vs. "The team *are* arguing among themselves." |
| Students treat informal or dialectal language as universally "wrong." | Emphasize that language varies by context and community. Dialect and informal language are rule-governed systems, not errors. Standard English is one register among many — the register expected in academic and professional settings. |
| Students set off all appositives and clauses with commas, including restrictive ones. | Clearly distinguish restrictive (essential) from nonrestrictive (nonessential) elements. Use the "crossing-out test": if removing the element changes the sentence's core meaning, it is restrictive and should not have commas. |
| Students believe longer, more complex sentences are always better. | Teach that sentence variety — mixing short, punchy sentences with longer, detailed ones — creates rhythm and emphasis. A single short sentence after several long ones can have powerful impact. |
| Students use "they" only as a plural pronoun and resist singular "they." | Acknowledge that singular "they" has a long history in English and is widely accepted for referring to indefinite antecedents (everyone, someone) and individuals whose gender is unspecified or nonbinary. |

### Assessment Guidance

The Unit Assessment contains five sections covering all four standards, with a total of approximately 60 points. Students should be assessed after completing all 10 lessons. Administer the assessment in a single sitting of approximately 40-50 minutes.

**Formative Assessment:** Each lesson includes a Check for Understanding (Exit Ticket) with 2-3 quick items. Use these to monitor progress daily and identify students who need additional support before the summative assessment.

**Summative Assessment:** The Unit Assessment uses a mix of multiple-choice, error correction, sentence combining, short writing, and analysis tasks to assess all four standards.

**Portfolio Option:** Have students collect their best writing samples from the unit — sentences and paragraphs that demonstrate effective use of agreement, appropriate register, nonrestrictive punctuation, and sentence variety. Students write a brief reflection identifying one grammar skill they improved most.

**Mastery Thresholds:**
- **Overall mastery:** 70% (42 out of 60 points)
- **Per-standard minimum:** 50% on each standard section
- Students who meet overall mastery but fall below 50% on any standard should receive targeted re-teaching on that standard before progressing.

### Differentiation at a Glance

**Below Level:**
- Provide a grammar reference card with subject-verb agreement rules, pronoun chart, and punctuation guidelines
- Reduce the number of Independent Practice items (complete odd-numbered items only)
- Offer sentence frames for all production tasks
- Allow oral responses before written ones
- Pair with an on-level peer for guided practice activities
- Highlight or underline the subject in each sentence before asking the student to choose the verb

**On Level:**
- Complete all Independent Practice items as written
- Use the standard lesson pacing
- Encourage self-checking with the answer key after independent work
- Use the "Stretch" tier for additional challenge when ready

**Above Level:**
- Assign the Challenge tier in Independent Practice consistently
- Ask students to write short paragraphs incorporating multiple grammar concepts from the unit
- Have students analyze grammar choices in published texts (mentor sentences from novels, articles, speeches)
- Encourage students to "teach" a concept to a peer, reinforcing their own understanding
- Provide more complex editing tasks with multiple error types embedded in a single passage

### Connection to Future Units

The grammar and language skills developed in Unit 4 form a critical foundation for the increasingly sophisticated writing and analysis expected in Grades 7 and 8. Subject-verb and pronoun-antecedent agreement (Lessons 1-4) are essential for the complex sentence structures students will encounter and produce in argumentative and informational writing. The code-switching awareness developed in Lessons 5-6 prepares students for the rhetorical analysis work in Grade 7, where understanding how authors make deliberate language choices is central. Punctuation of nonrestrictive elements (Lessons 7-8) connects directly to Grade 7 standards on using commas to separate coordinate adjectives and to the increasingly complex sentence structures in academic writing. Finally, sentence variety (Lessons 9-10) prepares students for Grade 7's focus on choosing among simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex sentences to signal differing relationships among ideas.
