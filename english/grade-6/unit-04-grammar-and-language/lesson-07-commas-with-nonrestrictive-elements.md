# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 7

## Topic
Commas with Nonrestrictive Elements

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define restrictive (essential) and nonrestrictive (nonessential) elements and explain the difference
- Apply the "crossing-out test" to determine whether an element is restrictive or nonrestrictive
- Use commas correctly to set off nonrestrictive appositives, adjective clauses, and participial phrases
- Avoid placing commas around restrictive elements that are essential to a sentence's meaning

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of commas in a series and after introductory elements (Grade 5, L.5.2a)
- Ability to identify subjects, verbs, and basic sentence structure (Lessons 1-2)
- Familiarity with appositives from Grade 5 sentence combining (L.5.3a)
- Knowledge of clauses beginning with "who," "which," and "that" (Grade 4, L.4.1a)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (purple for nonrestrictive elements, green for commas)
- Index cards for restrictive/nonrestrictive sorting activity
- Highlighters for marking practice passages

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate that removing certain parts of a sentence changes the meaning, while removing others does not.

**Activity: Does It Change the Meaning?**

Say: "I am going to show you two sentences. In each one, I have put a phrase in brackets. Read the sentence with the bracketed phrase and then without it. Does the core meaning change?"

Write on the board:
1. "The students [who finished early] may read quietly." → Remove: "The students may read quietly."
2. "Ms. Rivera, [our science teacher], assigned a new lab report." → Remove: "Ms. Rivera assigned a new lab report."

Say: "In sentence 1, removing 'who finished early' changes the meaning dramatically. Without it, ALL students may read quietly. With it, only the students who finished early may read. That phrase is essential — it restricts which students we mean."

Say: "In sentence 2, removing 'our science teacher' does not change who assigned the report. We already know it is Ms. Rivera. That phrase just gives us extra information. It is nonessential."

Say: "Today we learn how commas tell the reader whether information is essential or extra. This is one of the most important punctuation skills in English."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the distinction between restrictive and nonrestrictive elements and the comma rules for each.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Restrictive element (essential):** A word, phrase, or clause that is necessary to identify the noun it modifies — removing it changes the sentence's core meaning. No commas.
- **Nonrestrictive element (nonessential):** A word, phrase, or clause that adds extra information about an already-identified noun — removing it does not change the sentence's core meaning. Set off with commas.
- **Appositive:** A noun or noun phrase that renames or describes another noun right beside it (e.g., "my dog, a golden retriever")
- **Adjective clause:** A clause beginning with who, whom, which, or that, which modifies a noun
- **Participial phrase:** A phrase beginning with a present or past participle (e.g., "running down the hill," "exhausted from the hike") that modifies a noun

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Here is the rule."

Write on the board:
- **Nonrestrictive (extra info) = USE COMMAS** → My brother, who is twelve, plays the drums.
- **Restrictive (essential info) = NO COMMAS** → The boy who is twelve plays the drums.

Say: "In the first sentence, I already identified the person — 'my brother.' The clause 'who is twelve' is just bonus information. I can remove it and the sentence still makes sense: 'My brother plays the drums.' So I use commas."

Say: "In the second sentence, 'who is twelve' tells us which boy we mean. Without it, we would just have 'The boy plays the drums' — but which boy? The clause is essential. So no commas."

**The Crossing-Out Test:**

Write on the board:
1. Cross out the element in question.
2. Read the sentence without it.
3. Ask: Does the sentence still clearly identify who or what we are talking about?
   - **Yes** → Nonrestrictive → **add commas**
   - **No** → Restrictive → **no commas**

**Quick Guide for "Which" vs. "That":**

Say: "Here is a helpful pattern: in standard English, 'that' usually introduces restrictive clauses (no commas), and 'which' usually introduces nonrestrictive clauses (with commas)."

Write:
- **Restrictive:** The book **that** I borrowed from the library is overdue. (No commas — tells us which book)
- **Nonrestrictive:** The library book, **which** I borrowed last Tuesday, is overdue. (Commas — extra info)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 (nonrestrictive appositive):**
Write: "Thomas Edison a famous inventor held over one thousand patents."

Say: "Is 'a famous inventor' essential to identify who we are talking about? No — 'Thomas Edison' is already specific. This is extra information. I add commas."

Correct: "Thomas Edison**,** a famous inventor**,** held over one thousand patents."

**Example 2 (restrictive adjective clause):**
Write: "The students, who forgot their homework, must stay after class."

Say: "Let me apply the crossing-out test. Without the clause: 'The students must stay after class.' Does that change the meaning? Yes — now ALL students must stay, not just the ones who forgot. This clause is essential — it restricts which students. I need to remove the commas."

Correct: "The students who forgot their homework must stay after class."

**Example 3 (nonrestrictive participial phrase):**
Write: "The old lighthouse standing on the rocky cliff has guided ships for over a century."

Say: "Is 'standing on the rocky cliff' essential to identify which lighthouse? If there is only one lighthouse in context, then no — it is extra description. I add commas."

Correct: "The old lighthouse**,** standing on the rocky cliff**,** has guided ships for over a century."

**Example 4 (restrictive — no commas needed):**
Write: "The trail that leads through the forest is the most scenic."

Say: "Crossing-out test: 'The trail is the most scenic.' Which trail? We do not know without the clause. It is essential. No commas. This sentence is correct as written."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice distinguishing restrictive from nonrestrictive elements and applying comma rules.

Say: "For each sentence, decide: is the underlined element restrictive or nonrestrictive? Then add commas if needed, or write 'correct' if no commas are needed."

1. My grandmother _who turns seventy this year_ still runs three miles every morning.
2. The students _who are in the advanced math class_ will take the test on Friday.
3. Jupiter _the largest planet in our solar system_ has over eighty moons.
4. The dog _that barks all night_ belongs to our neighbor.
5. Our school librarian _Mrs. Castillo_ organized a book donation drive last month.
6. The athletes _who train every day_ will compete at the state tournament.
7. The Nile River _which flows through northeastern Africa_ is the longest river in the world.
8. The cookies _that my aunt made for the bake sale_ sold out in twenty minutes.

Work through items 1-3 together, applying the crossing-out test each time. For items 4-8, have students work independently, then compare with a partner.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate independent ability to punctuate restrictive and nonrestrictive elements correctly.

**Part A: Add Commas Where Needed (items 1-6)**
Add commas to set off nonrestrictive elements. If the sentence is correct (restrictive element, no commas needed), write "correct."

1. My best friend Jaylen has been playing piano since he was five years old.
2. The planet that is closest to the sun is Mercury.
3. Ms. Abrams our English teacher assigned a creative writing project this week.
4. The students who volunteered at the food bank received a community service award.
5. Mount Everest which stands at 29,032 feet is the tallest mountain on Earth.
6. The novel that we are reading for class has an unexpected ending.

**Part B: Write Your Own (items 7-10)**

7. Write a sentence with a nonrestrictive appositive about a family member. Use commas correctly.
8. Write a sentence with a restrictive clause (using "that" or "who") about a student at your school. Do not use commas around the clause.
9. Write a sentence with a nonrestrictive clause (using "which" or "who") about a place you have visited. Use commas correctly.
10. Take this base sentence and add a nonrestrictive element to it: "The concert lasted three hours."

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Part A (items 1-6) and items 7-8 from Part B.
**Stretch:** Complete all items in Part A and Part B.
**Challenge:** Complete all items, plus write a short paragraph (4-5 sentences) about a historical figure. Include at least two nonrestrictive elements and one restrictive element. Punctuate all correctly and label each in the margin.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1e) Fill in the correct correlative conjunction pair: "_______ the heavy rain _______ the muddy trail could discourage the determined hikers."
12. (Grade 5 review — L.5.3a) Expand this sentence by adding at least two meaningful details: "The bird flew away."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Add commas if needed: "The Amazon River which flows through South America is the second longest river in the world."
2. Explain why this sentence should NOT have commas around the underlined words: "The student _who won the spelling bee_ will represent our school at the district competition."

**Expected Answers:**
1. "The Amazon River**,** which flows through South America**,** is the second longest river in the world." (Nonrestrictive — "The Amazon River" is already identified; "which flows through South America" is extra information.)
2. The clause "who won the spelling bee" is restrictive because it tells us which specific student will represent the school. Without it, the sentence would be "The student will represent our school at the district competition," which does not identify which student. Since the clause is essential to the meaning, no commas are used.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct. For item 2, the student must identify the clause as restrictive/essential and explain that removing it changes the meaning.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the crossing-out test with additional simple examples
- Use index cards: write elements on cards and physically remove them from the sentence to see if the meaning changes
- Practice with appositives first (they tend to be easier to identify as restrictive or nonrestrictive) before moving to clauses
- Provide the "that = restrictive / which = nonrestrictive" guide as a reference tool

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 8, which introduces parentheses and dashes for nonrestrictive elements
- Encourage the student to look for nonrestrictive elements in their reading and notice how published writers punctuate them

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Published Punctuation Hunt:** Find five sentences in a published book or article that contain nonrestrictive elements set off by commas. Copy the sentences and explain why each element is nonrestrictive.
2. **Meaning Change Challenge:** Write two versions of the same sentence — one with commas (nonrestrictive) and one without (restrictive). Explain how the meaning changes. Example: "The players who practiced every day won the tournament" vs. "The players, who practiced every day, won the tournament."
3. **Editing Workshop:** Take a piece of your own writing and check every comma. Are any of your commas setting off restrictive elements that should not have commas? Are any nonrestrictive elements missing commas?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The restrictive vs. nonrestrictive distinction is one of the most conceptually challenging grammar topics at this level. Be patient and use the crossing-out test repeatedly — physical removal of the element makes the abstract concept concrete.
- The "that" vs. "which" guideline is helpful but not absolute. In informal writing and British English, "which" is sometimes used for restrictive clauses. Teach the guideline as a useful default for formal writing, not as an ironclad rule.
- Students often over-comma once they learn about nonrestrictive elements. Emphasize that many clauses (especially those beginning with "that") are restrictive and should NOT have commas. When in doubt, apply the crossing-out test.
- Connect this lesson to reading comprehension: understanding why a writer used commas (or did not) around a clause can change how a student interprets a passage. This is a punctuation skill with real reading consequences.
