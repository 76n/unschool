# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 5

## Topic
Recognizing Variations from Standard English

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define standard English and explain that it is one register among many, not the only "correct" form of English
- Identify at least three types of language variation (dialect, informal register, slang, regional expressions)
- Recognize specific features that differ between standard English and common spoken/informal varieties
- Explain why different situations call for different language choices without labeling any variety as inferior

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of subject-verb and pronoun-antecedent agreement from Lessons 1-4
- Awareness that language has rules and patterns (Grade 5 grammar instruction)
- Experience with writing for different purposes and audiences (Grade 5, W.5.4)
- Basic understanding of audience and purpose in communication

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences showing language variation
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- T-chart graphic organizer (formal/informal columns)
- Index cards with example sentences for sorting activity

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students notice that they already use different kinds of language in different situations.

**Activity: How Would You Say It?**

Say: "Imagine you are telling someone about a movie you just saw. I want you to write two versions of the same message."

Write on the board:
- **Version 1:** You are texting your best friend about the movie.
- **Version 2:** You are writing a review of the movie for your school newspaper.

Give students two minutes to write both versions. Then ask three or four students to share. Write a few examples on the board side by side.

Say: "Look at how different these sound. The text to your friend might say something like 'that movie was SO good omg you have to see it' while the review might say 'The film features impressive special effects and a compelling storyline.' Both versions communicate the same idea, but they sound completely different. Today we are going to explore why that is — and why both versions are perfectly valid."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept of language variation and distinguish standard English from other registers without creating a hierarchy of "good" vs. "bad" language.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Standard English:** The variety of English used in formal writing, academic work, professional communication, and published texts; often called "formal" or "academic" English
- **Register:** The level of formality in language, which shifts depending on the audience, purpose, and situation
- **Dialect:** A variety of a language spoken by a particular group or in a particular region, with its own consistent grammar rules and vocabulary
- **Slang:** Informal words or expressions that are often specific to a group, generation, or time period
- **Code-switching:** The practice of shifting between different registers or language varieties depending on the context

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Here is something important that I want you to remember throughout this lesson and the next one: there is no such thing as a 'bad' or 'broken' version of English. Every dialect, every register, every variety of English follows its own set of rules and patterns. People who speak non-standard dialects are not making mistakes — they are following the rules of their own language variety."

Pause and let that sink in.

Say: "So why do we study standard English at all? Because standard English is the shared language of certain important contexts — school essays, college applications, job interviews, news articles, legal documents. Knowing how to use standard English gives you power in those situations. But knowing when to use your other language styles is just as important. A text to your friend written in formal academic English would sound stiff and weird. A job application written in text-speak would not serve you well either. The skill we are building is called code-switching — choosing the right language for the right moment."

Write on the board: **Code-switching = choosing the right register for the situation**

Say: "Let me show you some common ways that everyday spoken English differs from standard written English."

Write examples on the board:

| Feature | Informal/Spoken | Standard Written |
|---------|----------------|-----------------|
| Subject-verb agreement | "She don't know the answer." | "She doesn't know the answer." |
| Double negative | "I don't have no homework." | "I don't have any homework." |
| Pronoun case | "Me and him went to the store." | "He and I went to the store." |
| Verb form | "We was running late." | "We were running late." |
| Informal vocabulary | "That test was super hard." | "That test was very difficult." |

Say: "Notice I am not saying the left column is wrong. In many communities, 'she don't' and 'we was' are perfectly normal and understood. They follow consistent patterns in those dialects. But in academic writing and formal situations, standard English is expected. Our job is to be able to switch between them."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Regional variation**
Write: "I'm fixing to go to the store."

Say: "'Fixing to' is a regional expression used in many parts of the American South. It means 'about to' or 'getting ready to.' In standard English, this would be 'I am about to go to the store.' Both communicate the same thing. Neither is wrong — they belong to different contexts."

**Example 2: Informal register**
Write: "The science fair was, like, totally amazing and stuff."

Say: "This is casual, conversational language. 'Like' is used as a filler word, 'totally' is informal emphasis, and 'and stuff' is vague. In a formal report, you might write: 'The science fair was impressive and featured outstanding projects.' Same idea, different register."

**Example 3: Dialect feature**
Write: "They been knowing each other since kindergarten."

Say: "In some dialects, 'been' before a verb emphasizes that something has been true for a long time. This is a systematic grammar pattern, not an error. In standard English, you might write: 'They have known each other since kindergarten.' Both sentences communicate the same meaning with slightly different emphasis."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying features that differ from standard English and translating between registers.

Say: "For each sentence, first identify what makes it informal or non-standard. Then rewrite it in standard English. Remember — we are not saying the original is wrong. We are practicing the standard English version."

1. "Me and my friends went to the basketball game last Friday."
2. "She don't want to go to the dance because it's gonna be boring."
3. "I ain't never been to a water park before."
4. "That new video game is, like, the best one ever."
5. "We was supposed to meet at the library at four o'clock."
6. "Him and her are on the same team this year."
7. "Could of gone to the concert, but I didn't have a ride."
8. "Where's the homework at?"

Work through items 1-3 together on the board. For each, discuss: "What feature makes this informal or non-standard? What is the standard English version?"

For items 4-8, have students work independently and then compare with a partner.

Say after reviewing: "I want to emphasize — these kinds of phrases show up in everyday speech for almost everyone. The goal is not to stop using them in casual conversation. The goal is to recognize them and know how to shift to standard English when the situation calls for it."

---

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to identify non-standard features and translate between registers.

**Part A: Identify and Translate (items 1-6)**
For each sentence, underline the non-standard feature and rewrite in standard English.

1. "Them kids are always playing soccer in the park after school."
2. "She could of made the team if she had practiced more."
3. "My brother, he always takes forever to get ready in the morning."
4. "We don't got enough supplies for the whole class project."
5. "The teacher told him and I to stay after class."
6. "I seen the most amazing sunset at the beach last weekend."

**Part B: Explain the Context (items 7-8)**
7. Read this text message: "gonna b late 2 practice coach pls dont b mad lol." Rewrite it as a formal email to the coach. Then explain why you changed the language.
8. Read this formal sentence: "Students are required to submit their assignments by the designated deadline." Rewrite it as something you might say casually to a friend. Explain what changed and why.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Part A (items 1-6) and one item from Part B.
**Stretch:** Complete all of Part A and Part B.
**Challenge:** Complete all items, plus write a short paragraph explaining why code-switching is an important skill. Include at least two specific examples of situations where different registers would be appropriate.

### Interleaved Review
9. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1e) Correct the correlative conjunction error: "Not only did the choir sing well, and also they received a standing ovation."
10. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1d) Identify the inappropriate tense shift and correct it: "The explorers discovered the cave and begin mapping its tunnels."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. What is code-switching? Explain in your own words.
2. Rewrite this sentence in standard English: "Me and her don't got no time for that."

**Expected Answers:**
1. Code-switching is the ability to change the way you speak or write depending on the situation — using informal language with friends and standard English in academic or professional settings. (Accept any reasonable definition that includes the idea of shifting language based on context.)
2. "She and I do not have time for that." OR "She and I don't have any time for that." (Corrections needed: "Me and her" → "She and I"; "don't got" → "don't have" or "do not have"; "no time" → "any time" or remove double negative.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 items correct. For item 2, student must fix at least two of the three non-standard features.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the concept of code-switching using the warm-up activity (texting a friend vs. school newspaper)
- Focus on one non-standard feature at a time (e.g., just double negatives) before combining
- Use side-by-side comparisons on index cards for matching practice
- Emphasize that this is about adding to their language skills, not replacing anything

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 6, which focuses on using standard English strategically in writing
- Encourage students to notice code-switching in their own daily communication

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Language Journal:** For one day, keep track of times you notice yourself or others code-switching. Write down at least three examples: what was said, who the audience was, and what register was used.
2. **Dialect Research:** Choose a region of the United States (or another English-speaking country). Research two or three features of the English dialect spoken there. Write a short report explaining the features and give example sentences.
3. **Interview Project:** Ask a family member or adult you know if they notice themselves speaking differently in different situations (at work vs. at home, with friends vs. with a boss). Record their examples and write a brief summary.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This is a sensitive lesson. Some students may feel that their home language or dialect is being criticized. Be proactive: frame the entire lesson around adding a tool to the toolkit, not replacing the tools they already have. The phrase "language that is expected in certain contexts" is preferable to "correct language."
- Avoid asking students to "fix" their own speech or the speech of their family members. Focus on written examples and on the concept of choosing the right register for the situation.
- Some students may be bidialectal or multilingual and already skilled code-switchers. Celebrate this ability — these students have a linguistic advantage that can be highlighted as a model for the class.
- Be prepared for students to ask whether slang is "bad." Clarify that slang is creative, expressive, and socially meaningful — but it has specific contexts where it works and contexts where it does not, just like all language choices.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A:**
1. Underline: "Them kids." Standard English: "**Those** kids are always playing soccer in the park after school." ("Them" used as a determiner → "Those")
2. Underline: "could of." Standard English: "She **could have** made the team if she had practiced more." ("Could of" is a mishearing of "could've" / "could have")
3. Underline: "My brother, he." Standard English: "My brother always takes forever to get ready in the morning." (Remove the redundant pronoun "he" after "My brother")
4. Underline: "don't got." Standard English: "We **don't have** enough supplies for the whole class project." ("Don't got" → "don't have")
5. Underline: "him and I." Standard English: "The teacher told **him and me** to stay after class." ("I" is not correct after "told" — it should be the object pronoun "me." Also, "him" is correct as an object pronoun.)
6. Underline: "I seen." Standard English: "I **saw** the most amazing sunset at the beach last weekend." ("Seen" requires a helping verb — "I have seen" or use simple past "I saw")

**Part B:**
7. Example formal email: "Dear Coach, I apologize, but I am going to be a few minutes late to practice today. I hope this does not cause any inconvenience. Thank you for understanding." Explanation: Changed informal abbreviations (gonna, b, 2, pls, dont, lol) to full words; added proper greeting, complete sentences, and polite tone appropriate for communicating with a coach.
8. Example casual version: "Hey, just make sure you hand in your homework before it's due, okay?" Explanation: Replaced formal vocabulary ("required," "submit," "designated deadline") with everyday words; changed the impersonal, official tone to a friendly, direct tone appropriate for talking to a friend.

### Interleaved Review
9. Corrected: "Not only did the choir sing well, **but also** they received a standing ovation." (The pair is "not only/but also," not "not only/and also.")
10. Corrected: "The explorers discovered the cave and **began** mapping its tunnels." (Both verbs should be past tense: "discovered" and "began.")

See Expected Answers above.
