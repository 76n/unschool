# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 8

## Topic
Parentheses and Dashes with Nonrestrictive Elements

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Use parentheses to enclose supplementary or explanatory information within a sentence
- Use em dashes to set off nonrestrictive elements with emphasis or interruption
- Distinguish between commas, parentheses, and dashes for nonrestrictive elements and choose the most appropriate option based on the desired effect
- Correctly punctuate sentences using all three marks for nonrestrictive elements

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of restrictive vs. nonrestrictive elements from Lesson 7
- Ability to use commas to set off nonrestrictive appositives and clauses (Lesson 7)
- Knowledge of the crossing-out test to determine whether an element is essential
- Familiarity with basic sentence punctuation (Grade 5, L.5.2a)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (purple for parenthetical elements, red for dashes, green for commas)
- Side-by-side comparison chart (commas vs. parentheses vs. dashes)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review Lesson 7 comma skills and introduce the idea that other punctuation marks can also set off nonrestrictive elements.

**Activity: Three Ways to Say It**

Write on the board three versions of the same sentence:
1. "My neighbor, a retired firefighter, teaches a first aid class at the community center."
2. "My neighbor (a retired firefighter) teaches a first aid class at the community center."
3. "My neighbor — a retired firefighter — teaches a first aid class at the community center."

Say: "All three of these sentences are correct. They all contain the same nonrestrictive appositive: 'a retired firefighter.' But notice that each one uses different punctuation. Read them aloud — slowly. Do they feel different?"

Give students a moment to read them. Then ask: "Which one feels the most normal or neutral? Which one feels like the information is whispered in the background? Which one feels like the information is being emphasized?"

Say: "Today we learn two new punctuation marks for nonrestrictive elements — parentheses and dashes — and discover when each one works best."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the use of parentheses and em dashes for nonrestrictive elements and when each is the best choice.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Parentheses ( ):** Punctuation marks used to enclose supplementary information that is helpful but not essential — the information is de-emphasized, like a quiet aside
- **Em dash (—):** A long dash used to set off information with emphasis or to create a dramatic pause — the information is highlighted, as if the writer is saying "pay attention to this"
- **Parenthetical element:** Any word, phrase, or clause inserted into a sentence as additional information (can be set off by commas, parentheses, or dashes)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "You already know that commas set off nonrestrictive elements. Parentheses and dashes do the same thing, but with different effects."

Write on the board:

| Punctuation | Effect | Use When... |
|-------------|--------|-------------|
| **Commas** , , | Neutral, smooth | The information flows naturally with the sentence |
| **Parentheses** ( ) | De-emphasized, aside | The information is helpful but not the main point — a quiet addition |
| **Dashes** — — | Emphasized, dramatic | The information is surprising, important, or deserves special attention |

Say: "Think of it like volume control. Commas are normal volume. Parentheses turn the volume down — like whispering a side note. Dashes turn the volume up — like saying 'wait, listen to this part.'"

**Parentheses — Rules and Examples:**

Say: "Use parentheses when the added information is useful background, a definition, or a clarification, but it is not the focus of the sentence."

Write:
- "The Great Wall of China (built over many centuries) stretches more than thirteen thousand miles."
- "The school raised over three thousand dollars (enough to buy fifty new library books) during the fundraiser."
- "Ada Lovelace (1815-1852) is considered the first computer programmer."

Say: "Notice that the information inside the parentheses is helpful but not the main point. If you removed it, the sentence would still work fine — and the reader would not feel like they missed something important."

**Dashes — Rules and Examples:**

Say: "Use dashes when the added information is surprising, dramatic, or when you want the reader to pause and pay attention."

Write:
- "The final score — a shocking 42 to 7 — left the crowd in stunned silence."
- "Three students — Amara, Diego, and Priya — earned perfect scores on the exam."
- "The old house at the end of the street — abandoned for over twenty years — was finally being torn down."

Say: "Feel the difference? Dashes create a moment of drama. The reader pauses, absorbs the information, and then continues. It is like the writer is grabbing you by the shoulder and saying 'this part matters.'"

**When to Use Which:**

Say: "Here is my decision guide."

Write:
1. Is the extra information **surprising or dramatic**? → Use **dashes**.
2. Is the extra information a **quiet aside or background detail**? → Use **parentheses**.
3. Is the extra information **flowing naturally** with the sentence? → Use **commas**.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 (parentheses):**
Write: "The Sahara Desert — the largest hot desert in the world — covers most of northern Africa."

Say: "Is 'the largest hot desert in the world' surprising or dramatic? Not really — it is a factual detail. Parentheses might work better here because this is background information."

Revise: "The Sahara Desert (the largest hot desert in the world) covers most of northern Africa."

Say: "Both versions are correct, but the parentheses match the informational tone better. The dash version would work too if a writer wanted to emphasize the size."

**Example 2 (dashes):**
Write: "The magician revealed what was inside the box, a live rabbit, and the audience gasped."

Say: "The reveal of a live rabbit is meant to be surprising. Commas make it feel too calm. Dashes would add the drama this sentence needs."

Revise: "The magician revealed what was inside the box — a live rabbit — and the audience gasped."

**Example 3 (commas — no change needed):**
Write: "Our class pet, a hamster named Biscuit, escaped from its cage during lunch."

Say: "This is a standard nonrestrictive appositive. It flows naturally in the sentence. No drama needed, no quiet aside. Commas are the best fit here. This sentence is fine as is."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice choosing and applying the best punctuation for nonrestrictive elements.

Say: "For each sentence, decide which punctuation — commas, parentheses, or dashes — fits best. Then add the punctuation. There may be more than one correct answer, but choose the one that best matches the tone described."

1. The movie's twist ending _something no one in the audience expected_ made everyone gasp. (Tone: dramatic emphasis)
2. The school was founded in 1987 _almost forty years ago_ and has grown to serve over a thousand students. (Tone: background detail)
3. My older brother _a sophomore at Lincoln High School_ made the varsity basketball team. (Tone: neutral, flowing)
4. The winning goal _scored in the final three seconds of the game_ sent the fans into a frenzy. (Tone: dramatic emphasis)
5. The museum's collection includes works by Frida Kahlo _1907-1954_ and Diego Rivera. (Tone: factual aside)
6. Our neighbor's cat _the one with the orange stripes_ keeps climbing our fence. (Tone: neutral clarification)
7. The results of the experiment _completely unexpected_ forced the scientists to revise their hypothesis. (Tone: dramatic emphasis)
8. The tallest building in our city _originally built as a hotel_ now houses offices and apartments. (Tone: background information)

Work through items 1-3 together, discussing tone and effect. For items 4-8, have students complete independently and then share reasoning with a partner.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate independent ability to use commas, parentheses, and dashes for nonrestrictive elements.

**Part A: Add Punctuation (items 1-6)**
Punctuate each sentence using the most appropriate mark (commas, parentheses, or dashes) based on the context. Be prepared to explain your choice.

1. The ancient city of Pompeii _buried by volcanic ash in 79 AD_ was rediscovered centuries later.
2. Only one student _and she had studied all week_ earned a perfect score on the vocabulary test.
3. The school handbook _available online and in print_ outlines the dress code and attendance policies.
4. The championship game _watched by over two thousand fans_ went into double overtime.
5. The Pacific Ocean _the largest and deepest ocean on Earth_ covers more area than all the land on the planet combined.
6. My favorite author _someone I have been reading since third grade_ is coming to our local bookstore next month.

**Part B: Rewrite Three Ways (items 7-8)**
For each sentence, write three versions: one with commas, one with parentheses, and one with dashes. Then explain which version you prefer and why.

7. Base sentence: The roller coaster [the tallest one in the state] had a line stretching around the entire building.
8. Base sentence: The science fair winner [a seventh grader named Kenji] built a working solar-powered water filter.

**Part C: Write Your Own (items 9-10)**
9. Write a sentence that uses dashes to set off a dramatic or surprising detail.
10. Write a sentence that uses parentheses to include a quiet background detail.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Part A (items 1-6) and one item from Part B.
**Stretch:** Complete Part A, Part B, and one item from Part C.
**Challenge:** Complete all items, plus write a short paragraph (4-5 sentences) about a memorable experience. Use commas, parentheses, and dashes each at least once to set off nonrestrictive elements. Label each in the margin.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1d) Choose the correct verb tense: "By the time we arrived, the concert (already started / had already started)."
12. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1e) Complete with the correct correlative conjunction pair: "The meal was _______ delicious _______ affordable — exactly what we needed."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Punctuate this sentence using dashes: "The final question on the test worth twenty points stumped every student in the class."
2. Punctuate this sentence using parentheses: "Leonardo da Vinci 1452-1519 was a painter, inventor, and scientist."
3. In one sentence, explain the difference in effect between using parentheses and using dashes.

**Expected Answers:**
1. "The final question on the test **—** worth twenty points **—** stumped every student in the class."
2. "Leonardo da Vinci **(** 1452-1519 **)** was a painter, inventor, and scientist."
3. Parentheses de-emphasize the extra information (like a quiet aside), while dashes emphasize it (like calling attention to a surprising or important detail). Accept any response that captures this contrast.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the "volume control" analogy: commas = normal, parentheses = whisper, dashes = shout
- Practice with matching exercises: give students sentences with blanks and three punctuation options, and have them match based on tone descriptions
- Focus on one mark at a time — master parentheses before introducing dashes, or vice versa
- Provide sentence frames where students only need to add the punctuation, not write from scratch

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 9 on varying sentence patterns for meaning
- Encourage the student to experiment with parentheses and dashes in their own writing — a good place to start is in informational or persuasive pieces

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Published Punctuation Analysis:** Find three examples of parentheses and three examples of dashes in a published book, article, or website. For each, explain why the author chose that punctuation mark instead of commas.
2. **Tone Shift Challenge:** Take one sentence and write it six ways: with commas, parentheses, and dashes — in both a dramatic context and a neutral context. How does the punctuation change the reader's experience?
3. **Editing for Effect:** Take a paragraph from your own writing and find places where you could add a nonrestrictive element using parentheses or dashes. Add at least one of each and explain your choices.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students sometimes confuse hyphens (-) with em dashes (—). Clarify that a hyphen connects parts of a compound word (well-known, mother-in-law), while an em dash sets off information within a sentence. On most keyboards, an em dash can be typed as two hyphens (--) or using a special character.
- The "best" choice between commas, parentheses, and dashes is often a matter of style and judgment, not absolute rules. Encourage students to develop their own sense of when each mark feels right, rather than memorizing rigid rules.
- Some students will overuse dashes once they discover them — they create a sense of drama that appeals to many young writers. Gently guide students toward using dashes sparingly for maximum effect. Too many dashes in a single piece dilute their impact.
- Remind students that all three marks are only for nonrestrictive (nonessential) elements. Restrictive elements get no punctuation at all — this rule from Lesson 7 still applies.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A:**
1. "The ancient city of Pompeii **(** buried by volcanic ash in 79 AD **)** was rediscovered centuries later." (Parentheses — historical background detail. Commas also acceptable.)
2. "Only one student **—** and she had studied all week **—** earned a perfect score on the vocabulary test." (Dashes — dramatic emphasis on the aside.)
3. "The school handbook **(** available online and in print **)** outlines the dress code and attendance policies." (Parentheses — practical aside. Commas also acceptable.)
4. "The championship game **—** watched by over two thousand fans **—** went into double overtime." (Dashes — dramatic detail about the scale of the event. Commas also acceptable.)
5. "The Pacific Ocean **(** the largest and deepest ocean on Earth **)** covers more area than all the land on the planet combined." (Parentheses — factual background. Commas also acceptable for a more neutral flow.)
6. "My favorite author **—** someone I have been reading since third grade **—** is coming to our local bookstore next month." (Dashes — personal emphasis and excitement. Commas also acceptable.)

**Part B:**
7. Commas: "The roller coaster, the tallest one in the state, had a line stretching around the entire building."
   Parentheses: "The roller coaster (the tallest one in the state) had a line stretching around the entire building."
   Dashes: "The roller coaster — the tallest one in the state — had a line stretching around the entire building."
   Preference: answers vary. Dashes work well because the detail is impressive and worth emphasizing.

8. Commas: "The science fair winner, a seventh grader named Kenji, built a working solar-powered water filter."
   Parentheses: "The science fair winner (a seventh grader named Kenji) built a working solar-powered water filter."
   Dashes: "The science fair winner — a seventh grader named Kenji — built a working solar-powered water filter."
   Preference: answers vary. Commas provide a neutral, clean flow for this sentence.

**Part C:**
9. Answers vary. Example: "The package — delivered two weeks early — contained a birthday present from my grandmother."
10. Answers vary. Example: "The school auditorium (built in 1998) seats up to five hundred people."
