# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 9

## Topic
Varying Sentence Patterns for Meaning

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify four sentence types by structure: simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex
- Explain how different sentence structures create different relationships between ideas (addition, contrast, cause-effect, condition)
- Choose the sentence structure that best conveys the intended meaning and relationship between ideas
- Revise a passage by varying sentence structures to clarify meaning and show logical connections

## Prerequisites
- Experience expanding, combining, and reducing sentences (Grade 5, L.5.3a)
- Knowledge of coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or, so, yet) and correlative conjunctions (Grade 5, L.5.1e)
- Ability to identify subjects and verbs in a sentence (Lessons 1-2)
- Understanding of commas with nonrestrictive elements (Lessons 7-8)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example passages
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (for marking different clause types)
- Sentence structure reference chart (simple, compound, complex, compound-complex)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students see that the same information can be arranged in different ways, each creating a different emphasis.

**Activity: Same Idea, Different Structure**

Say: "I am going to show you four sentences. They all communicate the same basic facts: it rained, and the game was canceled. But watch how the structure changes the emphasis."

Write on the board:
1. "It rained. The game was canceled." (Two simple sentences)
2. "It rained, so the game was canceled." (Compound — shows cause and effect equally)
3. "Because it rained, the game was canceled." (Complex — emphasizes the cancellation)
4. "The game was canceled because it rained." (Complex — emphasizes the rain as the reason)

Say: "Same facts. Four different structures. Four slightly different effects. Today we learn how to choose the structure that says exactly what you mean."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the four sentence types and how each creates different meaning relationships between ideas.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Simple sentence:** One independent clause (one complete thought with a subject and a verb)
- **Compound sentence:** Two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) or a semicolon
- **Complex sentence:** One independent clause and one or more dependent (subordinate) clauses, joined by a subordinating conjunction (because, although, when, if, since, while, after, before, unless)
- **Compound-complex sentence:** Two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause
- **Subordinating conjunction:** A word that begins a dependent clause and shows the relationship between that clause and the main clause

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Choosing a sentence structure is not random — it is a decision about meaning. Each structure tells the reader something different about how the ideas connect."

Write on the board:

| Structure | What It Does | Signal Words |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| **Simple** | States one idea clearly and directly | (none needed) |
| **Compound** | Connects two equal ideas | and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor; semicolons |
| **Complex** | Shows one idea depends on or is subordinate to another | because, although, when, if, since, while, after, before, unless, until |
| **Compound-complex** | Shows multiple relationships at once | Combination of coordinating and subordinating conjunctions |

Say: "The key insight is this: in a complex sentence, the idea in the independent clause gets more emphasis. The idea in the dependent clause gets less emphasis — it becomes the background or the condition."

Write:
- "**Although** the test was difficult, most students passed." (The difficulty is background; the passing is the main point.)
- "Most students passed **although** the test was difficult." (Same ideas, but now passing comes first and gets slightly more emphasis.)

Say: "Compound sentences treat both ideas as equals."

Write:
- "The test was difficult, **but** most students passed." (Equal weight on both ideas, with a contrast.)

Say: "And simple sentences give a single idea maximum impact — especially after several longer sentences."

Write:
- "The teacher reviewed the material. The students studied for weeks. The practice tests were exhausting. Test day arrived. Most students passed." (The short sentence "Test day arrived" stands out because of its simplicity.)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Choosing structure for cause-effect**
Say: "I want to show that practicing caused improvement. Which structure works best?"

Write three options:
- Simple: "She practiced every day. Her skills improved dramatically." (Cause-effect is implied but not stated.)
- Compound: "She practiced every day, and her skills improved dramatically." ("And" connects the ideas but does not clearly show cause-effect.)
- Complex: "Because she practiced every day, her skills improved dramatically." ("Because" explicitly shows the cause-effect relationship.)

Say: "The complex sentence is the best choice here because 'because' makes the cause-effect relationship clear and explicit."

**Example 2: Choosing structure for contrast**
Say: "I want to contrast two ideas: the trail was steep, and the hikers kept going."

Write:
- Compound: "The trail was steep, but the hikers kept going." (Both ideas have equal weight; the contrast is clear.)
- Complex: "Although the trail was steep, the hikers kept going." (The steepness is background; the persistence is emphasized.)

Say: "Both work, but they create different emphasis. 'Although' puts the focus on the hikers' persistence. 'But' treats both ideas as equally important."

**Example 3: Choosing structure for a condition**
Say: "I want to state a condition: finishing homework → earning free time."

Write:
- Complex: "If you finish your homework before dinner, you can have an hour of free time." (The condition is clear.)
- Simple: "Finish your homework before dinner. You can have an hour of free time." (Works, but the conditional relationship is only implied.)

Say: "The complex sentence makes the condition explicit with 'if.' The reader knows exactly what has to happen first."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice choosing sentence structures to convey specific meaning relationships.

Say: "For each pair of ideas, I will tell you the relationship I want to show. Combine the ideas into a sentence using the structure that best conveys that relationship."

1. Ideas: "The museum was closed." "We went to the park instead." Relationship: cause and effect. (Because the museum was closed, we went to the park instead.)
2. Ideas: "The movie received terrible reviews." "It became a box-office hit." Relationship: contrast/surprise. (Although the movie received terrible reviews, it became a box-office hit.)
3. Ideas: "You study the vocabulary words." "You will do well on the quiz." Relationship: condition. (If you study the vocabulary words, you will do well on the quiz.)
4. Ideas: "The storm knocked out the power." "School was delayed." "Students cheered." Relationship: cause-effect + addition. (Because the storm knocked out the power, school was delayed, and students cheered.)
5. Ideas: "The library was quiet." "Students studied." Relationship: time/background. (While the library was quiet, students studied.)
6. Ideas: "Practice makes permanent." Stand-alone emphasis. (Practice makes permanent. — Simple, for impact.)
7. Ideas: "The team lost the first game." "They won the next five." Relationship: contrast with equal weight. (The team lost the first game, but they won the next five.)
8. Ideas: "The experiment failed." "The students learned from their mistakes." "They tried again with a new approach." Relationship: contrast + addition. (Although the experiment failed, the students learned from their mistakes, and they tried again with a new approach.)

Work through items 1-3 together. For items 4-8, have students work independently, then discuss in pairs.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to choose and use sentence structures that convey specific meaning relationships.

**Part A: Combine with the Best Structure (items 1-6)**
Combine each set of ideas into a single sentence. Choose the sentence structure (simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex) that best shows the relationship between the ideas. Write which structure you used.

1. "The concert was sold out." + "We watched the live stream at home." (Show cause-effect.)
2. "The teacher explained the directions." + "Several students still had questions." (Show contrast.)
3. "You arrive early." + "You can choose the best seat." (Show a condition.)
4. "The volcano erupted." (State this with maximum impact — stand-alone.)
5. "The weather was freezing." + "The roads were icy." + "The bus driver drove slowly and carefully." (Show cause-effect + addition.)
6. "The project was due on Monday." + "Half the class asked for an extension." + "The teacher was understanding." (Show contrast + addition.)

**Part B: Revise for Meaning (items 7-8)**
Each passage below uses only simple sentences. Revise it by varying the sentence structures to make the meaning relationships clear. Keep all the original information.

7. "The wolves live in packs. They hunt together. They share food with the entire group. Young wolves learn from the older members. Wolf packs have a clear social structure."

8. "The school garden needed volunteers. The garden club posted flyers. Many students signed up. The garden was ready for planting by March. The first vegetables appeared in May."

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Part A (items 1-6).
**Stretch:** Complete Part A and one item from Part B.
**Challenge:** Complete all items, plus write an original paragraph (5-6 sentences) about a topic of your choice that uses all four sentence types (simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex). Label each sentence type in the margin.

### Interleaved Review
9. (Grade 5 review — L.5.2a) Add correct punctuation: "The fruit salad contained strawberries blueberries kiwi and mango."
10. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1d) Correct the tense shift: "The hikers followed the trail map and soon finds the hidden waterfall."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Combine these two ideas into a complex sentence showing cause-effect: "The river flooded." + "Many families had to evacuate."
2. What is the difference in emphasis between these two sentences?
   - "Although Mia was nervous, she gave an excellent presentation."
   - "Mia was nervous, but she gave an excellent presentation."

**Expected Answers:**
1. "Because the river flooded, many families had to evacuate." (Also accept: "Since the river flooded, many families had to evacuate." Or: "Many families had to evacuate because the river flooded.")
2. In the first sentence (complex), Mia's nervousness is the background and the emphasis is on her excellent presentation. In the second sentence (compound), both ideas receive roughly equal weight — the reader focuses on both the nervousness and the excellent presentation equally. (Accept any answer that identifies the shift in emphasis between the subordinate clause and the compound structure.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the four sentence types using a simplified chart with one example each
- Focus on complex sentences first (the most important for meaning relationships) before adding compound and compound-complex
- Provide a list of subordinating conjunctions with their meaning relationships (because = cause, although = contrast, if = condition, when = time)
- Practice by giving students two short sentences and asking which subordinating conjunction best connects them

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 10 on varying sentence patterns for style
- Encourage the student to examine sentence variety in published texts and notice how authors use structure to guide meaning

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Sentence Structure Analysis:** Choose a paragraph from a favorite book. Label each sentence as simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex. Does the author use all four types? How does the variety affect the reading experience?
2. **Meaning Shift Experiment:** Take one pair of related ideas and write them as all four sentence types. Explain how the meaning or emphasis changes with each structure.
3. **Revision Workshop:** Take a recent piece of your own writing and identify places where you used only simple sentences or where you could clarify a relationship by changing the structure. Revise at least three sentences and explain your choices.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often conflate sentence length with sentence type. A simple sentence can be long ("The tall, tired, muddy soccer players from the championship game trudged slowly across the field toward the parking lot.") and a compound sentence can be short ("She ran, and he followed."). Emphasize that sentence type is about clause structure, not length.
- The subordinating conjunction list is the most important tool in this lesson. Consider posting it permanently in the classroom: because, since, although, though, even though, while, when, after, before, if, unless, until, as, whereas.
- Compound-complex sentences can feel overwhelming for some students. It is fine to focus primarily on simple, compound, and complex in this lesson and treat compound-complex as an advanced extension.
- Connect this lesson to reading comprehension: when students encounter complex sentences in texts, they should identify the independent clause as the main idea and the dependent clause as supporting or modifying information.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A:**
1. "Because the concert was sold out, we watched the live stream at home." (Complex — "because" shows cause-effect.)
2. "The teacher explained the directions, but several students still had questions." (Compound — "but" shows contrast with equal weight.) Also accept: "Although the teacher explained the directions, several students still had questions." (Complex.)
3. "If you arrive early, you can choose the best seat." (Complex — "if" shows condition.)
4. "The volcano erupted." (Simple — maximum impact as a stand-alone statement.)
5. "Because the weather was freezing and the roads were icy, the bus driver drove slowly and carefully." (Compound-complex — "because" shows cause-effect; "and" connects the two causes.) Also accept: "The weather was freezing, the roads were icy, and the bus driver drove slowly and carefully."
6. "Although the project was due on Monday and half the class asked for an extension, the teacher was understanding." (Compound-complex.) Also accept: "The project was due on Monday, and half the class asked for an extension, but the teacher was understanding." (Compound.)

**Part B:**
7. Answers vary. Example: "Wolves live in packs because hunting together and sharing food allows them to survive. Young wolves learn essential skills from older members, and the entire pack operates within a clear social structure."
8. Answers vary. Example: "When the school garden needed volunteers, the garden club posted flyers around the building. Because many students signed up, the garden was ready for planting by March, and the first vegetables appeared in May."

9. "The fruit salad contained strawberries, blueberries, kiwi, and mango." (Commas in a series with Oxford comma.)
10. Corrected: "The hikers followed the trail map and soon **found** the hidden waterfall." ("Finds" should be "found" — past tense to match "followed.")
