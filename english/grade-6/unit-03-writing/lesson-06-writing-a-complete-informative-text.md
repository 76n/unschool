# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 6

## Topic
Writing a Complete Informative Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Assemble a complete informative text with introduction, body paragraphs, transitions, and conclusion
- Write an informative conclusion that summarizes key information and offers a final insight or reflection
- Use transitions to connect ideas within and between paragraphs of an informative text
- Evaluate their own informative writing for completeness, development, and coherence

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 4: Ability to select a topic, create a Topic Web, and write an informative introduction
- Lesson 5: Ability to write well-developed body paragraphs with facts, definitions, and elaboration
- W.5.2c: Familiarity with transitional words, phrases, and clauses that link ideas
- W.5.2e: Experience writing concluding statements for informative texts

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Topic Web, introduction, and body paragraphs from Lessons 4-5 (student's own)
- Informative Transition Reference Chart (described below)
- Informative Writing Self-Evaluation Checklist (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review informative text structure and focus attention on transitions and conclusions — the skills that turn separate paragraphs into a unified text.

**Activity: Paragraph Order Puzzle**

Display the following four paragraph opening sentences in scrambled order:

- A. "In addition to the physical challenge of altitude, climbers must contend with unpredictable and often deadly weather."
- B. "The first major obstacle any Everest climber faces is the altitude itself."
- C. "Mount Everest, standing at 29,032 feet above sea level, is the tallest mountain on Earth — and one of the most dangerous places any human being can attempt to reach."
- D. "Despite the extreme risks, the summit of Everest continues to draw hundreds of climbers each year, proving that the human desire to explore and overcome has no ceiling."

Say: "These are the opening sentences of four paragraphs from an informative essay about climbing Mount Everest. They are scrambled. Work with a partner to put them in the correct order."

After discussion, reveal the correct order: C (introduction), B (body paragraph 1 — altitude), A (body paragraph 2 — weather), D (conclusion).

Ask: "How did you know the order? What clues helped you?" Guide toward: the introduction is broad and sets up the topic; "The first..." signals the first body paragraph; "In addition to..." signals a second body paragraph that builds on the first; the conclusion uses "Despite..." to synthesize and reflect.

Say: "Transitions are the glue that holds an informative text together. And the conclusion is the landing — it tells the reader what all of this information means. Today you are going to finish your informative text."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach transition strategies for informative writing and the structure of an informative conclusion.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Coherence:** The quality of a text in which all parts connect logically and flow smoothly from one to the next
- **Transition:** A word, phrase, or clause that connects ideas within or between paragraphs
- **Synthesis:** Combining the main points of a text into a unified message in the conclusion
- **Final insight:** A closing thought that gives the reader something new to think about — not just a repetition of the introduction
- **Self-evaluation:** The process of assessing your own writing against a set of criteria

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Let's talk about transitions first. In argumentative writing, transitions connect reasons. In informative writing, transitions connect categories of information. The job is slightly different."

Display the Informative Transition Reference Chart:

| Purpose | Transitions |
|---------|------------|
| Moving to a new category | Another key aspect of..., Equally important is..., Beyond [previous topic]..., A second factor is... |
| Adding information within a category | In addition, Furthermore, Adding to this, What makes this even more significant is... |
| Showing cause and effect | As a result, Because of this, This leads to, Consequently |
| Providing an example | For instance, One example of this is, To illustrate, Consider the case of |
| Contrasting | However, In contrast, Unlike [previous topic], On the other hand |
| Concluding | Taken together, In light of these facts, Ultimately, What all of this reveals is |

Say: "Now let's talk about conclusions. An informative conclusion has two jobs: summarize the key information and provide a final insight. The final insight is what separates a good conclusion from a great one."

**Three Informative Conclusion Strategies:**

1. **Summary + "So What?"** — Summarize the key points, then explain why the topic matters.
2. **Summary + Future Implications** — Summarize, then look ahead at what might happen next or what remains unknown.
3. **Summary + Connection to Reader** — Summarize, then connect the topic to the reader's own life or world.

### Worked Examples

Display the following model conclusion (for the bioluminescence text from Lesson 4):

> From the deepest ocean trenches to backyard summer evenings lit by fireflies, bioluminescence reminds us that nature's ingenuity has no limits. Scientists continue to study this phenomenon not only to understand the natural world but also to develop practical applications, from medical imaging techniques that use bioluminescent markers to detect cancer cells to environmentally friendly lighting inspired by nature's cold light. The more we learn about bioluminescence, the more it illuminates — in every sense of the word — the remarkable creativity of life on Earth.

Annotate together:
- **Summary:** "From the deepest ocean trenches to backyard summer evenings lit by fireflies, bioluminescence reminds us that nature's ingenuity has no limits." (Synthesizes the range of organisms discussed.)
- **Future implications:** "Scientists continue to study this phenomenon... from medical imaging... to environmentally friendly lighting." (Looks forward to practical applications.)
- **Final insight:** "The more we learn about bioluminescence, the more it illuminates — in every sense of the word — the remarkable creativity of life on Earth." (Wordplay that leaves the reader thinking.)

Say: "Notice that this conclusion does not repeat the introduction. It does not list the three categories again. Instead, it pulls back and looks at the big picture: why does bioluminescence matter beyond the essay itself?"

Now display a weak conclusion for comparison:

> "In conclusion, bioluminescence is really interesting. It happens in lots of animals. Some make blue light and some make green light. I hope you learned something about bioluminescence."

Ask: "What is wrong with this conclusion?" Guide toward: it restates facts rather than synthesizing; it uses vague language ("really interesting"); it does not provide any insight; "In conclusion" and "I hope you learned" are weak, formulaic.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively write a conclusion and add transitions to a shared informative text.

**Activity: Finishing the Tornado Text**

Return to the tornado text from Lessons 4-5. The student has an introduction and at least one body paragraph. Now complete the text together.

**Step 1 — Add a transition between body paragraphs:**

If the text has two body paragraphs (conditions and formation process), write a transition sentence that bridges them:

Sample: "Once these atmospheric conditions converge, the stage is set for one of nature's most violent phenomena — the formation of the tornado itself."

Say: "Notice how this transition does two things: it wraps up what we just discussed (conditions) and previews what comes next (formation). The best transitions look both backward and forward."

**Step 2 — Write the conclusion together:**

Say: "Let's use the summary + future implications strategy."

Guide the student:
1. **Synthesis sentence:** "From the collision of warm and cold air masses to the spinning descent of a funnel cloud, tornado formation is a process that depends on a precise and volatile combination of forces."
2. **Significance:** "Understanding this process is not just an academic exercise — it saves lives. Meteorologists use their knowledge of tornado formation to issue warnings that give communities precious minutes to seek shelter."
3. **Future implication:** "As technology improves and scientists develop more advanced radar systems, the hope is that tornado predictions will become even more precise, giving people not just minutes but hours to prepare for nature's most unpredictable storms."

**Step 3 — Self-evaluation:**

Introduce the Informative Writing Self-Evaluation Checklist:

```
INFORMATIVE WRITING SELF-EVALUATION

□ My introduction has a hook, context, and a focus statement
□ My focus statement previews my categories/subtopics
□ Each body paragraph has a clear topic sentence
□ Each body paragraph includes specific facts and concrete details
□ I defined domain-specific vocabulary in context
□ I used transitions between paragraphs
□ My conclusion summarizes key information
□ My conclusion provides a final insight (not just a restatement)
□ I maintained an objective tone throughout
□ I used precise language (no vague words like "stuff," "things," "really")
```

Read through the checklist together, checking off each item for the tornado text.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students assemble and complete their informative text.

**Task:** Using the introduction and body paragraph(s) you developed in Lessons 4-5, complete your informative text. You must:

1. Write any remaining body paragraphs to cover all categories in your Topic Web
2. Add transitions between all body paragraphs
3. Write a conclusion using one of the three conclusion strategies
4. Complete the Self-Evaluation Checklist and revise any areas where you check "no"

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete the informative text with at least two body paragraphs, transitions, and a conclusion. Complete the checklist.
**Stretch:** Complete the text with three body paragraphs, smooth transitions, and a conclusion that uses the summary + future implications strategy. Revise at least one body paragraph to add a concrete detail you did not include originally.
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task AND write a one-paragraph author's note explaining what organizational strategy you chose (chronological, categories, cause-effect) and why it was the best choice for your topic.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — W.5.3a) Write an opening sentence for a narrative about a student who gets lost during a field trip. The sentence must establish the character, setting, and situation. (Expected: Something like "The moment Maya looked up from the tide pool and realized her class had disappeared around the bend of the rocky shoreline, her stomach dropped.")
6. (Grade 5 review — W.5.1c) Rewrite the following sentence using a transitional clause: "Also, regular exercise helps students focus." (Expected: "In addition to its well-documented physical benefits, regular exercise has been shown to sharpen students' ability to focus during academic tasks." Or similar clause-level transition.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following informative conclusion and identify which conclusion strategy the writer used. Then explain what makes it effective.

> "Whether they roam the frozen tundra in search of caribou, dive beneath the Arctic ice to hunt for fish, or curl up in underground dens to wait out the darkest months, Arctic animals have developed an astonishing toolkit for surviving in one of the harshest environments on Earth. As global temperatures rise and the Arctic landscape changes faster than at any point in recorded history, scientists are watching closely to see whether these ancient adaptations can keep pace with a rapidly warming world."

**Expected Answers:**
- Strategy: Summary + Future Implications
- Effectiveness: The first sentence synthesizes the three categories (behavioral strategies: roaming, diving, denning) without simply listing them. The second sentence looks forward to the impact of climate change, giving the reader a reason to care about the topic beyond the essay itself. The conclusion connects the scientific information to a larger, ongoing issue.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the conclusion strategy AND explains at least one specific reason it is effective.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model conclusion from direct instruction and compare it to the weak example
- Ask: "What does the strong conclusion do that the weak one does not?" List the differences
- Have the student practice by rewriting the weak bioluminescence conclusion using one of the three strategies

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7, which transitions to narrative writing
- Encourage the student to read their complete informative text aloud, listening for places where transitions are missing or the writing sounds choppy

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Conclusion Remix:** Write three different conclusions for your informative text, one using each strategy (so what, future implications, connection to reader). Which is most effective? Why?
2. **Peer Review Exchange:** Trade informative texts with a partner. Use the Self-Evaluation Checklist to evaluate each other's writing. Write two specific pieces of feedback: one strength and one area for improvement.
3. **Cross-Modal Comparison:** Look at the argumentative essay you wrote in Lessons 1-3 and the informative text you wrote in Lessons 4-6. Write a paragraph comparing the two: How are the structures similar? How are they different? How does the purpose of each text shape its organization and tone?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson asks students to assemble a complete informative text. Some students may need extra time to draft missing body paragraphs. Prioritize helping them write at least two complete body paragraphs and a conclusion rather than rushing through three underdeveloped paragraphs.
- Transitions between informative paragraphs often feel more challenging to students than transitions in argumentative writing because the connection between categories may be less obvious. Help students find the logical thread: "How does Category 1 relate to Category 2? Does one cause the other? Does one build on the other? Are they different aspects of the same thing?"
- The Self-Evaluation Checklist is a powerful metacognitive tool. Encourage students to use it honestly, not just check every box. If a student marks everything as complete but the text clearly lacks development, conference individually and model how to use the checklist critically.
- If time is limited, the conclusion is the highest-priority new skill in this lesson. If students can write a strong conclusion that synthesizes and offers insight, the lesson objective is met.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
**Sample complete informative text structure (Arctic adaptations):**

*Introduction:* Hook + context about the Arctic environment + focus statement previewing three categories (insulation, behavioral, physical adaptations)

*Body 1 (Insulation):* Developed with facts about Arctic fox fur, walrus blubber, specific measurements, definitions in context

*Body 2 (Behavioral):* Transition from insulation topic + developed with facts about migration distances, hibernation strategies, huddling behavior

*Body 3 (Physical):* Transition from behavioral topic + developed with facts about compact body shapes, ear-to-body ratios, seasonal color changes

*Conclusion:* Synthesis of all three categories + future implication about climate change's impact on these adaptations

**Sample conclusion:**
> "Whether they roam the frozen tundra in search of caribou, dive beneath the Arctic ice to hunt for fish, or curl up in underground dens to wait out the darkest months, Arctic animals have developed an astonishing toolkit for surviving in one of the harshest environments on Earth. As global temperatures rise and the Arctic landscape changes faster than at any point in recorded history, scientists are watching closely to see whether these ancient adaptations can keep pace with a rapidly warming world."
