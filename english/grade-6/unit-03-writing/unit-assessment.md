# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3: Writing

## Unit Assessment

### Overview

This assessment evaluates student mastery of the four core writing standards addressed in Unit 3: argumentative writing (W.6.1), informative/explanatory writing (W.6.2), narrative writing (W.6.3), and drawing evidence from texts (W.6.9). The assessment has four tasks:

- **Task 1 — Argumentative Writing (W.6.1):** Write an argument with a claim, reasons, and evidence
- **Task 2 — Informative Writing (W.6.2):** Write an informative text examining a topic
- **Task 3 — Narrative Writing (W.6.3):** Write a narrative with relevant descriptive details
- **Task 4 — Text-Based Evidence Response (W.6.9):** Read a provided passage and write an analytical response drawing evidence from the text

**Time:** 75-90 minutes (recommended: 15-20 minutes per task)

**Total Points:** 60 points (15 points per task)

**Mastery Criteria:** Proficient (3) or above on at least three of the four writing tasks (average of 2.5+ per criterion on each task).

---

## Task 1 — Argumentative Writing (W.6.1)

**Prompt:**

Your school district is considering a new policy that would require all middle school students to complete 30 minutes of reading every evening as homework, with a parent or guardian signature confirming it was done. Some people support this policy because they believe it will improve reading skills and build lifelong habits. Others oppose it because they believe it turns reading into a chore and takes time away from other important activities.

Write an argumentative essay in which you take a clear position on this issue. Your essay must include:

- An introduction with a hook, context, and a clearly stated claim
- At least two body paragraphs, each developing a reason with specific, relevant evidence using the Introduce-Cite-Explain pattern
- Transitions between and within paragraphs
- A conclusion that synthesizes your argument and leaves the reader with a final thought

Write at least four paragraphs (introduction, two or more body paragraphs, conclusion).

---

### Argumentative Writing Rubric (15 points: 5 criteria x 3 points at Proficient)

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Claim (W.6.1a)** | No claim is stated, or the essay argues multiple positions without committing to one | A position is stated but is vague, buried in the text, or does not preview reasons | A clear, debatable claim is stated in the introduction, previewing at least two reasons | A compelling, precisely worded claim is stated with a strong hook and context that builds the reader's understanding of why the issue matters |
| **Reasons and Evidence (W.6.1b)** | No reasons or evidence are provided; the essay consists of unsupported opinions | One reason is provided with vague or missing evidence; the ICE pattern is not used | At least two reasons are logically ordered, each supported by specific evidence using the Introduce-Cite-Explain pattern with clear analysis | Three or more strategically ordered reasons are supported by convincing, well-chosen evidence with thorough analysis that explains how each piece of evidence strengthens the claim |
| **Transitions (W.6.1c)** | No transitions; ideas within and between paragraphs are disconnected | Only simple transitional words are used (first, also, next); no phrases or clauses | A mix of transitional words, phrases, and clauses connects ideas within and between paragraphs; transitions are purposeful | Transitions are varied, precise, and seamlessly integrated; some transitions add information while connecting ideas; the essay reads as a cohesive whole |
| **Conclusion (W.6.1d)** | No conclusion, or the essay ends abruptly | The conclusion restates the introduction without synthesizing or adding new perspective | The conclusion synthesizes the argument and uses a clear strategy (call to action, forward look, or thought-provoking question) | The conclusion powerfully synthesizes the argument, addresses a potential counterclaim, and leaves a lasting impression on the reader |
| **Conventions** | Frequent errors in spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and grammar that interfere with readability | Several errors are present; some interfere with meaning | Minor errors are present but do not interfere with meaning; the essay demonstrates solid command of Grade 6 conventions | The essay is virtually error-free and demonstrates strong command of all applicable conventions, including varied sentence structures |

**Scoring:** Rate each criterion 1-4. Multiply the average by 3.75 to convert to a 15-point scale. Or use simplified scoring: Proficient (3) on all criteria = 15 points. Round to the nearest whole number.

**Simplified Scoring:**
- Advanced (avg 3.5-4.0): 14-15 points
- Proficient (avg 2.5-3.4): 10-13 points
- Developing (avg 1.5-2.4): 6-9 points
- Beginning (avg 1.0-1.4): 1-5 points

### Sample Proficient Argumentative Response

> **Reading Should Not Be Homework**
>
> What if the one activity most likely to spark a child's imagination became the one they dreaded most? That is exactly what happens when schools turn reading into a mandatory homework assignment with a parent signature requirement. While the intention behind required reading logs is understandable, the policy is misguided. Schools should not require 30 minutes of nightly reading as homework because mandatory reading programs have been shown to decrease students' motivation to read, and because the parent signature requirement creates inequity rather than accountability.
>
> The most compelling reason to reject mandatory reading homework is that it undermines the very habit it seeks to build. According to a report by the National Literacy Trust, students who are required to read for school are 15% less likely to identify as readers who enjoy books compared to students who choose to read on their own. This finding is significant because it reveals a paradox: the more we force students to read, the less they want to. When reading becomes a box to check rather than a world to enter, students learn to associate books with obligation rather than pleasure. A policy that reduces students' long-term motivation to read is counterproductive, regardless of how many minutes it adds to their reading log.
>
> Furthermore, the parent signature requirement creates an unfair system that penalizes students for circumstances beyond their control. Not every student has a parent or guardian available each evening to monitor and sign a reading log. Some parents work night shifts; others are managing multiple children or dealing with their own challenges. A study by the Brookings Institution found that homework completion rates are strongly correlated with household income and parental availability, meaning that students from less stable home environments are more likely to be penalized — not for failing to read, but for failing to produce a signature. This policy does not measure reading; it measures privilege.
>
> Rather than mandating reading as homework, schools should invest in making reading irresistible during the school day. Well-stocked classroom libraries, dedicated silent reading time, and student choice in book selection are strategies that build genuine reading habits without the negative side effects of compulsory logs. The goal should not be to force students to read for 30 minutes — it should be to help them become the kind of people who want to.

## Task 2 — Informative Writing (W.6.2)

**Prompt:**

Write an informative text that examines one of the following topics:

1. How a specific technology works (examples: 3D printing, GPS navigation, solar panels)
2. How a specific animal species adapts to its environment (examples: desert animals, deep-sea creatures, migratory birds)
3. An important event or development in history (examples: the invention of the printing press, the construction of the Panama Canal, the Space Race)

Your informative text must include:

- An introduction with a hook, context, and a focus statement that previews your categories or subtopics
- At least two body paragraphs, each examining a different category with facts, definitions, concrete details, and/or examples
- At least three domain-specific vocabulary terms, each defined or explained in context
- Transitions that connect ideas within and between paragraphs
- A conclusion that summarizes key information and provides a final insight or reflection
- An objective, informative tone throughout (no personal opinions)

Write at least four paragraphs (introduction, two or more body paragraphs, conclusion).

---

### Informative Writing Rubric (15 points: 5 criteria x 3 points at Proficient)

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Introduction and Focus (W.6.2a)** | No introduction, or the text begins without a hook or focus statement | An introduction is present but is missing one or more key elements (hook, context, or focus statement) | The introduction includes a hook, context, and a clear focus statement that previews the categories or subtopics to be examined | The introduction is polished and engaging, with a compelling hook, insightful context, and a focus statement that builds curiosity and provides a clear roadmap |
| **Development (W.6.2b)** | Body paragraphs contain no facts, details, or definitions; ideas are vague or opinion-based | Some facts or details are present but are vague, inaccurate, or poorly connected to the category | Each body paragraph includes multiple specific facts and concrete details, clearly connected to the category; at least three domain-specific terms are used and defined in context | Body paragraphs are richly developed with multiple facts, definitions, concrete details, and elaboration that deepens understanding; domain-specific vocabulary is used fluently |
| **Organization and Transitions (W.6.2c)** | No organization; ideas within and between paragraphs are disconnected | Some structure is apparent; simple transitions are used within paragraphs but not between them | Information is grouped logically by category; transitions connect ideas within and between paragraphs purposefully | Organization is seamless; transitions are varied and contribute to the coherence, flow, and readability of the text |
| **Conclusion (W.6.2e)** | No conclusion, or the text ends abruptly | The conclusion restates the introduction without summarizing key points or offering insight | The conclusion summarizes key information and provides a final insight, reflection, or forward-looking statement | The conclusion synthesizes the information powerfully, offers a meaningful takeaway, and leaves the reader with a clear understanding of why the topic matters |
| **Tone and Conventions** | Tone is informal or opinionated; frequent errors interfere with readability | Tone is mostly objective but occasionally slips into opinion; several convention errors are present | Tone is consistently objective and informative; minor convention errors do not interfere with meaning | Tone is authoritative and engaging while remaining objective; the text is virtually error-free |

**Scoring:** Same scale as Task 1. Proficient on all criteria = 15 points.

### Sample Proficient Informative Response

> **How Solar Panels Turn Sunlight into Electricity**
>
> Every hour, the sun sends enough energy to the Earth's surface to power the entire planet for a full year. Yet until recently, humans had no practical way to capture and convert that energy into usable electricity. Solar panels — also called photovoltaic panels — have changed that. These devices, which are now found on rooftops, in fields, and even on spacecraft, convert sunlight directly into electrical current. This article examines how solar panels work by exploring three key concepts: the photovoltaic effect, the structure of a solar cell, and the process by which direct current is converted into the alternating current that powers homes and businesses.
>
> At the heart of every solar panel is a phenomenon called the photovoltaic effect, the process by which certain materials generate an electrical current when exposed to light. When photons — particles of light energy from the sun — strike the surface of a solar cell, they transfer their energy to electrons in the cell's semiconductor material, typically silicon. This energy boost knocks the electrons loose from their atoms, allowing them to flow as an electrical current. The photovoltaic effect was first observed by French physicist Edmond Becquerel in 1839, but it took more than a century of scientific development before the technology became efficient enough for practical use.
>
> A single solar cell is made up of two layers of silicon, each treated with different chemical additives in a process called doping. The top layer is doped with phosphorus, which gives it extra electrons and a negative charge (the N-type layer). The bottom layer is doped with boron, which creates gaps where electrons are missing, giving it a positive charge (the P-type layer). Where these two layers meet, an electric field forms — similar to the positive and negative ends of a battery. When sunlight knocks electrons free in the N-type layer, the electric field pushes them toward the P-type layer, creating a flow of electricity. Metal conductors on the top and bottom of the cell capture this current and direct it through wires to an external circuit.
>
> The electricity produced by solar cells is direct current (DC), which flows in one direction. However, most homes and businesses use alternating current (AC), which switches direction rapidly. To bridge this gap, solar panel systems include a device called an inverter, which converts DC to AC so the electricity can be used to power appliances, lights, and other devices. Modern inverters are remarkably efficient, converting more than 95% of the DC power into usable AC power with minimal energy loss.
>
> From Becquerel's nineteenth-century observation to the high-efficiency panels of today, solar technology has transformed from a scientific curiosity into one of the fastest-growing energy sources in the world. As manufacturing costs continue to fall and efficiency continues to rise, solar panels are becoming accessible to more communities and countries than ever before. Understanding how this technology works is not just a matter of scientific literacy — it is a window into the energy systems that will shape the coming decades.

## Task 3 — Narrative Writing (W.6.3)

**Prompt:**

Write a narrative about a character who faces an unexpected challenge and must find the courage to respond. The story may be realistic or entirely imagined. Your narrative must include:

- An engaging opening that hooks the reader and establishes the character, setting, and situation
- A clear sequence of events with rising action, a climax, and a resolution
- At least one expanded moment (slow motion writing) with sensory details from at least three senses
- At least one compressed section that summarizes less important events
- At least one scene with dialogue (correctly punctuated)
- Figurative language (at least one simile, metaphor, or personification)
- A conclusion that provides resolution and meaning

Write at least five paragraphs.

---

### Narrative Writing Rubric (15 points: 5 criteria x 3 points at Proficient)

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Orientation and Hook (W.6.3a)** | No character, setting, or situation is established; the reader is confused from the start | One or two elements are present but vague; no hook or the hook is ineffective | The opening hooks the reader using a clear technique (action, dialogue, sensory, mystery) and establishes character, setting, and situation | The opening is immediately compelling; character, setting, and situation are woven together seamlessly with a hook that creates strong emotional engagement |
| **Pacing and Structure (W.6.3b)** | Events are listed without variation in pacing; no expansion or compression | Some pacing variation is attempted; expansion or compression is present but not purposeful | At least one important moment is expanded with detail and at least one less important section is compressed; the story has a clear beginning, rising action, climax, and resolution | Pacing is deliberately controlled throughout; expansion and compression are used strategically to create tension, emotional impact, and narrative momentum |
| **Descriptive Details and Figurative Language (W.6.3d)** | No sensory details or figurative language; language is vague and generic | One or two sensory details and/or one piece of figurative language are present but feel forced or disconnected | Sensory details from at least three senses are woven into the narrative; at least one example of figurative language enhances the scene; details are relevant to the story | Sensory details are vivid, precise, and immersive throughout; figurative language is original and illuminating; every detail serves the story's mood, theme, or character development |
| **Dialogue and Technique (W.6.3b)** | No dialogue; narrative techniques are absent | Dialogue is attempted but feels unnatural or is incorrectly punctuated; limited narrative technique | Dialogue sounds natural, reveals character or advances the plot, and is correctly punctuated; narrative techniques (showing not telling, internal thought) are present | Dialogue is distinctive and memorable; narrative techniques are varied and sophisticated; the writer demonstrates confident control of craft |
| **Conclusion and Conventions (W.6.3e)** | No conclusion; the story stops abruptly; frequent convention errors | A conclusion is present but rushed or disconnected; several convention errors | The conclusion follows naturally from the events, provides closure and meaning; minor convention errors do not interfere with the reading experience | The conclusion is satisfying and resonant; it resolves the story and leaves the reader with something meaningful to consider; conventions are flawless |

**Scoring:** Same scale as Tasks 1 and 2. Proficient on all criteria = 15 points.

### Sample Proficient Narrative Response

> **Thirty Seconds**
>
> The gymnasium was silent except for the rhythmic squeak of Nadia's sneakers on the polished floor and the hollow bounce of the basketball against the hardwood. It was 6:47 a.m. — thirteen minutes before the rest of the team would arrive — and Nadia was practicing free throws alone, the way she had every morning for the past three weeks. The ball left her hands, arced through the stale gymnasium air, and rattled around the rim before dropping through the net. She exhaled. Twelve for fifteen. Not bad. Not great.
>
> The rest of the morning passed in the usual blur of warmups, drills, and scrimmages. Coach Tran split them into teams, and Nadia played well enough — a few good passes, a clean layup, nothing spectacular. It was the kind of practice where you survive without standing out. But as they gathered for the final huddle, Coach Tran looked directly at her.
>
> "Nadia, you are starting on Friday."
>
> The words hit her like cold water. Starting. The first game of the season. In front of the entire school. She opened her mouth to respond, but her throat was dry, and the only sound that came out was a small, uncertain "Okay."
>
> The days between Tuesday's announcement and Friday's game compressed into a single anxious blur. Nadia went to class, ate lunch, did homework — but none of it registered. Every spare thought circled back to the same image: herself standing at center court under the bright lights, hundreds of faces watching, and the terrifying possibility that she would freeze.
>
> Friday arrived whether she was ready or not. The gymnasium was transformed — banners hung from the rafters, the bleachers were packed, and the air smelled like popcorn and the sharp, chemical tang of fresh floor polish. The noise was a wall. Nadia stood at the edge of the court during warm-ups, bouncing the ball mechanically, and tried to remember how to breathe.
>
> "Hey." It was Aaliyah, the team captain, standing beside her with a calm expression. "You look like you are about to pass out."
>
> "I might," Nadia admitted.
>
> Aaliyah grinned. "Good. That means you care. Listen — you do not need to win the game tonight. You just need to play the next thirty seconds. That is it. Every time you feel scared, just play the next thirty seconds."
>
> The whistle blew. The ball was tipped. And Nadia played.
>
> The first thirty seconds were terrible. She caught a pass and nearly fumbled it, gripping the ball so tightly her fingers ached. But she held on. She passed to Aaliyah, who drove to the basket and scored. Thirty seconds.
>
> The next thirty seconds were better. Nadia set a screen, rolled to the elbow, and caught a bounce pass. The defender was close — she could hear the girl's breathing, feel the heat of her presence — but Nadia pivoted, found space, and released the ball in a high, clean arc. It kissed the backboard and dropped through the net. The crowd roared, and for a moment the sound felt like sunlight — warm, enormous, and everywhere at once.
>
> They lost the game by six points. Nadia scored eight. Afterward, sitting on the bench with a towel draped over her head and her legs aching, she did not feel like a loser. She felt like someone who had walked through a door she had been afraid to open and found that the room on the other side was not so frightening after all. Aaliyah sat down beside her and bumped her shoulder.
>
> "Thirty seconds at a time," Aaliyah said.
>
> Nadia smiled. "Thirty seconds at a time."

## Task 4 — Text-Based Evidence Response (W.6.9)

**Directions:** Read the following passage carefully. Then answer the prompt below it in a well-developed paragraph that draws specific evidence from the text to support your analysis.

### Passage: "The Language of Trees"

> (1) For centuries, scientists believed that trees were solitary organisms — tall, silent individuals competing with their neighbors for sunlight, water, and soil nutrients. Recent research, however, has overturned this view completely. Trees, it turns out, are social beings. They communicate, cooperate, and in some cases even care for one another through a vast underground network so complex that scientists have nicknamed it the "Wood Wide Web."
>
> (2) The foundation of this network is a symbiotic relationship between tree roots and a type of fungus called mycorrhizae. The fungal threads, which are far thinner than a human hair, extend outward from tree roots and weave through the soil, connecting the root systems of neighboring trees — sometimes across hundreds of meters. Through this fungal network, trees exchange nutrients and chemical signals. A study by ecologist Suzanne Simard at the University of British Columbia found that mature Douglas fir trees use the mycorrhizal network to send carbon, water, and nutrients to younger seedlings growing in the shade, effectively feeding their offspring through the soil.
>
> (3) Even more remarkably, trees appear to use the network to warn each other of danger. When a tree is attacked by insects, it releases chemical compounds into the fungal network that prompt neighboring trees to produce defensive chemicals before the insects reach them. Research published in the journal Ecology Letters demonstrated that trees connected through mycorrhizal networks were 40% more likely to survive an insect attack than trees whose fungal connections had been severed. The network, in other words, functions as a kind of forest immune system — a collective defense that protects the community, not just the individual.
>
> (4) Perhaps the most surprising finding is what happens when a tree is dying. Rather than hoarding its remaining resources, a dying tree often releases a pulse of carbon and nutrients into the network, distributing its remaining energy to its neighbors. Simard has described this as a "legacy" effect — the dying tree's final act is one of generosity, ensuring that its stored resources are not wasted but passed on to sustain the forest around it. This behavior challenges the long-held assumption that nature is governed purely by competition and survival of the fittest. In the forest, it seems, cooperation is just as essential as competition.

---

**Prompt:**

How does the passage use specific evidence to challenge the traditional view that trees are solitary, competitive organisms? In your response:

- State a clear claim that answers the question
- Use at least two pieces of evidence from the passage (at least one direct quotation)
- Analyze each piece of evidence, explaining how it supports your claim
- Write a concluding sentence that synthesizes your analysis

---

### Text-Based Evidence Response Rubric (15 points: 5 criteria x 3 points at Proficient)

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Claim (W.6.9)** | No claim is stated, or the response simply summarizes the passage | A general statement is made but does not directly answer the prompt or is too vague to guide analysis | A clear, specific claim directly answers the prompt and sets up the analysis that follows | A sophisticated, insightful claim demonstrates deep understanding of the passage and frames a compelling analysis |
| **Evidence Selection (W.6.9)** | No evidence from the passage is provided | One piece of evidence is provided but may be vague, inaccurately quoted, or only loosely connected to the claim | At least two pieces of evidence are provided (at least one direct quotation), both clearly relevant to the claim and accurately cited | Multiple, well-chosen pieces of evidence are integrated smoothly; quotations are precisely selected for maximum impact |
| **Analysis (W.6.9)** | No analysis; evidence is presented without explanation | Some analysis is present but is superficial (e.g., "This is important because it shows that trees communicate") without explaining WHY or HOW | Each piece of evidence is followed by analysis that explains what the evidence means and how it specifically supports the claim | Analysis is thorough, insightful, and demonstrates independent thinking; the writer draws connections between pieces of evidence and explores implications |
| **Organization and Coherence** | Response is disorganized; ideas are disconnected | Some structure is present, but the response jumps between ideas without clear connections | The response follows a clear claim-evidence-analysis structure with a synthesizing conclusion; ideas flow logically | The response is seamlessly organized; transitions between evidence and analysis are smooth; the synthesis adds genuine insight |
| **Conventions** | Frequent errors interfere with readability | Several errors are present; some interfere with meaning | Minor errors do not interfere with meaning; quotations are formatted correctly | Conventions are flawless; the response demonstrates polished, academic writing |

**Scoring:** Same scale as Tasks 1-3. Proficient on all criteria = 15 points.

### Sample Proficient Text-Based Evidence Response

> The passage systematically dismantles the traditional view of trees as isolated competitors by presenting three categories of scientific evidence showing that trees actively communicate, support one another, and even sacrifice for the forest community. The first major piece of evidence concerns resource sharing: the passage describes research by ecologist Suzanne Simard showing that "mature Douglas fir trees use the mycorrhizal network to send carbon, water, and nutrients to younger seedlings growing in the shade, effectively feeding their offspring through the soil" (paragraph 2). This directly contradicts the idea that trees compete for resources, because the mature trees are voluntarily transferring their own nutrients to smaller, weaker trees that cannot yet access sunlight on their own. If trees were purely competitive, this behavior would make no evolutionary sense — a competitor does not feed its rivals. The passage further challenges the competition model with evidence about collective defense: research in the journal Ecology Letters found that "trees connected through mycorrhizal networks were 40% more likely to survive an insect attack than trees whose fungal connections had been severed" (paragraph 3). This statistic is powerful because it demonstrates that the network provides a measurable survival advantage — trees are literally stronger together than apart. The passage describes this as "a kind of forest immune system," a metaphor that replaces the image of isolated individuals with the image of a coordinated community. Finally, the author presents the "legacy" effect described in paragraph 4, in which dying trees release their remaining resources into the network rather than hoarding them. This behavior is perhaps the strongest challenge to the competition model because it shows that trees act generously even at the end of their lives, prioritizing the health of the forest over their own survival. Taken together, this evidence reveals a forest ecosystem governed not by the ruthless competition of individuals but by a complex web of cooperation that benefits the entire community.

## Administration Notes

- **Timing:** Allow 75-90 minutes total. Students may work at their own pace within that window. Recommended allocation: 15-20 minutes per task. If students need more time, consider administering the assessment across two sessions (Tasks 1-2 in one session, Tasks 3-4 in another).
- **Reference materials:** Students may use the Transition Reference Chart from Lessons 3 and 6 and the Revision Checklist from Lesson 10. They should NOT use their previous drafts, graphic organizers, or model texts — this assessment measures independent application.
- **Task 4 passage:** The "Language of Trees" passage should be available throughout the response writing time. Students should be encouraged to annotate the passage and highlight evidence before writing.
- **Accommodations:** Students with IEPs or 504 plans should receive their documented accommodations (extended time, reduced quantity, dictation, etc.). For students who struggle with writing stamina, consider administering one task per session across four days.
- **Scoring priorities:** When scoring, read the entire piece before assigning scores on any criterion. Focus on what the student CAN do, not just what is missing. Look for evidence of the specific skills taught in the unit:
  - Argumentative: debatable claim, ICE pattern, logical organization, synthesizing conclusion
  - Informative: focus statement with previewed categories, domain-specific vocabulary defined in context, objective tone, development strategies
  - Narrative: engaging hook, show-don't-tell, expansion/compression, purposeful dialogue, meaningful ending
  - Text-based evidence: specific claim answering the question, accurately cited quotations, analysis (not just summary), synthesis
- **Re-assessment:** Students who do not meet mastery may be re-assessed after targeted reteaching. Focus reteaching on the specific standard(s) where the student scored Developing or Beginning. A re-assessment should use different prompts but assess the same skills.
