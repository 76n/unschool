# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 4

## Topic
Introduction to Informative Writing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between argumentative writing and informative/explanatory writing
- Select and narrow a topic for an informative text
- Write an engaging informative introduction with a hook, context, and thesis/focus statement
- Identify the organizational structure of a model informative text (categories, subtopics, or logical groupings)

## Prerequisites
- W.5.2: Experience writing informative/explanatory texts with a clear focus, grouped related information, and concluding statement
- W.5.2a: Ability to introduce a topic clearly and provide a general observation and focus statement
- Lessons 1-3: Understanding of claim-based writing (to contrast with informative writing)
- Familiarity with domain-specific vocabulary from content-area reading

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model informative text (provided below)
- Graphic organizer: Topic Web (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)
- Sticky notes for brainstorming

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish informative writing from argumentative writing and activate prior knowledge about informative texts.

**Activity: Argue It or Explain It?**

Display the following two passages side by side on the board:

**Passage A:** "Schools should adopt year-round calendars because the traditional summer break causes students to lose up to two months of learning, disproportionately affecting low-income students who have fewer enrichment opportunities."

**Passage B:** "The traditional school calendar in the United States was originally designed around the agricultural season, when children were needed to help with farming during the summer months. Today, approximately 3,700 schools across the country have shifted to year-round schedules, which distribute breaks more evenly throughout the year."

Say: "Both passages are about the same topic — school calendars. But they have very different purposes. Turn to a partner: Which one is trying to convince you, and which one is trying to teach you?"

After discussion, clarify:
- Passage A is argumentative — it takes a position ("should adopt") and supports it with evidence intended to persuade.
- Passage B is informative — it explains the topic with facts and historical context without taking a side.

Say: "In Lessons 1 through 3, we learned to argue. Starting today, we are shifting gears. Informative writing does not try to convince the reader of anything. Its job is to examine a topic clearly and help the reader understand something they did not know before."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the structure of an informative text and the process of selecting and narrowing a topic.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Informative/explanatory writing:** Writing that examines a topic and conveys ideas, concepts, and information clearly and accurately
- **Focus statement (thesis):** A sentence that tells the reader what the text will be about and previews the main subtopics or categories
- **Category:** A subtopic or group of related information within an informative text
- **Domain-specific vocabulary:** Specialized words related to a particular subject (e.g., "photosynthesis" in science, "democracy" in social studies)
- **Objective tone:** A neutral, fact-based writing voice that does not express personal opinions or emotions

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Informative writing in sixth grade has the same basic structure as in fifth grade — introduction, body, conclusion — but the expectations are higher. You need to examine your topic more deeply, organize your information more clearly, use more precise vocabulary, and maintain an objective tone throughout."

Write on the board:

**Informative Text Structure:**
1. **Introduction:** Hook + Context + Focus Statement (previews the categories/subtopics)
2. **Body Paragraphs:** Each paragraph examines one category with facts, definitions, concrete details, and/or examples
3. **Conclusion:** Summarizes key information and provides a final insight

Say: "One of the trickiest parts of informative writing is choosing and narrowing a topic. Watch me model this."

**Narrowing a Topic:**
- Too broad: "Animals" (millions of species — impossible to cover)
- Still too broad: "Ocean animals" (still thousands of species)
- Narrowed: "How octopuses use camouflage to survive" (specific, manageable, interesting)

Say: "A good informative topic is specific enough to cover well in a short essay but interesting enough that the reader will want to learn about it. Ask yourself: 'Can I explain this clearly in three to four paragraphs?' If the answer is no, narrow further."

### Worked Examples

Read the following model informative text aloud. Display it so the student can follow along.

> **The Hidden World of Bioluminescence**
>
> Deep beneath the surface of the ocean, where sunlight cannot reach, something extraordinary happens: living creatures produce their own light. This phenomenon, known as bioluminescence, is one of the most widespread yet least understood wonders of the natural world. Bioluminescence occurs in thousands of species across the globe, and scientists are only beginning to uncover its many purposes. This article examines what bioluminescence is, how organisms produce it, and why so many different creatures have evolved this remarkable ability.
>
> Bioluminescence is the production and emission of light by a living organism. The word itself comes from the Greek "bios," meaning life, and the Latin "lumen," meaning light. Unlike the light produced by a light bulb, which generates significant heat, bioluminescent light is remarkably efficient — nearly 100% of the energy is converted to light, with almost no heat produced. Scientists call this "cold light." Bioluminescence is found in a stunning variety of organisms: deep-sea fish, jellyfish, squid, certain species of fungi, fireflies, and even some types of plankton that make the ocean surface glow blue-green on warm nights.
>
> The chemical process behind bioluminescence involves two key substances: a light-producing molecule called luciferin and an enzyme called luciferase. When luciferase catalyzes the oxidation of luciferin — meaning it helps luciferin react with oxygen — energy is released in the form of visible light. Different species produce different colors depending on the exact structure of their luciferin molecules and the environment in which the reaction occurs. Deep-sea creatures tend to produce blue or green light, which travels farthest through water, while land-based organisms like fireflies produce yellow or green light.
>
> Perhaps the most fascinating aspect of bioluminescence is the variety of purposes it serves. Some organisms, like the anglerfish, use light as a lure to attract prey in the pitch-black deep sea. Others, like certain species of squid, use it for camouflage — they produce light on their undersides to match the faint glow of the surface above them, making them invisible to predators looking up from below. Fireflies use bioluminescence to find mates, flashing in species-specific patterns that act as a kind of Morse code. And when threatened, some deep-sea creatures release glowing clouds of bioluminescent chemicals to confuse predators, much like a squid releases ink.
>
> From the deepest ocean trenches to backyard summer evenings lit by fireflies, bioluminescence reminds us that nature's ingenuity has no limits. Scientists continue to study this phenomenon not only to understand the natural world but also to develop practical applications, from medical imaging techniques that use bioluminescent markers to detect cancer cells to environmentally friendly lighting inspired by nature's cold light. The more we learn about bioluminescence, the more it illuminates — in every sense of the word — the remarkable creativity of life on Earth.

Say: "Let's annotate this together." Use two highlighter colors:
- **Color 1:** Highlight the focus statement in the introduction and the summary in the conclusion.
- **Color 2:** Highlight the topic sentence of each body paragraph.

Ask: "What are the three categories this essay is organized around?" (What bioluminescence is, how it works chemically, why organisms use it.)

Ask: "What domain-specific vocabulary does the author use?" (Bioluminescence, luciferin, luciferase, oxidation, catalyze, cold light.) "Notice that the author defines these terms clearly so the reader can follow along."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively select a topic, narrow it, and plan an informative text using a Topic Web.

**Activity: Topic Web Planning**

Draw the following graphic organizer on chart paper:

```
         [NARROWED TOPIC]
         /       |       \
  [Category 1] [Category 2] [Category 3]
      |            |            |
  [Detail]     [Detail]     [Detail]
  [Detail]     [Detail]     [Detail]
```

Say: "Let's plan an informative text together. I will give you a broad topic, and we will narrow it down and plan three categories."

**Broad topic:** Weather

Guide the student through narrowing:
- Weather → Severe weather → How tornadoes form

Now fill in the Topic Web together:

- **Narrowed topic:** How tornadoes form
- **Category 1: Conditions needed** — Details: warm moist air meets cold dry air, wind shear (winds blowing at different speeds/directions at different altitudes), atmospheric instability
- **Category 2: The formation process** — Details: supercell thunderstorm develops, rotating updraft (mesocyclone), funnel cloud descends to the ground
- **Category 3: Measuring and classifying tornadoes** — Details: Enhanced Fujita Scale (EF0-EF5), based on damage and estimated wind speed, EF5 is the most destructive

Now write the introduction together:

Say: "Let's use a surprising fact as our hook."

Sample collaborative introduction:

> The United States experiences approximately 1,200 tornadoes every year — more than any other country on Earth. These violent rotating columns of air can reach wind speeds exceeding 300 miles per hour and leave paths of destruction miles long. But what causes a tornado to form in the first place? Understanding the science behind tornadoes requires examining three key elements: the atmospheric conditions that set the stage, the step-by-step process of formation, and the system scientists use to measure a tornado's power.

Say: "Notice that our focus statement previews the three categories, giving the reader a roadmap for the rest of the text."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently select a topic, narrow it, and plan an informative text.

**Task:** Choose a topic from the list below (or propose your own). Narrow it to a specific, manageable focus. Then complete a Topic Web with three categories and at least two details per category. Finally, write your introductory paragraph with a hook, context, and focus statement.

**Topic options:**
1. Space exploration (narrow to: how rovers explore Mars, the International Space Station, etc.)
2. Ancient civilizations (narrow to: how the Egyptians built pyramids, daily life in ancient Rome, etc.)
3. Animal adaptations (narrow to: how arctic animals survive extreme cold, desert survival strategies, etc.)
4. Technology (narrow to: how 3D printing works, the history of video game design, etc.)

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete the Topic Web and write the introductory paragraph as described.
**Stretch:** Complete the Topic Web, write the introductory paragraph, AND write one body paragraph that develops Category 1 with facts and domain-specific vocabulary.
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task AND include at least two domain-specific vocabulary terms in your body paragraph, each defined or explained within the text for the reader.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — W.5.2b) Read this informative sentence and identify one way to make it more specific: "The animal has interesting features that help it survive." (Expected: Replace vague language with concrete details. Example: "The Arctic fox has a thick double-layered fur coat and compact body shape that conserve heat in temperatures as low as minus 58 degrees Fahrenheit.")
6. (Grade 5 review — W.5.1a) Write a claim statement (not an informative focus statement) about the following topic: "Whether schools should teach typing instead of handwriting." (Expected: A debatable claim, e.g., "Schools should replace handwriting instruction with typing courses because digital communication dominates the modern workplace, and typing proficiency is a more practical skill for the twenty-first century.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following focus statement and identify how many categories the informative text will cover. Name each category.

"The monarch butterfly's annual migration is one of nature's most remarkable journeys, involving three stages: the southward flight to Mexico in autumn, the winter hibernation in mountain forests, and the multi-generational return trip northward in spring."

**Expected Answers:**
- Three categories
- Category 1: The southward flight to Mexico in autumn
- Category 2: The winter hibernation in mountain forests
- Category 3: The multi-generational return trip northward in spring

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies all three categories from the focus statement.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model informative text and re-read the focus statement together
- Ask: "What words signal the categories?" (highlight the list structure)
- Practice writing a focus statement for a simple topic (e.g., "This article explains the three main parts of a volcano: the magma chamber, the vent, and the crater")

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 5, where they will learn to develop informative body paragraphs with greater depth and sophistication
- Encourage the student to begin drafting body paragraphs for the topic they planned today

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Informative Mentor Text Analysis:** Find an informative article in a magazine, textbook, or website. Identify the focus statement, the categories, and three examples of domain-specific vocabulary. How does the author make complex information accessible to the reader?
2. **Topic Narrowing Chain:** Start with "Science" and narrow it through five levels until you reach a topic specific enough for a three-paragraph informative essay. Example: Science → Biology → Marine biology → Coral reefs → How coral bleaching happens.
3. **Compare the Modes:** Write a one-sentence claim (argument) and a one-sentence focus statement (informative) about the same topic. How do the sentences differ in purpose and tone?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students sometimes slip into argumentative writing during informative assignments, adding opinions like "This is really cool" or "Everyone should know about this." Remind them that informative writing maintains an objective tone — the goal is to teach, not to persuade.
- Topic selection and narrowing is a critical skill that students will use repeatedly. If a student picks a topic that is too broad (e.g., "the ocean"), spend extra time on narrowing rather than rushing to the planning stage. A well-narrowed topic makes every subsequent step easier.
- The bioluminescence model text is intentionally rich with domain-specific vocabulary to show students what sixth-grade informative writing looks like at its best. Not every student will produce writing at this level immediately, but they should understand what they are working toward.
- Encourage students to draw on knowledge from their science and social studies classes for informative topics. Cross-curricular connections make writing more meaningful and give students real content to work with.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
**Sample Topic Web (Arctic animal adaptations):**
- **Narrowed topic:** How Arctic animals survive extreme cold
- **Category 1: Insulation adaptations** — Details: thick fur (Arctic fox has double-layered coat), blubber (seals and walruses have up to 6 inches), countercurrent heat exchange in extremities
- **Category 2: Behavioral adaptations** — Details: migration (caribou travel 3,000 miles), hibernation (Arctic ground squirrel), huddling (emperor penguins)
- **Category 3: Physical adaptations** — Details: compact body shapes (reduce surface area), small ears and short limbs (minimize heat loss), color changes (Arctic hare turns white in winter)

**Sample introduction:**
> At temperatures that can plunge below minus 40 degrees, the Arctic seems like one of the most inhospitable places on Earth. Yet an astonishing variety of animals — from massive polar bears to tiny lemmings — not only survive in this frozen landscape but thrive. How do they do it? Arctic animals have evolved three categories of remarkable adaptations to withstand extreme cold: specialized insulation that traps body heat, behavioral strategies that conserve energy, and physical features that minimize heat loss.
