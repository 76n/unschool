# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3: Writing

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview

Unit 3 guides sixth-grade students through the three core modes of academic writing: argumentative, informative/explanatory, and narrative. Building on the opinion writing, informative writing, and narrative skills developed in Grade 5, students make the critical transition to formal argumentation with claims and evidence, produce informative texts that examine topics with greater depth and sophistication, and craft narratives with purposeful descriptive details and controlled pacing. The unit culminates with a text-based evidence writing task (W.6.9) and a revision and publishing workshop where students refine, polish, and share their strongest work. Each lesson includes original model texts, graphic organizers, and structured practice so that students internalize the qualities of effective sixth-grade writing through both analysis and production.

### Prerequisite Skills (Grade 5)

- W.5.1: Writing opinion pieces with logically ordered reasons supported by facts, details, and examples; using transitional words, phrases, and clauses to connect opinions and reasons
- W.5.2: Writing informative/explanatory texts with a clear focus, grouped related information, precise language, domain-specific vocabulary, and a concluding statement
- W.5.3: Writing narratives that orient the reader, develop events with dialogue, description, pacing, and sensory details, and provide a satisfying conclusion
- W.5.4: Producing clear and coherent writing appropriate to task, purpose, and audience
- L.5.1-L.5.3: Command of Grade 5 grammar, usage, capitalization, punctuation, and spelling conventions, including varied sentence structures

### Facilitation Tips

- **Emphasize the shift from opinion to argument.** In Grade 5, students wrote opinion pieces. In Grade 6, they write arguments. The key difference is that arguments require a formal claim supported by specific, relevant evidence and logical reasoning, not just personal reasons. Return to this distinction frequently throughout Lessons 1-3.
- **Model the writing process visibly.** Sixth graders benefit from watching the facilitator compose in real time. Think aloud as you draft on chart paper or a whiteboard, narrating your decisions about claims, evidence selection, transitions, and word choice.
- **Use graphic organizers as scaffolds that fade.** Introduce the Claim-Reason-Evidence chart, Topic Outline, and Story Map explicitly, but encourage students to internalize these structures so they can plan independently by the end of the unit.
- **Conference strategically during independent practice.** Circulate and hold focused 2-minute conferences with individual students. Address one skill per conference (e.g., strengthening a claim, adding a specific detail, varying sentence structure) rather than trying to fix everything.
- **Protect writing time.** Once students begin independent practice, minimize interruptions. Use a quiet signal or timer to mark transitions.
- **Normalize revision as the heart of good writing.** Emphasize that professional writers revise extensively. First drafts are for getting ideas down; quality comes from rewriting. Lesson 10 is entirely devoted to this process.
- **Build audience awareness.** Sixth graders are ready to consider how their writing sounds to a reader. Encourage students to read their work aloud and to imagine their audience's questions and reactions.
- **Connect writing modes.** Help students see that the skills transfer across modes: strong evidence supports arguments AND informative writing; vivid details enhance narratives AND informative texts; clear organization matters in all three modes.

### Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| An argument is the same as an opinion piece | An argument requires a formal claim supported by specific evidence and logical reasoning. An opinion piece may rely on personal reasons and preferences. In Grade 6, students move from "I think..." to "The evidence shows..." |
| A claim is just a topic sentence | A claim is a debatable position that can be supported or challenged with evidence. A topic sentence may simply introduce what a paragraph is about. Claims take a stand. |
| Evidence means copying long quotes from a source | Evidence should be carefully selected, introduced, and explained. Students must show HOW the evidence supports their claim, not just drop a quote and move on. Teach the "introduce-cite-explain" pattern. |
| Informative writing is the same as a research report | Informative writing can take many forms (articles, essays, explanations) and focuses on examining a topic clearly and thoroughly. It does not require a formal research process, though it does require accurate information organized logically. |
| Narrative writing is just telling what happened | Sixth-grade narratives require purposeful technique: engaging openings, descriptive details that serve the story, controlled pacing (expanding important moments, compressing less important ones), and meaningful conclusions. |
| Drawing evidence from a text means summarizing the text | Drawing evidence means selecting specific details, quotes, or examples from a text and using them to support an analytical point. The student's own analysis and reasoning must surround the evidence. |

### Assessment Guidance

**Formative Assessment (ongoing):**
- Exit tickets at the end of each lesson provide a quick snapshot of skill acquisition
- Writing conferences during independent practice allow for individualized feedback and targeted instruction
- Graphic organizers and planning documents reveal students' thinking processes before they draft

**Summative Assessment (Unit Assessment):**
- The unit assessment includes three writing tasks (one argumentative, one informative, one text-based evidence response) plus a narrative writing task
- Each writing task is evaluated using a 4-level rubric (Beginning / Developing / Proficient / Advanced)
- Proficiency requires a score of Proficient or above on at least three of the four tasks
- Total assessment: approximately 60 points

**Portfolio Assessment (Lesson 10):**
- Students select their strongest pieces from the unit, revise them, and present to peers
- The portfolio provides valuable information about student self-assessment, revision skills, and growth over the unit

### Differentiation at a Glance

**Below Grade Level:**
- Provide sentence starters and paragraph frames for each writing type
- Offer pre-filled graphic organizers with some sections completed as models
- Reduce the number of required body paragraphs (e.g., two instead of three for arguments)
- Allow students to dictate ideas before writing or use speech-to-text tools
- Provide word banks for academic vocabulary (claim, evidence, analysis, transition)
- Pair with a peer partner for collaborative planning before independent writing

**On Grade Level:**
- Follow lesson plans as written with the standard graphic organizers and model texts
- Encourage use of the transition reference resources and peer conferencing protocols
- Expect three-section body structure for arguments and informative writing
- Expect narratives with purposeful descriptive details and at least one expanded moment

**Above Grade Level:**
- Challenge students to address and refute a counterclaim in argumentative writing
- Encourage the integration of multiple sources or perspectives in informative writing
- Ask narrative writers to experiment with nonlinear structure, multiple points of view, or extended metaphor
- Have students write across modes on the same topic (e.g., an argument and an informative piece about the same issue) to analyze how purpose shapes writing
- Invite students to serve as peer writing coaches during the revision workshop
