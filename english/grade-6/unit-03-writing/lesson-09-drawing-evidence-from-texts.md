# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 9

## Topic
Drawing Evidence from Texts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read a literary or informational text and identify specific evidence relevant to an analytical question
- Write a text-based response that uses direct quotations and paraphrased evidence from a source text
- Apply the "claim-evidence-analysis" structure to connect evidence from a text to an analytical point
- Distinguish between summarizing a text and analyzing a text with evidence

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 2: Familiarity with the ICE (Introduce-Cite-Explain) pattern for integrating evidence
- W.5.1: Experience supporting opinions with facts and details from texts
- RL.6.1 / RI.6.1: Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (from Unit 1 or Unit 2)
- Basic understanding of how to quote from a text using quotation marks

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Source text passage (provided below — an original informational passage)
- Source text passage (provided below — an original literary passage)
- Evidence Tracking Sheet (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish between summarizing a text and drawing evidence from it to support an analytical point.

**Activity: Summary or Analysis?**

Display the following two student responses about a fictional text about a character named Rosa who refuses to sit out of a spelling bee despite being sick:

**Response A:** "In the story, Rosa is sick but she goes to the spelling bee anyway. She spells all the words correctly and wins the trophy. Her mom is proud of her."

**Response B:** "The author uses Rosa's decision to compete in the spelling bee despite her illness to reveal her determination and her fear of letting others down. When Rosa thinks, 'If I quit now, I am not just giving up the trophy — I am giving up on the person I told everyone I could be,' the reader understands that Rosa's motivation goes deeper than winning. She is driven by a need to prove something to herself."

Say: "Response A is a summary — it tells us what happened. Response B is analysis — it makes a point about the text and supports that point with a direct quotation. In sixth grade, when we draw evidence from a text, we are doing what Response B does: making an analytical claim and proving it with specific evidence from the source."

Ask: "What does Response B do that Response A does not?" (Makes a claim about what the author is showing, quotes directly from the text, explains what the quote reveals.)

Say: "Today we are going to practice drawing evidence from both informational and literary texts. This is one of the most important skills in sixth-grade writing because it combines your reading skills with your writing skills."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the structure of a text-based evidence response and the techniques for selecting, quoting, and analyzing evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Drawing evidence:** Selecting specific details, quotations, or examples from a text to support a point
- **Direct quotation:** The exact words from a text, placed in quotation marks and cited by author or title
- **Paraphrase:** Restating information from a text in your own words while preserving the original meaning
- **Analysis:** The writer's own interpretation and explanation of what the evidence means and why it matters
- **Text-based response:** A written response that uses evidence from a specific text to support an analytical claim

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Drawing evidence from a text follows a pattern very similar to the ICE pattern we learned in Lesson 2 for argumentative writing. But instead of finding evidence from research, you are finding evidence from the text itself — from the words on the page."

Write on chart paper:

**Text-Based Evidence Response Structure:**
1. **Claim:** State your analytical point about the text.
2. **Evidence:** Provide a direct quotation or paraphrase from the text.
3. **Analysis:** Explain what the evidence means and how it supports your claim.

**Rules for Using Quotations:**
- Copy the exact words from the text.
- Place them in quotation marks.
- Introduce the quote with a signal phrase: "The author states...", "According to the passage...", "In paragraph 3, the text explains..."
- Keep quotations short — quote only the most relevant words or phrase.
- Always follow a quotation with your own analysis. Never let a quotation be the last sentence in a paragraph.

**Rules for Paraphrasing:**
- Restate the information in your own words and sentence structure.
- Do not change the meaning.
- Still identify where the information comes from ("The passage explains that...").
- Use paraphrasing when the exact words are less important than the idea.

Say: "The biggest mistake students make with text evidence is the 'quote and run' — they drop in a quotation and move on without explaining what it means. Your analysis is where YOU show up as a thinker. The quotation is the evidence; the analysis is your brain."

### Worked Examples

**Source Text (Informational):**

Read the following original informational passage aloud. Display it so the student can follow along.

> **The Unseen Engineers: How Earthworms Shape the Soil Beneath Our Feet**
>
> (1) Earthworms are among the most important organisms in the terrestrial ecosystem, yet they receive a fraction of the attention given to more charismatic creatures like dolphins, eagles, or pandas. Despite their humble appearance, earthworms perform a suite of ecological services that are essential for healthy soil and, by extension, for nearly all terrestrial life. Charles Darwin, who spent over 40 years studying earthworms, wrote in 1881 that "it may be doubted whether there are many other animals which have played so important a part in the history of the world."
>
> (2) The most fundamental service earthworms provide is soil aeration. As they burrow through the ground, earthworms create a network of tunnels that allow air and water to penetrate deep into the soil. Without these tunnels, soil becomes compacted and waterlogged, making it difficult for plant roots to grow and for microorganisms to access the oxygen they need. A single earthworm can burrow up to six feet below the surface, and in healthy soil, earthworm tunnels can increase the rate of water infiltration by up to 10 times compared to soil without earthworms.
>
> (3) Beyond aeration, earthworms are nature's recyclers. They consume decaying plant material — fallen leaves, dead roots, and other organic debris — and break it down into nutrient-rich waste called castings. Earthworm castings are exceptionally fertile, containing five times more nitrogen, seven times more phosphorus, and eleven times more potassium than the surrounding soil. Farmers and gardeners have long recognized this, sometimes calling earthworm castings "black gold" for their ability to boost plant growth.
>
> (4) Despite their importance, earthworm populations are under threat from modern agricultural practices. Heavy pesticide use, deep plowing, and the removal of crop residues from fields reduce earthworm numbers dramatically. A study by the European Environmental Agency found that conventionally farmed fields contain an average of 150 earthworms per square meter, while organic fields — where pesticides are restricted and crop residues are left on the surface — contain an average of 400 per square meter. The implications are clear: how we farm the land directly determines the health of the organisms that sustain it.

Now model a text-based evidence response:

**Question:** How does the passage use evidence to support the idea that earthworms are vital to healthy soil?

**Model Response:**

> The passage makes a compelling case that earthworms are essential to soil health by providing specific, measurable evidence of their contributions. One key piece of evidence concerns soil aeration: the author explains that "earthworm tunnels can increase the rate of water infiltration by up to 10 times compared to soil without earthworms" (paragraph 2). This statistic is powerful because it shows that earthworm activity does not just slightly improve soil conditions — it transforms them, making the soil dramatically more capable of absorbing the water and air that plants and microorganisms need to survive. The passage further strengthens this argument with evidence about nutrient cycling, noting that earthworm castings contain "five times more nitrogen, seven times more phosphorus, and eleven times more potassium than the surrounding soil" (paragraph 3). These specific comparisons make the abstract concept of "soil health" concrete and measurable, helping the reader understand exactly how much earthworms contribute. Together, these pieces of evidence demonstrate that earthworms are not merely present in healthy soil — they actively create the conditions that make soil healthy.

Annotate the response:
- **Claim:** "earthworms are essential to soil health" (first sentence)
- **Evidence 1:** Direct quotation about water infiltration (from paragraph 2)
- **Analysis 1:** "This statistic is powerful because..." — explains why the evidence matters
- **Evidence 2:** Direct quotation about castings (from paragraph 3)
- **Analysis 2:** "These specific comparisons make..." — explains how the evidence works
- **Synthesis:** Final sentence ties both pieces of evidence together

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively practice drawing evidence from a literary text to support analysis.

**Activity: Analyzing a Literary Passage**

Read the following original literary passage:

> **From "The Weight of the Jersey"**
>
> (1) Coach Reeves held up the team jersey — navy blue with white numbers — and the locker room went quiet. "Number seven goes to the player who showed the most growth this season," she said, looking out over the row of faces. "Not the most talent. Growth."
>
> (2) Zara pressed her hands between her knees to keep them from trembling. Number seven had belonged to Aaliyah Brooks last year, and Aaliyah had gone on to play for the state team. The jersey meant something. It meant you were the kind of player who worked when nobody was watching, who stayed after practice to run drills alone, who got knocked down and came back stronger.
>
> (3) "Zara Okafor," Coach Reeves said.
>
> (4) The room erupted. Zara stood, but her legs felt uncertain, as if the floor had turned to something softer than wood. She walked to the front of the room and took the jersey from Coach Reeves's hands. The fabric was surprisingly light — strange, she thought, for something that carried so much meaning.
>
> (5) Later, sitting alone on the bench outside the gym, Zara unfolded the jersey across her lap and traced the white stitching of the number with her finger. Six months ago, she had been the slowest player on the team, the one who missed the easiest passes, the one the other girls were too polite to complain about but not polite enough to pass to. She had thought about quitting every single week. But she had not quit. And now the jersey was in her hands — not because she was the best, but because she had refused to stay the worst.

**Question:** How does the author develop the theme of perseverance in this passage?

**Step 1 — Identify the claim:**

Say: "What is the passage saying about perseverance? Let's state our claim."

Guide: "The author develops the theme of perseverance by showing that Zara's growth comes not from natural talent but from her refusal to give up despite repeated struggles."

**Step 2 — Find evidence:**

Introduce the Evidence Tracking Sheet:

```
EVIDENCE TRACKING SHEET

Question/Claim: _________________________________

| Evidence (quote or paraphrase) | Paragraph | How it supports the claim |
|-------------------------------|-----------|---------------------------|
| 1.                            |           |                           |
| 2.                            |           |                           |
| 3.                            |           |                           |
```

Work together to fill it in:
- Evidence 1: "Not the most talent. Growth." (paragraph 1) — establishes that the award is about effort, not ability
- Evidence 2: "She had been the slowest player on the team, the one who missed the easiest passes" (paragraph 5) — shows where Zara started
- Evidence 3: "She had thought about quitting every single week. But she had not quit." (paragraph 5) — the core of perseverance is continuing despite wanting to stop

**Step 3 — Draft the response together:**

Write a response using claim-evidence-analysis, incorporating at least two quotations from the passage.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write a text-based evidence response.

**Task:** Using the informational passage about earthworms (provided above), answer the following question in a well-developed paragraph:

**Question:** How does the author of "The Unseen Engineers" use paragraph 4 to add urgency to the topic of earthworm conservation?

Your response must include:
- A clear claim that answers the question
- At least two pieces of evidence from the passage (at least one direct quotation)
- Analysis that explains how each piece of evidence supports your claim
- A concluding sentence that synthesizes your analysis

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write a response paragraph as described, with two pieces of evidence and analysis.
**Stretch:** Write the response paragraph AND a second paragraph that answers: How does the Charles Darwin quotation in paragraph 1 strengthen the passage's overall argument?
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task AND compare the two types of evidence used in the passage (statistics vs. expert quotation). Which type is more convincing in this context, and why?

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — W.5.2d) Rewrite this sentence using precise, domain-specific vocabulary: "The bug eats dead leaves and turns them into stuff that helps plants grow." (Expected: "The earthworm consumes decaying organic matter — such as fallen leaves and dead roots — and converts it into nutrient-rich castings that significantly enhance plant growth.")
6. (Grade 5 review — W.5.3c) Add a transitional clause to connect these two narrative sentences: "Maya crossed the finish line in first place. She collapsed on the grass." (Expected: "Although Maya crossed the finish line in first place, she immediately collapsed on the grass, her legs finally surrendering to the miles they had carried her." Or similar.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this student's text-based response and identify what is missing:

"The passage shows that earthworms are important. In paragraph 2, the author says that earthworms dig tunnels in the soil. This is important because tunnels are good for the soil."

**Expected Answers:**
Three things are missing:
1. **A specific claim:** The response says "earthworms are important" but does not state a clear analytical point.
2. **A direct quotation:** The response paraphrases vaguely ("dig tunnels") instead of quoting specific evidence.
3. **Meaningful analysis:** "Tunnels are good for the soil" does not explain HOW or WHY. The analysis should specify what the tunnels do (allow air and water infiltration) and why that matters (prevents compaction, supports plant roots and microorganisms).

**Mastery = Student identifies at least two of the three missing elements (specific claim, direct quotation, meaningful analysis).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model response and highlight the claim, evidence, and analysis in different colors
- Ask: "If I took away the quotation marks, could you tell which words are the author's and which are the student writer's?" If not, the response needs clearer integration of evidence
- Provide a frame: "The author demonstrates [claim] by stating, '[quotation]' (paragraph __). This evidence is significant because [analysis]."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10, the Writing Workshop where they will revise, polish, and publish their strongest work from the unit
- Encourage the student to review all three of their major writing pieces (argument, informative, narrative) and identify which one they want to revise and strengthen for their portfolio

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Drawing evidence from texts is a bridge skill between reading and writing — it asks students to be careful readers AND skilled writers simultaneously. If a student struggles, determine whether the difficulty is in reading comprehension (they do not understand the text), evidence selection (they do not know what to quote), or analysis (they cannot explain what the evidence means). Address the specific gap.
- "Quote and run" — dropping a quotation without analysis — is the most common error. Make a habit of asking: "What does this quotation prove? Why does it matter? How does it connect to your claim?" These questions should become automatic for students.
- The informational passage about earthworms is intentionally substantive and accessible. Students should be able to engage with it without specialized background knowledge. If a student struggles with reading comprehension, read the passage aloud together before beginning the writing task.
- This lesson addresses W.6.9 explicitly. Point out to students that this standard asks them to combine what they learned as readers (citing evidence, making inferences) with what they have learned as writers (organizing ideas, developing analysis). This is the integration of skills that makes sixth-grade ELA challenging and rewarding.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
**Sample response (on-level):**

> In paragraph 4, the author creates a sense of urgency about earthworm conservation by contrasting the condition of earthworms in conventional and organic farming systems. The passage presents a striking comparison: "conventionally farmed fields contain an average of 150 earthworms per square meter, while organic fields — where pesticides are restricted and crop residues are left on the surface — contain an average of 400 per square meter" (paragraph 4). This evidence is significant because it reveals that human farming practices are reducing earthworm populations by more than 60% in conventional fields compared to organic ones. The implication is that the very organisms responsible for soil aeration and nutrient cycling — processes the passage has already shown to be essential — are being destroyed by the methods we use to grow food. The author reinforces this urgency with a direct concluding statement: "how we farm the land directly determines the health of the organisms that sustain it" (paragraph 4). This sentence forces the reader to confront the idea that soil health is not an abstract environmental issue but a direct consequence of human choices. Together, these pieces of evidence transform the passage from an informative article about earthworms into a call for awareness about how agricultural practices are undermining the very systems that make farming possible.

**Sample stretch response (Darwin quotation):**

> The Charles Darwin quotation in paragraph 1 strengthens the passage's argument by lending historical and scientific authority to its central claim. When the author cites Darwin's 1881 assertion that "it may be doubted whether there are many other animals which have played so important a part in the history of the world," the reader encounters a startling claim from one of the most respected scientists in history — a claim that elevates earthworms from humble garden creatures to globally significant organisms. This quotation is particularly effective because it appears at the beginning of the passage, before the reader encounters the specific evidence about aeration and nutrient cycling. By establishing Darwin's endorsement early, the author primes the reader to take the subsequent evidence seriously and to reconsider any tendency to dismiss earthworms as insignificant.
