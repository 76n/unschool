# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 2

## Topic
Organizing Arguments with Reasons

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Organize reasons in a logical, strategic order (strongest-last, cause-effect, or problem-solution)
- Write body paragraphs that develop each reason with specific, relevant evidence
- Use the "introduce-cite-explain" pattern to integrate evidence into a paragraph
- Select the most relevant evidence from a set of options to support a reason

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 1: Ability to write a debatable claim and identify claims, reasons, and evidence
- W.5.1b: Experience providing logically ordered reasons supported by facts and details
- W.5.1c: Familiarity with using transitional words, phrases, and clauses to connect ideas
- Basic understanding of paragraph structure with topic sentences and supporting details

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Claim-Reason-Evidence chart from Lesson 1 (student's own)
- Model body paragraph (provided below)
- Highlighters (two colors)
- Evidence Sorting Cards (described below — facilitator prepares on index cards or strips of paper)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review claim writing from Lesson 1 and introduce the idea that the order of reasons matters.

**Activity: Which Order Is Strongest?**

Display the following claim and three reasons on the board:

**Claim:** "Middle schools should offer cooking classes as a required course."

**Reasons (scrambled):**
- A. Cooking teaches math skills like measurement, fractions, and ratios.
- B. Students who learn to cook make healthier food choices as adults, reducing long-term health costs.
- C. Cooking is a basic life skill that every person needs to live independently.

Say: "All three of these reasons support the claim. But a skilled writer does not just list them randomly — they arrange them strategically. Turn to a partner: Which order would you put these reasons in, and why?"

After discussion, reveal that skilled argument writers often use one of these organizational strategies:
- **Strongest-last:** Save your most compelling reason for the end (C, A, B or A, C, B)
- **Problem-solution:** Start with the problem, then build toward how the claim solves it
- **Building complexity:** Start with the simplest reason and build toward the most complex

Say: "Today we are going to learn how to organize your reasons AND how to develop each one into a strong body paragraph."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the structure of an argument body paragraph and the "introduce-cite-explain" evidence integration pattern.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Body paragraph:** A paragraph in the body of an essay that develops one reason with evidence and explanation
- **Topic sentence:** The first sentence of a body paragraph that states the reason being developed
- **Introduce-Cite-Explain (ICE):** A three-step pattern for integrating evidence: introduce the evidence, cite (present) the evidence, explain how it supports the claim
- **Logical order:** An arrangement of reasons that builds the argument strategically rather than listing them randomly
- **Elaboration:** The writer's own explanation and analysis that connects evidence to the claim

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In Lesson 1, you learned to write a claim and identify reasons and evidence. Today, we are going to take those ingredients and cook them into strong body paragraphs. A body paragraph is not just a reason plus a fact. It is a reason, developed with evidence, and connected back to the claim through YOUR explanation."

Write the ICE pattern on chart paper:

```
I — INTRODUCE the evidence: Tell the reader what's coming.
    "According to..."  "Research from ___ shows..."  "A study conducted by..."

C — CITE the evidence: Present the specific fact, statistic, or example.
    "Students who participated in cooking classes scored 12% higher on math assessments..."

E — EXPLAIN the connection: Tell the reader WHY this evidence supports the claim.
    "This demonstrates that..."  "This is significant because..."  "This proves that..."
```

Say: "The most common mistake writers make is skipping the E — the explanation. They drop in a piece of evidence and move on, leaving the reader to figure out how it connects. In sixth grade, YOU do the thinking for the reader. You explain the connection."

### Worked Examples

Display the following model body paragraph:

> One important reason middle schools should offer cooking classes is that cooking teaches essential math skills in a hands-on context. According to a report by the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics, students who practice measurement and fractions through real-world activities — such as doubling a recipe or converting tablespoons to cups — retain those skills 30% longer than students who learn them only through textbook problems. This is significant because it shows that cooking is not a distraction from academics but rather a powerful way to reinforce them. When a student halves a recipe and must calculate that three-fourths of a cup becomes three-eighths, they are not just cooking — they are doing mathematics. Schools that add cooking to the curriculum are not taking time away from learning; they are creating a new pathway into it.

Annotate together:
- **Topic sentence (Reason):** "One important reason middle schools should offer cooking classes is that cooking teaches essential math skills in a hands-on context."
- **Introduce:** "According to a report by the National Council of Teachers of Mathematics..."
- **Cite:** "...students who practice measurement and fractions through real-world activities retain those skills 30% longer..."
- **Explain:** "This is significant because it shows that cooking is not a distraction from academics but rather a powerful way to reinforce them."
- **Further elaboration:** The rest of the paragraph deepens the explanation with a specific example and a strong closing sentence.

Say: "Notice how the paragraph does not just state a fact and move on. It introduces the evidence, presents it, explains it, gives a concrete example, and then wraps up by connecting back to the claim. This is what makes sixth-grade argumentative writing different from fifth-grade opinion writing."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice organizing reasons and writing a body paragraph together using the ICE pattern.

**Activity: Evidence Sorting and Paragraph Building**

Present the following claim and reason:

**Claim:** "Schools should replace one hour of traditional homework each week with outdoor learning activities."

**Reason to develop:** "Outdoor learning reduces student stress and improves mental health."

Provide the following four pieces of evidence on separate cards or strips (the student must choose the two most relevant):

- A. "A 2019 study published in the journal Frontiers in Psychology found that students who spent 30 minutes outdoors during the school day reported 28% lower stress levels than students who remained indoors."
- B. "Outdoor activities like hiking and bird-watching are popular weekend hobbies for many families."
- C. "The American Psychological Association reports that time in nature reduces cortisol levels — the body's primary stress hormone — in children and adults alike."
- D. "Some schools have outdoor classrooms that were originally built for science classes."

Say: "Not all evidence is equally relevant. Which two pieces of evidence most directly support the reason that outdoor learning reduces stress?"

Guide the student to select A and C (specific, relevant, from credible sources). Discuss why B and D are not strong evidence for this particular reason — B is about personal hobbies rather than school-based learning, and D describes a facility but does not address stress reduction.

Now write a body paragraph together using the ICE pattern:

1. **Topic sentence:** Write a sentence stating the reason.
2. **Introduce and Cite (Evidence 1):** Use evidence A with an introduction.
3. **Explain:** Write a sentence explaining why this matters.
4. **Introduce and Cite (Evidence 2):** Use evidence C.
5. **Explain and connect back to claim:** Write two sentences tying it all together.

Sample collaborative paragraph:

> A compelling reason to replace homework with outdoor learning is that spending time outside significantly reduces student stress. A 2019 study published in Frontiers in Psychology found that students who spent just 30 minutes outdoors during the school day reported 28% lower stress levels than students who stayed inside. This finding is important because it suggests that even a small amount of outdoor time can make a meaningful difference in how students feel. Supporting this, the American Psychological Association reports that time in nature reduces cortisol — the body's main stress hormone — in both children and adults. Taken together, this evidence demonstrates that outdoor learning is not just a pleasant break from routine; it is a scientifically supported strategy for improving student well-being. Schools that replace an hour of homework with outdoor activities are investing in their students' mental health.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently organize their reasons and write one complete body paragraph using the ICE pattern.

**Task:** Return to the claim and Claim-Reason-Evidence chart you created in Lesson 1. Complete the following:

1. **Organize your reasons:** Decide on the order (strongest-last, problem-solution, or building complexity). Number your reasons 1, 2, 3 in the order you will present them. Write one sentence explaining your organizational choice.
2. **Write one body paragraph:** Choose your strongest reason and write a complete body paragraph using the ICE pattern. Your paragraph must include a topic sentence, at least one piece of evidence with introduction and citation, and at least two sentences of explanation connecting the evidence to the claim.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Organize reasons and write one body paragraph as described above.
**Stretch:** Write two body paragraphs (for two different reasons), each following the ICE pattern.
**Challenge:** Write two body paragraphs AND add a sentence at the end of the second paragraph that creates a transition to the next reason (e.g., "Beyond the academic benefits, there is an even more compelling reason to consider...").

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — W.5.1c) Rewrite the following sentence using a transitional clause instead of a simple transitional word: "Also, exercise is good for the brain." (Expected: "In addition to its physical benefits, exercise has been shown to improve brain function and memory." or similar clause-level transition.)
6. (Grade 5 review — W.5.2d) Replace the vague words in this sentence with precise language: "The thing about the stuff is that it does a lot of good things for people." (Expected: Answers will vary. Example: "The benefit of daily physical activity is that it strengthens the cardiovascular system, improves mood, and reduces the risk of chronic disease.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following body paragraph and identify the three parts of the ICE pattern by labeling them I (Introduce), C (Cite), and E (Explain):

"Research from leading health organizations supports the claim that schools should serve healthier lunches. [___] The World Health Organization reports that [___] children who eat balanced meals with fruits, vegetables, and whole grains perform 20% better on tests of memory and attention than children who eat processed, high-sugar meals. [___] This evidence makes it clear that what students eat at school directly affects their ability to learn, which means that improving cafeteria food is not just a health decision — it is an academic one."

**Expected Answers:**
- I (Introduce): "Research from leading health organizations supports the claim that schools should serve healthier lunches."
- C (Cite): "The World Health Organization reports that children who eat balanced meals with fruits, vegetables, and whole grains perform 20% better on tests of memory and attention than children who eat processed, high-sugar meals."
- E (Explain): "This evidence makes it clear that what students eat at school directly affects their ability to learn, which means that improving cafeteria food is not just a health decision — it is an academic one."

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies all three parts of the ICE pattern.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model body paragraph from direct instruction and re-annotate together, color-coding I, C, and E
- Provide sentence starters for each part: "According to..." (I), state the fact (C), "This is important because..." (E)
- Have the student practice the ICE pattern orally before writing: say the introduction, state the evidence, then explain the connection aloud

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3, where they will write a complete argument with introduction, body paragraphs, and conclusion
- Encourage the student to draft their remaining body paragraphs using the ICE pattern before the next session

## Extension (Optional)
1. **ICE in the Wild:** Find a paragraph from a news article, textbook, or editorial that uses the ICE pattern (or something like it). Highlight the introduce, cite, and explain portions. If the explanation is missing, write one.
2. **Reason Ranking Debate:** With a partner, each person ranks the same three reasons for an argument in a different order. Defend your ranking — why is YOUR order the most persuasive?
3. **Evidence Strength Scale:** Create a scale from 1 (weakest) to 5 (strongest) for different types of evidence: personal anecdote, common knowledge, expert opinion, statistical data, research study. Where does each type fall, and why?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The ICE pattern is a foundational skill that will carry students through middle school and high school writing. Spend extra time on this if needed — it is worth the investment.
- Students often write the "I" and "C" but skip the "E." When conferencing, always ask: "Where is your explanation? How does this evidence prove your point?" Make this question a refrain throughout the unit.
- The evidence sorting activity is designed to build the critical skill of evaluating relevance. If the student chooses weaker evidence, do not simply correct them. Ask: "How directly does this fact support the reason? Could someone read this evidence and still not be convinced? What would be more convincing?"
- Some students may want to use all four pieces of evidence. Teach selectivity: "Good writers choose the BEST evidence, not ALL the evidence. Two strong pieces are better than four weak ones."

**Sample organized reasons (Topic: junk food ban from Lesson 1):**
1. High-sugar diets reduce concentration (sets up the problem)
2. Sugary snacks contribute to childhood obesity (deepens the problem)
3. Healthier food options improve academic performance (provides the solution — strongest last)

**Organizational rationale:** "I chose to put my reasons in problem-solution order, starting with the immediate effect on concentration, expanding to the broader health issue of obesity, and ending with the positive solution of healthier food improving grades. This builds toward the strongest, most hopeful reason."

**Sample body paragraph:**
> Perhaps the most compelling reason to ban junk food from schools is that healthier food options have been shown to improve academic performance. A study published in the Journal of School Health found that schools that replaced vending machine snacks with fruits, nuts, and whole-grain options saw a 7% increase in average test scores over two years. This is significant because it demonstrates a direct link between what students eat at school and how well they perform academically. When students fuel their bodies with nutritious food rather than candy bars and chips, their brains function more effectively — they concentrate longer, retain information better, and participate more actively in class. Banning junk food is not about restricting student choice; it is about giving every student the best possible chance to succeed.
