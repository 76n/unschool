# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 10

## Topic
Writing Workshop: Revision and Publishing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply a systematic revision process to strengthen a piece of writing (content, organization, style)
- Distinguish between revision (improving content and craft) and editing (correcting conventions)
- Use a Revision Checklist to evaluate and improve their own writing
- Select and prepare a polished piece for their writing portfolio

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-9: Completed drafts of at least one argumentative essay, one informative text, and one narrative
- W.5.4: Experience producing writing appropriate to task, purpose, and audience
- Familiarity with basic editing conventions (spelling, punctuation, capitalization, grammar)
- Understanding of the writing process (planning, drafting, revising, editing, publishing)

## Materials Needed
- All writing drafts from the unit (argument, informative, narrative, text-based evidence response)
- Revision Checklist (described below)
- Editing Checklist (described below)
- Colored pencils or pens (two colors: one for revision marks, one for editing marks)
- Clean lined paper for final drafts (or access to a computer for typing)
- Writing portfolio folders

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Establish the value of revision and distinguish it from editing.

**Activity: Revision vs. Editing Sort**

Display the following eight actions on the board. The student sorts each one into REVISION or EDITING:

1. Adding a stronger piece of evidence to a body paragraph — REVISION
2. Fixing a misspelled word — EDITING
3. Reordering the reasons in your argument for greater impact — REVISION
4. Adding a comma before a conjunction in a compound sentence — EDITING
5. Replacing a vague word ("stuff") with a precise term ("nutrient-rich castings") — REVISION
6. Capitalizing a proper noun — EDITING
7. Cutting a sentence that does not support the main idea — REVISION
8. Adding a transition between two paragraphs — REVISION

After sorting, say: "Notice that revision is about making your writing better — stronger claims, clearer organization, more vivid details, more precise language. Editing is about making your writing correct — fixing errors in spelling, punctuation, and grammar. We always revise first, then edit. There is no point in perfecting the grammar of a sentence you might cut during revision."

Say: "Today is your Writing Workshop. You are going to choose your best piece from this unit, revise it to make it the strongest it can be, edit it for correctness, and prepare it for your writing portfolio. Professional writers revise multiple times. Today you get to be a professional writer."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach a systematic revision process using the ARMS and CUPS strategies, and introduce the Revision Checklist.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Revision:** The process of improving the content, organization, and style of a piece of writing
- **Editing:** The process of correcting errors in grammar, spelling, punctuation, and capitalization
- **ARMS:** A revision acronym: Add, Remove, Move, Substitute
- **CUPS:** An editing acronym: Capitalization, Usage, Punctuation, Spelling
- **Publishing:** Preparing a final, polished version of a piece of writing for an audience

### Instruction Notes

Say: "We are going to use two strategies today. ARMS for revision, CUPS for editing."

Write on chart paper:

**ARMS — Revision Strategy:**
- **A = Add:** What is missing? Do I need more evidence, more details, a stronger transition, a better hook?
- **R = Remove:** What does not belong? Is there a sentence that goes off-topic, a detail that clutters the story, or a reason that does not really support the claim?
- **M = Move:** Is everything in the right place? Should I reorder my reasons? Should the strongest detail come at the end? Does my conclusion make more sense earlier?
- **S = Substitute:** What could be better? Can I replace a vague word with a precise one? Can I swap a weak transition for a stronger one? Can I replace "telling" with "showing" in my narrative?

**CUPS — Editing Strategy:**
- **C = Capitalization:** Are all proper nouns and sentence beginnings capitalized?
- **U = Usage:** Do subjects and verbs agree? Are pronouns used correctly? Are verb tenses consistent?
- **P = Punctuation:** Are commas, periods, quotation marks, and other punctuation used correctly?
- **S = Spelling:** Are all words spelled correctly? (Read backward — word by word — to catch spelling errors your eyes skip over when reading forward.)

### Worked Examples

Say: "Let me model the revision process with a paragraph from an argumentative essay."

Display the following draft paragraph:

> "Another reason schools should have gardens is because gardens are good. Students can learn stuff in gardens. It is also fun. Some schools have gardens and the students like them a lot."

Say: "Let's apply ARMS."

**A (Add):** "This paragraph has no evidence, no specific details, and no explanation. Let's add a statistic and a concrete example."

**R (Remove):** "'It is also fun' does not support the claim with evidence — it is a vague opinion. Let's cut it."

**M (Move):** "The structure is fine — we just need content."

**S (Substitute):** "'Gardens are good' is vague — substitute with specific benefits. 'Stuff' should become specific skills. 'Like them a lot' should become measurable outcomes."

Draft the revised version together:

> "Beyond the physical health benefits, school gardens provide a powerful hands-on learning environment that deepens understanding across subjects. A study by the Royal Horticultural Society found that students who participated in regular garden-based learning scored 15% higher on science assessments because they could observe concepts like photosynthesis, water cycles, and ecosystems in real time rather than only reading about them in textbooks. At Lincoln Middle School in Portland, Oregon, sixth graders who maintained a school garden applied fractions and measurement skills weekly — calculating planting distances, measuring growth rates, and converting units — reinforcing math skills in a context that felt purposeful rather than abstract. School gardens do not replace classroom instruction; they extend it into the living world."

Say: "The revision transformed a four-sentence paragraph with no evidence into a rich, developed paragraph with specific evidence, named sources, and analysis. This is what revision does — it is not about fixing commas. It is about making your writing substantive."

Now model a quick editing pass. Read the revised paragraph aloud, checking CUPS:
- Capitalization: Royal Horticultural Society (correct), Lincoln Middle School (correct)
- Usage: Subject-verb agreement (check each sentence)
- Punctuation: Dashes, commas, periods (check each)
- Spelling: Read each word

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Guide the student through revising a portion of their own writing using the Revision Checklist.

**Activity: Guided Revision Conference**

Say: "Choose the piece you are most proud of from this unit — your argument, your informative text, or your narrative. We are going to revise it together."

Introduce the Revision Checklist:

```
REVISION CHECKLIST — Grade 6 Writing

FOR ALL WRITING:
□ My opening hooks the reader and makes them want to continue
□ My writing has a clear structure (introduction, body, conclusion)
□ Each paragraph focuses on one main idea
□ I used transitions between paragraphs (not just "also" or "next")
□ My conclusion synthesizes rather than just repeating the introduction
□ I replaced vague words with precise, specific language
□ I varied my sentence lengths and structures
□ Every sentence earns its place — nothing is off-topic or redundant

FOR ARGUMENTATIVE WRITING (add these):
□ My claim is debatable and clearly stated
□ Each reason is supported by specific evidence using the ICE pattern
□ My evidence comes from credible sources (named or described)
□ My analysis explains HOW the evidence supports the claim

FOR INFORMATIVE WRITING (add these):
□ My focus statement previews the categories I will cover
□ I used domain-specific vocabulary and defined terms in context
□ I maintained an objective tone (no opinions or personal reactions)
□ I used development strategies (fact + elaboration, comparison, cause-effect)

FOR NARRATIVE WRITING (add these):
□ I used "show, don't tell" — emotions are conveyed through actions and details
□ I expanded at least one important moment with sensory details
□ I compressed less important moments
□ My dialogue sounds natural and reveals character or advances the plot
□ My ending provides resolution and meaning, not just a stop
```

Work through the checklist with the student, reading their piece aloud and pausing at each checkpoint.

**Revision protocol:**
1. Read the entire piece aloud first. Circle any place where you stumble, pause unnaturally, or lose the flow.
2. Go through the checklist item by item. For each item marked "no," use ARMS to decide what to Add, Remove, Move, or Substitute.
3. Make revision marks using one color. (Arrows for moves, carets for additions, strikethroughs for removals, substitutions written above.)
4. After revision is complete, switch to the second color and do an editing pass using CUPS.

Guide the student through revising at least two specific elements:
- One content revision (adding evidence, strengthening a claim, expanding a moment)
- One style revision (improving a transition, replacing vague language, varying sentence structure)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently revise, edit, and prepare a final draft of their selected piece.

**Task:** Complete the revision and editing of the piece you chose during guided practice. Then prepare a clean final draft (rewrite on clean paper or type).

Your final draft should reflect:
1. All revisions from the guided practice conference
2. A complete ARMS revision pass (at least two additions, one removal or substitution)
3. A complete CUPS editing pass
4. A title and your name at the top

After completing your final draft, write a brief (3-5 sentence) Author's Reflection answering:
- Which writing skill from this unit was hardest for you? How did you improve?
- What is the strongest part of this piece? What would you revise further if you had more time?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Revise and edit one piece, prepare a clean final draft, and write the Author's Reflection.
**Stretch:** Complete the on-level task and also write a brief revision log documenting three specific changes you made and why each change improved the piece.
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task and prepare a second piece for the portfolio (a different writing mode), noting how the revision strategies differed between the two modes.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — W.5.4) A teacher assigns the following: "Write a letter to the mayor about improving parks in your neighborhood." Identify the task, purpose, and audience. Then write one sentence explaining how the audience should affect the writer's tone. (Expected: Task: formal letter; Purpose: persuade/inform; Audience: the mayor. Because the audience is a government official, the tone should be respectful, formal, and evidence-based rather than casual or emotional.)
6. (Grade 5 review — L.5.2a) Add correct punctuation to this sentence: "The students who completed the project early received three rewards extra credit a homework pass and a free reading period." (Expected: "The students who completed the project early received three rewards: extra credit, a homework pass, and a free reading period." — colon before the list, commas between items.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following draft sentence and revise it using ARMS. Write the improved version.

Draft: "The experiment was interesting and the results were good and the scientist was happy."

**Expected Answers:**
Answers will vary. The revised sentence should demonstrate at least two ARMS moves. Sample:

- **Substitution + Addition:** "The experiment yielded unexpected results: the bacterial colony doubled in size within 24 hours, far exceeding the researcher's initial predictions and suggesting that the new growth medium was significantly more effective than the standard formula."

Key improvements: replaced vague words ("interesting," "good," "happy") with specific, precise language; added concrete details; combined the three short clauses into a more sophisticated sentence structure.

**Mastery = The revised sentence replaces at least two vague words with precise language AND improves the sentence structure.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Identify the vague words together ("interesting," "good," "happy") and brainstorm precise replacements
- Ask: "What was the experiment about? What exactly were the results? How specifically did the scientist respond?" Turn the vague sentence into three or four specific sentences first, then combine
- Provide a template: "The experiment revealed [specific finding], which [significance]"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student has completed the instructional portion of the unit and is prepared for the Unit Assessment
- Encourage the student to read their polished piece aloud to a family member or friend and to continue adding to their writing portfolio

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Peer Review Letter:** Write a letter to an imaginary peer whose argumentative essay you have "reviewed." Provide three specific pieces of feedback: one strength, one area for revision (using ARMS language), and one editing note.
2. **Before-and-After Showcase:** Select one paragraph from your first draft and place it side by side with the revised version. Write a brief explanation of each change you made and why it improved the writing.
3. **Writing Goals:** Based on your experience in this unit, set three specific writing goals for yourself (e.g., "I will practice the ICE pattern in every argumentative paragraph," "I will use sensory details from at least four senses in my narratives," "I will eliminate vague language like 'stuff' and 'things' from all my writing"). Write each goal and one action step you will take to achieve it.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The guided revision conference is the most valuable part of this lesson. Prioritize it over independent practice if time is tight. A student who experiences one thorough, facilitated revision will internalize the process far more effectively than a student who revises independently without support.
- Some students may resist revision because they are emotionally attached to their first draft or because revision feels like criticism. Frame revision positively: "Revision is not about what is wrong with your writing. It is about making something good even better. Every published author revises — usually many times."
- The Author's Reflection is a metacognitive exercise that helps students consolidate their learning. Even a brief reflection forces students to articulate what they have learned and where they need to grow. Take the reflections seriously — they provide valuable assessment data.
- If a student has not completed drafts for all three writing modes, use this lesson to complete the most important missing piece rather than attempting to revise something they do not have. A complete first draft is more valuable than a perfectly revised fragment.
- Consider celebrating the end of the unit: invite students to read their polished pieces aloud, display final drafts on a bulletin board, or create a class anthology. Making writing public validates the effort students have invested.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
**Sample Author's Reflection:**
> The hardest writing skill for me in this unit was writing analysis after using evidence. In my argumentative essay, I kept doing what my teacher calls "quote and run" — I would put in a quotation and then just move to the next reason without explaining what the evidence meant. During revision, I went back and added at least two sentences of analysis after every quotation, asking myself "So what? Why does this matter?" The strongest part of my essay is my conclusion, where I used the synthesis + call to action strategy to end with a sentence that I think would make the reader want to do something. If I had more time, I would revise my introduction — my hook is a question, but I think a surprising statistic might be more effective for this topic.

**Sample revision log (stretch):**
1. **Change:** Added the statistic "students who slept fewer than eight hours per night earned grades one full letter grade lower" to body paragraph 2. **Why:** My original paragraph only had a general claim about sleep affecting grades. The specific statistic from a named university makes the evidence concrete and credible.
2. **Change:** Replaced "Also, another reason is..." with "Beyond the academic costs of sleep deprivation, there is an equally pressing concern:" as my transition between body paragraphs 2 and 3. **Why:** The original transition was a simple word ("Also") that did not connect the ideas. The new transition looks back at the previous paragraph and forward to the next one.
3. **Change:** Cut the sentence "I really believe this is important" from my conclusion. **Why:** In argumentative writing, we do not use "I believe" — the evidence speaks for itself. The sentence weakened the formal tone of the conclusion.
