# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Introduction to Argumentative Writing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between an opinion and a formal argument with a claim
- Write a clear, debatable claim statement on an age-appropriate topic
- Identify claims, reasons, and evidence in a model argumentative text
- Understand the transition from Grade 5 opinion writing to Grade 6 argumentative writing

## Prerequisites
- W.5.1: Experience writing opinion pieces with reasons supported by facts, details, and examples
- W.5.1a: Ability to introduce a topic, state an opinion, and create an organizational structure
- Familiarity with the difference between fact and opinion from prior reading units
- Basic paragraph structure (topic sentence, supporting details, concluding sentence)

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Copy of the model argumentative text (provided below)
- Graphic organizer: Claim-Reason-Evidence chart (described below)
- Highlighters (three colors)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about opinion writing from Grade 5 and introduce the concept of a claim.

**Activity: Opinion or Argument?**

Display the following two statements on the board:

1. "I think schools should have longer lunch periods because students need more time to eat and relax."
2. "Schools should extend lunch periods to at least 40 minutes because research from the American Academy of Pediatrics shows that students who have adequate time to eat consume 25% more fruits and vegetables, leading to improved afternoon concentration and fewer behavioral incidents."

Say: "Both of these take a position on the same topic. But one sounds like something you might write in fifth grade, and one sounds like something you would write in sixth grade. Turn to a partner and discuss: What is different about statement 2?"

After 90 seconds of partner discussion, take two or three responses. Guide students toward noticing that statement 2 has specific evidence, names a source, explains the connection between the evidence and the claim, and uses more formal language.

Say: "In fifth grade, you wrote opinion pieces. This year, we are leveling up to argumentative writing. The biggest difference? In an argument, you make a claim and back it up with specific, relevant evidence. Today we are going to learn exactly what that means."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the structure of a claim and introduce the core components of an argument: claim, reasons, and evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Claim:** A debatable statement that takes a clear position on an issue and can be supported with evidence
- **Reason:** A logical point that explains WHY the claim is valid
- **Evidence:** Specific facts, statistics, expert opinions, or examples that support each reason
- **Counterclaim:** The opposing position; what someone who disagrees might argue
- **Relevant:** Directly connected to and supportive of the point being made

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Let me show you the difference between an opinion and a claim."

Write on the board:

| Opinion (Grade 5) | Claim (Grade 6) |
|---|---|
| States what you believe | States a debatable position |
| Supported by reasons | Supported by reasons AND evidence |
| Uses "I think" or "I believe" | Uses confident, formal language |
| Personal connection is OK | Evidence-based reasoning required |

Say: "A claim is like an opinion that has grown up. It does not say 'I think' or 'I believe.' It states a position confidently and then proves it with evidence. Watch me write a claim."

Model: "The topic is whether sixth graders should be allowed to choose their own reading books. Here is an opinion: 'I think students should choose their own books because reading is more fun that way.' Here is a claim: 'Sixth-grade students should be given the freedom to select their own independent reading books because student-selected reading increases reading volume, builds intrinsic motivation, and leads to higher comprehension scores.'"

Say: "Notice the claim is specific, formal, and previews the three reasons I will develop in my argument."

### Worked Examples

Read the following model argumentative text aloud. Display it so the student can follow along.

> **Should Middle Schools Start Later in the Morning?**
>
> Every weekday morning, millions of middle school students drag themselves out of bed before 7:00 a.m., struggling through the early hours of school in a fog of exhaustion. This is not laziness — it is biology. Middle schools should shift their start times to no earlier than 8:30 a.m. because adolescent sleep patterns require later wake times, because sleep deprivation damages academic performance, and because later start times have already proven successful in schools across the country.
>
> The primary reason middle schools should start later is that adolescent biology demands it. During puberty, the brain's internal clock shifts forward by one to two hours, making it physically difficult for teenagers to fall asleep before 11:00 p.m. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that children ages 6 to 12 get 9 to 12 hours of sleep per night. When school starts at 7:30 a.m. and the bus arrives at 6:45, students must wake by 6:00 — which means they would need to fall asleep by 9:00 p.m., a time when their brains are not yet ready for sleep. The math simply does not work.
>
> Furthermore, sleep deprivation directly harms academic performance. A study conducted by researchers at the University of Minnesota found that students who slept fewer than eight hours per night earned grades that were, on average, one full letter grade lower than students who slept nine or more hours. Sleep-deprived students also showed reduced ability to concentrate, slower reaction times, and higher rates of absenteeism. When students cannot focus in class, even the best teaching cannot reach them.
>
> Finally, schools that have already adopted later start times report measurable improvements. When the Seattle Public Schools shifted their start time from 7:50 a.m. to 8:45 a.m. in 2016, researchers at the University of Washington documented a 4.5% increase in student grades and a significant reduction in tardiness and absences. These results demonstrate that later start times are not just a nice idea — they are a proven solution.
>
> The evidence is clear: middle school start times should be pushed to at least 8:30 a.m. Students deserve a schedule that works with their biology, not against it. When schools make this change, students sleep more, learn better, and show up ready to succeed.

Say: "Let's annotate this together." Use three highlighter colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** Highlight the claim (last sentence of the first paragraph).
- **Color 2 (green):** Highlight the three reasons (first or second sentence of each body paragraph).
- **Color 3 (blue):** Highlight the specific evidence in each body paragraph (statistics, study results, expert recommendations).

Ask: "What makes this different from an opinion piece you might have written in fifth grade?" Guide students to notice: named sources, specific statistics, formal tone, logical structure, and the explanation of HOW the evidence connects to the claim.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively develop a claim and begin planning an argument using the Claim-Reason-Evidence chart.

**Activity: Building a Claim Together**

Draw the following graphic organizer on chart paper:

```
CLAIM: _______________________________________________

| Reason | Evidence | How It Supports the Claim |
|--------|----------|---------------------------|
| 1.     |          |                           |
| 2.     |          |                           |
| 3.     |          |                           |
```

Say: "Our topic is: Should students be required to participate in community service to pass sixth grade? First, let's decide our position and write a claim."

Guide the student through these steps:

1. **Decide a position:** Take a stand — for or against required community service.
2. **Write the claim:** Use the formula: Topic + Position + Preview of Reasons. Example: "Sixth-grade students should be required to complete 20 hours of community service because it builds empathy, strengthens connections to the local community, and develops real-world skills that the classroom alone cannot teach."
3. **Fill in Reason 1:** "Builds empathy." Evidence: "Students who volunteer at food banks or nursing homes see the challenges others face firsthand, which research shows increases perspective-taking ability." How it supports: "This shows that community service develops the ability to understand others, which is a life skill that supports the claim."
4. **Fill in Reason 2:** Work with the student to generate a reason and find or create a plausible piece of evidence.
5. **Fill in Reason 3:** The student attempts this one with minimal assistance.

If the student struggles with generating evidence, prompt: "What specific example, fact, or expert opinion could prove this reason is true? Think about what you have read, experienced, or learned in other subjects."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write a claim and complete a Claim-Reason-Evidence chart.

Choose ONE of the following topics (or propose your own):

1. Should schools ban junk food from all vending machines and cafeterias?
2. Should all students be required to learn a second language starting in elementary school?
3. Should middle schools allow students to use personal laptops or tablets in class?

**Task:** Write a claim statement on your chosen topic. Then complete a Claim-Reason-Evidence chart with at least two reasons, one piece of evidence per reason, and an explanation of how each piece of evidence supports the claim.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write a claim and complete the chart with two reasons and evidence as described.
**Stretch:** Complete the on-level task, then write the introductory paragraph of your argument, including a hook, context, and your claim statement.
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task, then write a sentence identifying the counterclaim (what someone who disagrees would argue) and explain why your evidence is stronger.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — W.5.1) Revise the following opinion statement to make it stronger by adding a preview of reasons: "I think homework should be optional." (Expected: "Homework should be optional for middle school students because mandatory homework does not improve test scores, increases student stress, and reduces time for physical activity and family.")
6. (Grade 5 review — L.5.3a) Combine these two sentences into one using a transitional clause: "Many students dislike school uniforms. Uniforms reduce morning decision-making and save families money." (Expected: "Although many students dislike school uniforms, uniforms reduce morning decision-making and save families money.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following statement and answer the two questions:

"Video games should be considered a sport because they require skill, strategy, and practice."

1. Is this statement a claim? Why or why not?
2. Name one piece of evidence that could support this claim.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Yes, this is a claim because it takes a debatable position (video games should be considered a sport) and previews reasons (skill, strategy, practice). People could reasonably disagree with this statement.
2. Answers will vary. Examples: "Professional esports athletes practice 8-10 hours a day, similar to traditional athletes" or "A study found that competitive gamers have reaction times comparable to fighter pilots."

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the statement as a claim with a valid explanation AND provides one relevant piece of evidence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Revisit the comparison chart (opinion vs. claim) and have the student sort five sample statements into "opinion" and "claim" categories
- Provide the sentence frame: "[Topic] should/should not [position] because [reason 1], [reason 2], and [reason 3]"
- Re-read the model text together, pausing after each paragraph to identify the reason and evidence

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 2, where they will learn to organize reasons strategically and develop evidence with deeper explanation
- Encourage the student to begin drafting body paragraphs for the argument they planned today

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Claim Scavenger Hunt:** Find an editorial or opinion column in a newspaper, magazine, or kid-friendly news site. Identify the claim, reasons, and evidence. Evaluate: Is this an opinion piece or a formal argument? What makes it one or the other?
2. **Counterclaim Practice:** For the claim you wrote during independent practice, write the strongest possible counterclaim. Then write one sentence explaining why your position is still stronger.
3. **Real-World Arguments:** Listen to or watch a short persuasive speech (e.g., a student council campaign speech, a TED-Ed talk). Note the claim and the evidence used. Did the speaker rely on facts, personal stories, or emotional appeals? Which was most convincing and why?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The transition from "opinion" to "argument" is one of the most important shifts in Grade 6 ELA. Students may resist dropping "I think" and "I believe" from their writing. Gently redirect by saying, "In sixth grade, your evidence speaks for itself. You do not need to tell the reader it is your opinion — the strength of your argument makes that clear."
- Some students may struggle to generate evidence without access to research sources. Remind them that evidence can include logical reasoning, commonly known facts, real-world examples, and hypothetical scenarios supported by logic. Formal source citation is not required at this stage, but students should practice attributing information ("According to...", "Research shows...").
- The model text uses specific statistics and named sources. Point out that this is the standard students are working toward. In the early lessons, accept plausible evidence and logical reasoning; by Lesson 3, push for greater specificity.
- If a student chooses a topic they feel strongly about but struggles to find evidence, help them brainstorm by asking: "What would a scientist, a teacher, or a parent say about this topic? What facts do you already know?"
