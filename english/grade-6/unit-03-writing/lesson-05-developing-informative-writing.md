# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Developing Informative Writing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write informative body paragraphs that develop a category with facts, definitions, concrete details, and examples
- Use domain-specific vocabulary and define terms within the text for the reader
- Apply formatting strategies (definitions in context, comparisons, cause-effect) to make complex information accessible
- Distinguish between surface-level and in-depth development of a topic

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 4: Ability to select and narrow a topic, create a Topic Web, and write an informative introduction with a focus statement
- W.5.2b: Experience developing informative writing with facts, definitions, concrete details, and quotations
- W.5.2d: Familiarity with precise language and domain-specific vocabulary
- Understanding of paragraph structure with topic sentences and supporting details

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Topic Web and introduction from Lesson 4 (student's own)
- Model body paragraphs (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Illustrate the difference between thin (underdeveloped) and thick (well-developed) informative writing.

**Activity: Thin or Thick?**

Display the following two paragraphs side by side:

**Paragraph A (Thin):**
> "Tornadoes are really strong. They can be very destructive. There are different levels of tornadoes. Some are worse than others. Scientists measure them on a scale."

**Paragraph B (Thick):**
> "Scientists classify tornadoes using the Enhanced Fujita Scale, which ranges from EF0 to EF5 based on estimated wind speeds and the severity of damage they cause. An EF0 tornado, with wind speeds between 65 and 85 miles per hour, may snap tree branches and damage gutters, while an EF5 tornado, the most catastrophic category, produces winds exceeding 200 miles per hour — powerful enough to sweep well-built homes completely off their foundations. Since the scale was updated in 2007, only seven EF5 tornadoes have been recorded in the United States, making them exceptionally rare but devastatingly powerful when they do occur."

Say: "Both paragraphs are about tornado classification. But Paragraph A is thin — it tells us almost nothing specific. Paragraph B is thick — it is packed with facts, precise vocabulary, specific numbers, and comparisons that help the reader truly understand the topic. Today we are going to learn how to write thick, fully developed informative paragraphs."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach strategies for developing informative body paragraphs with depth and precision.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Concrete detail:** A specific, factual piece of information (a number, a measurement, a name, a place) as opposed to a vague generality
- **Definition in context:** Explaining what a specialized term means within the flow of the sentence, rather than interrupting with a dictionary definition
- **Comparison/contrast:** A technique for making unfamiliar information understandable by relating it to something the reader already knows
- **Elaboration:** The sentences that explain, connect, or extend a fact — telling the reader WHY the information matters or HOW it connects to the topic

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In fifth grade, you learned to develop informative writing with facts and details. In sixth grade, we are pushing deeper. A sixth-grade informative paragraph does not just state facts — it examines the topic. That means you explain how the facts connect, you define unfamiliar terms, and you help the reader understand why the information matters."

Write on chart paper:

**Four Development Strategies:**

1. **Fact + Elaboration:** State a fact, then explain its significance.
   - Fact: "The Great Wall of China stretches over 13,000 miles."
   - Elaboration: "To put that in perspective, this is roughly the distance from New York City to Sydney, Australia — a length so vast that it took multiple dynasties and over 2,000 years to complete."

2. **Definition in Context:** Define a term naturally within your sentence.
   - "The process of photosynthesis — in which plants convert sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide into glucose and oxygen — is the foundation of nearly all food chains on Earth."

3. **Comparison/Contrast:** Relate the unfamiliar to the familiar.
   - "A single colony of honeybees can contain up to 60,000 individuals, roughly the population of a small city, all working together in a system more organized than most human governments."

4. **Cause and Effect:** Show how one fact leads to or results from another.
   - "Because the Arctic reflects a large portion of the sun's energy back into space, the melting of Arctic ice creates a feedback loop: less ice means more heat is absorbed by the dark ocean water, which in turn causes more ice to melt."

Say: "Strong informative writers use all four of these strategies, sometimes within the same paragraph. Let me show you what that looks like."

### Worked Examples

Display the following model body paragraph (from an informative text about honeybee communication):

> One of the most remarkable behaviors in the animal kingdom is the honeybee waggle dance, a complex form of communication that allows bees to share precise information about the location of food sources. When a forager bee discovers a patch of flowers rich in nectar, it returns to the hive and performs a figure-eight dance on the surface of the honeycomb. The direction of the straight run in the middle of the figure-eight indicates the direction of the food source relative to the sun, while the duration of the waggle — the rapid side-to-side movement during the straight run — communicates the distance. A waggle lasting one second, for example, indicates that the food source is approximately 1,000 meters away. This system is so precise that other bees can follow the instructions and fly directly to the correct location, even if it is several miles from the hive. The Austrian scientist Karl von Frisch first decoded the waggle dance in the 1940s, a discovery so groundbreaking that it earned him the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1973. His research revealed that honeybees possess a form of symbolic communication once believed to be unique to humans — a finding that continues to reshape our understanding of animal intelligence.

Annotate together using three colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** Facts and concrete details (figure-eight dance, one second = 1,000 meters, Karl von Frisch, Nobel Prize 1973)
- **Color 2 (green):** Definitions in context ("a complex form of communication that allows bees to share precise information," "the rapid side-to-side movement during the straight run")
- **Color 3 (blue):** Elaboration and significance ("This system is so precise that other bees can fly directly to the correct location," "a form of symbolic communication once believed to be unique to humans")

Say: "Notice how the paragraph moves from fact to definition to more facts to significance. Every sentence adds something new to the reader's understanding. There is no filler, no vague language, and no wasted words."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively write a well-developed informative body paragraph using the four development strategies.

**Activity: Developing a Body Paragraph Together**

Return to the tornado topic from Lesson 4's guided practice. Choose one category to develop (e.g., Category 1: Conditions needed for tornado formation).

Say: "Let's write a body paragraph together. I will draft the topic sentence, and then we will build it up using our four strategies."

**Step 1 — Topic sentence:**
"For a tornado to form, a specific set of atmospheric conditions must come together in exactly the right combination."

**Step 2 — Fact + Definition in Context:**
Ask: "What is the first condition? How can we explain it so the reader understands?"

Guide toward: "The most critical ingredient is wind shear — a difference in wind speed or direction at different altitudes. When warm, moist air near the ground flows in one direction while cooler, drier air above it moves in another, the atmosphere becomes unstable."

**Step 3 — Cause and Effect:**
"This instability causes the warm air to rise rapidly, creating powerful updrafts inside a thunderstorm. If the wind shear is strong enough, the updraft begins to rotate, forming a mesocyclone — a spinning column of air within the storm cloud."

**Step 4 — Fact + Elaboration:**
"Meteorologists estimate that roughly 30% of supercell thunderstorms produce mesocyclones, but only a fraction of those — perhaps one in five — ever produce a tornado that reaches the ground. This means that even when all the conditions align, a tornado is far from guaranteed, which is one reason they remain so difficult to predict."

**Step 5 — Review together:**
Read the complete paragraph aloud. Ask: "Which development strategies did we use?" (Definition in context, cause and effect, fact + elaboration, comparison of proportions.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write well-developed informative body paragraphs.

**Task:** Return to the Topic Web and introduction you created in Lesson 4. Write one fully developed body paragraph for ONE of your categories. Your paragraph must include:
- A clear topic sentence stating the category
- At least two specific facts or concrete details
- At least one domain-specific vocabulary term defined in context
- At least one sentence of elaboration that explains why the information matters or how it connects to the topic

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write one body paragraph as described above.
**Stretch:** Write two body paragraphs (for two different categories), each using at least two of the four development strategies.
**Challenge:** Write two body paragraphs and include a transition between them that connects the ideas logically (e.g., "While insulation protects Arctic animals from the cold, their survival also depends on behavioral adaptations that conserve energy in different ways.").

(Expected: "The water cycle is a continuous process that sustains all life on Earth, involving three main stages: evaporation, condensation, and precipitation.")
6. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1d) Correct the verb tense error in this sentence: "Yesterday, the scientist discovers a new species of beetle in the rainforest." (Expected: "Yesterday, the scientist discovered a new species of beetle in the rainforest.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following sentence from an informative paragraph and revise it to make it more developed. Add at least one concrete detail and one sentence of elaboration.

Original: "The Amazon Rainforest is very big and has a lot of different animals."

**Expected Answers:**
Answers will vary. Sample revision: "The Amazon Rainforest covers approximately 2.1 million square miles — an area larger than the entire European Union — and is home to an estimated 10% of all known species on Earth, including more than 1,300 species of birds and 3,000 species of fish. This extraordinary biodiversity makes the Amazon not just a forest but one of the planet's most important reservoirs of life."

**Mastery = The revised version includes at least one specific fact or number AND at least one sentence explaining the significance of the information.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the "Thin vs. Thick" comparison from the warm-up
- Ask prompting questions: "How big is the Amazon? How many species live there? Why does that matter?"
- Provide a sentence frame: "[Topic] [specific measurement or number], which means that [explanation of significance]."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 6, where they will assemble a complete informative text with introduction, developed body paragraphs, and conclusion
- Encourage the student to continue developing their remaining categories before the next session

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Thin-to-Thick Revision Challenge:** Take a paragraph from a younger student's informative text (or write a deliberately thin paragraph) and revise it using all four development strategies. Compare the before and after versions.
2. **Domain-Specific Vocabulary Glossary:** For your informative topic, create a mini-glossary of five domain-specific terms. For each term, write a definition in context — a sentence that uses the word and explains it naturally.
3. **Infographic Planning:** Redesign one of your informative paragraphs as an infographic. What information would you include? What visuals would help the reader understand? How does the format change what you emphasize?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The "Thin vs. Thick" framework is a useful metaphor throughout this unit. When conferencing, you can simply ask: "Is this sentence thin or thick? How can we thicken it?" Students quickly internalize this language.
- Students often struggle most with elaboration — they state facts but do not explain their significance. Model the thinking behind elaboration: "When I write a fact, I always ask myself: So what? Why does this matter? What does this mean for the reader?"
- Domain-specific vocabulary should be defined in context, not in a separate vocabulary list within the essay. Show students that skilled informative writers weave definitions seamlessly into their sentences using dashes, commas, or relative clauses.
- If a student is writing about a topic they know well, they may rely on general knowledge rather than specific facts. Push for precision: "You said 'really cold.' How cold exactly? You said 'a long time ago.' What year or century?"

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
**Sample body paragraph (Arctic animal adaptations — Insulation):**
> The most essential adaptation for surviving Arctic winters is insulation, and Arctic animals have evolved some of the most effective insulating systems in the animal kingdom. The Arctic fox, for example, possesses a double-layered fur coat: a dense underlayer of short, fine hairs that trap warm air close to the skin, and an outer layer of longer guard hairs that repel wind and moisture. This two-layer system is so efficient that Arctic foxes do not begin to shiver until the temperature drops below minus 70 degrees Celsius — a threshold far colder than any human could tolerate without advanced technology. Marine mammals like walruses and seals rely on a different strategy: blubber, a thick layer of fat beneath the skin that can measure up to six inches in some species. Blubber serves a dual purpose, functioning both as insulation against the frigid water and as an energy reserve during periods when food is scarce. These insulation adaptations demonstrate that Arctic survival is not about avoiding the cold — it is about being built for it.
