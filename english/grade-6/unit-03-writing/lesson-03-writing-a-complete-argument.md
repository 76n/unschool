# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 3

## Topic
Writing a Complete Argument

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write a complete argumentative essay with an introduction, body paragraphs, and conclusion
- Craft an introduction that hooks the reader, provides context, and states a clear claim
- Write a conclusion that reinforces the claim without merely restating the introduction
- Use transitions to connect ideas within and between paragraphs

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 1: Ability to write a debatable claim and identify claims, reasons, and evidence
- Lesson 2: Ability to organize reasons logically and write body paragraphs using the ICE pattern
- W.5.1d: Experience providing a concluding statement or section in opinion writing
- W.5.1c: Familiarity with transitional words, phrases, and clauses

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Claim-Reason-Evidence chart and body paragraphs from Lessons 1-2
- Model argumentative essay introduction and conclusion (provided below)
- Transition Reference Sheet (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the argumentative essay structure and focus attention on the introduction and conclusion — the two missing pieces.

**Activity: What's Missing?**

Display the following "skeleton" of an argumentative essay on the board:

```
INTRODUCTION: ???
BODY 1: [Reason + Evidence + Explanation]
BODY 2: [Reason + Evidence + Explanation]
BODY 3: [Reason + Evidence + Explanation]
CONCLUSION: ???
```

Say: "In Lessons 1 and 2, we built the body of our argument — the reasons, evidence, and explanations. But an essay without an introduction is like a movie that starts in the middle, and an essay without a conclusion is like a story that just... stops. Today we are going to learn how to write introductions that pull the reader in and conclusions that leave a lasting impression. By the end of today, you will have all the pieces you need to write a complete argument."

Ask: "Think about a time you started reading something and immediately wanted to keep going. What made you want to read more?" Take two or three responses. Guide the discussion toward the idea that strong openings create curiosity, surprise, or connection.

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the structure of an argumentative introduction and conclusion, including hook types and conclusion strategies.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Hook:** An opening sentence or two designed to capture the reader's attention and interest
- **Context:** Background information that helps the reader understand the topic and why it matters
- **Conclusion:** The final paragraph that reinforces the claim, synthesizes the argument, and leaves the reader with a final thought
- **Synthesis:** Combining the key points of the argument into a unified final message (not just repeating them one by one)
- **Call to action:** A conclusion strategy that urges the reader to do something specific

### Instruction Notes

Say: "An argumentative introduction has three jobs: hook the reader, provide context, and state the claim. Let me show you four types of hooks."

Write on chart paper:

**Hook Types for Argumentative Writing:**
1. **Surprising fact or statistic:** "Every year, American schools throw away 530,000 tons of uneaten cafeteria food."
2. **Thought-provoking question:** "What if the key to better grades was not more studying but more sleeping?"
3. **Vivid scenario:** "Picture a classroom where every student stares at a screen for six hours straight, never touching a book, a pencil, or a classmate's hand."
4. **Bold statement:** "The traditional school schedule was designed for a world that no longer exists."

Say: "After the hook, you provide context — one to three sentences that give background on the topic. Then you end the introduction with your claim. The claim is always the last sentence of the introduction because it sets up everything that follows."

Now teach conclusion strategies:

**Conclusion Strategies:**
1. **Synthesis + Call to Action:** Summarize the key argument, then urge the reader to do something. ("The evidence is clear... It is time for schools to...")
2. **Synthesis + Forward Look:** Summarize, then describe what the future could look like. ("If schools adopt this change, students will...")
3. **Synthesis + Thought-Provoking Question:** Summarize, then leave the reader thinking. ("The question is not whether we can afford to make this change, but whether we can afford not to.")

Say: "The one thing a conclusion should never do is simply copy the introduction. A conclusion should feel like the end of a journey — the reader has traveled through your evidence and arrived at a destination."

### Worked Examples

Display the following model introduction and conclusion (these bookend the school start time essay from Lesson 1):

**Model Introduction:**

> Every weekday morning, millions of middle school students drag themselves out of bed before 7:00 a.m., struggling through the early hours of school in a fog of exhaustion. This is not laziness — it is biology. As children enter adolescence, their internal clocks shift, making it physically difficult to fall asleep before 11:00 p.m. and wake before 8:00 a.m. Yet most middle schools start well before that, forcing students to choose between enough sleep and being on time. Middle schools should shift their start times to no earlier than 8:30 a.m. because adolescent sleep patterns require later wake times, because sleep deprivation damages academic performance, and because later start times have already proven successful in schools across the country.

Annotate: "Notice the hook (vivid scenario of exhausted students), the context (explanation of adolescent biology and the conflict with school schedules), and the claim (last sentence, with three reasons previewed)."

**Model Conclusion:**

> The evidence from sleep science, academic research, and real-world school experiments all points in the same direction: middle school students need — and deserve — a schedule that respects their biology. Shifting start times to 8:30 a.m. is not a luxury or a matter of convenience; it is a health-based decision supported by decades of data. Some may argue that later start times create logistical challenges for bus schedules and after-school activities, but these obstacles are solvable — and they are far less costly than the ongoing damage of sleep deprivation. It is time for school boards to stop asking students to fight their own biology and start building schedules that set them up to succeed.

Annotate: "Notice the synthesis (combines all three threads of evidence into one statement), the brief acknowledgment of a counterclaim ('some may argue...'), and the call to action (last sentence). This conclusion does not copy the introduction — it builds on it."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively write an introduction and conclusion for a shared argument.

**Activity: Building the Bookends Together**

Return to the community service claim from Lesson 1's guided practice (or use this one):

**Claim:** "Sixth-grade students should be required to complete 20 hours of community service because it builds empathy, strengthens connections to the local community, and develops real-world skills that the classroom alone cannot teach."

**Step 1 — Write the introduction together:**

Say: "First, let's choose a hook type. Which of the four would work best for this topic?"

Guide the student to select one. For example, a thought-provoking question: "What if the most important lessons you learned in sixth grade happened outside the classroom?"

Then add context together: "Across the country, schools are looking for ways to prepare students not just for tests but for life. Community service — volunteering at shelters, cleaning up parks, reading to younger children — gives students experiences that textbooks cannot replicate."

End with the claim.

**Step 2 — Write the conclusion together:**

Say: "We used the synthesis + call to action strategy for our model. Let's try synthesis + forward look for this one."

Guide the student to write a conclusion that:
1. Synthesizes the three reasons into one or two sentences
2. Looks forward: "If schools adopt this requirement, the next generation of students will enter high school not only with stronger academic skills but with the compassion, community awareness, and practical experience that define a truly educated person."

**Step 3 — Add transitions between paragraphs:**

Display a Transition Reference Sheet:

| Purpose | Transitions |
|---------|------------|
| Adding a reason | Furthermore, In addition, Moreover, Beyond this |
| Showing cause/effect | As a result, Consequently, Because of this, This leads to |
| Contrasting | However, On the other hand, Despite this, Nevertheless |
| Emphasizing | Most importantly, Above all, The strongest evidence |
| Concluding | In light of this evidence, Taken together, Ultimately |

Practice adding a transition between two body paragraphs. Example: "Beyond the personal growth that comes from developing empathy, community service also strengthens the bonds between students and the neighborhoods they live in."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students write a complete argumentative essay by assembling introduction, body paragraphs, and conclusion.

**Task:** Using the claim, organized reasons, and body paragraph(s) you developed in Lessons 1-2, write a complete argumentative essay. You must include:

1. An introduction with a hook, context, and your claim statement
2. At least two body paragraphs, each with a topic sentence, evidence (using ICE), and explanation
3. Transitions between paragraphs
4. A conclusion that synthesizes your argument and uses one of the three conclusion strategies (call to action, forward look, or thought-provoking question)

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write the complete essay as described above (introduction, two body paragraphs, conclusion).
**Stretch:** Write the complete essay with three body paragraphs and include at least one transitional clause (not just a word or phrase) between paragraphs.
**Challenge:** Write the complete essay with three body paragraphs, include transitions, AND add a sentence in your conclusion that briefly acknowledges a counterclaim and explains why your argument is still stronger.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — W.5.3e) Write a concluding sentence for this narrative situation: "After weeks of practice, Mia finally performed her gymnastics routine perfectly at the competition." (Expected: Something that reflects on the experience and provides closure, such as: "As she stepped off the mat, Mia realized that the medal around her neck mattered less than the hundreds of falls that had taught her to get back up.")
6. (Grade 5 review — L.5.1e) Combine these sentences using the correlative conjunction "not only...but also": "Exercise strengthens your body. Exercise improves your mood." (Expected: "Exercise not only strengthens your body but also improves your mood.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following conclusion and answer: What strategy does this conclusion use (call to action, forward look, or thought-provoking question)? Then identify one strength and one weakness of the conclusion.

> "In conclusion, schools should have later start times. Students need more sleep. Later start times help students learn better. Schools should change their start times."

**Expected Answers:**
- Strategy: Attempted call to action ("Schools should change their start times"), but it is very weak.
- Strength: It does restate the claim and mention a reason.
- Weakness: It merely repeats points from the essay without synthesizing them. It does not combine the evidence into a new insight. The language is flat and repetitive ("In conclusion" is a weak opening). It reads like a list, not a conclusion.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the strategy (or attempted strategy) AND provides at least one substantive weakness showing understanding of what a strong conclusion requires.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model conclusion from direct instruction and compare it side-by-side with the weak exit ticket conclusion
- Ask: "What does the strong conclusion do that the weak one does not?" Guide toward synthesis, specific language, and a final impactful sentence
- Have the student rewrite the weak conclusion using the synthesis + forward look strategy

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 4, which transitions to informative writing
- Encourage the student to read their complete argument aloud, listening for places where the writing sounds choppy or repetitive, and to make revisions before the next session

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Conclusion Makeover:** Rewrite the weak conclusion from the exit ticket using each of the three strategies (call to action, forward look, thought-provoking question). Which version is strongest? Why?
2. **Hook Collection:** Collect three to five hooks from published articles, editorials, or essays. Classify each by type (surprising fact, question, scenario, bold statement). Which type do you find most engaging as a reader?
3. **Peer Argument Exchange:** Trade complete arguments with a partner. Read each other's essay and write a one-paragraph response: Was the argument convincing? What was the strongest piece of evidence? What could be improved?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson asks students to assemble a complete essay for the first time in this unit. Some students may feel overwhelmed. Reassure them that they have already done most of the work in Lessons 1 and 2 — today is about connecting the pieces.
- The most common weaknesses in student conclusions are (1) beginning with "In conclusion" (teach them this is a crutch), (2) copying the introduction, and (3) introducing new evidence. A conclusion should synthesize, not summarize or add new material.
- Transitions between paragraphs are a new skill for many sixth graders. If the student is struggling, focus on just adding one strong transition between body paragraphs rather than trying to perfect all of them.
- If the student has not completed body paragraphs from Lesson 2, use the first portion of independent practice time to draft them, then write the introduction and conclusion.
