# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 7

## Topic
Narrative Writing: Engaging the Reader

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write a narrative opening that establishes the narrator/character, setting, and situation in an engaging way
- Use a narrative hook to draw the reader into the story immediately
- Organize a sequence of events using a Story Map before drafting
- Distinguish between sixth-grade narrative expectations and fifth-grade narrative writing

## Prerequisites
- W.5.3: Experience writing narratives that orient the reader, develop events, and provide a conclusion
- W.5.3a: Ability to introduce a narrator or character, establish a situation, and organize an event sequence
- W.5.3d: Familiarity with concrete words, phrases, and sensory details
- Basic understanding of story elements (character, setting, conflict, resolution)

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model narrative opening (provided below)
- Graphic organizer: Story Map (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)
- Sticky notes for brainstorming

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about narrative writing and introduce the idea that great stories hook the reader from the first sentence.

**Activity: First Sentence Showdown**

Display the following two opening sentences and ask the student which one makes them want to keep reading:

**Opening A:** "One day, a girl named Sofia went to a new school. She was nervous."

**Opening B:** "Sofia stood at the edge of the cafeteria, tray in her hands, scanning the crowded room for a single empty seat — and finding none."

Say: "Both openings are about the same character in the same situation. But Opening B drops us right into the moment. We can see Sofia standing there. We can feel her anxiety. We do not need to be told she is nervous because the details show us."

Ask: "What specific details in Opening B make it more engaging?" (Standing at the edge, tray in her hands, scanning, finding no empty seat.)

Say: "In fifth grade, you learned to orient the reader — to introduce the character, setting, and situation. In sixth grade, we go further. You do not just set up the story; you pull the reader in from the very first sentence. Today we are going to learn how to write narrative openings that make the reader unable to stop reading."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach narrative hook techniques and the elements of an engaging sixth-grade narrative opening.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Narrative hook:** An opening sentence or paragraph designed to grab the reader's attention and create immediate interest
- **In medias res:** A Latin term meaning "in the middle of things" — starting the story in the middle of the action rather than at the very beginning
- **Orientation:** The part of a narrative that establishes who, where, when, and what situation the character faces
- **Point of view:** The perspective from which the story is told (first person: "I"; third person: "she/he/they")
- **Conflict:** The problem, challenge, or tension that drives the story forward

### Instruction Notes

Say: "A narrative hook is like the first note of a song — it sets the tone and draws you in. There are four types of narrative hooks you can use."

Write on chart paper:

**Narrative Hook Types:**
1. **Action opening:** Start with something happening. "The ball sailed past the goalkeeper's outstretched fingers and slammed into the back of the net."
2. **Dialogue opening:** Start with a character speaking. "'You have exactly thirty seconds to explain why you are standing in my flower bed at midnight,' said Grandma, arms crossed."
3. **Sensory opening:** Start with a vivid sensory detail. "The smell of burned popcorn filled the entire second floor of the school, and everyone knew it was coming from Room 214."
4. **Question or mystery opening:** Start with something that raises a question in the reader's mind. "The locker had been empty all year. So why was there a note inside it with Marcus's name on it?"

Say: "After the hook, you still need to orient the reader. Within the first few sentences, the reader should know: Who is the main character? Where and when is this happening? What is the situation or conflict? But you weave this information in naturally — you do not stop the story to provide a list of facts."

**The Story Map:**

Draw on chart paper:

```
STORY MAP

Character(s): ____________________
Setting (where/when): ____________________
Conflict/Problem: ____________________

BEGINNING (Hook + Orientation):
  What happens first? ____________________

MIDDLE (Rising Action — 2-3 key events):
  Event 1: ____________________
  Event 2: ____________________
  Event 3: ____________________

CLIMAX (Turning Point):
  What is the most intense moment? ____________________

END (Resolution + Reflection):
  How is the conflict resolved? ____________________
  What does the character learn or realize? ____________________
```

Say: "A Story Map is your blueprint. You do not have to follow it exactly when you write — stories evolve — but it gives you a plan so you do not get lost in the middle."

### Worked Examples

Read the following model narrative opening aloud. Display it so the student can follow along.

> **The Tryout**
>
> The gymnasium smelled like floor wax and sweat, and the squeak of sneakers on polished wood echoed off the cinder-block walls like a hundred tiny alarms. Dante wiped his palms on his shorts for the third time and stared at the clipboard taped to the door. BASKETBALL TRYOUTS — SIXTH GRADE. His name was on the sign-up sheet, fourth from the top, written in his own careful handwriting two weeks ago when making the team had seemed like a reasonable goal rather than an approaching disaster.
>
> Forty-two students had signed up. Twelve would make the team.
>
> Dante had played basketball at the park every weekend for the past two years, but park basketball was not the same as organized basketball. At the park, nobody ran drills. Nobody timed your sprints. Nobody watched you dribble and wrote things on a clipboard. At the park, you just played.
>
> "You coming in or you gonna read that list all day?" said a voice behind him. Dante turned to find Kezia Okonkwo leaning against the wall, her sneakers already laced tight and a worn basketball tucked under one arm. She nodded toward the gym door. "Come on. Standing out here is not going to make it less scary."
>
> She was right. Dante took a breath that tasted like floor wax, pushed through the door, and stepped onto the court.

Annotate together using two colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** Hook and sensory details (floor wax, sweat, squeak of sneakers, wiped palms, breath that tasted like floor wax)
- **Color 2 (green):** Orientation elements — character (Dante), setting (gymnasium, sixth-grade basketball tryouts), conflict (42 students, 12 spots, fear of failure), and the introduction of a second character (Kezia)

Ask: "What type of hook does this opening use?" (Sensory opening — the smells and sounds of the gym.)

Ask: "How does the writer show us that Dante is nervous without ever writing the word 'nervous'?" (Wiping palms, staring at the clipboard, the thought about 'approaching disaster,' the breath that 'tasted like floor wax.')

Say: "This is the golden rule of narrative writing in sixth grade: show, do not tell. Do not say 'Dante was nervous.' Show me his sweaty palms, his racing thoughts, his hesitation at the door. Let the reader feel the nervousness through the details."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively plan a narrative using the Story Map and draft an opening together.

**Activity: Planning and Drafting a Story Opening**

Say: "Let's plan a narrative together. Our prompt is: Write a story about a time a character had to stand up for someone else."

**Step 1 — Complete the Story Map together:**

- **Character(s):** Amara (sixth grader), her younger brother Eli (third grader), a group of older students
- **Setting:** The school bus on a rainy afternoon
- **Conflict:** Older students are making fun of Eli's stutter, and Amara has to decide whether to speak up
- **Beginning:** Amara and Eli get on the crowded bus; Amara sits in the middle, Eli sits two rows behind her
- **Middle events:** (1) Amara hears older students mimicking Eli's stutter; (2) She freezes, unsure what to do; (3) She remembers her father's words: "The right thing and the easy thing are almost never the same thing"
- **Climax:** Amara stands up, walks to the back of the bus, and tells the students to stop
- **End:** The bus goes quiet; Eli looks up at his sister; Amara sits down next to him without a word; she realizes courage is not about not being afraid

**Step 2 — Choose a hook type and draft the opening together:**

Say: "Which hook type should we use? Let's try a sensory opening combined with in medias res — we will start right in the moment."

Draft collaboratively:

> The rain drummed against the windows of Bus 47 like a thousand impatient fingers, and the usual roar of thirty conversations blurred into a single wave of noise. Amara pressed her forehead against the cool glass and watched the school parking lot slide away, her reflection a ghost floating over puddles and yellow lines. Two rows behind her, she could hear Eli's voice — quiet, careful, the way it always was when he was talking to people who were not family.

Say: "Let's check: Do we have our hook? (Sensory details — rain, noise, cool glass.) Do we have our character? (Amara.) Setting? (Bus 47, rainy afternoon after school.) Have we hinted at the conflict? (Eli's careful voice suggests something is about to happen.) Good — we have oriented the reader and pulled them in at the same time."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently plan a narrative and draft an engaging opening.

Choose ONE of the following prompts (or propose your own):

1. Write a story about a character who discovers something unexpected in a place they visit every day.
2. Write a story about a character who must make a difficult choice between what is easy and what is right.
3. Write a story about a character's first day at a new school, camp, or activity.
4. Write a story about a character who learns an important lesson from a mistake.

**Task:**
1. Complete a Story Map for your narrative (character, setting, conflict, beginning/middle/climax/end).
2. Choose a hook type and write your narrative opening (first two to three paragraphs). Your opening must establish the character, setting, and situation while engaging the reader from the first sentence.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete the Story Map and write a narrative opening of two to three paragraphs that uses one of the four hook types and establishes character, setting, and conflict.
**Stretch:** Complete the on-level task and include at least one line of dialogue in your opening that reveals something about the character's personality or situation.
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task and write your opening using in medias res — start in the middle of the action, then weave in the orientation details naturally.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review — W.5.3d) Rewrite this "telling" sentence as a "showing" sentence using sensory details: "The kitchen was messy and smelled bad." (Expected: Something like "Dishes towered in the sink, crusted with last night's spaghetti sauce, and the garbage can near the back door released a sour, vinegar-sharp smell that made her wrinkle her nose.")
6. (Grade 5 review — W.5.1) Write a claim statement about the following topic: "Whether students should be required to participate in sports." (Expected: A debatable claim, not an opinion. Example: "Schools should require all students to participate in at least one sport per year because physical activity improves academic performance, teaches teamwork, and reduces rates of childhood anxiety and depression.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Write a one-sentence narrative hook for the following situation: A student opens their locker on the last day of school and finds a note from someone they do not know.

Then identify which hook type you used.

**Expected Answers:**
Answers will vary. The hook must be engaging and must match the claimed type. Examples:
- **Action:** "The folded square of paper fell from the top shelf of the locker and landed on the toe of Jordan's sneaker."
- **Dialogue:** "'Do not open this until you are completely alone,' the note said, in handwriting Jordan had never seen before."
- **Sensory:** "The note smelled like lavender and old paper, as if it had been waiting inside the locker for years."
- **Question/Mystery:** "The locker had been locked all year — so how did a note get inside it?"

**Mastery = The hook is engaging (creates curiosity or interest), matches the identified type, and relates to the given situation.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the four hook types on the chart and practice writing a hook of each type for a simple scenario (e.g., "A character is about to ride a roller coaster for the first time")
- Ask: "Does your first sentence make the reader want to know what happens next? If you stopped reading after this sentence, would you be curious?"
- Provide sentence starters: "The sound of..." (sensory), "'___,' said..." (dialogue), "Without warning,..." (action)

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8, where they will learn to develop narratives with descriptive details and controlled pacing
- Encourage the student to continue drafting their story beyond the opening before the next session

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Hook Collection Journal:** Over the next week, collect the opening sentences of five stories, novels, or articles you read. Write each one down and classify it by hook type. Which types do professional authors use most often?
2. **Rewrite Challenge:** Take the opening you wrote in independent practice and rewrite it using a different hook type. Compare the two versions: How does the hook type change the tone and mood of the story?
3. **Character Profile:** Before continuing your story, write a one-page character profile for your main character: name, age, appearance, personality traits, fears, strengths, and what they want more than anything. How might knowing these details deepen your story?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- "Show, don't tell" is one of the most important writing principles for sixth graders to internalize, but it takes time and practice. When conferencing, circle any "telling" sentences ("She was sad," "He was scared") and ask: "How can you show me this instead of telling me?"
- Some students may want to begin their story with extensive backstory ("Once there was a boy named Jake. He was twelve years old. He lived in Ohio. He had a dog named Rex..."). Redirect toward in medias res: "Start where the story gets interesting. You can weave in the background details later."
- The Story Map should be treated as a planning tool, not a rigid contract. If a student's story evolves as they write and diverges from the map, that is a sign of good writing instincts, not a problem.
- Sixth graders are developing their sense of identity, and many will want to write about personal experiences. This is valuable — but remind students that the story can be based on real events and still be shaped and enhanced for narrative purposes. They are writers, not reporters.

### Independent Practice
**Sample Story Map (Prompt 1 — unexpected discovery):**
- **Character:** Kai, a quiet sixth grader who walks through the same park every day on the way home from school
- **Setting:** Riverside Park, late October, after school
- **Conflict:** Kai finds a handmade wooden box buried under a tree and must decide whether to open it
- **Beginning:** Kai walks through the park, notices a patch of disturbed earth under the old oak tree
- **Middle:** (1) Finds a wooden box with initials carved on it; (2) Opens it and finds letters from decades ago; (3) Reads a letter from a girl writing to her future self
- **Climax:** The letter describes a wish that someone would find it and remember her
- **End:** Kai researches the initials and discovers the letter writer was a former student at the same school; realizes that small acts of connection transcend time

**Sample opening (action hook with in medias res):**
> Kai almost missed it. The patch of dark, freshly turned earth at the base of the old oak tree could have been the work of a squirrel or a dog, and on any other afternoon, Kai would have walked right past it without a second thought. But today the October light hit the ground at just the right angle, and something glinted beneath the soil — a dull metallic flash, like a coin catching the sun. Kai knelt down, set the backpack on the grass, and brushed the dirt away with careful fingers. It was not a coin. It was a latch.
