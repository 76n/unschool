## Section 1: Passage A — "The Rosetta Stone: Unlocking Ancient Egypt"

Read the passage. Then answer questions 1-6.

> For centuries, the ancient Egyptian writing system known as hieroglyphics remained one of the greatest unsolved puzzles in the history of human communication. Thousands of inscriptions covered the walls of temples, tombs, and monuments across Egypt, yet no living person could decipher their meaning. The knowledge of how to read hieroglyphics had been lost sometime around the fifth century AD, when the last communities that understood the script disappeared. Scholars, travelers, and treasure hunters studied the symbols for hundreds of years, proposing theories that ranged from the insightful to the wildly incorrect. Some believed each symbol represented a single word. Others thought the symbols were purely decorative or mystical rather than a true writing system.
>
> The breakthrough came unexpectedly in 1799, when French soldiers stationed in Egypt discovered a large stone slab near the town of Rashid, known in English as Rosetta. The Rosetta Stone, as it came to be called, contained the same official decree written in three scripts: hieroglyphics at the top, Demotic script in the middle, and ancient Greek at the bottom. Because scholars could already read ancient Greek, the stone offered a potential key to unlocking the other two scripts. However, the process of decipherment was neither quick nor simple. British physicist Thomas Young made early progress by identifying that some hieroglyphic symbols enclosed in oval frames, called cartouches, represented the names of rulers. But it was French linguist Jean-Francois Champollion who achieved the decisive breakthrough in 1822. Champollion recognized that hieroglyphics were not purely symbolic -- they represented sounds as well as ideas, functioning as a combination of phonetic and ideographic elements. His discovery meant that for the first time in more than a thousand years, human beings could read the words of the ancient Egyptians.
>
> The decipherment of hieroglyphics transformed our understanding of ancient Egypt from speculation into scholarship. Historians could now read royal decrees, religious texts, personal letters, and administrative records that revealed the daily lives, beliefs, and governance of a civilization that had thrived for three thousand years. The Rosetta Stone itself became one of the most famous artifacts in the world, drawing millions of visitors to the British Museum in London, where it has been housed since 1802.

## Section 2: Passage B — "Should Schools Ban Smartphones?"

Read the passage. Then answer questions 7-11.

> A growing number of schools across the United States and Europe are banning students from using smartphones during the school day, and the evidence suggests they may be right to do so. Research published in the Journal of Educational Psychology found that students who had their phones visible on their desks -- even when the phones were turned off -- scored significantly lower on tests of attention and memory than students whose phones were stored out of sight. The mere presence of a smartphone, researchers concluded, diverts cognitive resources away from the task at hand. A nationwide study in France, which banned smartphones in all primary and middle schools in 2018, reported improvements in student test scores, classroom behavior, and social interaction within the first year. Students themselves reported feeling less anxious and more focused once the initial adjustment period passed.
>
> Critics of phone bans argue that smartphones are essential safety tools, allowing parents to contact their children in emergencies. They also point out that smartphones can be valuable learning devices when used for research, educational apps, and collaborative projects. However, these arguments do not hold up under scrutiny. Schools can maintain landline phones and intercoms for emergency communication, and dedicated classroom devices such as tablets and laptops serve educational purposes without the constant temptation of social media notifications, text messages, and games. The real question is not whether smartphones have any educational value -- they do -- but whether the distractions they introduce outweigh the benefits. The research overwhelmingly points in one direction: for the majority of students, smartphones in school are a net negative for learning.

## Section 3: Passage C — "Chien-Shiung Wu: The First Lady of Physics"

Read the passage. Then answer questions 12-16.

> In January 1957, a physics experiment conducted at Columbia University overturned one of the most fundamental assumptions in the field -- and the scientist who designed and executed it, Chien-Shiung Wu, was denied the Nobel Prize that her work made possible. Wu's experiment demonstrated that the law of conservation of parity, which physicists had assumed held true for all physical interactions, did not apply to certain nuclear reactions. Two theoretical physicists, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, had proposed this possibility in a 1956 paper, but it was Wu who devised the extraordinarily difficult experiment that proved them right. When the Nobel Committee awarded the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics, Lee and Yang received the honor. Wu, whose experimental brilliance had provided the proof, was not included.
>
> Born in 1912 in the town of Liuhe, near Shanghai, Wu grew up in a China where most girls did not attend school. Her father, an educator and advocate for women's rights, founded a school for girls and insisted that his daughter receive the same education as any boy. Wu excelled in science from an early age and left China in 1936 to pursue graduate studies in physics at the University of California, Berkeley. She earned her doctorate in 1940 and quickly established herself as one of the most skilled experimental physicists in the world. During World War II, she contributed to the Manhattan Project, helping to develop the process for enriching uranium. After the war, she joined the faculty at Columbia University, where she built a reputation for experimental precision that colleagues described as unmatched in her generation.
>
> Despite the Nobel oversight, Wu received numerous other honors during her career, including the National Medal of Science, the Wolf Prize in Physics, and the first honorary doctorate ever awarded to a woman by Princeton University. She spent her later years advocating for women in science, speaking publicly about the barriers she had faced and encouraging young women to pursue careers in physics. Wu died in 1997, and in 2021, the United States Postal Service issued a stamp in her honor -- a recognition that, while overdue, acknowledged her enduring legacy as one of the most important experimental physicists of the twentieth century.

## Remediation Guidance

If a student does not meet mastery on a specific standard, use the following targeted reteaching plan:

**RI.6.1 (Citing Evidence and Making Inferences):**
- Reteach using Lesson 1, focusing on the C-E-C method (Claim, Evidence, Connection)
- Provide two additional short passages and have the student practice selecting the most relevant evidence for specific questions
- For inference questions, review the distinction between explicit information and inferences, and practice supporting inferences with cited evidence
- Common issue: students cite evidence but fail to explain the connection to their claim

**RI.6.2 (Central Idea and Objective Summary):**
- Reteach using Lessons 2-3, focusing on the Three-Pass Method and Detail Mapping
- Provide a passage with a clearly stated central idea and have the student identify it, then move to passages where the central idea is implied
- For summaries, provide a completed summary with hidden opinions and have the student identify and remove them
- Common issue: students confuse the topic with the central idea or include opinions in summaries

**RI.6.3 (Introduction and Elaboration of Key Individuals, Events, Ideas):**
- Reteach using Lessons 4-5, focusing on the Introduction Toolkit and Elaboration Toolkit
- Provide two versions of the same passage with different introductions and have the student compare the impressions created
- Practice labeling elaboration techniques (examples, statistics, cause-and-effect, comparison, expert testimony) in a passage
- Common issue: students describe what the author says without analyzing how or why the author says it

**RI.6.6 (Author's Purpose and Point of View):**
- Reteach using Lessons 6-7, focusing on the Purpose Detectors and Three Shaping Forces
- Provide passages with clearly different purposes and have the student identify textual clues in each
- Practice analyzing emphasis, omission, and framing with paired passages on the same topic
- Common issue: students identify purpose as simply "to inform" without recognizing more specific purposes like "to persuade" or "to raise awareness"

**RI.6.7 (Integrating Information from Different Media and Formats):**
- Reteach using Lessons 8-9, focusing on Text-Visual Integration and Compare-Combine-Conclude
- Provide a passage with a simple table and walk the student through the three-step integration process
- Practice identifying what each source contributes uniquely before attempting synthesis
- Common issue: students summarize each source separately rather than integrating them into a combined understanding
