# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 5

## Topic
How Events and Ideas Are Elaborated

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Analyze how an author elaborates on a key event or idea after its initial introduction
- Identify specific elaboration techniques: examples, facts and statistics, cause-and-effect explanations, comparisons, and expert testimony
- Explain why an author chose particular elaboration strategies and how they deepen the reader's understanding
- Trace how an idea grows from introduction through elaboration across multiple sentences or paragraphs

## Prerequisites
- Ability to analyze how a key individual is introduced in a text (Lesson 4)
- Ability to determine central idea and how it is conveyed through details (Lessons 2-3)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lesson 1)
- Understanding of cause-and-effect relationships in informational text (RI.5.3)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Elaboration Tracker graphic organizer (columns: Idea/Event Introduced, Elaboration Technique, Specific Details Added, How Understanding Deepens)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two or more colors (recommended)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect Lesson 4's focus on introduction to today's focus on elaboration, showing how authors build on initial information.

**Activity: Tell Me More**

Say: "In Lesson 4, we studied how authors introduce key individuals. Today, we are looking at the next step: elaboration. Introduction tells you about something for the first time. Elaboration is how the author expands on that initial introduction to deepen your understanding."

Write on the board:
- Introduction: The eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD destroyed the Roman city of Pompeii.
- Elaboration: When the volcano erupted, it launched a column of volcanic ash and rock more than twenty miles into the sky. Within hours, a deadly surge of superheated gas and debris -- called a pyroclastic flow -- raced down the mountain at speeds exceeding 100 miles per hour, burying the city under nearly twenty feet of ash. Approximately 2,000 residents who had not yet fled were killed almost instantly.

Say: "The introduction gives the basic event. The elaboration tells us how it happened, how fast, how deep the ash was, and how many people died. Each piece of elaboration adds a new layer of understanding. Today you will learn to recognize and analyze the techniques authors use to elaborate."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify and analyze five common elaboration techniques in informational text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Elaboration:** The process of expanding on an idea, event, or individual with additional details, evidence, and explanation
- **Cause-and-effect explanation:** Elaboration that explains why something happened or what resulted from it
- **Comparison:** Elaboration that helps the reader understand by relating one thing to another familiar thing
- **Expert testimony:** A statement from a specialist or authority figure used to support or elaborate on an idea
- **Statistical evidence:** Specific numbers, percentages, or measurements that provide concrete support for a claim

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Authors elaborate on events and ideas using five common techniques. I call them the Elaboration Toolkit."

Write on the board:
1. **Examples** -- Specific instances that illustrate the idea or event
2. **Facts and statistics** -- Numerical data that make the idea concrete and measurable
3. **Cause-and-effect** -- Explanations of why something happened or what its consequences were
4. **Comparisons** -- Relating the idea to something familiar to help the reader understand scale or significance
5. **Expert testimony** -- Quotes or references to authorities who add credibility

Say: "Most passages use several of these techniques together. Let me show you how to spot them."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Dust Bowl"**

> In the 1930s, a catastrophic series of dust storms devastated the Great Plains of the United States, turning one of the country's most productive farming regions into a wasteland. The disaster, known as the Dust Bowl, began after decades of intensive farming practices stripped the native grassland from millions of acres. Without the deep root systems of prairie grasses to hold the soil in place, the topsoil dried out and became vulnerable to wind erosion. When severe drought struck in 1931 and continued for nearly a decade, massive storms lifted the loose soil into towering clouds of dust that blackened the sky. On April 14, 1935 -- a day known as Black Sunday -- a single dust storm carried an estimated 300 million tons of topsoil across the Plains, reducing visibility to near zero and depositing dust as far east as Washington, D.C., and New York City. The storms destroyed crops, killed livestock, and rendered farmland unusable. Historian Timothy Egan described the Dust Bowl as "the worst prolonged environmental disaster in American history." An estimated 2.5 million people fled the affected regions, many heading to California in search of work and a new beginning.

Say: "Let me trace how the author introduces and elaborates on the Dust Bowl."

Write on the board:
- **Introduction:** "A catastrophic series of dust storms devastated the Great Plains" -- this introduces the event.
- **Elaboration 1 (Cause-and-effect):** Intensive farming stripped grassland, roots no longer held soil, drought dried the topsoil, wind lifted it -- explains why the disaster happened.
- **Elaboration 2 (Example):** Black Sunday on April 14, 1935 -- a specific instance that makes the event vivid and concrete.
- **Elaboration 3 (Statistics):** 300 million tons of topsoil, 2.5 million people fled -- makes the scale measurable and real.
- **Elaboration 4 (Comparison):** Dust deposited "as far east as Washington, D.C., and New York City" -- helps the reader understand the geographic scale.
- **Elaboration 5 (Expert testimony):** Historian Timothy Egan calls it "the worst prolonged environmental disaster in American history" -- adds authority and significance.

Say: "See how the author does not just tell you the Dust Bowl happened. The author builds your understanding layer by layer: first why it happened, then what a single day looked like, then how big it was, then how it compares to other events, and then what an expert says about it. Each elaboration technique adds something different."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying elaboration techniques with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us analyze a new passage together. I want you to find the introduction of the key idea and then identify each elaboration technique the author uses."

**Passage B: "The Global Decline of Insect Populations"**

> Scientists are warning of what they call an insect apocalypse -- a dramatic, worldwide decline in insect populations that could have far-reaching consequences for ecosystems and human food production. A landmark 2019 study reviewing 73 separate research reports found that more than 40 percent of insect species are declining globally and that a third are classified as endangered. The rate of decline is approximately 2.5 percent per year, meaning that insect populations could fall by half within the next fifty years. Insects play essential roles in nearly every ecosystem on Earth. Bees, butterflies, and other pollinators are responsible for the reproduction of approximately 75 percent of flowering plants and 35 percent of global food crops. Without these pollinators, crops such as almonds, apples, and blueberries would produce dramatically smaller yields. Insects also serve as the primary food source for birds, bats, freshwater fish, and amphibians, meaning that insect decline ripples through entire food chains. Entomologist Dave Goulson of the University of Sussex has called the situation "terrifying," noting that "if we lose the insects, everything is going to collapse."

Say: "What key idea is introduced at the beginning of this passage?"

Expected response: The dramatic, worldwide decline of insect populations and its potential consequences.

Say: "Good. Now let us identify how the author elaborates on that idea. What techniques do you see?"

Guide the student through:
- **Facts and statistics:** "More than 40 percent of insect species are declining," "2.5 percent per year," "75 percent of flowering plants," "35 percent of global food crops"
- **Example:** Specific crops -- almonds, apples, blueberries -- that would be affected
- **Cause-and-effect:** Insect decline leads to smaller crop yields; decline ripples through food chains affecting birds, bats, fish, amphibians
- **Expert testimony:** Entomologist Dave Goulson calls the situation "terrifying" and says "if we lose the insects, everything is going to collapse"

Say: "Which elaboration technique does the author rely on most heavily?"

Expected response: Facts and statistics -- the passage is packed with specific numbers.

Say: "Why might the author have chosen that approach?"

Expected response: Statistics make the problem feel concrete, measurable, and urgent. Saying "40 percent are declining" is more alarming than saying "many insects are dying." The numbers force the reader to take the problem seriously.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how an author elaborates on events and ideas in a new passage.

**Passage C: "The Construction of the Panama Canal"**

> The Panama Canal, a 50-mile waterway connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans, was one of the most ambitious engineering projects in human history. When the canal opened in 1914, it eliminated the need for ships to travel around the southern tip of South America, reducing the voyage between New York and San Francisco from approximately 13,000 miles to just 5,000 miles. The project was first attempted by the French in 1881 under the direction of Ferdinand de Lesseps, the engineer who had successfully built the Suez Canal. However, the French effort collapsed after eight years, defeated by tropical diseases, landslides, and engineering challenges. Malaria and yellow fever alone killed more than 20,000 workers during the French attempt. When the United States took over the project in 1904, its first priority was conquering disease. Colonel William Gorgas led a massive campaign to eliminate mosquito breeding grounds by draining swamps, spraying insecticides, and installing screens on workers' housing. Once disease was controlled, engineers tackled the terrain. Rather than carving a sea-level canal through the mountainous landscape, as the French had attempted, American engineers designed a system of locks that raised and lowered ships 85 feet through a series of water-filled chambers. The completed canal required the excavation of more than 200 million cubic yards of earth -- enough to build a wall ten feet tall and ten feet wide stretching from New York to San Francisco.

**Directions:** Analyze how the author elaborates on the construction of the Panama Canal. Cite textual evidence in your responses.

1. Identify the key event that is introduced in the first two sentences. Then identify at least three different elaboration techniques the author uses to develop this event throughout the passage.
2. How does the author use cause-and-effect elaboration to explain the failure of the French attempt?
3. The passage includes the comparison that excavated earth was "enough to build a wall ten feet tall and ten feet wide stretching from New York to San Francisco." Why is this comparison effective? What does it help the reader understand that the raw number (200 million cubic yards) might not convey on its own?
4. The passage elaborates on the American approach by describing Colonel Gorgas's disease-fighting campaign before describing the engineering solution. Why might the author have chosen this order?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with accurate identification of elaboration techniques and textual evidence.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. For question 1, label each elaboration technique by name and explain what it adds to the reader's understanding.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a paragraph evaluating whether the author's elaboration effectively communicates the scale and difficulty of building the Panama Canal. Cite at least three specific examples from the passage.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.3) Explain the relationship between the French failure and the American approach. What did the Americans learn from or do differently than the French?
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.1) Quote the sentence that describes the impact of disease on the French attempt.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> In 1969, astronaut Neil Armstrong became the first person to walk on the Moon. The journey from Earth to the Moon took approximately three days, covering a distance of 238,855 miles. Armstrong's first step was watched live on television by an estimated 600 million people -- roughly one-fifth of the world's population at the time.

1. Identify two elaboration techniques the author uses after introducing the Moon landing event.
2. Choose one and explain how it deepens the reader's understanding.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The author uses facts/statistics ("238,855 miles," "600 million people," "one-fifth of the world's population") and comparison ("one-fifth of the world's population at the time" compares the audience to a global scale).
2. Example: The statistic that 600 million people watched live, combined with the comparison to one-fifth of the world's population, deepens understanding by showing that the Moon landing was not just a scientific achievement but a shared global experience. The raw number 600 million is large, but relating it to the world's population makes the reader feel the collective scale of the moment. (Accept any technique with a clear explanation.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with two elaboration techniques accurately identified and one clearly explained**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Elaboration Toolkit using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Provide a passage with each elaboration technique labeled and discuss why each one matters
- Have the student create the Elaboration Tracker for a simple passage, filling in each column together
- Practice matching elaboration examples to their technique names with a sorting activity

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 6 on determining author's purpose
- Encourage the student to notice elaboration techniques in science textbooks and news articles

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Elaboration Remix:** Give the student a basic event sentence (e.g., "A massive earthquake struck the coast"). Have them write five elaborations, one for each technique in the Elaboration Toolkit, creating a full paragraph.
2. **Technique Tally:** Have the student read an informational article and tally how many times the author uses each elaboration technique. Then write a paragraph analyzing the author's preferred method and why it suits the topic.
3. **Before and After:** Provide a bare-bones passage (just introduction, no elaboration) and a fully elaborated version. Have the student highlight each elaboration, label its technique, and explain how the elaborated version is more effective.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students may initially struggle to distinguish between elaboration techniques that overlap. For example, a comparison might also contain a statistic. Reassure them that overlap is normal in good writing; the goal is to recognize the tools, not to force every detail into a single category.
- The distinction between introduction and elaboration can be blurry. A helpful rule of thumb: the introduction tells you what the event or idea is. Elaboration tells you more about how, why, how much, or what it means.
- When students identify elaboration techniques, always follow up with "Why does this matter?" or "What does this add to your understanding?" Identifying is the first step; explaining the effect is the deeper analytical skill.
- This lesson pairs naturally with Lesson 4 on introduction. Consider having students revisit a Lesson 4 passage and now analyze the elaboration that follows each introduction.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Key event: The construction of the Panama Canal, one of the most ambitious engineering projects in history, created a 50-mile waterway that dramatically shortened shipping routes. Elaboration techniques include: (a) Facts and statistics -- 13,000 miles reduced to 5,000 miles, 20,000 workers killed, 85 feet of elevation change, 200 million cubic yards of earth. (b) Cause-and-effect -- tropical diseases and engineering challenges caused the French failure; the American mosquito-elimination campaign enabled construction to proceed. (c) Comparison -- excavated earth compared to a wall from New York to San Francisco. (d) Example -- Colonel Gorgas's disease-fighting campaign as a specific instance of the American approach. (Accept any three techniques with evidence.)
2. The author explains that the French attempt "collapsed after eight years, defeated by tropical diseases, landslides, and engineering challenges." The cause-and-effect chain is: malaria and yellow fever caused the deaths of more than 20,000 workers, which combined with landslides and engineering difficulties to make the project unsustainable, leading to its failure.
3. The comparison is effective because 200 million cubic yards is a number most readers cannot visualize. By comparing it to a wall stretching from New York to San Francisco, the author translates an abstract number into a concrete image. The reader can imagine the distance between those two cities and picture a massive wall running that entire length, which makes the scale of the excavation feel real and astonishing.
4. The author likely chose this order because disease was the first problem the Americans had to solve before any engineering work could proceed. By describing Gorgas's campaign first, the author shows that the Americans learned from the French failure and prioritized the deadliest threat. This order also mirrors the actual sequence of events and creates a logical narrative: first solve the disease problem, then tackle the engineering challenge.

### Interleaved Review
5. The Americans learned from the French failure by addressing disease first. While the French lost more than 20,000 workers to malaria and yellow fever, the Americans under Colonel Gorgas launched a campaign to eliminate mosquito breeding grounds before beginning major construction. The Americans also changed the engineering approach, designing a lock system to raise and lower ships rather than attempting to carve a sea-level canal through mountains as the French had done.
6. "Malaria and yellow fever alone killed more than 20,000 workers during the French attempt."
