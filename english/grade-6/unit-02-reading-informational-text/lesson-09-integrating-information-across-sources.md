# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic
Integrating Information Across Sources

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Integrate information from two different written sources on the same topic to develop a coherent understanding
- Identify what each source contributes uniquely and where sources overlap or complement each other
- Recognize when sources present conflicting information and evaluate which source provides stronger evidence
- Write a synthesis statement that draws on information from multiple sources

## Prerequisites
- Ability to integrate text and visual formats (Lesson 8)
- Ability to determine central idea and how it is conveyed through details (Lessons 2-3)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lesson 1)
- Experience integrating information from multiple texts on the same topic (RI.5.9)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages (paired texts on the same topic)
- Source Integration Matrix graphic organizer (columns: Source 1 Only, Source 2 Only, Both Sources, Synthesis)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (one per source)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the concept of integration from Lesson 8 and extend it from text-visual integration to cross-source integration.

**Activity: Two Experts, One Topic**

Say: "In Lesson 8, you learned to integrate text with visual information -- charts, tables, and diagrams. Today, we are integrating information from two different written sources. This is a skill you will use constantly in school and in life: reading multiple sources on a topic and building a complete understanding from all of them."

Write on the board:
- Source 1 (marine biologist): "Great white sharks can detect a single drop of blood in 25 gallons of water and can sense electrical fields generated by the muscles of nearby fish."
- Source 2 (ocean safety expert): "Despite their fearsome reputation, great white sharks are responsible for fewer than five fatal attacks on humans worldwide per year. You are far more likely to be struck by lightning than to be killed by a shark."

Say: "Each source tells you something true about great white sharks. But each one gives you a different piece of the picture. The marine biologist explains what sharks can do. The safety expert puts the danger in perspective. If you only read Source 1, you might be terrified. If you only read Source 2, you might wonder what the fuss is about. When you integrate both, you understand that sharks are remarkably capable predators but rarely attack humans. That is a richer, more accurate understanding than either source alone provides."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students a systematic process for integrating information from two written sources on the same topic.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Synthesis:** Combining information from multiple sources to create a new, unified understanding that goes beyond what any single source provides
- **Overlap:** Information that appears in both sources, often confirming shared facts or ideas
- **Unique contribution:** Information that only one source provides, adding a dimension the other source lacks
- **Conflicting information:** When two sources present different facts, claims, or interpretations of the same topic

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When integrating two sources, I use a strategy called Compare, Combine, Conclude."

Write on the board:
1. **Compare** -- Read both sources. What does each say? Where do they agree? Where do they differ? What does each uniquely contribute?
2. **Combine** -- Put the information together. Fill in the Source Integration Matrix: Source 1 Only, Source 2 Only, Both Sources.
3. **Conclude** -- Write a synthesis statement: What do I now understand about this topic that I could not have understood from either source alone?

Say: "Let me model this with two real passages."

### Worked Examples

**Source 1: "Mars: The Next Frontier"**

> Mars has captured the imagination of scientists and explorers for centuries, and recent technological advances have brought the dream of human settlement closer to reality. The planet's surface features include vast canyons, extinct volcanoes, and polar ice caps that contain frozen water. NASA's Perseverance rover, which landed in 2021, has been analyzing Martian rock and soil samples for signs of ancient microbial life. Private companies have announced plans to send crewed missions to Mars within the next two decades. Advocates argue that establishing a human presence on Mars would ensure the long-term survival of our species by making humanity a multi-planetary civilization.

**Source 2: "The Challenges of Living on Mars"**

> While the idea of colonizing Mars captures public enthusiasm, scientists caution that the practical obstacles are enormous and potentially insurmountable. Mars has no breathable atmosphere; its air is 96 percent carbon dioxide with almost no oxygen. Surface temperatures average minus 80 degrees Fahrenheit, and the planet has no global magnetic field to shield inhabitants from deadly solar and cosmic radiation. A one-way trip to Mars would take approximately seven months, during which astronauts would face muscle and bone loss from prolonged weightlessness. Growing food on Mars would require constructing sealed greenhouses, importing soil nutrients, and developing systems to extract water from ice deposits -- all without the supply chains that sustain life on Earth. The estimated cost of a crewed Mars mission exceeds $100 billion.

Say: "Step 1 -- Compare. Let me identify what each source covers."

Write on the board:
- **Source 1 only:** Mars features (canyons, volcanoes, ice caps), Perseverance rover research, private company plans, multi-planetary civilization argument
- **Source 2 only:** No breathable atmosphere (96% CO2), average temperature (-80 degrees F), no magnetic field/radiation risk, muscle/bone loss during travel, food-growing challenges, cost ($100 billion+)
- **Both sources:** Mars has water (ice), humans may go to Mars in the future

Say: "Step 2 -- Combine. Source 1 focuses on the excitement and potential. Source 2 focuses on the dangers and costs. Together, they give me a balanced picture."

Say: "Step 3 -- Conclude. Here is my synthesis statement."

Write on the board:
**Synthesis:** While Mars has features that make human exploration scientifically compelling -- including water ice and evidence that might reveal ancient life -- the practical challenges of surviving on Mars are severe, ranging from a toxic atmosphere and extreme radiation to the staggering cost of a crewed mission. A complete understanding of Mars colonization requires considering both the inspiring possibilities and the formidable obstacles.

Say: "That synthesis statement could not have come from either source alone. It weaves together the potential from Source 1 with the challenges from Source 2 into a unified understanding."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice integrating two sources with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us work through two new sources together."

**Source 1: "The Benefits of Bilingualism"**

> Research in cognitive science has revealed that bilingual individuals -- people who speak two or more languages fluently -- enjoy significant mental advantages over monolingual speakers. Studies show that bilingual people are better at multitasking, problem-solving, and filtering out irrelevant information. The constant practice of switching between languages strengthens executive function, the set of mental skills that includes working memory, flexible thinking, and self-control. Bilingualism has also been linked to delayed onset of dementia: bilingual individuals develop symptoms of cognitive decline an average of four to five years later than monolinguals. These benefits appear regardless of which languages a person speaks or when they learned them.

**Source 2: "The Struggles of Bilingual Students"**

> For many bilingual students in American schools, speaking two languages is less a cognitive advantage and more a daily challenge. Students who are classified as English Language Learners often struggle on standardized tests that measure knowledge through English-only assessments, even when they understand the underlying content. Teachers who speak only English may misinterpret language-processing delays as learning disabilities, leading to inappropriate special education referrals. Bilingual students also face social pressures: some feel embarrassed about speaking their home language at school, while others feel caught between two cultural identities. Researchers note that the cognitive benefits of bilingualism are most fully realized when both languages are supported and valued, yet many school systems provide inadequate resources for heritage language maintenance.

Say: "Let us compare. What does Source 1 uniquely contribute?"

Expected response: Cognitive benefits -- better multitasking, problem-solving, stronger executive function, delayed dementia onset.

Say: "What does Source 2 uniquely contribute?"

Expected response: Real-world challenges -- testing barriers, misidentification as learning disabled, social pressures, inadequate school support for home languages.

Say: "Where do the sources connect or overlap?"

Expected response: Both are about bilingualism. Source 2's final sentence -- that cognitive benefits require both languages to be "supported and valued" -- actually connects to Source 1's findings by suggesting that the benefits are conditional, not automatic.

Say: "Good. Now write a synthesis statement that combines both perspectives."

Expected response: While research shows that bilingualism provides significant cognitive benefits including stronger executive function and delayed dementia, these benefits are most fully realized when schools and communities support both languages. In practice, many bilingual students face testing barriers, social pressures, and inadequate support that prevent them from fully experiencing the advantages their bilingualism could offer. (Accept reasonable variations that draw from both sources.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently integrate information from two sources on the same topic.

**Source 1: "The Promise of Vertical Farming"**

> Vertical farming -- the practice of growing crops in stacked, indoor layers using artificial lighting and controlled environments -- could revolutionize food production. Unlike traditional farms, vertical farms can operate year-round regardless of weather or season, producing harvests every few weeks rather than once or twice per year. These indoor farms use up to 95 percent less water than conventional agriculture because water is recirculated through closed-loop systems. Vertical farms can be built in cities, dramatically reducing the distance food must travel from farm to plate. A single vertical farm occupying one acre of urban land can produce the equivalent yield of 10 to 20 acres of outdoor farmland. Leafy greens, herbs, and strawberries are already being grown commercially in vertical farms in cities including Newark, New Jersey, and Copenhagen, Denmark.

**Source 2: "The Limitations of Vertical Farming"**

> Despite enthusiastic projections, vertical farming faces significant limitations that prevent it from replacing conventional agriculture at scale. The most critical challenge is energy consumption: the artificial lighting required to grow crops indoors demands enormous amounts of electricity, making vertical farms far more energy-intensive than sunlit outdoor fields. A 2020 analysis estimated that growing wheat in a vertical farm would require energy inputs eight times greater than the value of the wheat produced. Current vertical farms are limited almost entirely to leafy greens and herbs because staple crops such as wheat, rice, and corn require far too much light and space to be grown indoors economically. The construction cost of a commercial vertical farm typically ranges from $10 million to $100 million, putting the technology out of reach for most communities. Critics argue that investing in sustainable outdoor farming practices, such as drip irrigation and cover cropping, would produce greater food security at a fraction of the cost.

**Directions:** Use the Compare, Combine, Conclude strategy. Cite evidence from both sources.

1. Create a Source Integration Matrix: list at least two pieces of information unique to Source 1, two unique to Source 2, and one area of overlap or connection.
2. Source 1 states that vertical farms use "up to 95 percent less water." Source 2 does not mention water savings. Does this mean Source 2 disagrees? Explain why a source might omit certain information.
3. Source 2 notes that vertical farms are "limited almost entirely to leafy greens and herbs." How does this connect to or complicate Source 1's claim that vertical farms could "revolutionize food production"?
4. Write a 3-4 sentence synthesis statement about vertical farming that integrates information from both sources. Your synthesis should present a balanced understanding that neither source alone provides.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with evidence from both sources.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. In your synthesis, cite at least one specific detail from each source.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a paragraph evaluating which source provides a more complete picture of vertical farming and why. Consider what each source includes and omits.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.9) What is the shared topic of both sources? Write a sentence identifying the topic and what each source's perspective on it is.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.2) What is the central idea of Source 2? Write it as a complete sentence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Source A: "Honeybees pollinate approximately one-third of the food crops consumed worldwide, making them essential to global food security."
>
> Source B: "Honeybee populations in North America have declined by nearly 40 percent since 2010, primarily due to pesticide exposure, habitat loss, and a parasitic mite called Varroa destructor."

1. What does each source uniquely contribute to your understanding of honeybees?
2. Write one synthesis sentence that integrates information from both sources.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Source A uniquely contributes the importance of honeybees to food production (pollinating one-third of food crops). Source B uniquely contributes the severity of population decline (40 percent since 2010) and the specific causes (pesticides, habitat loss, Varroa mites).
2. Synthesis: The 40 percent decline in North American honeybee populations since 2010 is an urgent threat to global food security because honeybees pollinate approximately one-third of the world's food crops, and the factors driving this decline -- pesticides, habitat loss, and parasitic mites -- show no signs of disappearing on their own. (Accept reasonable variations that combine specific information from both sources.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with unique contributions identified from each source and synthesis statement integrating both**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Compare, Combine, Conclude strategy using the Mars passages from Direct Instruction
- Use the Source Integration Matrix graphic organizer with simpler, shorter passages (2-3 sentences each)
- Practice identifying unique contributions by color-coding: highlight Source 1 information in one color and Source 2 information in another

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10, the integrated analysis review
- Encourage the student to practice integrating information when reading about the same topic in two different textbook sections or articles

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Three-Source Challenge:** Provide a third short source on vertical farming (or another topic from the lesson) and have the student expand their synthesis to include all three sources. Discuss how adding a third perspective changes the understanding.
2. **Conflict Resolution:** Provide two sources that present directly contradicting claims about a topic. Have the student identify the conflict, evaluate which source provides stronger evidence, and write a paragraph explaining their reasoning.
3. **Research Simulation:** Give the student a research question (e.g., "Should schools start later in the morning?"). Provide two sources with different perspectives and have the student write a short research paragraph that cites both sources.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often treat integration as merely listing what each source says. Push them beyond parallel summaries toward true synthesis: "How do these sources work together to give me a complete picture?"
- The distinction between omission and disagreement (question 2 of Independent Practice) is important. Just because a source does not mention something does not mean it disagrees. Sources have different purposes and scopes. Help students understand this nuance.
- When sources conflict, students may feel uncomfortable because they expect texts to agree. Teach them that disagreement between sources is normal and that evaluating conflicting claims is a critical thinking skill, not a problem to avoid.
- This lesson connects strongly to research writing. When students can integrate sources analytically, they are prepared for the multi-source research projects they will encounter in Grades 7-8 and beyond.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Source Integration Matrix:
   - Source 1 only: (a) Vertical farms use up to 95 percent less water than conventional agriculture. (b) A single vertical farm on one acre can yield the equivalent of 10 to 20 acres of outdoor farmland.
   - Source 2 only: (a) Growing wheat in a vertical farm would require energy inputs eight times greater than the wheat's value. (b) Construction costs range from $10 million to $100 million.
   - Overlap/connection: Both mention that vertical farms currently grow leafy greens and herbs. Source 1 presents this as a success; Source 2 presents it as a limitation showing that staple crops cannot yet be grown economically.
2. Source 2 does not disagree about water savings -- it simply does not mention them. A source might omit information because it falls outside the scope of the author's purpose. Source 2's purpose is to discuss limitations, so the author focuses on energy costs, crop restrictions, and construction expenses. Water savings, while real, do not serve the author's argument. This is an example of how purpose shapes content (as learned in Lesson 7).
3. Source 1 claims vertical farms could "revolutionize food production," implying broad transformation. Source 2 complicates this by noting that vertical farms are "limited almost entirely to leafy greens and herbs" and that staple crops like wheat, rice, and corn cannot be grown economically. Since staple crops feed most of the world's population, the inability to grow them indoors means vertical farming cannot replace conventional agriculture. The revolution Source 1 describes may be limited to a small segment of food production, not the entire system.
4. Synthesis: Vertical farming offers real advantages for food production, including dramatic water savings and the ability to grow crops year-round in urban settings, but its current limitations prevent it from replacing conventional agriculture. The technology works well for leafy greens and herbs, yet staple crops remain economically impossible to grow indoors due to enormous energy costs. With construction expenses reaching $100 million, vertical farming may serve as a valuable supplement to traditional agriculture rather than a wholesale revolution in how the world feeds itself.

### Interleaved Review
5. Both sources are about vertical farming. Source 1 focuses on the potential benefits and promises of the technology, while Source 2 focuses on the practical limitations and economic challenges that prevent vertical farming from scaling up.
6. Central idea of Source 2: Vertical farming faces significant limitations -- including high energy costs, restriction to a narrow range of crops, and enormous construction expenses -- that prevent it from replacing conventional agriculture at scale.
