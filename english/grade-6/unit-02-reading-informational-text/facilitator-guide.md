# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2: Reading Informational Text

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview
Unit 2 builds students' capacity to read, analyze, and critically evaluate informational texts at the sixth-grade level. Moving beyond the Grade 5 emphasis on quoting accurately, identifying multiple main ideas, and integrating two sources, students now learn to cite textual evidence with precision, determine how central ideas are conveyed through supporting details, analyze how authors introduce and elaborate on key individuals, events, and ideas, assess an author's point of view or purpose, and integrate information presented in different media and formats. By the end of this unit, students will approach informational reading as critical analysts who can trace how an author builds meaning and synthesize information across varied sources and formats.

### Prerequisite Skills (Grade 5)
Students should be able to:
- Quote accurately from a text when explaining what the text says explicitly and when drawing inferences (RI.5.1)
- Determine two or more main ideas of a text and explain how they are supported by key details; summarize the text (RI.5.2)
- Explain the relationships or interactions between two or more individuals, events, ideas, or concepts in a historical, scientific, or technical text (RI.5.3)
- Integrate information from several texts on the same topic in order to write or speak about the subject knowledgeably (RI.5.9)

### Facilitation Tips
- **Elevate citation beyond quoting.** In Grade 5, students learned to quote accurately. In Grade 6, citing evidence means selecting the most relevant evidence and explaining how it supports a specific analytical claim. Push students to connect their evidence to their reasoning, not just drop in a quote.
- **Model analytical thinking aloud.** During Direct Instruction, verbalize your thought process: how you reread a passage to identify the central idea, why you select one detail over another, and how you notice an author's word choices signaling purpose. Students at this age benefit from seeing the invisible cognitive work.
- **Teach summary as a disciplined skill.** RI.6.2 requires summaries "distinct from personal opinions or judgments." Explicitly teach students to strip out reactions and evaluations. Practice identifying opinion language ("I think," "It was interesting") and replacing it with neutral restatement.
- **Use annotation as a habit.** Encourage students to annotate consistently: underline central ideas, circle key vocabulary, bracket evidence, and write margin notes about author's purpose. This active reading builds the analytical muscles needed for all five standards.
- **Connect standards across lessons.** Help students see that citing evidence (RI.6.1) supports every other standard. When they determine a central idea (RI.6.2), they must cite evidence. When they analyze author's purpose (RI.6.6), they must cite evidence. Reinforce this connection throughout the unit.
- **Leverage diverse topics for engagement.** Passages cover science, history, geography, technology, and social issues. Use topic variety to sustain student interest and show that informational reading skills apply across all disciplines.
- **Use the difficulty tiers flexibly.** Assign tiers based on exit ticket performance from the previous lesson, not on fixed ability groupings. A student might be "on-level" for central idea work but need "stretch" support for author's purpose. Let the data guide your decisions.
- **Prepare for multimedia integration.** Lessons 8-9 involve text-described charts, tables, and visual elements. While the passages describe these elements in words, consider projecting or drawing simple versions on the board to make the multimedia integration more concrete.

### Common Misconceptions
| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| Students think citing evidence means copying any sentence from the text | Teach that effective citation requires selecting the most relevant evidence and explaining its connection to the analytical point. Not all sentences qualify as strong evidence for a given claim. |
| Students confuse the topic of a passage with its central idea | The topic is what the text is about in one or two words (e.g., "coral reefs"). The central idea is the specific point the author makes about the topic (e.g., "Coral reefs are declining due to rising ocean temperatures"). Practice distinguishing these with multiple examples. |
| Students include personal opinions in summaries | RI.6.2 explicitly requires summaries distinct from personal opinions. Teach students to check their summaries for opinion language and replace it with neutral restatement. Provide before-and-after examples. |
| Students think author's purpose is always one of three options: to inform, persuade, or entertain | While these are useful starting categories, RI.6.6 asks students to determine point of view or purpose with more nuance. An author might write to raise awareness, challenge assumptions, inspire action, or document history. Expand the vocabulary of purpose. |
| Students treat visual and text information as separate rather than integrated | RI.6.7 requires integration, not parallel reading. Teach students to ask: "What does the visual add that the text alone does not? What does the text explain that the visual alone does not?" The goal is a combined, richer understanding. |
| Students struggle to distinguish between how a person is introduced versus how they are elaborated | Introduction is the author's first presentation of a key figure -- what details are given first, what context is established. Elaboration is how the author builds on that introduction with examples, anecdotes, quotes, or data. Teach these as sequential moves in a text. |

### Assessment Guidance
- **Formative:** Use exit tickets after every lesson to assess mastery of that lesson's target standard. Review results before the next session and reteach as needed. Pay particular attention to whether students are citing evidence effectively (RI.6.1), as this skill underpins all other standards in the unit.
- **Summative:** The Unit Assessment includes original informational passages with approximately 25 questions covering all five standards. It features a mix of multiple-choice, short-answer, and extended-response items totaling approximately 60 points. Mastery is 70% overall (42 out of 60 points), with at least 50% on each individual standard.
- **Portfolio:** Consider having students maintain a portfolio of annotated passages and written responses throughout the unit. This provides additional evidence of growth and allows students to see their own progress in analytical reading.

### Differentiation at a Glance
- **Below grade level:** Provide shorter passages (80-120 words) or pre-annotated versions with key evidence highlighted. Pre-teach vocabulary before each lesson. Use sentence frames for written responses (e.g., "The central idea of this passage is ___ because the author states, '___'"). Allow students to discuss answers orally before writing. Pair reading with audio support when possible.
- **On grade level:** Use the lesson passages and questions as written. Students complete the On-level tier of independent practice. Encourage students to write responses in complete sentences that cite specific evidence and explain its relevance.
- **Above grade level:** Assign the Challenge tier of independent practice. Encourage students to compare passages across lessons, identify patterns in author techniques, and write their own informational paragraphs that use specific elaboration strategies. Have students analyze real-world informational texts (news articles, science reports) using the skills from this unit.

### Connection to Future Units
The analytical reading skills developed in this unit form the foundation for increasingly sophisticated work with informational and argumentative texts in Grades 7-8. Citing evidence (RI.6.1) prepares students for Grade 7, where they must cite several pieces of evidence and distinguish among them. Central idea analysis (RI.6.2) builds toward Grade 7's requirement to analyze the development of two or more central ideas. The author introduction and elaboration skills (RI.6.3) prepare students to analyze how authors distinguish their positions from others. Author's purpose work (RI.6.6) leads directly into Grade 7-8 argument evaluation and claim analysis. Media integration (RI.6.7) prepares students for the increasingly multimodal texts they will encounter in later grades and in everyday life.
