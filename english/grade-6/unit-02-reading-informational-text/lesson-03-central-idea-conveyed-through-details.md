# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 3

## Topic
Central Idea Conveyed Through Details

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Analyze how specific details in an informational text convey and develop the central idea
- Trace the relationship between individual details and the central idea they collectively support
- Explain how removing or changing a key detail would affect the reader's understanding of the central idea
- Distinguish between details that directly support the central idea and details that provide background context

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine the central idea of an informational text (Lesson 2)
- Ability to write an objective summary distinct from personal opinions (Lesson 2)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lesson 1)
- Understanding of supporting details from Grade 5 (RI.5.2)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Detail-to-Central-Idea Map graphic organizer (arrows pointing from detail boxes to central idea box)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (one for central idea, one for supporting details)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review central idea identification from Lesson 2 and introduce the concept of how details convey meaning.

**Activity: Building Blocks**

Say: "In Lesson 2, you learned to determine the central idea of a passage. Today, we are going deeper. We are going to examine how the author uses specific details to build the central idea piece by piece -- like bricks in a wall."

Write on the board:
- Central Idea: Space exploration has produced technologies that benefit everyday life on Earth.
- Detail A: NASA's research on water purification for astronauts led to filtration systems used in homes today.
- Detail B: Memory foam, originally developed for spacecraft seats, is now used in mattresses and shoes.
- Detail C: The International Space Station orbits Earth every 90 minutes.

Say: "Which details directly support the central idea? Which one does not?"

Allow the student to respond.

Say: "Details A and B directly support the central idea because they give specific examples of space technologies that benefit everyday life. Detail C is interesting, but it does not connect to the idea of technologies benefiting life on Earth. Authors choose their details carefully. Today we are going to analyze why the details an author includes matter and how they work together to convey the central idea."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to trace how individual details work together to convey and develop the central idea across a passage.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Convey:** To communicate or express a meaning; when details convey a central idea, they communicate it to the reader through specific evidence
- **Develop:** To build or expand on an idea by adding information, examples, and evidence that make the idea clearer and more convincing
- **Background detail:** Information that provides context but does not directly support the central idea
- **Supporting detail:** A fact, example, or explanation that directly reinforces or proves the central idea

### Instruction Notes

Say: "To analyze how details convey a central idea, I use a strategy called Detail Mapping. Here is how it works."

Write on the board:
1. **Identify the central idea** using the Three-Pass Method from Lesson 2.
2. **Highlight each major detail** in the passage.
3. **For each detail, ask:** Does this directly support the central idea? If so, how? If not, what purpose does it serve?
4. **Map the connections:** Draw arrows from each supporting detail to the central idea and label what each detail contributes.

Say: "Let me show you how this works with a real passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Hidden Cost of Fast Fashion"**

> The global fashion industry produces more than 100 billion garments each year, yet a significant portion of this clothing ends up in landfills within months of being purchased. Fast fashion -- the rapid production of inexpensive, trendy clothing -- has made it possible for consumers to buy more clothes than ever before, but the environmental cost is staggering. The textile industry is responsible for approximately 10 percent of global carbon emissions, more than international flights and maritime shipping combined. Dyeing and treating fabrics uses an estimated 20 percent of the world's industrial wastewater, polluting rivers and groundwater in manufacturing regions across Asia and Central America. Synthetic fabrics such as polyester, which make up roughly 60 percent of all clothing produced today, shed microscopic plastic fibers during washing that end up in oceans and waterways. Meanwhile, the average American discards approximately 80 pounds of clothing per year, most of which takes decades or centuries to decompose in landfills.

Say: "First, I identify the central idea. What is the author's most important point? The passage is about fast fashion and its environmental harm. So the central idea is: The fast fashion industry causes severe environmental damage through carbon emissions, water pollution, plastic contamination, and textile waste."

Say: "Now I highlight each major detail and ask how it conveys the central idea."

Write on the board:
- Detail 1: "Textile industry is responsible for approximately 10 percent of global carbon emissions" -- conveys environmental damage through air pollution
- Detail 2: "Dyeing and treating fabrics uses an estimated 20 percent of the world's industrial wastewater" -- conveys environmental damage through water pollution
- Detail 3: "Synthetic fabrics shed microscopic plastic fibers" -- conveys environmental damage through ocean contamination
- Detail 4: "Average American discards approximately 80 pounds of clothing per year" -- conveys environmental damage through massive waste
- Background detail: "More than 100 billion garments each year" -- provides scale and context but does not describe specific environmental harm

Say: "Notice how each supporting detail adds a different dimension of environmental damage. The author does not just say 'fast fashion is bad for the environment.' Instead, the author conveys that central idea through four specific types of harm: carbon emissions, water pollution, plastic contamination, and landfill waste. Each detail makes the central idea more convincing and complete."

Say: "Now ask yourself: If the author removed Detail 2 about wastewater, would the central idea change? No, but it would be weaker. The reader would not understand the full scope of the damage. That is how details convey and develop a central idea -- each one adds evidence that makes the point stronger."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice tracing how details convey central ideas with facilitator support.

Say: "Let us work through this passage together using Detail Mapping."

**Passage B: "The Power of Sleep for Young Athletes"**

> For young athletes between the ages of ten and sixteen, sleep may be the single most important factor in athletic performance and injury prevention. During deep sleep, the body releases human growth hormone, which repairs muscle tissue damaged during exercise and builds new muscle fibers. Research from Stanford University found that when basketball players extended their sleep to ten hours per night for several weeks, their sprint speed improved by five percent and their free-throw accuracy increased by nine percent. A study published in the Journal of Pediatric Orthopedics reported that adolescent athletes who slept fewer than eight hours per night were 1.7 times more likely to suffer an injury compared to those who slept eight or more hours. Sleep also plays a critical role in reaction time and decision-making. The brain processes and consolidates motor skills during sleep, meaning that a basketball dribbling drill or a tennis serve practiced during the day is refined and strengthened during the night. Athletes who prioritize sleep consistently outperform those who sacrifice rest for additional training.

Say: "Step 1 -- what is the central idea of this passage?"

Expected response: Sleep is essential for young athletes because it improves performance, prevents injuries, and enhances mental processing of skills.

Say: "Good. Now Step 2 -- let us identify the major details. What details does the author include?"

Guide the student to identify:
- Growth hormone released during deep sleep repairs and builds muscle
- Stanford study: extended sleep improved sprint speed by 5% and free-throw accuracy by 9%
- Pediatric Orthopedics study: athletes sleeping less than 8 hours were 1.7 times more likely to be injured
- Brain processes and consolidates motor skills during sleep
- Athletes who prioritize sleep outperform those who sacrifice rest

Say: "Step 3 -- how does each detail convey the central idea? What does each one contribute?"

Expected responses:
- Growth hormone detail: conveys the physical recovery benefit of sleep
- Stanford study: conveys the performance improvement benefit with measurable data
- Injury study: conveys the injury prevention benefit with a specific statistic
- Motor skills detail: conveys the mental processing benefit of sleep
- Final sentence: directly restates the central idea as a conclusion

Say: "Excellent. Notice that the author uses a different type of evidence for each aspect of the central idea: biological explanation, research data, injury statistics, and cognitive science. This variety of detail types makes the central idea more convincing than if the author had only used one type."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how details convey the central idea in a new passage.

**Passage C: "The Amazon Rainforest as Earth's Climate Regulator"**

> The Amazon rainforest, spanning approximately 2.1 million square miles across nine South American countries, plays a role in Earth's climate system that extends far beyond its borders. The forest's trees absorb an estimated 2 billion tons of carbon dioxide each year through photosynthesis, effectively removing a significant portion of the greenhouse gases that contribute to global warming. This process earns the Amazon the title of "the lungs of the planet," though scientists note that the forest also produces about half the oxygen it consumes. The Amazon generates roughly 20 percent of its own rainfall through a process called transpiration, in which trees release moisture into the atmosphere that forms clouds and falls again as rain. This moisture cycle does not stop at the forest's edge; wind patterns carry Amazonian moisture southward, supplying rainfall to agricultural regions in Brazil and Argentina that produce food for millions of people. Deforestation disrupts these processes. When trees are cut and burned, the stored carbon is released back into the atmosphere, turning the forest from a carbon absorber into a carbon source. Scientists warn that if deforestation continues at current rates, the Amazon could reach a tipping point where it can no longer sustain its own rainfall cycle, transforming from dense rainforest into dry savanna.

**Directions:** Use Detail Mapping for each question. Cite textual evidence in your responses.

1. What is the central idea of this passage?
2. Identify four specific details the author uses to convey the central idea. For each detail, explain what aspect of the central idea it supports.
3. The passage mentions that "the forest also produces about half the oxygen it consumes." Is this a supporting detail for the central idea or a background detail? Explain your reasoning.
4. How would the passage's central idea be weakened if the author removed the information about deforestation in the final three sentences?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with accurate detail identification and clear explanations.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. In question 2, categorize each detail by type (statistic, scientific explanation, comparison, example).
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a paragraph explaining whether the author's choice of details makes the central idea convincing. Cite at least three specific details from the passage in your evaluation.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.2) Write an objective summary of the passage in 3-4 sentences. Do not include personal opinions.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.1) Quote the sentence that explains what happens when Amazon trees are cut and burned.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Honeybees communicate the location of food sources through an elaborate dance. A bee that discovers a rich flower patch returns to the hive and performs a figure-eight pattern called a waggle dance. The angle of the dance indicates the direction of the food relative to the sun, and the duration of the waggling portion signals the distance.

1. What is the central idea of this passage?
2. Name one detail and explain how it conveys the central idea.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Central idea: Honeybees use a sophisticated dance to communicate precise information about food locations to other bees in the hive.
2. Example: The detail that "the angle of the dance indicates the direction of the food relative to the sun" conveys the central idea by showing that the dance communicates specific directional information, not just general excitement about food. (Accept any detail with a clear explanation of its connection.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with central idea expressed as a complete sentence and detail-to-central-idea connection clearly explained**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review Detail Mapping using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Provide a passage with three details and have the student physically draw arrows connecting each detail to the central idea on paper
- Practice with simpler passages where the central idea is stated explicitly and details are clearly labeled with numbers
- Have the student verbally explain: "This detail supports the central idea because..."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 4 on how key individuals are introduced in a text
- Encourage the student to practice identifying how details support central ideas in textbook chapters or magazine articles

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Detail Surgery:** Give the student a passage and ask them to identify which one detail could be removed with the least impact on the central idea, and which detail would weaken the central idea most if removed. They explain their reasoning in writing.
2. **Build a Passage:** Provide a central idea (e.g., "Space debris is a growing threat to satellites and space exploration"). The student writes 4-5 supporting details that convey this central idea, then arranges them into a coherent paragraph.
3. **Detail Type Analysis:** Have the student categorize the details in the passage by type (statistics, scientific explanations, examples, expert quotes, comparisons) and discuss which type is most persuasive and why.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often list details without explaining the connection to the central idea. Always push for the "so what" -- why does this detail matter for the author's main point?
- The distinction between supporting details and background details is nuanced. Background details provide necessary context (like the size of the Amazon) while supporting details directly prove the central idea (like the carbon absorption statistic). Accept reasonable debate about borderline cases.
- When students struggle to see how details work together, try covering up all but one detail and asking, "Is the central idea convincing with just this one fact?" Then reveal details one at a time to show how each adds strength.
- This lesson pairs well with writing instruction. When students understand how published authors use details to convey central ideas, they can apply the same technique in their own informational writing.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Central idea: The Amazon rainforest plays a critical role in regulating Earth's climate through carbon absorption and rainfall generation, but deforestation threatens to destroy these functions. (Accept reasonable variations that capture both the climate role and the threat of deforestation.)
2. Four details and their connections:
   - "Trees absorb an estimated 2 billion tons of carbon dioxide each year" -- supports the central idea by showing the Amazon's role as a carbon absorber that fights global warming.
   - "The Amazon generates roughly 20 percent of its own rainfall through transpiration" -- supports the central idea by showing the forest's role in creating its own water cycle.
   - "Wind patterns carry Amazonian moisture southward, supplying rainfall to agricultural regions" -- supports the central idea by showing the forest's climate influence extends beyond its borders.
   - "When trees are cut and burned, the stored carbon is released back into the atmosphere" -- supports the central idea by showing how deforestation reverses the forest's climate benefits.
3. The oxygen detail is a background detail, not a direct support for the central idea. While it provides interesting context about the forest's biological processes, the central idea focuses on the Amazon's role as a climate regulator (carbon absorption and rainfall generation). The oxygen fact actually qualifies the "lungs of the planet" nickname rather than strengthening the argument about climate regulation.
4. If the deforestation information were removed, the reader would understand that the Amazon is important for climate regulation but would not understand the urgency of the threat. The passage would describe a stable system rather than one in danger. The central idea would shift from "the Amazon is critical but threatened" to merely "the Amazon is important," which is a weaker and less complete argument.

### Interleaved Review
5. Objective summary: The Amazon rainforest absorbs approximately 2 billion tons of carbon dioxide annually and generates much of its own rainfall through transpiration. This moisture cycle extends to agricultural regions beyond the forest's borders. However, deforestation releases stored carbon back into the atmosphere and threatens to disrupt the rainfall cycle, potentially transforming the forest into dry savanna.
6. "When trees are cut and burned, the stored carbon is released back into the atmosphere, turning the forest from a carbon absorber into a carbon source."
