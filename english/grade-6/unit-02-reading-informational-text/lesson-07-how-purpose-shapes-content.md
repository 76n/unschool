# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 7

## Topic
How Purpose Shapes Content

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain how an author's purpose influences which facts, examples, and details are included or excluded from a text
- Analyze how the same topic is treated differently depending on the author's purpose
- Identify how an author's point of view is conveyed through emphasis, omission, and framing
- Evaluate whether an author's purpose affects the reliability or completeness of the information presented

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine an author's purpose using word choice, detail selection, and structure clues (Lesson 6)
- Ability to analyze how events and ideas are elaborated in a text (Lesson 5)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lesson 1)
- Understanding of author's point of view from Grade 5

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages (including paired passages on the same topic)
- Purpose-Content Connection Chart graphic organizer (columns: Author's Purpose, Details Included, Details Likely Omitted, Effect on Reader)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate how purpose determines content by showing two versions of the same topic with dramatically different approaches.

**Activity: Two Views of the Same City**

Say: "In Lesson 6, you learned to detect an author's purpose. Today, we are going one step deeper: we are going to analyze how purpose shapes what an author chooses to include and what they leave out. An author's purpose acts like a filter -- it determines which facts make it into the text and which do not."

Write on the board:
- Writer A (tourism bureau): "Our city boasts 300 days of sunshine per year, world-class restaurants, and miles of pristine beaches that attract visitors from around the globe."
- Writer B (environmental report): "The city's coastline is eroding at a rate of three feet per year due to rising sea levels, and overdevelopment along the shore has destroyed critical wetland habitats."

Say: "Same city. Completely different content. Why? Because the purpose is different. Writer A wants to attract tourists. Writer B wants to raise environmental concerns. Each author selected only the facts that serve their purpose. Today, you will learn to see what the author chose to include, what they likely left out, and what that tells you about their purpose and point of view."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to analyze the relationship between author's purpose and the content decisions an author makes.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Framing:** The way an author presents information to influence how the reader interprets it; the angle or lens through which a topic is presented
- **Omission:** Information that the author chooses not to include; what is left out of a text can be as significant as what is included
- **Emphasis:** The details or ideas an author highlights through repetition, placement, or language intensity
- **Point of view:** The author's perspective on or attitude toward the subject, shaped by their experiences, beliefs, and purpose
- **Bias:** A tendency to favor one perspective over others, which can influence which facts an author includes or omits

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When an author has a clear purpose, that purpose shapes the text in three ways. I call them the Three Shaping Forces."

Write on the board:
1. **Emphasis** -- What the author spends the most time on, repeats, or highlights
2. **Omission** -- What the author leaves out or mentions only briefly
3. **Framing** -- How the author presents facts to guide the reader's interpretation

Say: "These forces work together. An author who wants to persuade you that space exploration is worth the cost will emphasize the discoveries and technologies gained from space programs, omit or minimize the financial costs and risks, and frame the investment as essential for humanity's future. Let me show you with a real example."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Zoos: Conservation Champions"**

> Modern zoos play a vital role in protecting endangered species from extinction. Through carefully managed breeding programs, zoos have brought species such as the California condor, the Arabian oryx, and the black-footed ferret back from the brink of disappearance. The Association of Zoos and Aquariums coordinates Species Survival Plans for more than 500 at-risk species, ensuring genetic diversity and maintaining healthy captive populations that could one day be reintroduced to the wild. Zoos also invest heavily in field conservation, contributing more than $230 million annually to protect habitats and support wildlife research around the world. Education is another critical function: the roughly 183 million people who visit accredited zoos in the United States each year leave with a deeper understanding of wildlife and the threats animals face in the wild. Research conducted in zoos has led to breakthroughs in animal medicine, reproductive science, and our understanding of animal behavior. Without zoos, many species that exist today would have no safety net against the threats of habitat destruction, poaching, and climate change.

Say: "Let me apply the Three Shaping Forces."

Write on the board:
- **Purpose:** To persuade the reader that zoos are beneficial for conservation
- **Emphasis:** Breeding success stories (condor, oryx, ferret), funding for conservation ($230 million), education (183 million visitors), research breakthroughs. The author repeatedly highlights positive outcomes.
- **Omission:** The passage does not mention criticisms of zoos -- concerns about animal welfare in captivity, limited space for large animals, animals displaying stress behaviors, or the argument that captive breeding is less effective than protecting wild habitats. These perspectives are entirely absent.
- **Framing:** Zoos are framed as "conservation champions" and a "safety net." This language presents zoos as heroic and essential rather than as institutions with both benefits and drawbacks.

Say: "Now I can see how the author's purpose -- to promote zoos as conservation tools -- shaped every content decision. The author included impressive statistics and success stories while omitting common criticisms. That does not mean the facts are wrong, but it means the picture is incomplete. A reader who only reads this passage would have a one-sided understanding of the issue."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice analyzing how a different author's purpose shapes content on the same topic.

Say: "Now let us read a passage on the same topic -- zoos -- but written with a different purpose. Compare the content choices."

**Passage B: "The Ethics of Captivity"**

> Despite the conservation claims made by the zoo industry, a growing number of scientists, ethicists, and animal welfare organizations argue that keeping wild animals in captivity raises serious moral concerns. Large, intelligent animals such as elephants, orcas, and great apes frequently exhibit signs of psychological distress in zoo environments, including repetitive pacing, self-harm, and abnormal social behavior -- symptoms collectively known as zoochosis. Studies have shown that elephants in zoos typically live less than half as long as elephants in protected wild reserves, suggesting that captivity itself may compromise animal health. Critics also point out that only a small fraction of the species held in zoos are actually endangered; the vast majority are common species kept primarily for public entertainment and revenue. The funds spent on maintaining zoo facilities, critics argue, could achieve far greater conservation impact if directed toward protecting wild habitats and combating poaching. While some breeding programs have produced genuine success stories, the broader claim that zoos are essential for species survival oversimplifies a complex reality. Many conservation biologists believe that protecting and restoring natural habitats is a more effective and more ethical long-term strategy.

Say: "What is the author's purpose in this passage?"

Expected response: To challenge the idea that zoos are beneficial and to raise ethical concerns about keeping animals in captivity.

Say: "Good. Now let us apply the Three Shaping Forces. What does this author emphasize?"

Expected response: The author emphasizes animal suffering (zoochosis, shorter lifespans), the fact that most zoo animals are not endangered, and the argument that money would be better spent on habitat protection.

Say: "What does this author omit?"

Expected response: The author omits or minimizes the specific conservation successes of zoos (the condor, oryx, and ferret are not mentioned). The author does not mention the $230 million in conservation funding or the educational value of zoos. The one mention of breeding success ("some breeding programs have produced genuine success stories") is immediately qualified.

Say: "How does the framing differ from Passage A?"

Expected response: Passage A frames zoos as heroes ("conservation champions," "safety net"). Passage B frames zoos as potentially harmful ("serious moral concerns," "compromise animal health," "primarily for public entertainment and revenue").

Say: "Excellent. Now here is the critical question: Which passage gives you a more complete picture?"

Expected response: Neither one alone is complete. Passage A omits the criticisms. Passage B omits the successes. A reader who wants a full understanding needs information from both. (Guide toward this conclusion.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how purpose shapes content in a new passage.

**Passage C: "Nuclear Energy: A Clean Power Source"**

> As the world searches for ways to reduce carbon emissions and combat climate change, nuclear energy offers one of the most promising solutions. Nuclear power plants generate electricity without burning fossil fuels, producing virtually zero greenhouse gas emissions during operation. A single nuclear plant can power more than 750,000 homes while occupying a fraction of the land required by wind or solar farms of equivalent capacity. France, which generates approximately 70 percent of its electricity from nuclear power, has one of the lowest carbon footprints per capita of any industrialized nation. Modern reactor designs incorporate multiple layers of safety systems that make the risk of a major accident extraordinarily small. The nuclear industry has also made significant progress in reducing waste: the total amount of high-level radioactive waste produced by all nuclear plants in the United States over the past sixty years would fit on a single football field stacked less than ten yards high. With global energy demand projected to increase by 50 percent by 2050, nuclear power provides a proven, scalable technology capable of delivering reliable electricity without contributing to the warming of the planet.

**Directions:** Analyze how the author's purpose shapes the content of this passage. Cite textual evidence in your responses.

1. What is the author's purpose? Cite at least two textual clues that reveal this purpose.
2. What does the author emphasize? Identify at least three facts or details the author highlights and explain how each serves the author's purpose.
3. What important information about nuclear energy does the author likely omit? Name at least two topics or concerns that a balanced treatment would include.
4. How would the content of this passage change if the author's purpose were to raise concerns about nuclear energy? Describe at least two content decisions that would be different.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with textual evidence and clear explanations.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. For question 4, write a brief alternative opening paragraph (3-4 sentences) that treats the same topic with a different purpose.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a paragraph evaluating this passage's reliability as a source for a research project about nuclear energy. Explain what a critical reader should consider when using a purpose-driven text as a source.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.2) What is the central idea of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.3) Explain the cause-and-effect relationship the author draws between nuclear power and France's carbon footprint. Use a signal word.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Community gardens transform neglected urban spaces into vibrant gathering places where neighbors build relationships while growing fresh, healthy food. In cities with high rates of food insecurity, these gardens provide families with access to fruits and vegetables that would otherwise be unaffordable. Studies show that neighborhoods with community gardens experience lower crime rates and higher property values.

1. What is the author's purpose, and what is one detail likely omitted?
2. How does the author's framing (word choice) serve the purpose?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Purpose: To promote community gardens (or to persuade the reader of their value). Likely omitted details: challenges of maintaining gardens (funding, vandalism, volunteer burnout), the limited amount of food a small garden can produce, disagreements about land use, or the fact that community gardens alone cannot solve food insecurity.
2. The author's framing uses positive, appealing language: "transform," "vibrant gathering places," "build relationships," "fresh, healthy food." The word "transform" implies dramatic positive change, and "vibrant" suggests energy and life. This language makes community gardens sound universally beneficial, which serves the persuasive purpose.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with purpose identified, omission named, and framing analyzed with specific word examples**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Three Shaping Forces using Passages A and B from the lesson (the zoo comparison)
- Provide a passage and a list of five possible omissions. Have the student identify which omissions are most significant and explain why.
- Practice framing analysis by rewriting a single sentence in neutral and then in loaded language

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8 on integrating text and visual formats
- Encourage the student to notice how news articles, advertisements, and social media posts frame topics differently depending on purpose

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Omission Detective:** Give the student a persuasive passage and a list of additional facts about the topic. Have the student identify which facts the author omitted and write a paragraph explaining how including those facts would change the reader's understanding.
2. **Purpose Flip:** Have the student rewrite Passage C (nuclear energy) with the opposite purpose -- to raise concerns. They should keep the same topic but change the emphasis, omission, and framing.
3. **Real-World Analysis:** Find two articles about the same current event from different news sources. For each, identify the author's purpose, what is emphasized, what is omitted, and how the framing differs. Write a paragraph comparing the two.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students may feel uncomfortable with the idea that informational texts can have agendas. Emphasize that recognizing purpose does not mean the text is dishonest -- it means the reader is empowered to seek additional perspectives. Critical readers ask, "What else might I need to know?"
- The zoo comparison (Passages A and B) is the most powerful teaching tool in this lesson because students can directly see how two legitimate texts on the same topic present radically different content. Use this comparison to anchor discussions.
- Omission is the hardest concept for students because it requires thinking about what is not there. Help by providing a list of common aspects of a topic and asking, "Which of these did the author include? Which are missing? Why might they be missing?"
- This lesson connects strongly to media literacy. Consider discussing how advertising, political messaging, and social media posts all use emphasis, omission, and framing to shape content for a particular purpose.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Purpose: To persuade the reader that nuclear energy is a clean, safe, and necessary power source. Textual clues: (a) The opening phrase "nuclear energy offers one of the most promising solutions" is a positive judgment, not a neutral statement. (b) The conclusion that nuclear power provides "a proven, scalable technology" frames nuclear energy as the answer to future energy needs. (c) The passage's language throughout is promotional: "virtually zero greenhouse gas emissions," "extraordinarily small" risk, "significant progress in reducing waste."
2. The author emphasizes: (a) Zero greenhouse gas emissions during operation -- this serves the purpose by positioning nuclear as a climate solution. (b) The comparison to France (70% nuclear, low carbon footprint) -- this provides a real-world success story that makes the argument tangible. (c) The small physical volume of waste (fitting on a football field) -- this minimizes the waste concern by framing the amount as surprisingly small. (d) The comparison to wind and solar requiring more land -- this positions nuclear as more efficient than other clean energy options.
3. Likely omissions: (a) The history of nuclear accidents (Three Mile Island, Chernobyl, Fukushima) and their devastating consequences. (b) The challenge of storing radioactive waste safely for thousands of years. (c) The extremely high cost of building new nuclear plants. (d) Concerns about nuclear proliferation and weapons risks. (e) The time required to build new plants (often 10-15 years). A balanced treatment would address these concerns alongside the benefits.
4. If the purpose were to raise concerns: (a) The author would emphasize the history of nuclear disasters and their long-term human and environmental costs instead of highlighting safety improvements. (b) The author would emphasize the thousands-of-years storage requirement for radioactive waste instead of framing the total volume as small. The passage might open with Chernobyl or Fukushima instead of with climate change solutions.

### Interleaved Review
5. Central idea: Nuclear energy is a promising, clean power source that can help combat climate change by generating large amounts of electricity without producing greenhouse gas emissions.
6. Because France generates approximately 70 percent of its electricity from nuclear power, it has one of the lowest carbon footprints per capita of any industrialized nation. The nuclear energy production directly caused (or led to) the lower emissions.
