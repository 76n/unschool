# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 10

## Topic
Informational Text Integrated Analysis

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply all five unit standards (RI.6.1, RI.6.2, RI.6.3, RI.6.6, RI.6.7) to analyze a single informational text
- Cite textual evidence to support both explicit analysis and inferences
- Determine central idea, analyze how individuals and ideas are introduced and elaborated, evaluate author's purpose, and integrate visual and textual information in a unified analysis
- Demonstrate readiness for the unit assessment by completing an integrated analytical task

## Prerequisites
- All prior lessons in Unit 2 (Lessons 1-9)
- Ability to cite evidence (RI.6.1), determine central idea (RI.6.2), analyze introduction and elaboration (RI.6.3), determine author's purpose (RI.6.6), and integrate information across formats and sources (RI.6.7)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Unit Review Checklist (listing all five standards with student self-assessment columns)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- All graphic organizers from the unit (available for reference)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all five standards covered in the unit and help the student see how they work together as an interconnected set of analytical reading skills.

**Activity: Standards Connection Web**

Say: "Over the past nine lessons, you have learned five powerful skills for reading informational text. Today, we are going to put them all together. Let me remind you of what each standard asks you to do."

Write on the board:
- RI.6.1: Cite textual evidence for explicit analysis AND inferences
- RI.6.2: Determine central idea and how details convey it; write objective summaries
- RI.6.3: Analyze how key individuals, events, or ideas are introduced and elaborated
- RI.6.6: Determine author's purpose or point of view and how it is conveyed
- RI.6.7: Integrate information from text and visual formats or across sources

Say: "Here is the important thing: these skills are not separate. They connect. When you determine the central idea (RI.6.2), you must cite evidence (RI.6.1). When you analyze author's purpose (RI.6.6), you look at how details are selected, which connects to how ideas are elaborated (RI.6.3). When you integrate sources (RI.6.7), you apply all the other skills to each source. Today, you will use every skill together on one passage."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model a complete integrated analysis of an informational text, demonstrating how all five standards work together.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Integrated analysis:** Applying multiple analytical skills simultaneously to develop a thorough, multi-layered understanding of a text
- **Analytical claim:** A statement about a text that goes beyond summary to offer an interpretation or evaluation
- **Multi-layered understanding:** Seeing a text from multiple angles -- what it says, what it means, how it is built, and why the author wrote it

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When I do an integrated analysis, I work through the standards in a logical order. Here is the sequence I follow."

Write on the board:
1. Read and annotate (foundation for everything)
2. Determine central idea and trace how details convey it (RI.6.2)
3. Analyze how key individuals, events, or ideas are introduced and elaborated (RI.6.3)
4. Determine author's purpose and how it shapes the text (RI.6.6)
5. Cite evidence throughout to support every analytical point (RI.6.1)
6. Integrate any visual/quantitative information with the text (RI.6.7)

Say: "Watch how I apply all five standards to one passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Fight for Clean Air in Beijing"**

> For decades, Beijing's air was among the most polluted on Earth. Residents routinely wore face masks outdoors, and on the worst days, the city's iconic skyline disappeared behind a thick gray haze that reduced visibility to a few hundred meters. Concentrations of PM2.5 -- fine particulate matter small enough to penetrate deep into the lungs -- regularly exceeded 300 micrograms per cubic meter, more than twelve times the level the World Health Organization considers safe. The health consequences were devastating: respiratory diseases, heart conditions, and lung cancer rates all climbed in direct proportion to pollution levels. In 2013, the Chinese government launched what officials called a "war on pollution," committing more than $270 billion to clean-air initiatives. Coal-burning power plants near the city were shut down or converted to natural gas. Millions of older, high-emission vehicles were removed from the roads. Factories that violated new emissions standards were closed or fined heavily. By 2020, Beijing's average PM2.5 levels had dropped by more than 50 percent compared to 2013 levels.
>
> **Table: Beijing PM2.5 Levels (Annual Average, micrograms per cubic meter)**
>
> | Year | PM2.5 Level | WHO Safe Level |
> |------|-------------|----------------|
> | 2013 | 89 | 15 |
> | 2015 | 81 | 15 |
> | 2017 | 58 | 15 |
> | 2019 | 42 | 15 |
> | 2021 | 33 | 15 |

Say: "Let me walk through my integrated analysis."

Write on the board:
- **RI.6.2 (Central Idea):** Beijing's severe air pollution problem has been significantly reduced through aggressive government action, though pollution remains above safe levels. The details convey this through the contrast between the dire starting point (PM2.5 over 300) and the dramatic improvement (50 percent reduction by 2020).
- **RI.6.3 (Introduction and Elaboration):** The pollution crisis is introduced through vivid description (face masks, invisible skyline, gray haze). It is then elaborated through statistics (PM2.5 exceeding 300), health consequences (respiratory diseases, cancer), and the government response (specific actions: closing coal plants, removing vehicles, fining factories).
- **RI.6.6 (Author's Purpose):** The author's purpose is to raise awareness about Beijing's pollution crisis while highlighting the effectiveness of government action. The language shifts from alarming ("devastating," "war on pollution") to more hopeful ("dropped by more than 50 percent"), suggesting the author wants readers to see both the severity of the problem and the possibility of improvement.
- **RI.6.7 (Integration):** The table adds precision that the text alone does not provide. The text says pollution "dropped by more than 50 percent," but the table shows the actual trajectory year by year: 89, 81, 58, 42, 33. The table also makes visible what the text mentions only indirectly: even at 33, Beijing's air is still more than double the WHO safe level of 15. This nuance -- that progress is real but the problem is not solved -- emerges from integrating text and table.
- **RI.6.1 (Evidence):** Every point above is supported by cited evidence from the passage or table.

Say: "That is an integrated analysis. I used all five skills on one text, and each skill revealed a different layer of meaning."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice integrated analysis with facilitator support, applying all five standards to a new passage.

Say: "Now it is your turn. Let us work through an integrated analysis of this passage together. I will guide you through each standard."

**Passage B: "Dr. Mae Jemison: From Star Trek to the Stars"**

> When Mae Jemison stepped aboard the Space Shuttle Endeavour on September 12, 1992, she carried more than scientific equipment -- she carried the dreams of every young girl who had ever looked up at the night sky and wondered if someone like her could go to space. Jemison, a physician, engineer, and former Peace Corps medical officer, became the first African American woman to travel to space that day. Born in 1956 in Decatur, Alabama, and raised in Chicago, Jemison was inspired as a child by the actress Nichelle Nichols, who played Lieutenant Uhura on the television series Star Trek -- one of the first Black women to appear in a major television role. Jemison later recalled that seeing Uhura on the bridge of the starship Enterprise made her believe that space was not reserved for any one type of person. After earning degrees in chemical engineering and medicine from Stanford and Cornell, Jemison joined NASA's astronaut program in 1987, selected from among more than 2,000 applicants. During her mission, she conducted experiments on weightlessness and motion sickness, bone cell research, and the development of new materials. After leaving NASA in 1993, Jemison founded the Jemison Group, a technology consulting firm, and established the Dorothy Jemison Foundation for Excellence, which runs an international science camp for students aged 12 to 16. She has spent her post-NASA career advocating for science education and diversity in STEM fields, arguing that "the best way to make dreams come true is to wake up and pursue them."

Say: "Let us start with RI.6.3. How does the author introduce Jemison?"

Expected response: The author uses an anecdote lead, opening with Jemison stepping aboard the shuttle and describing what she carried symbolically -- "the dreams of every young girl who had ever looked up at the night sky." This introduction emphasizes her historic significance and the emotional weight of her achievement rather than her credentials.

Say: "Good. How is she elaborated on throughout the passage?"

Expected response: Through biographical context (born in Alabama, raised in Chicago), childhood inspiration (Nichelle Nichols in Star Trek), educational credentials (Stanford, Cornell), professional achievement (selected from 2,000 applicants), specific work during the mission (experiments), and post-NASA contributions (foundation, science camp, advocacy). The author elaborates using examples, anecdotes, and achievements.

Say: "Now RI.6.2. What is the central idea?"

Expected response: Mae Jemison broke barriers as the first African American woman in space and has devoted her career to expanding access to science and space for underrepresented groups. The details convey this through the contrast between a young girl watching Star Trek and an astronaut aboard a real shuttle, and through her post-NASA work advocating for diversity in STEM.

Say: "RI.6.6. What is the author's purpose?"

Expected response: The author's purpose is to celebrate and inspire -- to present Jemison as a role model who overcame barriers and continues to open doors for others. The language is admiring ("carried the dreams of every young girl"), the details emphasize her firsts and her advocacy, and the passage ends with an inspirational quote. The author's point of view is clearly positive.

Say: "And throughout, you need RI.6.1 -- every point must be supported with cited evidence. Excellent work."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently conduct an integrated analysis of a new passage.

**Passage C: "The Great Pacific Garbage Patch"**

> Floating in the Pacific Ocean between California and Hawaii is a vast accumulation of plastic debris that has come to be known as the Great Pacific Garbage Patch. Contrary to popular imagination, the patch is not a solid island of trash that can be seen from space. Instead, it is a diffuse zone of microplastics -- tiny fragments smaller than a grain of rice -- suspended in the water column across an area roughly twice the size of Texas. Ocean currents called gyres concentrate the debris by pulling floating material from coastlines around the Pacific Rim into a slowly rotating center. An estimated 80,000 metric tons of plastic currently float in the patch, with approximately 1.8 trillion individual pieces. Marine animals mistake these tiny fragments for food. A 2019 study found microplastics in the stomachs of 73 percent of deep-sea fish sampled in the North Atlantic, suggesting that plastic contamination has spread far beyond the visible surface accumulations. Nonprofit organizations such as The Ocean Cleanup have deployed experimental systems to capture plastic from the patch, removing more than 200,000 kilograms in their first operational year. However, scientists caution that cleanup alone cannot solve the problem. An estimated 11 million metric tons of new plastic enters the oceans every year, far exceeding the rate at which any technology can remove it.
>
> **Table: Estimated Sources of Ocean Plastic Pollution**
>
> | Source | Percentage of Total |
> |--------|-------------------|
> | Packaging (bottles, bags, wrappers) | 36% |
> | Fishing industry (nets, lines, traps) | 20% |
> | Textiles (synthetic fibers from laundry) | 15% |
> | Construction materials | 12% |
> | Automotive (tires, parts) | 8% |
> | Other sources | 9% |

**Directions:** Conduct a complete integrated analysis. Apply all five standards. Cite textual evidence throughout.

1. **(RI.6.2)** What is the central idea of this passage? How do the details convey it?
2. **(RI.6.3)** How does the author introduce the Great Pacific Garbage Patch? What technique does the author use, and how does this introduction shape the reader's understanding?
3. **(RI.6.6)** What is the author's purpose? Cite at least two textual clues that reveal it.
4. **(RI.6.7)** What does the table add to your understanding that the text alone does not provide? How does integrating the table with the text deepen your understanding of the problem?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with textual evidence and clear explanations.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. For each question, explicitly name the standard you are applying and cite at least one specific detail from the passage.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a 4-5 sentence integrated analysis paragraph that weaves together insights from at least three of the five standards. Your paragraph should demonstrate how the standards work together to produce a layered understanding of the text.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.3) Explain the cause-and-effect relationship between ocean gyres and the accumulation of plastic in the Garbage Patch. Use a signal word.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.1) Quote the sentence that challenges the popular image of the Garbage Patch.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> In 2018, fifteen-year-old Greta Thunberg began skipping school on Fridays to sit outside the Swedish Parliament building holding a sign that read "School Strike for Climate." Within a year, millions of young people around the world had joined her in demanding that governments take urgent action on climate change.

1. How does the author introduce Greta Thunberg? (RI.6.3)
2. What can you infer about the author's purpose? Cite one textual clue. (RI.6.1, RI.6.6)

**Expected Answers:**
1. The author introduces Thunberg through an anecdote lead -- the specific image of a fifteen-year-old skipping school to sit outside Parliament with a sign. This introduction emphasizes her youth and the simplicity of her action, which creates a contrast with the massive global movement that followed. The author characterizes her through action rather than credentials.
2. The author's purpose appears to be to inspire or raise awareness about youth climate activism by presenting Thunberg as someone whose small, bold action sparked a worldwide movement. The textual clue "millions of young people around the world had joined her" frames the outcome as massive and positive, suggesting admiration for the movement. The word "demanding" conveys urgency and agency.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with introduction technique accurately identified and author's purpose supported by a textual clue**

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is designed as a review and integration session before the unit assessment. Adjust the pacing based on the student's readiness. If the student demonstrates strong command of all five standards, move more quickly and spend extra time on the extension activities. If the student struggles with specific standards, use the lesson to reteach those areas.
- The integrated analysis is intentionally demanding. It is acceptable for students to work through it slowly and to refer back to strategies from earlier lessons. The goal is for students to see how the skills connect, not to perform perfectly under time pressure.
- Use this lesson as a formative assessment opportunity. Note which standards the student handles confidently and which need reinforcement before the summative unit assessment.
- Celebrate the student's growth throughout the unit. Point out specific improvements you have observed -- better evidence citation, stronger central idea identification, more nuanced purpose analysis. Confidence matters as much as skill heading into the assessment.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **(RI.6.2)** Central idea: The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is an enormous plastic pollution problem that cleanup efforts alone cannot solve because new plastic enters the ocean far faster than it can be removed. The details convey this through a contrast structure: the passage first establishes the scale of the problem (80,000 metric tons, 1.8 trillion pieces, area twice the size of Texas), then describes cleanup efforts (200,000 kilograms removed), and finally reveals that 11 million metric tons of new plastic enters annually. Each detail builds toward the conclusion that the problem requires prevention at the source, not just cleanup.
2. **(RI.6.3)** The author introduces the Garbage Patch by first naming it and locating it ("floating in the Pacific Ocean between California and Hawaii"), then immediately corrects a common misconception by stating it is "not a solid island of trash." This introduction technique -- naming followed by correction -- shapes the reader's understanding by replacing a vivid but inaccurate image with the more troubling reality that the patch is invisible microplastics "suspended in the water column." This makes the problem feel more insidious and harder to solve than a visible island of trash would be.
3. **(RI.6.6)** The author's purpose is to raise awareness about the severity of ocean plastic pollution and to challenge the assumption that cleanup technology alone can solve it. Textual clues: (a) The word "caution" in "scientists caution that cleanup alone cannot solve the problem" signals a warning, which serves an awareness-raising purpose. (b) The contrast between 200,000 kilograms removed and 11 million metric tons entering annually is structured to create a sense of futility that pushes readers toward thinking about prevention. (c) The correction of the "solid island" misconception suggests the author wants to replace popular myths with accurate understanding.
4. **(RI.6.7)** The table adds specific information about where ocean plastic comes from, which the text does not address. The text describes the problem (accumulation, scale, harm to wildlife) and the attempted solutions (cleanup). The table reveals the sources: packaging is the largest contributor at 36%, followed by the fishing industry at 20% and textiles at 15%. Integrating the table with the text deepens understanding because the text's claim that "cleanup alone cannot solve the problem" becomes actionable -- if you know packaging is 36% of the problem, then reducing packaging waste is likely the most impactful prevention strategy. Without the table, the reader understands that prevention matters but does not know where to focus.

### Interleaved Review
5. Because ocean currents called gyres pull floating material from coastlines into a slowly rotating center, plastic debris from around the Pacific Rim becomes concentrated in one area, forming the Great Pacific Garbage Patch.
6. "Contrary to popular imagination, the patch is not a solid island of trash that can be seen from space."
