# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 2

## Topic
Determining Central Idea

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between the topic of a text and the central idea the author conveys about that topic
- Determine the central idea of an informational passage by analyzing recurring themes across paragraphs
- Provide an objective summary of an informational text that is distinct from personal opinions or judgments

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine two or more main ideas in a text and explain how they are supported by key details (RI.5.2)
- Ability to summarize an informational text (RI.5.2)
- Understanding of the difference between a detail and a main idea
- Ability to cite textual evidence (Lesson 1)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Central Idea Map graphic organizer (center circle for central idea, surrounding circles for supporting details)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters (recommended)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about main ideas from Grade 5 and introduce the distinction between topic and central idea.

**Activity: Topic or Central Idea?**

Say: "In fifth grade, you identified main ideas in informational texts. In sixth grade, we call this the central idea, and we are going to be much more precise about what that means. Let me show you the difference between a topic and a central idea."

Write on the board:
- Topic: Bees
- Central Idea: Bee populations are declining worldwide due to pesticide use and habitat loss, threatening food production that depends on pollination.

Say: "The topic is one or two words -- what the text is about in general. The central idea is the specific, important point the author makes about that topic. It is like a complete thought -- a sentence, not just a word. Today, you are going to learn how to find the central idea in a text, even when the author does not state it in a single obvious sentence."

Ask: "If I say the topic is 'volcanoes,' what might a central idea be?"

Allow the student to respond. Guide toward a complete sentence such as: "Volcanic eruptions can be both destructive and beneficial to the environment."

Say: "Good. That is a central idea because it makes a specific claim about the topic. Let us practice this with real passages."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students a systematic process for identifying the central idea and crafting an objective summary.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Central idea:** The most important point the author communicates about the topic; what the author wants the reader to understand
- **Topic:** The general subject of a text, expressed in one or two words
- **Objective summary:** A brief restatement of the key ideas in a text without including personal opinions, judgments, or reactions
- **Supporting detail:** A fact, example, statistic, or explanation that develops or reinforces the central idea

### Instruction Notes

Say: "To find the central idea, I use a strategy I call the Three-Pass Method."

Write on the board:
1. **First Pass -- Read and identify the topic.** What is this text mostly about?
2. **Second Pass -- Track the details.** What specific facts, examples, and explanations does the author include? What do they have in common?
3. **Third Pass -- State the central idea.** What one important point do all the details work together to support?

Say: "Let me demonstrate this with a passage. Watch how I think through each pass."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Coral Reef Decline"**

> Coral reefs, often called the rainforests of the sea, support approximately 25 percent of all marine species despite covering less than one percent of the ocean floor. These underwater ecosystems provide food and shelter for thousands of fish species, protect coastlines from storm damage, and generate billions of dollars in tourism revenue each year. However, coral reefs around the world are under severe threat. Rising ocean temperatures caused by climate change trigger a process called coral bleaching, in which stressed corals expel the colorful algae living in their tissues and turn white. Without these algae, corals lose their primary food source and often die. Scientists report that more than half of the world's coral reefs have experienced significant bleaching events since 1980. In addition to warming waters, reefs face damage from ocean acidification, overfishing, and coastal pollution. Marine biologists warn that without significant intervention, 90 percent of the world's coral reefs could be lost by 2050.

Say: "First Pass -- I read the whole passage. The topic is coral reefs. But what about coral reefs specifically?"

Say: "Second Pass -- Let me track the details. The passage tells me reefs support 25 percent of marine species, protect coastlines, and generate tourism revenue. Then it tells me about threats: bleaching from warming water, acidification, overfishing, pollution. It ends with a warning that 90 percent could be lost by 2050. Every detail connects to two things: the importance of coral reefs and the danger they face."

Say: "Third Pass -- What one point do all the details support? The central idea is: Coral reefs are critically important ecosystems that face severe threats and could be largely destroyed without intervention. Notice that the author never writes that exact sentence. I built the central idea by synthesizing the details."

Write on the board:
- Central Idea: Coral reefs are vital ecosystems facing multiple threats that could lead to their near-total destruction without significant action.

Say: "Now let me write an objective summary. An objective summary restates the key ideas without adding my opinions."

Write on the board:
- **Objective summary:** Coral reefs support a large proportion of marine life and provide economic and environmental benefits. However, they are threatened by coral bleaching from rising temperatures, ocean acidification, overfishing, and pollution. Scientists warn that without intervention, most coral reefs could disappear by 2050.
- **NOT objective:** Coral reefs are amazing ecosystems and it is really sad that they are dying. I think people should do more to protect them.

Say: "The second version includes opinions -- 'amazing,' 'really sad,' and 'I think.' Those do not belong in an objective summary. A summary reports what the text says, not how you feel about it."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice determining central idea and writing an objective summary with facilitator support.

Say: "Now it is your turn. Let us work through the Three-Pass Method together."

**Passage B: "Urban Farming"**

> In cities around the world, a quiet revolution is transforming rooftops, vacant lots, and abandoned buildings into productive farms. Urban farming -- the practice of growing food within city limits -- has expanded rapidly in the past two decades as cities search for ways to provide fresh produce to residents who live far from traditional farmland. In neighborhoods known as food deserts, where grocery stores are scarce and fast food is the most accessible option, community gardens and rooftop farms are providing fresh vegetables and fruits that were previously unavailable. Detroit, Michigan, which lost much of its population and industry in the late twentieth century, now hosts more than 1,400 urban farms and gardens, producing an estimated 400,000 pounds of food annually. Urban farms also reduce the environmental cost of transporting food over long distances and help manage stormwater by absorbing rainfall that would otherwise overwhelm city drainage systems. Supporters argue that urban farming strengthens communities by bringing neighbors together around a shared purpose and teaching young people where their food comes from.

Say: "First Pass -- what is the topic?"

Expected response: Urban farming.

Say: "Good. Second Pass -- let us track the details. What specific information does the author include?"

Guide the student to identify: expansion in past two decades, food deserts receiving fresh produce, Detroit's 1,400 farms producing 400,000 pounds of food, environmental benefits (reduced transport, stormwater management), community-building benefits.

Say: "Third Pass -- what central idea do all these details support?"

Expected response: Urban farming is growing rapidly and provides significant food access, environmental, and community benefits for cities. (Accept reasonable variations.)

Say: "Now write an objective summary in 2-3 sentences. Remember -- no opinions."

Expected response: Urban farming has expanded in cities worldwide, providing fresh produce to neighborhoods that lack access to grocery stores. Cities like Detroit have developed hundreds of farms that produce significant amounts of food. Urban farms also offer environmental benefits such as reduced food transportation and stormwater management, while building community connections.

Say: "Check your summary. Did you include any opinion words like 'great,' 'important,' or 'should'? If so, revise to make it purely factual."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently determine central idea and write an objective summary of a new passage.

**Passage C: "The Rise of Electric Vehicles"**

> For more than a century, gasoline-powered cars dominated transportation worldwide, but that dominance is rapidly changing. Sales of electric vehicles have surged in the past decade, jumping from fewer than 200,000 globally in 2013 to more than 10 million in 2022. Several forces are driving this shift. Governments in Europe, China, and North America have set ambitious targets to phase out the sale of new gasoline-powered cars by 2035 or earlier. Improvements in battery technology have extended the driving range of electric vehicles from under 100 miles per charge to over 300 miles for many current models. Charging infrastructure is also expanding, with networks of fast-charging stations appearing along major highways and in urban centers. Environmental concerns play a significant role as well, since transportation accounts for approximately 27 percent of greenhouse gas emissions in the United States. However, challenges remain: electric vehicles are still more expensive than comparable gasoline models, battery production requires mining of lithium and cobalt under sometimes harmful conditions, and many regions lack sufficient charging stations.

**Directions:** Use the Three-Pass Method for each question. Cite textual evidence in your responses.

1. What is the topic of this passage?
2. What is the central idea? Write it as a complete sentence.
3. Write an objective summary of the passage in 3-4 sentences. Do not include personal opinions.
4. A classmate writes this summary: "Electric cars are awesome and everyone should buy one because they help the environment." Explain why this is not an objective summary. Then rewrite it to be objective.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with accurate central idea identification and an objective summary.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. In your summary, include at least two cited details with signal phrases.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a second paragraph explaining whether the passage presents a balanced or one-sided view of electric vehicles. Cite at least two pieces of evidence to support your analysis.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.1) Reread the passage. Quote the specific sentence that identifies the environmental reason behind the shift to electric vehicles.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.3) Explain the cause-and-effect relationship between battery technology improvements and electric vehicle adoption. Use a signal word.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Microplastics -- tiny plastic fragments smaller than five millimeters -- have been found in oceans, rivers, soil, and even the air we breathe. Recent studies have detected microplastics in human blood and lung tissue, raising concerns about potential health effects. Scientists are still researching whether microplastic exposure contributes to inflammation, cellular damage, or other long-term conditions.

1. What is the central idea of this passage?
2. Write a one-sentence objective summary.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Central idea: Microplastics are widespread in the environment and in the human body, and their potential health effects are a growing concern for scientists.
2. Objective summary: Microplastics have been found throughout the environment and in human tissue, prompting scientists to investigate possible health effects such as inflammation and cellular damage. (Accept reasonable variations that are factual and opinion-free.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with central idea expressed as a complete sentence and summary free of personal opinion**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Three-Pass Method using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Provide a passage with the central idea stated explicitly in the first or last sentence and have the student identify it
- Practice the topic vs. central idea distinction with three additional examples
- Give the student two summaries of the same passage -- one objective, one opinion-laden -- and have them identify and fix the opinion language

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3 on how central idea is conveyed through details
- Encourage the student to practice identifying central ideas in news articles or science texts at home

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Central Idea Detective:** Give the student three short informational paragraphs on different topics. For each, they write the topic (1-2 words) and the central idea (full sentence). Compare and discuss.
2. **Summary Makeover:** Provide three summaries that contain hidden opinions. The student identifies the opinion language and rewrites each summary objectively.
3. **Write Your Own:** Have the student write a short informational paragraph (5-6 sentences) with a clear central idea. Then have them exchange with a partner or family member to see if the reader can identify the central idea.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common mistake at this level is confusing the topic with the central idea. Students will say "the passage is about coral reefs" instead of "the passage argues that coral reefs are in danger." Consistently redirect by asking, "That is the topic. What does the author want you to understand about that topic?"
- Some students will write the central idea as a question rather than a statement. Remind them that the central idea is the answer to the question "What is the author's most important point?" -- not the question itself.
- Objective summaries are difficult for students who are accustomed to writing personal responses. Watch for subtle opinion language ("unfortunately," "interestingly," "hopefully") and help students recognize these as non-objective.
- When students struggle to identify the central idea, suggest they read the first and last sentences of each paragraph, as authors often place key points in these positions.

2. Central idea: Electric vehicle sales are growing rapidly due to government policies, improved battery technology, expanding charging infrastructure, and environmental concerns, but significant challenges such as cost, mining impacts, and insufficient charging networks remain. (Accept reasonable variations that capture both the growth and the challenges.)
3. Objective summary: Sales of electric vehicles have grown dramatically, rising from fewer than 200,000 in 2013 to more than 10 million in 2022. However, electric vehicles still face challenges including higher prices, environmental concerns about battery mining, and limited charging infrastructure in many areas. (Accept reasonable variations that include key details without opinion.)

These are the writer's feelings, not information from the text. An objective version: Electric vehicles help reduce greenhouse gas emissions from transportation, which accounts for approximately 27 percent of emissions in the United States. (Accept reasonable rewrites that remove opinion and stay factual.)
