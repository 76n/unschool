# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 6

## Topic
Determining Author's Purpose

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Determine an author's purpose in writing an informational text (to inform, to persuade, to raise awareness, to explain, to challenge assumptions)
- Identify specific textual clues that reveal an author's purpose, including word choice, detail selection, and tone
- Distinguish between an author's stated topic and the underlying purpose for writing about that topic
- Explain the author's purpose using evidence from the text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to analyze how events and ideas are elaborated in a text (Lesson 5)
- Ability to determine central idea and how it is conveyed through details (Lessons 2-3)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lesson 1)
- Familiarity with identifying author's point of view from Grade 5

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Author's Purpose Analysis Chart graphic organizer (columns: Text Clue, What It Suggests About Purpose, Purpose Category)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (one for neutral language, one for loaded or purposeful language)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept that informational texts are written with specific purposes that go beyond simply sharing facts.

**Activity: Same Topic, Different Purpose**

Say: "Every informational text is written for a reason. The author chose to write about a particular topic in a particular way, and understanding that purpose helps you read more critically. Let me show you what I mean."

Write on the board three openings about the same topic:
- Version A: "Plastic bags take an average of 500 years to decompose in a landfill."
- Version B: "If we do not act now to ban single-use plastic bags, our oceans and landfills will be overwhelmed with waste that outlasts us by centuries."
- Version C: "Plastic bags were first introduced in the 1960s as a lightweight, inexpensive alternative to paper bags."

Say: "All three are about plastic bags. But what is each author trying to do?"

Allow the student to respond.

Say: "Version A informs -- it gives a fact. Version B persuades -- it urges action with emotional language ('if we do not act now,' 'overwhelmed'). Version C explains -- it provides historical context. The topic is the same, but the purpose is different. Today you will learn to detect an author's purpose by examining the clues hidden in word choice, detail selection, and tone."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify an author's purpose through analysis of textual clues.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Author's purpose:** The reason an author writes a text -- what the author wants the reader to understand, feel, believe, or do after reading
- **Tone:** The author's attitude toward the subject, revealed through word choice and sentence structure (e.g., urgent, neutral, admiring, critical)
- **Loaded language:** Words chosen to evoke an emotional response (e.g., "devastating" vs. "significant," "crisis" vs. "situation")
- **Neutral language:** Words that present information without emotional coloring (e.g., "researchers found" vs. "researchers were shocked to discover")

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In sixth grade, we go beyond the simple categories of inform, persuade, and entertain. An author's purpose can be more specific and nuanced."

Write on the board:
- **To inform:** Present facts and data without guiding the reader toward a particular conclusion
- **To explain:** Help the reader understand how or why something works or happened
- **To persuade:** Convince the reader to adopt a viewpoint or take action
- **To raise awareness:** Draw attention to a problem or issue the reader may not know about
- **To challenge assumptions:** Present evidence that contradicts common beliefs

Say: "To determine purpose, I use three clue types. I call them the Purpose Detectors."

Write on the board:
1. **Word choice** -- Is the language neutral or loaded? Emotional or factual?
2. **Detail selection** -- Does the author include only one side, or multiple perspectives? Are the details alarming, celebratory, or balanced?
3. **Structure** -- Does the text end with a call to action, a question, a warning, or a neutral conclusion?

Say: "Watch how I use these detectors with a real passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Forgotten Language Crisis"**

> Of the approximately 7,000 languages currently spoken around the world, linguists estimate that nearly half will fall silent by the end of this century. Every two weeks, on average, the last fluent speaker of a language dies, taking with it centuries of accumulated knowledge, stories, and ways of understanding the world that exist in no other language. Indigenous communities in Australia, South America, and Southeast Asia are particularly affected, as younger generations shift to dominant languages such as English, Spanish, and Mandarin in pursuit of economic opportunities. When a language disappears, humanity loses more than words. Many indigenous languages contain detailed ecological knowledge -- names for plants, animals, and natural processes -- that has been developed over thousands of years and has no equivalent in any other language. Linguist K. David Harrison has argued that the loss of a language is comparable to "burning a library that can never be rebuilt." Some communities are fighting back, establishing language nests and immersion schools to teach endangered languages to children, but the scale of the crisis far outpaces current preservation efforts.

Say: "Let me apply the Purpose Detectors."

Write on the board:
- **Word choice:** "Forgotten" in the title suggests something neglected. "Fall silent," "taking with it centuries of accumulated knowledge," "humanity loses," "burning a library that can never be rebuilt" -- this is loaded, emotional language designed to create a sense of urgency and loss. These are not neutral choices.
- **Detail selection:** The author focuses heavily on what is lost when languages disappear (knowledge, stories, ecological terms). The only counterpoint -- preservation efforts -- is described as inadequate ("far outpaces current preservation efforts"). The author does not give equal weight to reasons why language shift might be natural or beneficial.
- **Structure:** The passage ends on a note of urgency, emphasizing that "the scale of the crisis far outpaces current preservation efforts."

Say: "Putting the clues together: the author's purpose is to raise awareness about language extinction and convey urgency about the loss. The author wants the reader to understand that this is a crisis and to feel that something important is at stake."

Say: "Now compare: if the author's purpose were simply to inform, the passage might read more like this."

Write on the board:
- "Linguists estimate that approximately 3,500 of the world's 7,000 languages may no longer be spoken by 2100. Several factors contribute to this trend, including urbanization, globalization, and economic incentives to learn widely spoken languages."

Say: "See the difference? Same topic, but neutral language, no emotional framing, no metaphor of burning libraries. Purpose changes how a text reads."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice determining author's purpose with facilitator support.

Say: "Let us analyze this passage together using the three Purpose Detectors."

**Passage B: "How Earthquakes Work"**

> The Earth's outer layer, called the lithosphere, is divided into large sections known as tectonic plates. These plates sit on top of a layer of hot, slowly flowing rock called the asthenosphere, and they are constantly moving, though typically at a rate of only a few centimeters per year. Where plates meet, they can push against each other, pull apart, or slide past one another. Stress builds up along these boundaries over years and decades. When the accumulated stress exceeds the strength of the rock, the rock fractures suddenly, releasing energy in the form of seismic waves that radiate outward from the point of rupture. This release of energy is what we experience as an earthquake. The severity of an earthquake is measured using the Richter scale, which is logarithmic, meaning that each whole-number increase represents a tenfold increase in the amplitude of seismic waves. Earthquakes are most common along the boundaries of tectonic plates, particularly in the Pacific Ring of Fire, where approximately 81 percent of the world's largest earthquakes occur.

Say: "Let us apply the Purpose Detectors. First, word choice -- is the language neutral or loaded?"

Expected response: The language is neutral and factual. Words like "lithosphere," "tectonic plates," "seismic waves," and "logarithmic" are scientific and precise. There is no emotional language or urgency.

Say: "Good. Second, detail selection -- what kinds of details does the author include?"

Expected response: The author includes scientific facts and explanations: how plates move, why stress builds up, how earthquakes are measured. There is no one-sided argument or emotional appeal.

Say: "Third, structure -- how does the passage end?"

Expected response: It ends with a factual statement about where earthquakes are most common. There is no call to action, warning, or question.

Say: "So what is the author's purpose?"

Expected response: To explain how earthquakes work. The author wants the reader to understand the scientific process behind earthquakes, not to feel alarmed or to take action.

Say: "Right. Now compare this passage to the language loss passage. One raises awareness and creates urgency. The other explains a process. The Purpose Detectors help you see the difference clearly."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently determine author's purpose in a new passage.

**Passage C: "The Case Against Homework"**

> For decades, homework has been treated as a cornerstone of education, but a growing body of research suggests that the amount of homework assigned to students may be doing more harm than good. A study by Stanford University researcher Denise Pope found that students in high-achieving communities who spent more than three hours per night on homework reported higher levels of stress, physical health problems, and social isolation. Elementary school students, research shows, gain little measurable academic benefit from homework at all. The National Education Association and the National Parent Teacher Association recommend a maximum of ten minutes of homework per grade level per night -- meaning a sixth grader should have no more than sixty minutes. Yet surveys show that many students regularly exceed these recommendations, with some middle schoolers reporting two or more hours of homework nightly. Critics argue that excessive homework robs children of time for creative play, physical activity, family interactions, and the unstructured exploration that is essential for healthy development. Meanwhile, advocates contend that homework builds discipline and reinforces classroom learning. The evidence, however, increasingly suggests that when it comes to homework, less may genuinely be more.

**Directions:** Use the three Purpose Detectors (word choice, detail selection, structure) to determine the author's purpose. Cite textual evidence in your responses.

1. Analyze the author's word choice. Identify at least two examples of loaded or purposeful language and explain what they suggest about the author's purpose.
2. Analyze the author's detail selection. Does the passage present both sides of the homework debate equally? Cite evidence to support your answer.
3. Analyze the passage's structure. How does the final sentence ("less may genuinely be more") serve the author's purpose?
4. Based on your analysis of all three Purpose Detectors, what is the author's primary purpose? Write a complete sentence that identifies the purpose and explains how the text conveys it.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with textual evidence and clear explanations.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. In question 4, explain how the author's purpose differs from simply informing the reader about homework research.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a paragraph rewriting the passage's opening from a purely informational perspective (neutral word choice, balanced details, no loaded language). Explain how the rewrite changes the purpose.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.2) What is the central idea of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.1) Quote the sentence that describes the recommendation from the NEA and PTA about homework amounts.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Every year, thousands of sea turtles become entangled in discarded fishing nets, a problem known as ghost fishing. Conservation groups are urging governments to require that all commercial fishing nets be fitted with biodegradable panels that would break down within two years, preventing abandoned nets from trapping wildlife indefinitely.

1. What is the author's purpose in this passage?
2. Identify one textual clue that reveals the purpose.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The author's purpose is to persuade the reader (or raise awareness and advocate for policy change) that biodegradable fishing net panels should be required to protect sea turtles.
2. Textual clue: The word "urging" signals advocacy rather than neutral reporting. The phrase "preventing abandoned nets from trapping wildlife indefinitely" uses emotional language that emphasizes the ongoing harm. The passage presents only the conservation groups' position without including counterarguments. (Accept any valid clue with explanation.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with purpose accurately identified and at least one textual clue identified and explained**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Purpose Detectors using the warm-up examples (Versions A, B, C about plastic bags)
- Provide three short passages with clearly different purposes (one to inform, one to persuade, one to explain) and have the student match each to its purpose
- Practice circling loaded vs. neutral language in a passage to build awareness of word choice clues

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7 on how author's purpose shapes content
- Encourage the student to identify the purpose of informational texts they encounter in daily life (news articles, advertisements, textbook sections)

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Purpose Spectrum:** Place "purely informational" on one end of a line and "strongly persuasive" on the other. Have the student place each passage from the lesson on the spectrum and justify the placement with evidence.
2. **Same Facts, Different Purpose:** Provide a set of five facts about a topic (e.g., deforestation). Have the student write two short paragraphs using the same facts -- one with a purpose to inform and one with a purpose to persuade. Then identify what changed between the two versions.
3. **Media Literacy Connection:** Have the student find two articles on the same current event from different sources. Compare the authors' purposes and identify specific word choices and details that reveal each purpose.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often default to the three basic purposes (inform, persuade, entertain) learned in earlier grades. Push them toward more specific and nuanced purpose statements. Instead of "to persuade," encourage "to persuade the reader that excessive homework harms students" or "to raise awareness about the homework debate and advocate for reduced assignments."
- The distinction between "inform" and "raise awareness" is important. An informational text presents balanced facts. A text that raises awareness selects facts strategically to draw attention to a problem. Help students see this difference.
- Some students may struggle with the idea that informational texts can be biased. Reassure them that having a purpose is not the same as being dishonest -- authors make choices about what to emphasize, and recognizing those choices makes readers stronger.
- Word choice analysis is a skill that benefits from repeated practice. Consider creating a class "word wall" of loaded language encountered throughout the unit.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Word choice examples: (a) "Doing more harm than good" is loaded language that frames homework negatively rather than neutrally. (b) "Robs children of time" uses the strong verb "robs," implying theft and injustice, rather than a neutral phrase like "reduces time available for." These choices suggest the author's purpose is to argue against excessive homework, not merely to report on it.
2. The passage does not present both sides equally. The author dedicates most of the passage to evidence against homework: the Stanford study on stress, the finding that elementary students gain "little measurable academic benefit," and the argument that homework "robs children of time." The pro-homework perspective receives only one sentence: "advocates contend that homework builds discipline and reinforces classroom learning." This uneven detail selection suggests the author favors the anti-homework position.
3. The final sentence "less may genuinely be more" serves as a thesis-like conclusion that reveals the author's position. The word "genuinely" emphasizes sincerity and conviction. By placing this statement at the end, the author leaves the reader with a clear takeaway: homework should be reduced. This structure is persuasive rather than informational.
4. The author's primary purpose is to challenge the common assumption that homework is beneficial and to persuade the reader that excessive homework harms students. The text conveys this purpose through loaded word choices ("robs," "more harm than good"), unbalanced detail selection that favors the anti-homework position, and a concluding statement that directly advocates for less homework.

### Interleaved Review
5. Central idea: Research increasingly suggests that excessive homework may harm students' well-being without providing meaningful academic benefits, particularly for younger students.
6. "The National Education Association and the National Parent Teacher Association recommend a maximum of ten minutes of homework per grade level per night -- meaning a sixth grader should have no more than sixty minutes."

See expected answers above.
