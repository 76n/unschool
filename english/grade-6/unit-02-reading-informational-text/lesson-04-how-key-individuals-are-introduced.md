# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 4

## Topic
How Key Individuals Are Introduced

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Analyze how an author introduces a key individual in an informational text
- Identify the specific details and context the author provides when first presenting a person
- Explain how the author's introduction shapes the reader's initial understanding of a key individual
- Compare different techniques authors use to introduce individuals (descriptive detail, anecdote, accomplishment, historical context)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine the central idea of a text and how it is conveyed through details (Lessons 2-3)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lesson 1)
- Understanding of relationships between individuals and events in informational text (RI.5.3)
- Ability to read Grade 6 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Introduction Analysis Chart graphic organizer (columns: First detail given, Context provided, Impression created, Why this introduction matters)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters (recommended)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about how informational texts present people and introduce the concept of authorial choices in introduction.

**Activity: First Impressions**

Say: "When you meet someone for the first time, the first things you learn about them shape your impression. The same is true when an author introduces a person in an informational text. The details an author chooses to include first are not random -- they are deliberate decisions that shape how you think about that person."

Write on the board two introductions of the same person:
- Version A: Mae Jemison, a physician and engineer, became the first African American woman to travel to space when she flew aboard the Space Shuttle Endeavour in 1992.
- Version B: As a young girl growing up in Chicago, Mae Jemison spent hours gazing at the stars and dreaming of exploring the universe, even though no one who looked like her had ever been an astronaut.

Say: "Both sentences are about Mae Jemison. But which facts does each one lead with? How does each introduction shape your first impression differently?"

Allow the student to respond.

Say: "Version A introduces her through her professional credentials and historic achievement. Version B introduces her through her childhood dreams and the barriers she faced. The author's choice of how to introduce a person reveals what the author thinks is most important about that individual. Today, we are going to learn to analyze those choices."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to analyze the specific techniques authors use when introducing key individuals in informational text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Introduce:** The way an author first presents a key individual to the reader, including what information is given and what context is established
- **Elaborate:** To expand on an introduction with additional details, examples, anecdotes, or evidence that deepen the reader's understanding
- **Anecdote:** A brief story about a specific event, often used to make a person or idea more relatable and memorable
- **Context:** Background information that helps the reader understand the significance of a person, event, or idea
- **Characterize:** To describe someone by highlighting specific traits, actions, or qualities

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Authors introduce key individuals using four common techniques. I call them the Introduction Toolkit."

Write on the board:
1. **Achievement Lead** -- Introduce the person through their most notable accomplishment
2. **Anecdote Lead** -- Introduce the person through a specific, vivid story or moment
3. **Context Lead** -- Introduce the person through historical or social context that explains why they mattered
4. **Description Lead** -- Introduce the person through physical, personal, or character details

Say: "Good authors often combine these techniques. Let me show you how to identify them in a real passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Wangari Maathai and the Green Belt Movement"**

> In 1977, when much of Kenya's countryside had been stripped bare by commercial farming and logging, a biology professor named Wangari Maathai planted seven trees in her backyard. That modest act grew into the Green Belt Movement, an organization that has since planted more than 51 million trees across Africa. Maathai was born in 1940 in a small village in the central highlands of Kenya, where dense forests once covered the hillsides. She earned a bachelor's degree from Mount St. Scholastica College in Kansas, becoming one of the first women from East Africa to earn a college degree in the United States. After completing a doctoral degree in veterinary anatomy at the University of Nairobi, she turned her attention to the environmental destruction she witnessed in rural Kenya. Maathai recognized that deforestation was not merely an ecological problem but a social one: as forests disappeared, women had to walk farther for firewood and clean water, and farmland became less productive. By training rural women to plant and care for native trees, Maathai addressed environmental restoration and women's empowerment simultaneously. In 2004, she became the first African woman to receive the Nobel Peace Prize.

Say: "Let me analyze how the author introduces Wangari Maathai."

Write on the board:
- **First detail:** "A biology professor named Wangari Maathai planted seven trees in her backyard" -- this is an anecdote lead. The author introduces Maathai through a specific, small action.
- **Why this choice matters:** By starting with seven trees, the author creates a contrast between a humble beginning and the massive result (51 million trees). This introduction shapes the reader's impression of Maathai as someone whose simple actions led to extraordinary outcomes.
- **Context follows:** The author then provides historical context (born in 1940, village with dense forests) and achievement details (first East African woman to earn a U.S. degree, doctoral degree, Nobel Peace Prize).
- **Introduction technique:** Anecdote lead combined with context and achievement

Say: "Notice the author's order. The introduction begins with an action, not a title or credential. The author could have started with 'Nobel Peace Prize winner Wangari Maathai' but instead chose to begin with seven trees. That deliberate choice shapes how we think about Maathai -- as a person of action, not just of honor."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice analyzing how an author introduces a key individual with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us analyze a different passage together. Pay attention to the very first things the author tells us about this person."

**Passage B: "Percy Julian, Hidden Genius"**

> Percy Lavoisier Julian was denied admission to graduate school after graduate school in the early 1920s -- not because he lacked ability, but because he was Black in an America that barred African Americans from most advanced educational opportunities. Julian had graduated first in his class from DePauw University in Indiana in 1920, yet leading chemistry programs refused to accept him. Undeterred, he taught at Fisk University while saving money and eventually earned his master's degree from Harvard. When Harvard still would not allow him to pursue a doctorate because of his race, Julian traveled to the University of Vienna, where he earned his Ph.D. in chemistry in 1931. He went on to become one of the most important chemists of the twentieth century, synthesizing compounds that led to affordable treatments for glaucoma and rheumatoid arthritis. His work on synthesizing cortisone from soybean oil made a life-saving medication accessible to millions of people who previously could not afford it. Despite his achievements, Julian faced persistent racism, including arson attacks on his home when he moved into a predominantly white neighborhood in Oak Park, Illinois.

Say: "What is the very first thing the author tells us about Percy Julian?"

Expected response: That he was denied admission to graduate schools because of racial discrimination.

Say: "Right. What introduction technique is the author using?"

Expected response: Context lead -- the author introduces Julian through the social barriers he faced.

Say: "Why might the author have chosen to start with rejection rather than achievement? Think about how this shapes your impression."

Expected response: Starting with rejection makes the reader see Julian as someone who overcame enormous obstacles. It makes his later achievements more impressive because the reader already knows how much he was working against. It also signals that the passage is about both his scientific genius and the racism he faced.

Say: "Excellent. Now compare this introduction to Passage A. Maathai was introduced through an action (planting trees). Julian was introduced through a barrier (denied admission). How does each choice serve the author's purpose differently?"

Expected response: The Maathai introduction emphasizes what she did, showing her as a person of action. The Julian introduction emphasizes what was done to him, showing the injustice he overcame. Both shape the reader's sympathy and respect, but through different routes.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how an author introduces a key individual in a new passage.

**Passage C: "Sylvia Earle, Explorer of the Deep"**

> At the age of sixteen, Sylvia Earle walked into the Gulf of Mexico wearing a borrowed scuba tank and descended beneath the waves for the first time. In that moment, she later recalled, the underwater world became her lifelong obsession. Born in 1935 in Gibbstown, New Jersey, Earle grew up exploring the woods and ponds near her home before her family moved to Clearwater, Florida, where the Gulf became her backyard laboratory. She earned her doctoral degree in marine botany from Duke University at a time when women were rare in the sciences, and male colleagues often dismissed her ambitions. In 1970, Earle led an all-female team of aquanauts in the Tektite II project, living and working in an underwater habitat for two weeks at a depth of fifty feet. The mission drew national media attention and helped establish Earle as a serious ocean scientist. By 1979, she set a record for the deepest untethered walk on the ocean floor, descending 1,250 feet in a pressurized suit called a JIM suit off the coast of Oahu, Hawaii. She has since led more than one hundred expeditions, logged more than 7,000 hours underwater, and served as the first female chief scientist of the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. Today, Earle continues to advocate for ocean conservation through her organization, Mission Blue, which works to establish protected marine areas worldwide.

**Directions:** Analyze how the author introduces Sylvia Earle. Cite textual evidence in your responses.

1. What is the very first detail the author provides about Earle? What introduction technique does this represent (achievement, anecdote, context, or description)?
2. What impression does this introduction create for the reader? How might a different opening (e.g., leading with her record-setting deep dive) change the reader's first impression?
3. After the opening, the author provides background context about Earle's childhood and education. How does this context develop the reader's understanding of Earle beyond the initial introduction?
4. The passage mentions that "male colleagues often dismissed her ambitions." What does the author accomplish by including this detail in the introduction section of the passage?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with accurate analysis and textual evidence.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. For question 2, write two alternative opening sentences for the passage using different introduction techniques and explain how each would change the reader's impression.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a paragraph comparing how the authors of Passages A, B, and C introduced their subjects. Which introduction do you find most effective and why? Cite evidence from at least two passages.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.3) Describe the relationship between Earle's childhood experiences and her career as an ocean scientist. Use at least one signal word.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.2) What is the central idea of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> When twelve-year-old Marley Dias noticed that almost none of the books assigned in her school featured Black girls as main characters, she decided to do something about it. In 2015, she launched the #1000BlackGirlBooks campaign, collecting and donating more than 13,000 books featuring Black female protagonists to schools and libraries across the country.

1. What introduction technique does the author use to present Marley Dias?
2. How does this introduction shape the reader's impression of Dias?

**Expected Answers:**
1. The author uses an anecdote lead (or context lead), introducing Dias through the specific observation that motivated her action -- noticing the lack of books featuring Black girls in her school assignments.
2. This introduction shapes the reader's impression by showing Dias as observant, proactive, and determined. By starting with a problem she noticed as a child rather than with the campaign's impressive numbers, the author presents her as someone who saw an injustice and took action rather than simply as someone who collected a large number of books.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with introduction technique identified and impression explained with reference to the text**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Introduction Toolkit using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Provide two versions of a short introduction for the same person and have the student compare the impressions each creates
- Practice labeling introduction techniques with three brief examples before returning to the exit ticket passage

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 5 on how events and ideas are elaborated in a text
- Encourage the student to notice how newspaper and magazine articles introduce the key figures in their stories

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Rewrite the Introduction:** Choose one of the passage subjects (Maathai, Julian, or Earle) and write an alternative introduction using a different technique from the Introduction Toolkit. Explain how the new introduction changes the reader's impression.
2. **Introduction Scavenger Hunt:** Find three informational articles (in a newspaper, magazine, or online) that feature a key individual. For each, identify the introduction technique the author uses and explain what impression it creates.
3. **Author's Choice Debate:** Write a paragraph arguing which introduction technique is most effective for biographical informational text. Support your argument with examples from the lesson passages.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students sometimes confuse "the first sentence" with "the introduction." In informational text, the introduction of a key individual may span several sentences or even a full paragraph. Help students see the introduction as the opening characterization, not just the first mention of a name.
- The concept of authorial choice can be abstract for sixth graders. Make it concrete by showing that the same facts can be arranged differently, producing a different reader experience. The warm-up activity is designed to make this visible.
- Encourage students to notice what the author does not include in the introduction. For example, the Maathai passage does not mention the Nobel Prize until the very end. Ask: "Why might the author save this fact for last?"
- This lesson connects naturally to writing. When students understand how published authors introduce individuals, they can make more deliberate choices in their own biographical or research writing.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The first detail is that at age sixteen, Earle "walked into the Gulf of Mexico wearing a borrowed scuba tank and descended beneath the waves for the first time." This is an anecdote lead -- the author introduces Earle through a specific, vivid moment from her youth.
2. This introduction creates the impression of Earle as someone who was passionate about the ocean from a young age and who was bold enough to dive into the unknown as a teenager. If the author had led with her record-setting 1,250-foot dive, the impression would emphasize her professional accomplishments and extreme achievement rather than her personal connection to the ocean. The anecdote lead makes her feel relatable and human, while an achievement lead would make her feel more distant and heroic.
3. The childhood context (exploring woods and ponds, moving to Florida, the Gulf as her "backyard laboratory") develops the reader's understanding by showing that Earle's passion grew gradually from childhood exploration. The education context (doctoral degree, being a woman when women were rare in sciences) adds depth by showing she faced barriers and persisted. Together, these details explain how the sixteen-year-old with a borrowed scuba tank became a world-renowned ocean scientist.
4. By including the detail about male colleagues dismissing her ambitions, the author establishes that Earle succeeded not just through talent but through persistence against gender-based obstacles. This detail adds a layer of social context to her story and signals that her achievements were won against resistance, making them more impressive.

### Interleaved Review
5. Because Earle grew up exploring nature near her home and then moved to Florida where the Gulf of Mexico was accessible, her childhood curiosity about the natural world led directly to her career as a marine scientist. Her early experiences built the foundation for her lifelong commitment to ocean exploration.
6. Central idea: Sylvia Earle is a pioneering ocean scientist who has dedicated her life to exploring and protecting the world's oceans, overcoming gender barriers and setting records throughout her career.
