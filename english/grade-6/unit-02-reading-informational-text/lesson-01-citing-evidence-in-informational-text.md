# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 1

## Topic
Citing Evidence in Informational Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Cite specific textual evidence to support an analysis of what the text says explicitly
- Cite textual evidence to support inferences drawn from the text
- Distinguish between strong and weak textual evidence for a given analytical claim
- Use signal phrases and proper formatting when incorporating evidence into written responses

## Prerequisites
- Ability to quote accurately from a text using quotation marks (RI.5.1)
- Ability to draw inferences from informational text (RI.5.1)
- Understanding of the difference between explicit information and inference
- Ability to read Grade 6 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Evidence Strength Chart graphic organizer (two columns: Evidence, Strong or Weak? Why?)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (recommended)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge from Grade 5 about quoting accurately and transition to the more rigorous Grade 6 expectation of citing evidence.

**Activity: Quote vs. Cite**

Say: "In fifth grade, you learned to quote accurately from a text -- using the author's exact words inside quotation marks. You became very good at that. In sixth grade, we are raising the bar. Citing evidence means more than just finding a quote. It means choosing the strongest, most relevant evidence and then explaining how that evidence supports your point."

Write on the board:
- Weak: The passage says, "The ocean is deep."
- Strong: The author emphasizes the ocean's extreme depth, noting that "the Mariana Trench plunges nearly 36,000 feet below the surface," which illustrates why so much of the ocean floor remains unexplored.

Say: "Both of these use a quote from the text. But which one is more convincing? Why?"

Allow the student to respond.

Say: "The second one is stronger because it selects specific, detailed evidence and then connects that evidence to a larger point. That connection -- the explanation of why the evidence matters -- is what makes the difference between quoting and citing. Today we are going to learn how to cite evidence like an analytical reader."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students the process of selecting, citing, and connecting evidence to support analysis of both explicit information and inferences.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Cite:** To refer to a specific piece of text as evidence, using the author's exact words or a close reference, to support a point or claim
- **Textual evidence:** Specific words, phrases, sentences, or data from the text used to support analysis
- **Explicit:** Information stated directly in the text; the reader does not need to figure it out
- **Inference:** A conclusion drawn by combining textual evidence with reasoning; the text suggests it but does not state it directly
- **Signal phrase:** An introductory phrase that attributes information to the text or author (e.g., "The author states," "According to the passage")

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When we cite evidence in sixth grade, we follow three steps. I call them the C-E-C method: Claim, Evidence, Connection."

Write on the board:
1. **Claim** -- State your analytical point clearly.
2. **Evidence** -- Select the most relevant quote or detail from the text.
3. **Connection** -- Explain how the evidence supports your claim.

Say: "Watch how I use the C-E-C method with this passage. I am going to think aloud so you can hear my reasoning process."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Deep Ocean"**

> Scientists estimate that more than 80 percent of the ocean remains unmapped, unobserved, and unexplored. The deep ocean, defined as waters below 200 meters, presents extreme challenges for exploration. At depths beyond 1,000 meters, sunlight cannot penetrate the water, leaving the environment in complete darkness. Water pressure at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, the deepest known point in the ocean, reaches more than 15,000 pounds per square inch -- roughly the equivalent of having 50 jumbo jets stacked on top of a person. Despite these obstacles, deep-sea explorers have discovered extraordinary life forms, including tube worms that thrive near volcanic vents at temperatures exceeding 400 degrees Fahrenheit and fish that produce their own light through a chemical process called bioluminescence. Each expedition reveals species previously unknown to science, suggesting that the deep ocean may harbor the planet's greatest remaining biodiversity.

Say: "Here is an explicit question: What challenges does the deep ocean present for exploration? Let me work through C-E-C."

Write on the board:
- **Claim:** The deep ocean presents extreme environmental challenges that make exploration difficult.
- **Evidence:** The passage states that "at depths beyond 1,000 meters, sunlight cannot penetrate the water" and that pressure at the bottom of the Mariana Trench "reaches more than 15,000 pounds per square inch."
- **Connection:** These details show that explorers must contend with total darkness and crushing pressure, which explains why specialized equipment and technology are required.

Say: "Now let me try an inference question: Why might the deep ocean be important for scientific research?"

Write on the board:
- **Claim:** The deep ocean likely holds significant value for scientific discovery.
- **Evidence:** The author notes that "each expedition reveals species previously unknown to science," and that the deep ocean "may harbor the planet's greatest remaining biodiversity."
- **Connection:** If every new expedition discovers unknown species, then vast numbers of organisms likely remain undiscovered. This suggests the deep ocean is a frontier for biological research that could transform our understanding of life on Earth.

Say: "Notice that the text does not say 'the deep ocean is important for scientific research.' I inferred that by combining the evidence about new species with my reasoning about what that implies."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice citing evidence with facilitator support, working through both explicit and inference questions.

Say: "Now let us practice together with a new passage. Remember: Claim, Evidence, Connection."

**Passage B: "The Great Migration"**

> Every year, approximately 1.5 million wildebeest, along with hundreds of thousands of zebras and gazelles, undertake a circular journey across the Serengeti ecosystem in East Africa. This movement, known as the Great Migration, covers roughly 1,800 miles and is driven primarily by the search for fresh grazing land and water. The migration follows a predictable seasonal pattern: herds move northward from the southern Serengeti in Tanzania toward Kenya's Maasai Mara reserve as the dry season takes hold, then return south when the rains begin again. The journey is perilous. Crocodiles lie in wait at river crossings, and predators such as lions, hyenas, and cheetahs follow the herds, picking off the weak and the young. Scientists estimate that more than 250,000 wildebeest die during the migration each year from drowning, exhaustion, predation, and disease. Yet the migration persists because the animals that survive gain access to nutrient-rich grasslands that sustain them through the breeding season.

Say: "Question 1 -- explicit: What drives the Great Migration? Find the strongest evidence and walk me through C-E-C."

Allow the student to work for one minute. Guide as needed.

Expected response:
- **Claim:** The Great Migration is driven by the animals' need for food and water.
- **Evidence:** The passage states that the migration "is driven primarily by the search for fresh grazing land and water."
- **Connection:** This shows that the migration is a survival behavior -- the animals move because they must find resources to sustain themselves.

Say: "Question 2 -- inference: Why does the migration continue even though so many animals die? Use C-E-C."

Expected response:
- **Claim:** The migration continues because the survival benefits outweigh the risks.
- **Evidence:** The passage states that "the migration persists because the animals that survive gain access to nutrient-rich grasslands that sustain them through the breeding season."
- **Connection:** Even though more than 250,000 wildebeest die each year, the remaining animals need those grasslands to reproduce and raise their young. If they did not migrate, they might not find enough food to breed successfully.

Say: "Excellent. Notice how your connection went beyond just repeating the evidence. You explained the reasoning behind it."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently cite evidence from a new passage, supporting both explicit analysis and inferences.

**Passage C: "The Invention of the Printing Press"**

> Before Johannes Gutenberg introduced his printing press around 1440, books in Europe were copied entirely by hand, a process so slow and expensive that only wealthy individuals, churches, and universities could afford them. A single handwritten Bible could take a scribe more than a year to complete. Gutenberg's innovation was not the concept of printing itself, which had existed in China for centuries, but the development of movable metal type -- individual letter blocks that could be rearranged and reused to print different pages. This system allowed printers to produce books far more quickly and at a fraction of the previous cost. Within fifty years of Gutenberg's first printed Bible, an estimated 20 million volumes had been produced across Europe. The sudden availability of affordable books transformed European society. Literacy rates climbed as ordinary people gained access to written knowledge for the first time. New ideas in science, religion, and philosophy spread rapidly, contributing to the intellectual movements of the Renaissance and the Reformation.

**Directions:** Answer each question using the C-E-C method (Claim, Evidence, Connection). Cite specific textual evidence with quotation marks and a signal phrase.

1. What was the state of book production in Europe before Gutenberg's printing press? Cite evidence from the text.
2. What was Gutenberg's specific innovation? Cite evidence to explain why it mattered.
3. What can you infer about the relationship between the printing press and social change? Cite evidence to support your inference.
4. Why might the passage emphasize that printing "had existed in China for centuries"? Cite evidence and explain.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 using the C-E-C method with accurate citations.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions using the C-E-C method. In each response, use a different signal phrase (e.g., "The author explains," "According to the passage," "The text reveals," "As the passage notes").
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a paragraph arguing whether the printing press or the internet had a greater impact on the spread of information. Cite at least one piece of evidence from the passage in your argument.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.2) Reread the passage. What are two main ideas? Write each as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.3) Explain the cause-and-effect relationship between the printing press and rising literacy rates. Use a signal word (because, as a result, therefore).

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> The monarch butterfly migrates up to 3,000 miles each fall from Canada and the northern United States to mountain forests in central Mexico. Scientists have discovered that monarchs use a combination of the sun's position and the Earth's magnetic field to navigate. Climate change threatens this migration because rising temperatures are shifting the timing of plant growth that monarchs depend on for food.

1. Cite evidence that explains how monarchs navigate during migration. Use a signal phrase and quotation marks.
2. What can you infer about the future of monarch migration if climate change continues? Cite evidence from the passage to support your inference.

**Expected Answers:**
1. According to the passage, "monarchs use a combination of the sun's position and the Earth's magnetic field to navigate" during their long migration.
2. Inference: Monarch migration may be disrupted if climate change continues. The passage states that "rising temperatures are shifting the timing of plant growth that monarchs depend on for food." If monarchs cannot find food along their route because plants bloom at different times, their migration could fail and their population could decline.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with specific evidence cited and inference supported by textual evidence**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the C-E-C method using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Provide a short passage (3-4 sentences) and guide the student through selecting the strongest evidence for a given claim
- Practice distinguishing between explicit questions (answer is stated directly) and inference questions (answer requires reasoning beyond the text)
- Use the Evidence Strength Chart to sort strong and weak evidence options

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 2 on determining central idea
- Encourage the student to practice citing evidence when reading informational articles independently

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Evidence Strength Ranking:** Provide a claim about the passage and five possible pieces of evidence. Have the student rank them from strongest to weakest and explain their reasoning.
2. **Inference Journal:** Have the student read a short informational article and write three inferences, each supported by cited evidence using the C-E-C method.
3. **Reverse Engineering:** Provide a completed C-E-C response and ask the student to identify which part is the claim, which is the evidence, and which is the connection.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common error at this level is students who cite evidence but fail to explain the connection. They quote a sentence and stop. Consistently prompt: "You found good evidence. Now tell me -- how does that evidence support your point?"
- Some students will select evidence that is only tangentially related to the question. Help them practice asking, "Does this specific quote directly support my specific claim?" If not, they need to search further.
- Signal phrases are a mechanical skill that improves with practice. Post a list of options (The author states, According to the passage, The text reveals, As the author explains, The passage notes) and encourage students to vary their choices.
- For inference questions, remind students that a strong inference is supported by the text plus reasonable logic. Wild guesses are not inferences. If a student's inference has no textual support, redirect them to the evidence first.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Claim:** Before the printing press, book production was extremely slow and expensive. **Evidence:** The passage states that "books in Europe were copied entirely by hand, a process so slow and expensive that only wealthy individuals, churches, and universities could afford them" and that "a single handwritten Bible could take a scribe more than a year to complete." **Connection:** These details show that books were luxury items beyond the reach of ordinary people, making knowledge scarce and inaccessible.
2. **Claim:** Gutenberg's innovation was the development of reusable letter blocks that made printing faster and cheaper. **Evidence:** The passage explains that his innovation was "the development of movable metal type -- individual letter blocks that could be rearranged and reused to print different pages." **Connection:** This system was revolutionary because it eliminated the need to carve a new block for each page, allowing printers to produce many different books efficiently.
3. **Claim:** The printing press was a catalyst for major social transformation. **Evidence:** The text reveals that "literacy rates climbed as ordinary people gained access to written knowledge for the first time" and that "new ideas in science, religion, and philosophy spread rapidly, contributing to the intellectual movements of the Renaissance and the Reformation." **Connection:** By making books affordable and widely available, the press shifted power from the few who controlled written knowledge to the many who could now read and think independently.
4. **Claim:** The passage emphasizes Chinese printing to clarify that Gutenberg's contribution was not the idea of printing but a specific technical improvement. **Evidence:** The author notes that Gutenberg's innovation "was not the concept of printing itself, which had existed in China for centuries, but the development of movable metal type." **Connection:** This distinction matters because it shows that innovation often builds on existing ideas. Gutenberg did not invent printing from nothing; he improved the technology in a way that made it practical for European languages and mass production.

### Interleaved Review
5. Main Idea 1: Gutenberg's printing press revolutionized book production by making it faster and cheaper through the use of movable metal type. Main Idea 2: The widespread availability of affordable books transformed European society by increasing literacy and accelerating the spread of new ideas.
6. As a result of the printing press making books affordable, ordinary people gained access to written knowledge for the first time, which caused literacy rates to climb across Europe.
