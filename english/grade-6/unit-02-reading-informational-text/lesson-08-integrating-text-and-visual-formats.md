# English Language Arts — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 8

## Topic
Integrating Text and Visual Formats

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Integrate information presented in text with information presented in visual formats (charts, tables, diagrams, maps)
- Explain what a visual element adds to a text that the words alone do not convey
- Explain what the text adds that the visual element alone does not convey
- Develop a more complete understanding of a topic by combining information from both text and visual sources

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine author's purpose and how it shapes content (Lessons 6-7)
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis (Lesson 1)
- Ability to interpret information presented visually (charts, graphs, tables) from Grade 5
- Understanding of how details convey central ideas (Lesson 3)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages with text-described visual elements
- Text-Visual Integration Chart graphic organizer (columns: Information from Text Only, Information from Visual Only, Information from Both, Combined Understanding)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Ruler or straightedge (optional, for drawing simple charts)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept that informational texts often present information in multiple formats, and effective reading requires integrating them.

**Activity: What the Numbers Add**

Say: "When you read a science textbook, a news article, or a report, you often see more than just words. You see charts, graphs, tables, maps, diagrams, and photographs. These visual elements are not decoration -- they contain information that the text alone may not fully communicate."

Write on the board:
- Text: "Solar energy has grown significantly in the United States over the past decade."
- Table:

| Year | Solar capacity (gigawatts) |
|------|---------------------------|
| 2010 | 2.5 |
| 2015 | 25 |
| 2020 | 76 |
| 2023 | 162 |

Say: "The text says solar energy has grown 'significantly.' The table shows it went from 2.5 to 162 gigawatts -- a 65-fold increase. Which gives you a better understanding?"

Allow the student to respond.

Say: "Neither one alone tells the full story. The text gives context (this is about the United States, it happened over a decade). The table gives precise data the text does not include. When you put them together, your understanding is much richer. Today, you are going to learn how to integrate text and visual information to build a complete picture."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students a systematic approach to integrating information from text and visual formats.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Integrate:** To combine information from different sources or formats into a unified, complete understanding
- **Visual format:** Information presented as a chart, graph, table, map, diagram, illustration, or other non-text element
- **Quantitative information:** Data expressed as numbers, measurements, percentages, or statistics
- **Complement:** When text and visual elements work together, each providing information the other does not

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When a text includes a visual element, I use a three-step strategy called Text-Visual Integration."

Write on the board:
1. **Read the text first.** Understand the main ideas and details from the words.
2. **Study the visual.** What specific information does the visual show? Read titles, labels, axes, and data points.
3. **Integrate.** Ask three questions: What does the text tell me that the visual does not? What does the visual tell me that the text does not? How do they work together to build a more complete understanding?

Say: "Let me demonstrate with a passage that includes a described table."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Water Use Around the World"**

> Water is essential for life, yet it is not used equally across the globe. Agriculture is by far the largest consumer of freshwater worldwide, but the balance between agricultural, industrial, and domestic water use varies dramatically from one region to another. In countries with large farming sectors and limited industrial infrastructure, agriculture can account for more than 90 percent of all water use. In highly industrialized nations, factories, power plants, and manufacturing facilities consume a much larger share. Understanding these patterns is critical for developing effective water conservation strategies, because a solution that works in one region may be irrelevant in another.
>
> **Table: Percentage of Water Use by Sector in Selected Regions**
>
> | Region | Agriculture | Industry | Domestic |
> |--------|-------------|----------|----------|
> | South Asia | 91% | 2% | 7% |
> | Sub-Saharan Africa | 81% | 4% | 15% |
> | Europe | 33% | 54% | 13% |
> | North America | 39% | 48% | 13% |
> | Middle East | 85% | 5% | 10% |

Say: "Step 1 -- the text tells me that agriculture is the largest water consumer globally, that usage patterns vary by region, and that understanding patterns matters for conservation. Step 2 -- the table shows me exact percentages for five regions. Step 3 -- let me integrate."

Write on the board:
- **Text only:** Explains why patterns vary (farming sectors, industrial infrastructure), explains why it matters (conservation strategies differ by region)
- **Visual only:** Shows precise percentages for each region and sector, allows direct comparison across regions
- **Combined understanding:** The text explains the why, and the table shows the what. Together, I understand that South Asia and the Middle East use water overwhelmingly for agriculture (91% and 85%), while Europe and North America split water use more evenly between agriculture and industry. This means conservation strategies in South Asia should focus on irrigation efficiency, while European strategies should address industrial water use. Neither the text nor the table alone gives me this complete picture.

Say: "This is what integration looks like. You are not just reading the text and then separately reading the table. You are weaving the information together to understand something neither source communicates on its own."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice integrating text and visual information with facilitator support.

Say: "Let us work through a new example together."

**Passage B: "Endangered Species Recovery"**

> The Endangered Species Act, signed into law in the United States in 1973, has been credited with preventing the extinction of hundreds of species. Before the Act was passed, species such as the bald eagle, the gray wolf, and the American alligator were in severe decline due to hunting, habitat loss, and pesticide use. The Act established legal protections that prohibited hunting of listed species, preserved critical habitats, and required recovery plans for each species. While the Act has achieved notable successes, critics point out that the recovery process is extremely slow and that the vast majority of listed species have not yet recovered to the point where they can be removed from the endangered list.
>
> **Table: Selected Species Recovery Under the Endangered Species Act**
>
> | Species | Year Listed | Population at Listing | Current Population | Status |
> |---------|-------------|----------------------|-------------------|--------|
> | Bald eagle | 1967 | 417 nesting pairs | 71,400 nesting pairs | Recovered, delisted 2007 |
> | Gray wolf | 1974 | ~1,000 | ~5,700 | Partially recovered |
> | Grizzly bear | 1975 | ~700 | ~1,900 | Still listed |
> | Florida manatee | 1967 | ~600 | ~7,500 | Downlisted to threatened |
> | Whooping crane | 1967 | 54 | ~800 | Still listed |

Say: "Step 1 -- what does the text tell you?"

Expected response: The Endangered Species Act has prevented extinctions and achieved successes, but recovery is very slow and most species are still listed.

Say: "Step 2 -- what does the table tell you?"

Expected response: It shows specific population numbers for five species at the time of listing and currently. The bald eagle had an enormous recovery (417 to 71,400 pairs). The whooping crane grew from 54 to about 800 but is still listed. The grizzly bear nearly tripled but is still listed.

Say: "Step 3 -- integrate. What do you understand by combining text and table that you would not understand from either alone?"

Expected response: The text says the Act has "notable successes" but the "vast majority" have not recovered. The table illustrates both -- the bald eagle is a dramatic success story, but species like the grizzly bear and whooping crane, despite population growth, still have not recovered enough to be delisted. The table also shows how much populations varied at the time of listing: from 54 whooping cranes to about 1,000 wolves. The text does not give these numbers. Together, the text explains the policy and the table shows the measurable results. (Guide toward this integrated understanding.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently integrate text and visual information from a new passage.

**Passage C: "The Science of Nutrition Labels"**

> When the United States government required standardized nutrition labels on packaged food beginning in 1994, the goal was to give consumers clear, comparable information about what they were eating. Nutrition labels list key information including serving size, calories, and the amounts of specific nutrients such as fat, sodium, sugar, protein, and various vitamins. However, research suggests that many consumers struggle to interpret the information correctly. One common source of confusion is serving size: the amount listed on the label is often much smaller than what a person actually eats in one sitting. Another challenge is that labels present information as raw numbers and percentages, which requires mathematical reasoning that not all consumers find intuitive.
>
> **Sample Nutrition Label (described):**
>
> | Nutrition Facts | Amount |
> |----------------|--------|
> | Serving size | 2/3 cup (55g) |
> | Servings per container | 8 |
> | Calories per serving | 230 |
> | Total fat | 8g (10% Daily Value) |
> | Sodium | 160mg (7% Daily Value) |
> | Total sugars | 12g |
> | Added sugars | 10g (20% Daily Value) |
> | Protein | 3g |
>
> The full container holds 8 servings. If a person eats the entire container, they consume 1,840 calories (230 x 8), 64 grams of fat, and 80 grams of added sugars.

**Directions:** Integrate information from the text and the described nutrition label. Cite evidence from both sources in your responses.

1. What does the text tell you about nutrition labels that the label itself does not communicate? Identify at least two pieces of information.
2. What does the nutrition label show that the text does not explain? Identify at least two pieces of information.
3. The text mentions that "serving size is often much smaller than what a person actually eats." How does the full-container calculation at the end of the passage illustrate this point? Use specific numbers from the label.
4. How do the text and the visual element work together to develop the central idea that nutrition labels can be confusing?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with evidence from both text and visual elements.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. For question 4, explain what a reader would miss if they only read the text without examining the label, and what they would miss if they only examined the label without reading the text.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a paragraph proposing how nutrition labels could be redesigned to be less confusing. Use evidence from both the text and the label to support your proposal.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.2) What is the central idea of the text portion of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 5 review -- RI.5.1) Quote the sentence that identifies when standardized nutrition labels were first required.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Scientists tracked the average global temperature from 1900 to 2020. The text states: "Global temperatures have risen steadily since the mid-twentieth century, with the rate of warming accelerating in recent decades due to increased greenhouse gas emissions."
>
> | Decade | Average temperature change from 1900 baseline |
> |--------|-----------------------------------------------|
> | 1900s | 0.0 degrees Celsius |
> | 1950s | +0.2 degrees Celsius |
> | 1980s | +0.5 degrees Celsius |
> | 2000s | +0.8 degrees Celsius |
> | 2020s | +1.2 degrees Celsius |

1. What information does the table provide that the text alone does not?
2. How do the text and table together support the claim that warming is "accelerating"?

**Expected Answers:**
1. The table provides specific temperature measurements for each decade, showing exact numbers (0.0 to +1.2 degrees Celsius). The text says temperatures rose "steadily" and the warming is "accelerating," but does not give the precise figures.
2. Together, they show acceleration because the table reveals that the temperature increase per decade is growing larger over time: +0.2 from 1900s to 1950s, +0.3 from 1950s to 1980s, +0.3 from 1980s to 2000s, and +0.4 from 2000s to 2020s. The text names the cause (greenhouse gas emissions) and the table provides the measurable proof. Without the table, "accelerating" is just a word; with it, the reader can see the acceleration in the numbers.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with specific data from the table cited and integration of text and visual clearly demonstrated**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Text-Visual Integration strategy using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Provide a simple passage with a bar graph and practice the three-step process: read, study, integrate
- Use the Text-Visual Integration Chart graphic organizer to physically separate what comes from each source before combining

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9 on integrating information across sources
- Encourage the student to practice reading charts and graphs alongside text in science and social studies materials

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Create the Visual:** Give the student a text-only passage that describes numerical data. Have the student create a table, chart, or diagram that presents the data visually. Then explain what the visual adds to the text.
2. **Missing Half:** Provide a table without any accompanying text. Have the student write 3-4 sentences of text that complement the table -- adding context, explanation, or significance that the numbers alone do not convey.
3. **Real-World Integration:** Find an article in a newspaper or magazine that includes a chart or graph. Analyze how the text and visual work together, identifying what each contributes and how integration deepens understanding.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Since the visual elements in this lesson are text-described tables and charts (rather than actual images), consider drawing simple versions on the whiteboard or projecting them in a spreadsheet to make the visual integration more concrete and engaging.
- Students often read the text and skip the visual, or glance at the visual without studying it carefully. Teach them to spend as much time on a chart or table as they spend on a paragraph of text. Each data point is the equivalent of a sentence.
- The integration step is the most challenging. Students can usually identify what the text says and what the visual shows, but combining them into a unified understanding requires practice. Model the thinking: "The text tells me A, the table shows me B, and together I now understand C."
- This lesson connects to math skills (reading data, understanding percentages, calculating totals). If the student struggles with the mathematical aspects, provide support without letting math difficulty block the reading comprehension learning.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The text tells the reader: (a) The historical context -- that nutrition labels were required starting in 1994 and their goal was to give consumers clear information. (b) Research findings showing that consumers struggle to interpret labels, particularly regarding serving sizes and mathematical reasoning required. The label itself does not communicate any of this context or research.
2. The nutrition label shows: (a) Specific amounts of each nutrient (8g of fat, 160mg of sodium, 10g of added sugars). (b) The Daily Value percentages (10% for fat, 7% for sodium, 20% for added sugars). (c) The number of servings per container (8). The text does not include any of these specific numbers.
3. The label says the serving size is 2/3 cup with 230 calories. But the full container holds 8 servings, meaning the entire container contains 1,840 calories and 80 grams of added sugars. If a person eats the whole container thinking it is a single serving, they consume 8 times the listed amounts. This illustrates the text's point because the label's serving size (2/3 cup) is much smaller than the full container a person might eat, and without doing the math, a consumer might not realize the enormous difference.
4. The text and visual work together by showing the theory (text: labels are confusing) and the proof (visual: the label demonstrates exactly why). The text explains that serving sizes are misleading and math is required. The label makes this concrete: 230 calories sounds reasonable until you realize there are 8 servings in the container. Neither source alone fully conveys the central idea. The text without the label is abstract. The label without the text is just data -- you would not know that it represents a widespread consumer confusion problem.

### Interleaved Review
5. Central idea: Although nutrition labels were designed to help consumers understand what they are eating, many people struggle to interpret the information correctly due to confusing serving sizes and mathematical complexity.
6. "When the United States government required standardized nutrition labels on packaged food beginning in 1994, the goal was to give consumers clear, comparable information about what they were eating."
