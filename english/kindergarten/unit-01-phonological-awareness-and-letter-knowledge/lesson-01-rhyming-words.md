# English Language Arts — Kindergarten — Unit 1, Lesson 1

## Topic
Recognizing and Producing Rhyming Words

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Recognize whether two words rhyme (same ending sound)
- Produce at least one rhyming word when given a starter word

## Prerequisites
- Ability to listen to and repeat spoken words
- No formal literacy prerequisites — this is the starting point

## Materials Needed
- Familiar nursery rhyme books or printed nursery rhymes (e.g., "Jack and Jill," "Humpty Dumpty," "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star")
- 10-12 picture cards or small objects with simple, rhymable names (e.g., cat, hat, bat, dog, log, frog, sun, bun, fun, star, car, jar)
- Crayons and paper (optional, for extension)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build comfort and activate any rhyming knowledge the student already has.

**Activity: Nursery Rhyme Sing-Along**
Choose a familiar nursery rhyme and recite or sing it together. Good options:

*Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star*
*Jack and Jill went up the hill*
*Humpty Dumpty sat on a wall*

After reciting, say: "Did you hear any words that sounded alike at the end? Listen again..."

Recite slowly, emphasizing the rhyming words: "Twinkle, twinkle, little **star**, how I wonder what you **are**." Say: "Star and are — they sound the same at the end! That means they rhyme."

The goal is to hear what the student already notices about rhyming. Just observe and enjoy the rhyme together.

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept of rhyming — words that share the same ending sound.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Rhyme:** When two words sound the same at the end (cat/hat, dog/log)
- **Ending sound:** The last sound or sounds you hear in a word

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Define rhyming in a child-friendly way.**
Say: "Words that rhyme sound the same at the end. Listen: **cat** — **hat**. Cat, hat. They both end with the -at sound. They rhyme!"

Say: "Here are two more: **sun** — **bun**. Sun, bun. They both end with the -un sound. They rhyme!"

**Step 2: Show a non-example.**
Say: "Now listen to these: **cat** — **dog**. Cat, dog. Do they sound the same at the end? No! -at and -og are different. Cat and dog do NOT rhyme."

**Step 3: Rhyme song/chant.**
Teach this simple chant (to any rhythm the student enjoys):

*"Rhyming words sound the same at the end,*
*Cat and hat, they are rhyming friends!*
*Dog and log, they are rhyming friends!*
*If they match at the end, then they rhyme!"*

Clap along as you chant. Repeat 2-3 times.

**Step 4: Practice hearing rhymes.**
Say pairs of words. After each pair, ask: "Do these rhyme?"

1. **ball — tall** (Yes)
2. **cup — pup** (Yes)
3. **fish — dish** (Yes)
4. **bed — car** (No)
5. **run — sun** (Yes)
6. **book — tree** (No)

For each "yes," emphasize the matching ending: "Ball, tall — they both end with -all. They rhyme!"
For each "no," point out the difference: "Bed ends with -ed. Car ends with -ar. They don't sound the same at the end, so they don't rhyme."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "Do **man** and **can** rhyme?"
Think aloud: "Man ends with -an. Can ends with -an. They end with the same sound — -an. Yes, they rhyme!"

**Example 2:** "Do **hat** and **hill** rhyme?"
Think aloud: "Hat ends with -at. Hill ends with -ill. The ending sounds are different — -at and -ill. No, they do not rhyme."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice recognizing and producing rhymes together with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Rhyme Sorting (5 minutes)**
Lay out picture cards or objects in a group. Pick up one card (e.g., "cat") and say: "Cat. Can you find another picture that rhymes with cat?"

Work through 3-4 sets:
- **cat** — find **hat** and **bat**
- **dog** — find **log** and **frog**
- **sun** — find **bun** and **fun**
- **star** — find **car** and **jar**

*Facilitator prompt: "Say both words — cat, hat. Do they sound the same at the end? Yes! They rhyme."*

*If the student struggles:* Say the words slowly, emphasizing the ending. "C-**at**. H-**at**. Listen to the end — at, at. The same!" If the student picks a wrong match, say: "Let's check. Cat ends with -at. Dog ends with -og. Those are different. Let's try another one."

**Activity 2: Finish the Rhyme (5 minutes)**
Say a short sentence with a missing rhyming word. The student fills in a word that rhymes.

1. "I have a cat who wears a ___." (hat, bat, mat)
2. "The dog sat on a ___." (log, fog, hog)
3. "I see the sun, let's have some ___." (fun, bun, run)
4. "Look at the bee sitting on my ___." (knee, tree, key)

*Facilitator prompt: "The first word was 'cat.' What sounds like 'cat' at the end? -at! What word ends with -at?"*

*If the student struggles:* Offer a choice: "Does 'hat' or 'cup' rhyme with cat?" This lets them practice recognition before production.

---

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices rhyming with minimal support.

**Activity: Rhyme Time Game**
Say a word and ask the student to come up with as many rhyming words as they can. Nonsense words are allowed and should be celebrated — they show the student understands the sound pattern.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):** Choose whether the words rhyme.
- Do "pig" and "wig" rhyme? (Yes)
- Do "red" and "bed" rhyme? (Yes)
- Do "cat" and "pen" rhyme? (No)
- Do "hop" and "mop" rhyme? (Yes)
- Do "fish" and "ball" rhyme? (No)

**★★ (Stretch):** Finish the rhyme.
- "I see a frog sitting on a ___." (log)
- "The big red bug is on the ___." (rug, mug, jug)
- "A mouse is in my ___." (house)

**★★★ (Challenge):** Think of your own rhyming words.
- "Tell me two words that rhyme with 'cake.'" (lake, make, bake, rake, snake, wake)
- "Tell me two words that rhyme with 'ring.'" (sing, king, wing, thing, spring)
- "Can you make up a silly two-line rhyme?" (e.g., "I saw a bear combing his hair!")

### Interleaved Review
Not applicable — this is the first lesson. No prior material to review.

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to gauge mastery.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "Do **pan** and **man** rhyme?" (Yes)
2. "Do **hop** and **sit** rhyme?" (No)
3. "Tell me a word that rhymes with **bug**." (Any real or nonsense word ending in -ug: rug, mug, hug, dug, tug, lug, zug, etc.)

**Mastery = correctly answers both yes/no questions AND produces at least one rhyming word (real or nonsense) for question 3.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
This is completely normal. Rhyming is a new concept for many kindergarteners.
- Continue to recite nursery rhymes together daily, pausing before the rhyming word and letting the student fill it in
- Play "Do these rhyme?" with simple pairs throughout the day (during meals, walks, bath time)
- Repeat this lesson next session with different word pairs before moving to Lesson 2

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 2 (Beginning Sounds) next session. Continue to play rhyming games as warm-up activities in future lessons.

---

## Extension (Optional)

**For students who find rhyming easy:**
- Read a rhyming picture book (e.g., any Dr. Seuss book, "Sheep in a Jeep," or "Each Peach Pear Plum") and have the student predict the rhyming word before you say it
- Challenge the student to make up a silly two-line rhyme about a family member or pet
- Play "Rhyme Chain" — you say a word, the student says a rhyme, you say a rhyme for that word, and so on until someone gets stuck (cat, hat, bat, mat, sat, rat...)

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can run shorter than 30 minutes for students who already know nursery rhymes by heart. That is fine — use the remaining time for extension activities or read a rhyming book together.

**Nonsense words are welcome:** If the student says "dat" rhymes with "cat," celebrate it! The student is demonstrating understanding of the sound pattern. Nonsense rhymes show the student hears the -at ending and can manipulate it — that is exactly the skill we are building.

**Why rhyming matters:** Rhyming is one of the earliest phonological awareness skills. It teaches children that words can be broken apart and put back together based on their sounds. This is the foundation for everything that follows — segmenting, blending, decoding, and spelling.

**Cultural connection:** If the student's family speaks another language, explore rhyming in that language too. Many languages have rich rhyming traditions in their nursery rhymes and songs. Rhyming in any language builds the same underlying phonological awareness skill.

**Don't worry about:** Letters or print (this is an entirely oral/auditory lesson), whether the student can define "rhyme" in abstract terms (they just need to hear and produce them), or perfect pronunciation (focus on whether they hear the rhyming pattern, not on articulation).
