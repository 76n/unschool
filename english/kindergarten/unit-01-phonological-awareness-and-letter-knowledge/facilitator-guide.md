# Facilitator Guide — Kindergarten ELA, Unit 1: Phonological Awareness & Letter Knowledge

**Grade:** Kindergarten
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 1 — Phonological Awareness & Letter Knowledge
**Recommended Duration:** 6 lessons (approximately 2-3 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)

---

## Unit Overview

This is the foundational unit for all of kindergarten reading and writing. Students develop the ability to hear and manipulate the sounds in spoken language (phonological awareness) and then connect those sounds to written letters (letter knowledge).

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Recognize and produce rhyming words
- Isolate the beginning, ending, and middle sounds in simple words
- Blend individual sounds together to form words (e.g., /c/-/a/-/t/ = "cat")
- Segment simple words into their individual sounds
- Recognize all 26 uppercase and lowercase letters
- State the sound each letter makes (letter-sound correspondence)
- Form (write) all 26 uppercase and lowercase letters

**Why this matters:** Before children can read, they must understand that spoken words are made up of individual sounds, and that written letters represent those sounds. This unit builds the bridge between speaking and reading. A child who can hear the sounds in words and connect them to letters is ready to decode (read) and encode (spell) real words. Do not rush through this unit. The ear training in Lessons 1-4 is every bit as important as the letter work in Lessons 5-6 — children who struggle with reading later often have gaps in phonological awareness, not letter knowledge.

---

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, informally check that the student can:

- [ ] Speak in short sentences and follow simple oral directions
- [ ] Listen to a short story or song and show engagement (looking, clapping, responding)
- [ ] Hold a crayon or pencil with a basic grip (does not need to be a perfect tripod grip)
- [ ] Recognize that books have print, pictures, and are read from left to right

If the student cannot do these things, spend a few sessions on oral language activities (reading picture books aloud, singing nursery rhymes together, practicing holding a crayon) before beginning Lesson 1.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

Most materials are common household items. Gather these before starting:

- **Picture cards or small objects:** Items whose names are familiar to the child — a toy cat, a hat, a small ball, a cup, etc. You can also use hand-drawn picture cards or cut pictures from magazines. You need at least 20 picture cards/objects with clear, simple names.
- **Letter cards:** Index cards or paper squares with each uppercase and lowercase letter written large (Aa, Bb, Cc, etc.). You can make these together as a pre-unit activity.
- **Crayons, pencils, and blank paper**
- **A tray or shallow box with sand, salt, or rice** (for multi-sensory letter writing — a baking sheet with a thin layer of salt works perfectly)
- **A simple alphabet chart or poster** (hand-drawn or printed — one row showing all 26 letters with a picture cue for each, e.g., A-apple, B-ball)
- **Familiar nursery rhyme books or printed rhymes** (optional but helpful for Lesson 1)

You do NOT need workbooks, phonics kits, or special letter tiles. If you have them, great — but they are not required.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Rhyming Words | 1 session |
| 2 | Beginning Sounds | 1 session |
| 3 | Ending and Middle Sounds | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Blending and Segmenting | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Letter Recognition and Formation: A-M | 2-3 sessions |
| 6 | Letter Recognition and Formation: N-Z + Cumulative Review | 2-3 sessions |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate short session (15-20 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- If your student already knows nursery rhymes and can identify rhyming words, Lesson 1 may take only 20 minutes. Use the extra time for the extension activity.
- Lessons 3 and 4 (sound isolation and blending/segmenting) are the most cognitively demanding for young learners. If the student needs more time, split each across two sessions. This is expected and completely fine.
- Lessons 5 and 6 each cover 13 letters. Most kindergarteners will need 2-3 sessions per lesson to learn all the letters and practice formation. Do not try to rush through all 13 letters in a single sitting. A good pace is 4-5 new letters per session, with review of previously learned letters.
- If your student already recognizes most letters, Lessons 5-6 can focus primarily on formation (writing) and letter-sound correspondence.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student says "cat" and "car" rhyme because they start the same | Confusing rhyming (same ending sound) with alliteration (same beginning sound) | Emphasize: "Rhyming words sound the same at the END. Cat-hat rhyme because they both end with -at. Cat and car start the same, but they don't end the same." Use lots of examples side by side. |
| Student cannot isolate the first sound in a word — says the whole word instead | Sound isolation is a new skill; the student hears the word as one unit, not as separate sounds | Start with continuant sounds that can be stretched (/s/, /m/, /f/) rather than stop sounds (/b/, /t/, /k/). Say "mmmmouse" and stretch the first sound so the student can hear it separate from the rest. |
| Student confuses letter names with letter sounds (says "see" instead of /k/ for the letter C) | Letter names and sounds are different things, and this distinction takes time | Always teach the sound alongside the name. Say: "This letter's name is C. The sound it makes is /k/. When we see C in a word, we say /k/." Practice both, but emphasize sounds for reading readiness. |
| Student writes letters backwards (reversed b/d, reversed S, reversed N) | Normal developmental stage — spatial orientation for letters takes time to develop | Provide consistent starting points and verbal cues ("b starts with a line going down, then bumps out to the right"). Use the sand/salt tray for tactile reinforcement. Do not over-correct — most reversals self-correct by mid-first grade. |
| Student can blend sounds when you say them slowly but not quickly | Auditory blending speed develops gradually | Start very slowly (/c/..../a/..../t/) with long pauses, then gradually speed up. Use physical motions — slide your hand across the table from left to right as you blend, showing the sounds connecting. |
| Student can hear rhymes but cannot produce a rhyming word | Recognition comes before production — this is a normal progression | Start with a choice: "Does 'hat' rhyme with 'cat' or 'dog'?" Then move to fill-in: "Cat, hat, ___." Finally move to open production: "Tell me a word that rhymes with cat." |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or in everyday moments to build phonological awareness:

- "I'm thinking of a word that rhymes with 'dog.' Can you guess it?" (log, fog, hog)
- "Let's play a game. What sound does your name start with?"
- "I'm going to say a word really slowly: /b/-/i/-/g/. What word is that?"
- "Can you clap the sounds in 'sun'? /s/ (clap) /u/ (clap) /n/ (clap) — three sounds!"
- "Let's find things in the room that start with the /s/ sound."
- "I spy something that starts with the letter M. What could it be?"
- "Can you write the first letter of your name in the air with your finger?"
- "Let's say the alphabet together and point to each letter on our chart."

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support than these materials provide. They are NOT cause for alarm — they are signals to investigate further:

- After 2-3 weeks of daily practice, the student cannot identify whether two words rhyme (even with strong, obvious rhymes like "cat/hat")
- The student consistently cannot isolate the beginning sound of words, even with stretched continuant sounds (/mmm/ouse, /sss/un)
- The student shows significant difficulty remembering letter names or sounds despite repeated multi-sensory practice — learning 2-3 letters, then forgetting them the next session
- The student appears to have difficulty hearing distinctions between similar sounds (confuses /b/ and /p/, or /m/ and /n/ consistently)
- The student shows significant frustration, anxiety, or avoidance during phonological awareness or letter activities despite encouragement and patience

If you see these signs, consider:
- Consulting with a pediatrician or speech-language pathologist (particularly if sound discrimination is a concern)
- Having the student's hearing checked (mild hearing loss can look like phonological awareness difficulty)
- Exploring whether the student might benefit from occupational therapy (for persistent fine motor or letter formation concerns)
- Looking into early intervention or pre-K literacy programs in your area

Remember: children develop at different rates, especially at this age. Many of these challenges resolve with time and patient practice. The goal is to catch genuine difficulties early, not to create anxiety about normal variation.

---

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 2** (covering high-frequency sight words and CVC word reading), where they use their letter-sound knowledge and blending skills to read their first real words. The strong phonological awareness and letter knowledge built here makes decoding in Unit 2 much smoother.

Between units, continue to reinforce sound awareness and letter knowledge in everyday life. Point out letters on signs, cereal boxes, and book covers. Play rhyming games during car rides or at mealtimes. The more automatic letter-sound connections become, the more mental energy is freed for the new reading skills ahead.
