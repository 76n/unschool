# English Language Arts — Kindergarten — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Drawing and Dictating to Tell a Story

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Draw a picture that tells a simple story with a beginning, middle, and end
- Dictate at least two sentences about their drawing for the facilitator to write down
- Identify that writing tells a story just like talking does
- Connect a drawn picture to spoken words that the facilitator records as text

## Prerequisites
- Unit 2 complete (reads 10 high-frequency words, reads simple sentences, retells stories)
- Can hold a crayon or pencil and draw recognizable shapes and pictures
- Can speak in complete sentences to describe events

## Materials Needed
- Blank paper (at least 3 sheets — one for modeling, two for the student)
- Crayons, colored pencils, or markers
- A pencil for the facilitator to write dictated sentences
- A simple picture book or the "My Pet" story from Unit 2 (for reviewing what stories look like)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate the student's storytelling instincts by connecting oral storytelling to written stories.

**Activity: Tell Me About Your Day**
Say: "Tell me something that happened to you today — or yesterday. Anything at all!"

Listen to the student's response. Then say: "That is a story! You just told me a story with your words. Did you know that we can also write stories down? When we write a story, other people can read it even when we are not there to tell it."

Hold up the picture book or "My Pet" story. Say: "Someone told this story by writing it down. Today, you are going to tell YOUR own story — first with a picture, then with words that I will help you write."

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model the process of drawing a picture to plan a story and then dictating sentences for someone to write.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Story:** Something that tells what happened — it has a beginning, a middle, and an end
- **Dictate:** Telling your words to someone so they can write them down for you
- **Narrative:** A story about something that happened (real or made up)
- **Illustrate:** Drawing a picture to go with a story

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Show what a story needs.**
Say: "Every story has three parts. Something happens at the BEGINNING." Hold up one finger. "Then something happens in the MIDDLE." Hold up two fingers. "And something happens at the END." Hold up three fingers. "Beginning, middle, end — just like when you told me about your day."

**Step 2: Model drawing a story.**
Take a sheet of paper. Say: "I'm going to tell a story about a time I went to the park. First, I'll draw my story."

Draw a simple picture (stick figures are fine). As you draw, narrate: "Here I am walking to the park — that is the beginning. Here I am on the swing — that is the middle. Here I am going home — that is the end."

Your drawing does not need to be good. In fact, a simple drawing helps the student see that art skill is not the point — communication is.

**Step 3: Model dictating.**
Say: "Now I'm going to say my story out loud, and I'll write down what I say. Watch." Speak slowly and write as you talk:

"I went to the park." (Write this sentence under or next to your drawing.)
"I played on the swing." (Write this.)
"Then I went home." (Write this.)

Point to each sentence and read them back. Say: "Look — my story is in the picture AND in the words. Someone could read my words and know my story, even if they could not see my picture."

**Step 4: Name the process.**
Say: "When you say your story out loud and someone writes it down, that is called DICTATING. Today, you are going to draw a story and dictate it to me. I will write your words down for you."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Point to your drawing. Ask yourself aloud: "What happened first?" Point to the beginning part. "I went to the park. That is my first sentence." Write it. "What happened next?" Point to the middle. "I played on the swing. That is my second sentence."

**Example 2:** Draw a quick second picture — a stick figure eating an apple. Say: "What could my story be? Hmm... 'I ate a big red apple. It was yummy.' Two sentences! That is a short story." Write the two sentences below the drawing.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student creates their own story drawing and practices dictating with facilitator support.

**Activity: Draw Your Story**

Say: "Now it is your turn! Think of something that happened to you. It can be anything — playing with a friend, eating dinner, going somewhere, seeing an animal. Close your eyes for a moment and think of your story."

Give the student a moment to think. If the student cannot think of anything, offer prompts:
- "What did you eat for breakfast today?"
- "Did you play with any toys today?"
- "Tell me about a time you were really happy."

Once the student has an idea, say: "Draw a picture of your story. Try to show what happened at the beginning, what happened in the middle, and what happened at the end."

Give the student about 5 minutes to draw. As they draw, ask guiding questions:
- "What is happening in this part of your picture?"
- "And then what happened?"
- "How did it end?"

**Dictation Phase:**
When the drawing is done, say: "Beautiful! Now tell me your story. Start at the beginning. What happened first?"

Write down exactly what the student says, word for word. Do NOT correct grammar or change the wording. Write in large, clear print so the student can see the words appearing.

After writing each sentence, read it back while pointing to each word: "You said: 'I played with my dog.' Look — here are your words!"

*Facilitator prompts:*
- "What happened first?"
- "Then what happened?"
- "How did the story end?"
- "Is there anything else you want to add?"

*If the student struggles to dictate:* Ask about the picture. "I see you drew a dog. What is the dog doing?" Use the student's answer as the sentence: "The dog is running. Should I write that?"

*If the student gives only one sentence:* That is okay for the first attempt. Gently encourage: "Can you tell me one more thing about your picture?"

---

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student creates a second story with less support.

Give the student a fresh sheet of paper. Say: "Let's make another story! This time, you pick what to draw. When you are done drawing, tell me the words and I will write them down again."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Student draws a picture and dictates at least 2 sentences. Facilitator writes the sentences. Student watches the writing and can point to words afterward.

**Stretch:** Student draws a picture and dictates at least 3 sentences that include a clear beginning, middle, and end. After the facilitator writes, the student attempts to read the sentences back using sight words they recognize.

**Challenge:** Student draws a picture, dictates 3 sentences, AND attempts to write at least one sentence independently (invented spelling is expected and accepted). For example, the student might write "I LIK MI DOG" — this is a success.

### Interleaved Review
After the dictation, point to individual words the student should know from Unit 2. "Can you read this word?" Point to sight words (the, a, I, is, it, and, to, in, we, my) that appear in the dictated sentences. This connects new writing content to prior reading skills.

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What did you do to make your story today?" (Acceptable answers: drew a picture and told/dictated the words; drew and talked about it so you could write it)
2. Point to the dictated sentences. "Can you read any of the words in your story?" (Student should recognize at least 1-2 sight words)
3. "Does your story have a beginning and an end?" (Student should be able to point to or describe the beginning part and the ending part)

**Mastery = correctly addresses all 3 items.** Item 1 shows understanding of the process. Item 2 connects to prior reading skills. Item 3 shows basic narrative structure awareness.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice oral storytelling daily: "Tell me a story about ___" at mealtimes or bedtime
- Draw pictures together and talk about what is happening in them
- Read simple stories aloud and pause to identify the beginning, middle, and end
- Repeat this lesson next session with a different topic for the story

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 2 (Writing Simple Sentences). Continue encouraging the student to draw and tell stories between sessions.

---

## Extension (Optional)

**For students who want to do more:**
- Create a "story book" by stapling 3 sheets of paper together. The student draws one part of the story on each page (beginning, middle, end) and dictates sentences for each page.
- Share the story with a family member. The student "reads" their story by looking at the pictures and the dictated sentences. The facilitator can read the sentences aloud if needed, and the student points to each word.
- Have the student dictate a story about a favorite character from a book — a made-up story, not a retelling.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Why drawing comes first:** For five-year-olds, drawing IS planning. When a child draws a picture before writing, they are organizing their thoughts visually. The drawing serves as a scaffold for the words that follow. Research consistently shows that drawing-before-writing produces more detailed and coherent narratives in young children than writing alone.

**Why dictation matters:** The goal of this lesson is COMPOSITION — organizing thoughts into a story and expressing them in sentences. Dictation separates the cognitive work of composition from the physical challenge of letter formation. A child who cannot yet write fluently can still compose a rich, complex story through dictation. This builds the understanding that writing is communication, not just handwriting practice.

**Accept all stories:** There is no wrong story. If the student dictates "I like pizza. Pizza is good." — that is a valid narrative about their experience. Do not push for more complexity than the student is ready for. The goal is getting words on paper, not literary excellence.

**Transcribe faithfully:** Write exactly what the student says, including non-standard grammar ("Me and my mom goed to the store"). This validates the student's voice. You can model standard grammar naturally in your own speech without correcting the student's dictated sentences.

**W.K.3 alignment:** This lesson addresses Writing standard W.K.3 — "Use a combination of drawing, dictating, and writing to narrate a single event or several loosely linked events, tell about the events in the order in which they occurred, and provide a reaction to what happened."

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice and Independent Practice:** All answers are student-generated. There are no fixed correct answers. Evaluate based on:
- Did the student draw a picture with identifiable content?
- Did the student dictate at least 2 sentences?
- Do the sentences relate to the drawing?
- Is there evidence of sequence (something happened, then something else happened)?

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Student describes the draw-then-dictate process in their own words
2. Student reads at least 1-2 sight words from their dictated story
3. Student identifies or describes a beginning and end to their story
