# English Language Arts — Kindergarten — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Informative Writing — Teaching About Something

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Name a topic they know about and share at least two facts about it
- Draw a picture and write or dictate at least two sentences that give information about a topic
- Distinguish between opinion writing ("I like ___") and informative writing ("A ___ has/can/is ___")
- Use a simple informative sentence frame to organize facts about a topic

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 1 complete (drawing and dictating)
- Lesson 2 complete (writing sentences with capital, spaces, period)
- Lesson 3 complete (understands nouns and verbs)
- Lesson 4 complete (understands the difference between fact and opinion)

## Materials Needed
- Blank paper (at least 3 sheets — top half for drawing, bottom half for writing)
- Pencil and crayons
- Sentence frame strips written in large print:
  - **A ___ has ___.**
  - **A ___ can ___.**
  - **A ___ is ___.**
- A familiar animal toy, picture card, or book page showing an animal or common object (for modeling)
- Optional: a non-fiction picture book about animals or another topic the student knows

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate the student's knowledge about a topic and distinguish informative talk from opinion talk.

**Activity: What Do You Know?**
Say: "Let's play a game. I am going to pick a topic and tell you some things about it. Tell me if I am sharing a FACT or my OPINION."

Use the topic of dogs:
- "Dogs have four legs." (Fact)
- "Dogs are the best pet." (Opinion)
- "Dogs can run and jump." (Fact)
- "I like dogs more than cats." (Opinion)
- "Some dogs are big and some dogs are small." (Fact)

For each one, ask: "Fact or opinion?" Praise correct answers.

Then say: "Today we are going to write about FACTS — true things about a topic. This is called INFORMATIVE writing. Informative means you are TEACHING someone. You are sharing what you know, not just what you like."

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model informative writing using sentence frames and a familiar topic.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Informative writing:** Writing that teaches the reader facts about a topic
- **Topic:** What you are writing about (the subject)
- **Fact:** Something that is true — you can check it or see it
- **Detail:** A small piece of information that tells the reader more about the topic

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain the goal of informative writing.**
Say: "In Lesson 4, we wrote about what we LIKE. That was opinion writing. Today, we are going to write about what we KNOW. This is informative writing — we are teaching someone about a topic."

Say: "Pretend someone has never seen a cat before. What would you TEACH them about cats? You would not say 'Cats are the best' — that is your opinion. You would say things that are TRUE about all cats, so the person could learn."

**Step 2: Model with sentence frames.**
Display the three sentence frames:
- **A ___ has ___.**
- **A ___ can ___.**
- **A ___ is ___.**

Say: "I am going to write about cats. I will use these sentence frames to teach someone about cats."

Think aloud and write each sentence:
1. "What does a cat HAVE? A cat has soft fur." Write: **A cat has soft fur.**
2. "What CAN a cat do? A cat can jump and run." Write: **A cat can jump and run.**
3. "What IS a cat like? A cat is a small animal." Write: **A cat is a small animal.**

Read all three sentences back. Say: "Look — I taught someone three facts about cats! They know what a cat has, what a cat can do, and what a cat is like. That is informative writing."

Draw a simple cat above the sentences. Say: "My picture shows what I am writing about, just like in our other writing."

**Step 3: Compare to opinion writing.**
Write side by side:
- **Opinion: I like cats because they are cute.**
- **Informative: A cat has soft fur. A cat can jump.**

Say: "See the difference? The opinion tells what I THINK. The informative writing tells what IS TRUE. Both are good writing — they just have different jobs."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "Let's write about a fish. What does a fish have?" (Fins, scales, a tail.) Write: **A fish has fins.** "What can a fish do?" (Swim.) Write: **A fish can swim.** "Two facts! Someone could learn about fish from reading this."

**Example 2:** "Let's try the sun. What IS the sun?" Write: **The sun is hot.** "What CAN the sun do?" Write: **The sun can make us warm.** Point out: "We used a noun (sun) and verbs (is, can make) — just like we learned in Lesson 3!"

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student chooses a topic and composes an informative piece with facilitator support.

**Activity: Teach Me About It!**

Say: "Now it is YOUR turn to be the teacher! Pick a topic — something you know a lot about. It can be an animal, a food, a toy, a sport, a person in your family — anything you can teach me about."

Help the student choose a topic. Good options for kindergarteners:
- Their pet or a favorite animal
- A food they eat often
- A toy they play with
- Rain, snow, or the sun
- Their school or home

If the student cannot choose, offer: "Do you want to write about an animal, a food, or something else?" Narrow the choices.

**Step 1: Talk about it.**
Say: "Tell me about your topic. What do you know about ___?"

Let the student share facts. Listen and gently redirect opinions back to facts:
- If the student says "Dogs are the best," say: "That is a great opinion! For informative writing, tell me something TRUE about dogs that everyone would agree on."

**Step 2: Draw it.**
Say: "Draw a picture of your topic at the top of the page." Give 3-4 minutes.

**Step 3: Write the facts.**
Display the sentence frames. Say: "Pick one or two of these frames and fill them in about your topic."

Help the student write. Depending on writing level:
- **Independent writer:** Student writes their own sentences. Accept invented spelling.
- **Supported writer:** Facilitator writes the sentence frame and student fills in the blanks.
- **Emergent writer:** Student dictates and facilitator writes. Student reads back, pointing to words.

Target: at least 2 sentences with facts about the topic.

*Facilitator prompts:*
- "What does a ___ have?"
- "What can a ___ do?"
- "What is a ___ like? Is it big or small? What color is it?"
- "You told me two facts — that is great teaching!"

*If the student mixes in opinions:* Gently note: "That is your opinion — and it is a good one! Can you also tell me a FACT that is true for everyone?" Do not reject opinions outright; just redirect to facts.

*If the student can only think of one fact:* Ask specific questions to pull out more: "What does it look like? What does it feel like? Where does it live? What does it eat?"

---

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student creates a second informative piece with less support.

Say: "Let's write about a NEW topic — something different from what you just wrote about. Pick a new topic, draw it, and write at least two facts about it."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Student draws a picture and writes or dictates 2 informative sentences about a topic using the sentence frames. Sentences include facts (not opinions). Capital, spaces, period present.

**Stretch:** Student writes 3 informative sentences without relying on the sentence frames. The student includes at least one noun and one verb they can identify. Drawing matches the writing.

**Challenge:** Student writes 4 sentences: a topic sentence ("I know about ___"), two fact sentences, and a closing sentence ("Now you know about ___ too!"). Student writes independently with invented spelling accepted.

### Interleaved Review
After writing, review all prior skills:
- **Lesson 2:** "Check your sentence. Capital letter? Spaces? Period?"
- **Lesson 3:** "Can you find a noun in your sentence? Can you find a verb?"
- **Lesson 4:** "Is your sentence a fact or an opinion? How do you know?"
- **Unit 2:** Point to sight words in the student's writing. "Can you read this word?"

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What is informative writing?" (Writing that teaches facts about a topic; writing that tells true things so someone can learn.)
2. "Is this informative or opinion? 'A dog has four legs.'" (Informative — it is a fact.)
3. "Is this informative or opinion? 'Dogs are the best pet.'" (Opinion — not everyone agrees.)
4. "Tell me two facts about your topic." (Student states two facts that are true, not opinions.)

**Mastery = correctly answers items 1-3 AND states at least 2 facts in item 4.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice throughout the day: pick an object and say two facts about it ("This cup is red. It can hold water.")
- Read a simple non-fiction book together and identify facts on each page
- Play "Fact or Opinion?" as a daily game during meals or car rides
- Repeat this lesson next session with a different topic

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6 (Writing Review — Putting It All Together). Continue encouraging the student to share facts about topics they encounter.

---

## Extension (Optional)

**For students who want to do more:**
- Create an "All About ___" mini-book: 3 pages, each with a drawing and a fact sentence. Staple together for a "real book."
- Teach a family member about a topic using the informative sentences the student wrote. The student reads or presents their writing as a "lesson."
- Explore a new topic together: look at a picture of an animal the student has never seen before (penguin, octopus, elephant). The facilitator shares 3 facts. The student draws and writes about it.
- Compare writing types: the student writes one opinion sentence and one informative sentence about the same topic. "I like apples because they are yummy." vs. "An apple is a fruit. It can be red or green."

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Informative writing is hard for kindergarteners because opinions come naturally.** Five-year-olds are wired to express preferences. Stating objective facts requires a different cognitive mode. Be patient with the distinction. If a student keeps writing opinions, gently redirect but do not make them feel wrong. Over time, repeated exposure to the fact/opinion distinction will build the skill.

**The sentence frames are essential scaffolds.** "A ___ has ___" and "A ___ can ___" give students a structure to hang their knowledge on. Without frames, many kindergarteners will struggle to convert what they know into sentences. Frames reduce cognitive load so the student can focus on content, not sentence construction.

**Accept approximation.** "A dog is big" is a perfectly acceptable informative sentence, even though not all dogs are big. At this level, we are teaching the concept of informative writing, not demanding scientific precision. If the student writes about THEIR dog and says "My dog is brown," that is informative writing about a specific topic.

**Invented spelling continues to be welcomed.** "A KAT HAS FER" demonstrates informative writing, CVC knowledge, and sentence conventions. Celebrate the composition.

**W.K.2 alignment:** This lesson addresses Writing standard W.K.2 — "Use a combination of drawing, dictating, and writing to compose informative/explanatory texts in which they name what they are writing about and supply some information about the topic."

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice and Independent Practice:** All answers are student-generated. There are no fixed correct answers. Evaluate based on:
- Does the student name a topic?
- Does the student provide at least 2 facts (not opinions)?
- Does the drawing relate to the topic?
- Do the sentences follow conventions (capital, spaces, period)?

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Informative writing teaches facts about a topic (accept student's own wording)
2. "A dog has four legs" is informative (fact)
3. "Dogs are the best pet" is an opinion
4. Student states 2 true facts about their chosen topic
