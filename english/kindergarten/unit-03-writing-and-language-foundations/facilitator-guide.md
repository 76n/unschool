# Facilitator Guide — Kindergarten ELA, Unit 3: Writing & Language Foundations

**Grade:** Kindergarten
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 3 — Writing & Language Foundations
**Recommended Duration:** 6 lessons (approximately 4-5 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)

---

## Unit Overview

In Units 1 and 2, your student learned to recognize letters, connect them to sounds, read high-frequency words, read simple sentences, and retell stories. Now it is time to move from reading to WRITING. This unit teaches the student that writing is communication — that they can put their own thoughts, stories, opinions, and knowledge onto paper for others to read.

The unit begins with the most accessible entry point into writing: drawing a picture and dictating a story aloud while the facilitator writes the words. From there, the student learns to write simple sentences independently, discovers that words come in different types (nouns and verbs), and practices three distinct purposes for writing: telling a story (narrative), sharing an opinion, and teaching someone facts (informative). By the end, the student can produce simple written compositions across all three types.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Use drawing, dictating, and writing to compose a narrative (a story about something that happened)
- Use drawing, dictating, and writing to compose an opinion piece (stating what they like and why)
- Use drawing, dictating, and writing to compose an informative text (teaching facts about a topic)
- Write simple sentences with a capital letter at the beginning, spaces between words, and a period at the end
- Identify and use frequently occurring nouns (naming words) and verbs (action words)
- Distinguish between facts and opinions

**Why this matters:** Writing is where reading and thinking become visible. When a child writes "I like my dog because he is fun," they are simultaneously applying letter formation, sight word knowledge, sentence structure, and the ability to organize thoughts into coherent language. Writing also builds reading — children who write regularly become better readers because they understand how language works from the inside out. Most importantly, writing gives children a voice. A five-year-old who learns they can put their ideas on paper and have them read by others gains a powerful sense of agency.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should have completed Unit 2 (High-Frequency Words & Emergent Reading) or demonstrate equivalent skills:

- [ ] Reads at least 8 of 10 high-frequency words by sight (the, a, I, is, it, and, to, in, we, my)
- [ ] Understands concepts of print (left-to-right, top-to-bottom, word spacing)
- [ ] Can read simple sentences composed of sight words and CVC words
- [ ] Can retell a familiar story including characters and at least 2 events
- [ ] Recognizes all 26 uppercase and lowercase letters
- [ ] Can form recognizable versions of most letters when writing
- [ ] Speaks in complete sentences

If the student has significant gaps in sight word recognition (cannot read at least 6 of 10 words), return to Unit 2 Lessons 2-3 for targeted review. Strong sight word knowledge is essential because those same words form the foundation of the student's early writing.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Blank paper:** Plenty of blank or lightly lined paper. The best format for kindergarten writing is paper with a large drawing space at the top and 2-3 writing lines at the bottom. You can create this by drawing a horizontal line across the middle of blank paper.
- **Pencils and crayons:** Pencils for writing, crayons for drawing. Thick pencils or pencil grips may help students who struggle with fine motor control.
- **A spacer tool:** A popsicle stick, a thin piece of cardboard, or the student's finger — used to create consistent spaces between words. This is essential for Lesson 2 and beyond.
- **Index cards or paper strips:** For sight word cards (carry over from Unit 2), noun/verb word cards (Lesson 3), and sentence frame strips (Lessons 4-5).
- **Sentence frame strips:** Write these in large, clear print on strips of paper:
  - **I like ___ because ___.**
  - **A ___ has ___.**
  - **A ___ can ___.**
  - **A ___ is ___.**
- **Three label cards:** **Story (Narrative)**, **Opinion**, **Informative (Facts)** — used in Lesson 6 for sorting and identifying writing types.
- **Two sorting containers or areas:** For the noun/verb word sort in Lesson 3 (two paper plates, two sections of the table, or two small boxes).
- **Simple picture books:** Any books you have available for read-alouds and examples.

You do NOT need handwriting workbooks, pre-printed worksheets, or commercial writing programs. All materials can be handmade.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Drawing and Dictating to Tell a Story | 1 session |
| 2 | Writing Simple Sentences | 1-2 sessions |
| 3 | Nouns and Verbs | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Opinion Writing — My Favorite | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Informative Writing — Teaching About Something | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Writing Review — Putting It All Together | 1-2 sessions |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session (25-30 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 1 (Drawing and Dictating) is typically quick and enjoyable. Most students are eager to draw and tell stories. If the student finishes early, have them make a second story — extra practice is always welcome.
- Lesson 2 (Writing Simple Sentences) is a milestone. This is the student's first independent writing with conventions. It may take 2 sessions — one for learning the rules, one for practicing. Do not rush. If the student is not consistently leaving spaces or remembering the period after one session, spend an extra session on practice before moving on.
- Lesson 3 (Nouns and Verbs) is conceptual. Some students grasp the noun/verb distinction immediately; others need more time with physical sorting and acting out. If the student is confused, spend a second session focused entirely on sorting games before moving to Lesson 4.
- Lessons 4 and 5 (Opinion and Informative Writing) introduce new writing purposes. Each may need 2 sessions — one for learning the type with heavy facilitation, one for more independent practice.
- Lesson 6 (Review) can be done in one session if the student has mastered all previous lessons. If there are gaps, use Lesson 6 as a diagnostic and reteach weak areas before the assessment.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student does not leave spaces between words — writing looks like one long string of letters | The student has not internalized word boundaries in writing (even though they can see spaces when reading). This is extremely common in early kindergarten writers. | Introduce the spacer tool immediately. After each word, the student places the tool on the paper and starts the next word on the other side. Use it consistently until spacing becomes automatic. Also, have the student count words orally before writing: "How many words? Three. So you will write three groups of letters with spaces between them." |
| Student forgets capital letters at the beginning of sentences | The concept of "sentence" as a unit of writing is still developing. The student may see writing as a continuous stream rather than as separate sentences. | Use a checklist the student can touch after each sentence: "Capital? Spaces? Period?" Make it physical — the student touches each item on the checklist before writing the next sentence. Color-code the capital letter (write it in a special color) to make it visually salient. |
| Student confuses opinion and informative writing — gives opinions when asked for facts | The distinction between "what I think" and "what is true" is developmentally sophisticated. Five-year-olds naturally express preferences. | Use the "everyone agrees" test: "Would EVERYONE agree with this? If your friend might disagree, it is an opinion. If everyone would say the same thing, it is a fact." Give lots of practice examples. Do not worry about mastery — exposure to the distinction is the goal. |
| Student can dictate rich, complex stories but cannot write more than a few words independently | Oral language far outpaces written language at this age. This is normal and expected. The student's ideas are not the problem — the physical act of writing is the bottleneck. | Continue allowing dictation alongside independent writing. Let the student write as much as they can, then switch to dictation for the rest. Gradually increase the writing portion over time. Never limit the student's composition to what they can physically write — their ideas should always be the priority. |
| Student writes only about the same topic every time | This could mean the student is comfortable with one topic or uncertain about how to write about new subjects. Repetition is not a problem at this stage. | Gently offer new topics: "You wrote a great story about your dog last time. Today, can you write about something different — maybe your family, or a food you like?" If the student resists, allow the repeated topic but push for a different writing TYPE: "You wrote a story about your dog. Now write an opinion about your dog." |
| Student refuses to write because they cannot spell words correctly | Fear of making mistakes can paralyze early writers. The student may have received the message that spelling must be "right" before writing counts. | Explicitly validate invented spelling: "In this class, writers are brave. You write the sounds you hear, and that is exactly right for right now. 'KAT' is how a brave writer spells 'cat.' I can read it! You will learn the grown-up spelling later — for now, just get your ideas on paper." Model invented spelling yourself occasionally. |
| Student identifies all words as nouns or all as verbs | The noun/verb distinction has not clicked yet. The student may be guessing or applying one label to everything. | Return to the physical test: "Can you DO this word? Act it out. If you can act it out, it is a verb. If it is a THING you can see, touch, or name, it is a noun." Use actual objects: hold up a cup (noun), then clap (verb). Repeat the sorting activity from Lesson 3 with new words. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to reinforce writing and language skills:

- "Tell me a story about something that happened today. I'll write it down if you want!"
- "What is your opinion about this? Do you like it or not? Why?"
- "Can you teach me something you know? Tell me a fact about ___."
- "I spy a noun! Can you point to a naming word?"
- "What verb are you doing right now? Running? Eating? Sitting?"
- "Let's write a sentence together. What do you want to say?"
- "Check your sentence — does it start with a capital letter?"
- "Read me what you wrote. Point to each word."
- "Is that a fact or an opinion? How do you know?"
- "You are a writer! What will you write about today?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- After 3-4 weeks of regular practice, the student cannot write any recognizable letters — letter formation remains extremely difficult despite repeated instruction from Units 1-3
- The student cannot dictate a coherent sentence (2+ words that express a thought) even with prompting and support
- The student shows extreme frustration or distress during any writing activity, consistently refusing to draw, dictate, or write despite a supportive environment
- The student cannot distinguish between a noun and a verb after multiple sessions with physical sorting and acting out — cannot categorize even the most concrete examples (e.g., "cat" as a naming word, "jump" as an action word)
- The student cannot produce any written output beyond copying — they can copy sentences but cannot generate original words, even with sentence frames

If you see these signs, consider:
- Reviewing fine motor skills — difficulty with letter formation may indicate a need for occupational therapy or fine motor activities (play-doh, cutting with scissors, stringing beads) before extensive writing instruction
- Having the student's vision checked — difficulty with letter formation or spacing on paper can sometimes relate to visual-motor integration challenges
- Consulting with a speech-language pathologist if the student struggles to form sentences orally (difficulty with dictation may indicate an oral language delay, not a writing problem)
- Allowing more time at the drawing and dictating stage before pushing for independent writing — some students need a longer runway

Remember: kindergarteners develop writing readiness at highly variable rates. A student who can only dictate and draw in January may be writing sentences independently by April. The goal is steady progress and a positive relationship with writing, not a specific timeline.

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 4**, where they will continue building on the writing foundation established here. The three writing types (narrative, opinion, informative) will become more developed, sentence structures will grow more complex, and the student will begin working toward longer compositions.

Between units, continue encouraging daily writing of any kind. Keep a writing journal where the student writes (or dictates) one sentence per day. Post the student's writing on the refrigerator or wall — making writing public validates the student as a real author. Read the student's writing back to them regularly so they experience the complete writing-to-reading cycle.

Continue reviewing Unit 2 sight words — the student will use these words constantly in their writing. The more automatic sight word recognition becomes, the more fluently the student can write.
