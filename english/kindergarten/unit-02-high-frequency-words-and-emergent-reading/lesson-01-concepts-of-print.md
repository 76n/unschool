# English Language Arts — Kindergarten — Unit 2, Lesson 1

## Topic
Concepts of Print

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Demonstrate left-to-right directionality when looking at a line of print
- Demonstrate top-to-bottom page progression (start at the top, move down)
- Point to individual words in a sentence, showing understanding that words are separated by spaces
- Distinguish between a letter and a word

## Prerequisites
- Unit 1 complete (letter recognition, letter-sound correspondence)
- Familiarity with being read to (has been exposed to picture books)

## Materials Needed
- A simple picture book with large print (one sentence per page is ideal)
- A pointer (pencil, chopstick, or the student's finger)
- Index cards or paper strips — one with a single letter, one with a single word, one with a sentence
- Crayons and paper

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect the student's existing book experience to the new skills.

**Activity: Book Exploration**
Hand the student a picture book. Say: "Show me how you would hold this book to read it. Which way does it go?"

Observe:
- Does the student hold the book right-side up?
- Does the student open it from the correct side (front cover)?
- Does the student look at the pages from left to right?

Say: "You already know a lot about books! Today we're going to learn exactly how the words work on the page — where to start reading, which way to go, and how to tell one word from the next."

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the four key concepts of print explicitly.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Print:** The written words on a page (not the pictures)
- **Word:** A group of letters with a space on each side
- **Letter:** A single symbol from the alphabet (like A, b, or M)
- **Space:** The empty gap between words

### Instruction Notes

**Concept 1: We read the words, not just the pictures.**
Open the picture book to a page with both text and an illustration. Point to the illustration and say: "This is the picture. It helps us understand the story." Then point to the text and say: "These are the words. This is what we actually read. The words tell us exactly what the story says."

**Concept 2: We start at the top left and go right.**
Point to the first word on the page. Say: "When we read, we always start HERE — at the top, on the left side." Sweep your finger slowly under the text from left to right. "We go this way — from left to right, just like this."

If there is a second line, continue: "When we get to the end of this line, we go back to the left side and drop down to the next line. Like this." Demonstrate the return sweep — finger goes back to the left margin and drops down one line.

**Concept 3: Each word is separate — spaces show us where one word ends and the next begins.**
Point to the sentence on the page. Say: "Watch my finger. I'm going to point to each word, one at a time." Touch each word individually, pausing briefly between words.

Say: "See these little gaps?" Point to the spaces between words. "These spaces tell us where one word stops and the next word starts. Without spaces, all the letters would be smushed together and we couldn't tell the words apart."

Write on paper: "Iseeacat" and below it: "I see a cat." Ask: "Which one is easier to read? The spaces help!"

**Concept 4: A letter is not the same as a word.**
Hold up the card with a single letter (e.g., "B"). Say: "This is a letter. Just one letter — B."
Hold up the card with a word (e.g., "cat"). Say: "This is a word. It has three letters together — c, a, t — and they make the word 'cat.'"
Hold up the card with a sentence (e.g., "I see a cat."). Say: "This is a sentence. It has four words, and lots of letters."

Ask: "Can you point to a letter? Can you point to a word?"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Open to a page of the picture book. "Show me where I should start reading." (Student should point to the top-left word.) "Now show me which way I go." (Student should sweep left to right.)

**Example 2:** Point to a sentence in the book. "How many words are in this sentence? Let's count them together." Touch each word and count aloud.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice the concepts of print together with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Pointer Reading (5 minutes)**
Read a page of the picture book aloud, slowly. The student's job is to point to each word as you read it. Give the student the pointer.

Say: "I'm going to read the words. You point to each word as I say it. Ready?"

Read slowly enough for the student to track. If the student falls behind or points to the wrong word, gently guide their hand: "This word is next. See the space? That tells us we've moved to a new word."

Read 3-4 pages this way.

*Facilitator prompt: "Where do we start? At the top, on the left. Point to the first word. Good — now move to the next word. See the space? That means a new word is coming."*

*If the student struggles:* Slow down. Read one word at a time with a clear pause. Tap the table between words to make the rhythm physical.

**Activity 2: Word Counting (5 minutes)**
Write these sentences on paper (large, clear print):

1. I see a cat.
2. The dog is big.
3. We go to school.

For each sentence, ask the student to:
1. Point to where we start reading
2. Point to each word (left to right)
3. Count the words
4. Find the spaces between words
5. Point to one letter and one word

*Facilitator prompt: "Touch each word. Count as you touch. How many words?"*

---

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices concepts of print with minimal support.

**Activity: Draw and Label**
Give the student paper and crayons. Ask them to draw a picture of something they like (a pet, a toy, food, etc.).

Then say: "Now let's write a sentence about your picture." Help the student write a simple sentence (e.g., "I like cats."). If the student cannot write independently, write the sentence yourself in dotted letters for the student to trace.

Then ask:
- "Point to the first word."
- "How many words did we write?"
- "Show me a space between two words."
- "Point to a letter. Now point to a word."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Student points to words, counts words, and identifies spaces in the sentences you wrote together.

**Stretch:** Student attempts to write their own sentence (with invented spelling encouraged). Check that they leave spaces between words.

**Challenge:** Give the student a new page from the picture book they have not seen yet. Ask them to point to each word while you read it aloud — no practice run first.

### Interleaved Review
Review 3-4 letter sounds from Unit 1: show letter cards and ask "What sound does this letter make?" This keeps letter-sound correspondence active while adding new print skills.

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Open the picture book to a new page. "Point to where I should start reading." (Top left)
2. "Which way do I go?" (Left to right)
3. Write "The cat is big." on paper. "How many words are in this sentence?" (4)
4. Point to the "a" in "cat" and the word "cat." "Which one is a letter and which one is a word?" (Student correctly identifies the letter and the word)

**Mastery = correctly answers all 4 items.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice pointer reading daily — have the student point to words as you read aloud at bedtime or storytime
- Place green sticker dots at the start of lines in books to remind the student where to begin
- Practice counting words in short spoken sentences first ("I like pizza" — hold up a finger for each word)
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 2

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 2 (High-Frequency Words Set 1) next session. Continue to have the student point to words during read-alouds to reinforce tracking.

---

## Extension (Optional)

**For students who find concepts of print easy:**
- Go on a "word hunt" around the house — find print on cereal boxes, signs, labels, and mail. The student identifies where the words start, points to individual words, and counts how many words they can find on each item.
- Have the student "teach" a stuffed animal or sibling how books work: "Show teddy where we start reading. Show teddy which way we go."
- Write a sentence with no spaces (e.g., "Ilikecats") and challenge the student to figure out where the spaces should go.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson often runs shorter than 30 minutes for students who have been read to regularly. That is fine — use the extra time for a read-aloud where the student practices pointing to words.

**Left-handed students:** Concepts of print apply the same way regardless of handedness. However, left-handed students may naturally want to start on the right side of the page. Be patient and consistent with left-to-right guidance.

**Why concepts of print matter:** These skills seem obvious to literate adults, but they are not obvious to children. Children who have not been read to may not know that the print (not the pictures) carries the story, or that English text goes left to right. Without these basics, phonics and word recognition cannot function — the student would not know where to look or in what order to process the letters.

**Bilingual families:** If the family reads in a language with different directionality (Arabic, Hebrew, Urdu, Chinese), acknowledge this explicitly: "In Arabic, we read this way (right to left). In English, we read this way (left to right). You're learning two different ways — that's amazing!" Do not frame one directionality as "correct" — frame it as "what English does."
