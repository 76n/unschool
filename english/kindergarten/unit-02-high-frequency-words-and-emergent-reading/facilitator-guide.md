# Facilitator Guide — Kindergarten ELA, Unit 2: High-Frequency Words & Emergent Reading

**Grade:** Kindergarten
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 2 — High-Frequency Words & Emergent Reading
**Recommended Duration:** 6 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)

---

## Unit Overview

In Unit 1, your student learned to hear the sounds in spoken language and connect those sounds to written letters. Now it is time to read. This unit bridges letter knowledge and actual reading by teaching two things simultaneously: high-frequency words that appear constantly in simple texts, and the concepts of print that let a child navigate a page.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Read at least 10 common high-frequency words by sight (the, a, I, is, it, and, to, in, we, my)
- Identify basic concepts of print: left-to-right directionality, top-to-bottom, return sweep, word spacing, difference between a letter and a word
- Read simple sentences composed of known high-frequency words and decodable CVC words
- Retell a familiar story including the characters, setting, and major events
- Identify the characters, setting, and key events when listening to a story read aloud

**Why this matters:** High-frequency words make up roughly 50-75% of the words in any text a beginning reader encounters. A child who can recognize "the," "is," "and," and "I" on sight — without needing to sound them out — can begin reading real sentences almost immediately. Meanwhile, understanding how print works (we read left to right, top to bottom, words have spaces between them) is the physical navigation system that makes reading possible. And retelling stories builds the comprehension habit from the very start — reading is not just decoding words, it is understanding what those words mean together.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should have completed Unit 1 (Phonological Awareness & Letter Knowledge) or demonstrate equivalent skills:

- [ ] Recognizes and produces rhyming words
- [ ] Isolates beginning and ending sounds in simple words
- [ ] Blends 3 individual sounds into a word (/c/-/a/-/t/ = "cat")
- [ ] Segments simple CVC words into individual sounds
- [ ] Recognizes all 26 uppercase and lowercase letters by name
- [ ] States the sound each letter makes
- [ ] Writes recognizable forms of most letters

If the student has gaps in letter-sound correspondence (cannot state the sound for most letters), return to Unit 1 Lessons 5-6 for targeted review before beginning this unit. Strong letter-sound knowledge is essential for the CVC decoding that begins in Lesson 4.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **High-frequency word cards:** Index cards or paper strips with these words printed large and clearly: **the, a, I, is, it, and, to, in, we, my**. Write each word on its own card. Make two sets — one for teaching, one for games.
- **Simple picture books or printed stories:** You need 2-3 very simple books appropriate for emergent readers. Look for books with:
  - Large print
  - One sentence per page
  - Pictures that match the text
  - Repetitive sentence patterns (e.g., "I see a ___.")
  - If you do not have books, you can write simple sentences on paper and draw pictures to go with them
- **Letter cards from Unit 1** (for CVC word building in Lesson 4)
- **A pointer** (a pencil, chopstick, or finger works fine) for tracking print
- **Crayons, pencils, and paper**
- **A familiar storybook** the student has heard before (for retelling practice in Lesson 5) — any picture book with a clear beginning, middle, and end

You do NOT need pre-made readers, leveled book sets, or flash card kits. Handmade materials work just as well.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Concepts of Print | 1 session |
| 2 | High-Frequency Words Set 1: the, a, I, is, it | 1-2 sessions |
| 3 | High-Frequency Words Set 2: and, to, in, we, my | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Reading Simple Sentences | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Retelling Familiar Stories | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Reading and Retelling — Putting It All Together | 1-2 sessions |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate short session (20-25 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 1 (Concepts of Print) is often quick for students who have been read to regularly. If your student already tracks left to right and knows what a "word" is on the page, this lesson may take 15-20 minutes. Use the extra time for a read-aloud.
- Lessons 2 and 3 (high-frequency words) are cumulative. The student must remember Set 1 words while learning Set 2. If the student is not retaining Set 1 words after two sessions, do NOT move to Set 2. Spend an extra session reviewing Set 1 with games and repetition.
- Lesson 4 (reading sentences) is a milestone. This is where the student reads connected text for the first time. Allow extra time and celebrate every sentence read, no matter how slow or halting.
- Lessons 5 and 6 can be done across multiple read-alouds. Use different stories each time to give the student varied practice with retelling.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student tries to sound out high-frequency words letter by letter and gets confused (e.g., tries to decode "the" as /t/-/h/-/e/) | Many high-frequency words are not fully decodable with basic phonics (e.g., "the" does not follow simple CVC patterns). The student is applying their Unit 1 skills — which is great — but these words need a different approach. | Explain: "Some words are tricky. We can't sound them out the regular way, so we learn them by heart — like learning someone's name. This word is 'the.' Let's say it together and remember what it looks like." Always acknowledge the sounding-out attempt before redirecting. |
| Student reads right-to-left, bottom-to-top, or randomly across the page | Directionality is learned, not innate. Some students (especially those exposed to right-to-left languages) may start differently. | Use a pointer consistently. Say: "We always start here (point to top left) and go this way (sweep right)." Place a green dot at the start of each line and a red dot at the end as a visual cue. |
| Student memorizes the pictures instead of reading the words | The student is using context clues (a valid reading strategy!) but not attending to the actual print | Partially cover the picture and ask the student to read the words first, then uncover the picture to check. Say: "You're right — the picture shows a cat! Now let's look at the words and see if they match." |
| Student confuses similar-looking high-frequency words (e.g., "is" and "it," "in" and "I") | These words share letters and differ by only one letter. Visual discrimination of similar words takes practice. | Place confusable words side by side. Point out the difference: "Look — 'is' ends with S and 'it' ends with T. The first letter is the same, but the last letter is different." Use color to highlight the different letter. |
| Student can read individual words on flash cards but cannot read them in a sentence | Reading words in isolation and in context are different cognitive tasks. In a sentence, the student must track left to right, hold earlier words in memory, and process the next word simultaneously. | Start with very short sentences (2-3 words) and build up. Point to each word as the student reads. Let the student practice the same sentence multiple times — fluency on familiar sentences builds confidence for unfamiliar ones. |
| Student retells a story by listing details randomly rather than in sequence | Narrative sequencing (first, then, finally) is a developing skill at this age | Use hand signals or visual cues: hold up one finger for "first," two for "then/next," three for "at the end." Ask: "What happened first?" before "What happened next?" Give the student a simple three-part organizer (beginning, middle, end). |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to reinforce emergent reading skills:

- "Can you find the word 'the' on this page? How many times do you see it?"
- "I'm going to read this sentence. Can you point to each word as I read it?"
- "Let's read the signs we see. Can you find any words you know?"
- "What happened at the beginning of the story? What happened at the end?"
- "Who was the story about? Where did it take place?"
- "Can you read this cereal box? Do you see any words you know?"
- "Let's write a sentence together. You pick a word and I'll help with the rest."
- "Show me where I should start reading on this page."

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- After 2-3 weeks of daily practice, the student cannot reliably read any of the 5 Set 1 high-frequency words (the, a, I, is, it) — even with repeated multi-sensory exposure
- The student consistently cannot track left to right across a line of print, even with a pointer and visual cues
- The student shows no interest in or awareness of print in the environment (signs, labels, book titles) despite regular exposure
- The student can decode CVC words in isolation but shows extreme difficulty reading even 2-word sentences
- The student cannot retell any part of a story after hearing it read aloud with picture support (cannot name a single character or event)

If you see these signs, consider:
- Reviewing Unit 1 skills — gaps in letter-sound knowledge may be making word recognition harder than it needs to be
- Having the student's vision checked (difficulty tracking print or recognizing similar-looking words can sometimes indicate vision issues)
- Consulting with a reading specialist or early literacy interventionist
- Exploring whether the student might benefit from more structured phonics-first instruction before sight word memorization

Remember: kindergarteners develop reading readiness at very different rates. Some students arrive reading simple sentences; others are just beginning to connect letters to sounds. Both are normal. The goal is steady progress, not a specific timeline.

---

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 3: Writing & Language Foundations**, where they begin composing their own texts — first through drawing and dictating, then through writing simple sentences using the high-frequency words and CVC words they now know. The reading skills built in this unit directly support writing: a student who can read "I see a cat" is ready to write "I see a dog."

Between units, continue to practice reading simple sentences together daily. Point out high-frequency words everywhere — in books, on signs, on food packaging. The more automatic word recognition becomes, the more the student can focus on meaning and enjoyment when reading.
