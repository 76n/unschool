# English Language Arts — Kindergarten — Unit 2, Lesson 5

## Topic
Retelling Familiar Stories — Characters, Setting, and Major Events

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Retell a familiar story in sequence (beginning, middle, end)
- Identify the characters in a story (who the story is about)
- Identify the setting of a story (where and when the story takes place)
- Identify the major events in a story (what happens)

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-4 complete (concepts of print, 10 high-frequency words, reading simple sentences)
- The student has heard at least one storybook read aloud before (any familiar story)

## Materials Needed
- A familiar picture book the student knows well (one that has been read aloud at least 2-3 times before) — good options include "The Three Little Pigs," "Goldilocks and the Three Bears," "The Very Hungry Caterpillar," or any story with a clear beginning, middle, and end
- A second picture book for guided practice (one the student has heard but not as many times)
- Three large index cards or paper labeled: **Beginning**, **Middle**, **End**
- Crayons and paper (for drawing)
- Sight word cards (for warm-up review)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review sight words and transition into story thinking.

**Activity: Sight Words + Story Bridge**

Part 1 (2 minutes): Quick sight word flash card review — shuffle all 10 cards, student reads each one.

Part 2 (3 minutes): Say: "You've been learning to read words and sentences. But reading isn't just about saying the words — it's about understanding the story. Today we're going to practice telling stories back in our own words. That's called retelling."

Ask: "What's your favorite story? Can you tell me one thing that happens in it?" Let the student share freely. This activates story knowledge and builds excitement.

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the concepts of character, setting, and major events, and model retelling.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Character:** A person or animal in the story — who the story is about
- **Setting:** Where and when the story happens — the place and time
- **Events:** The things that happen in the story — what the characters do
- **Retell:** Telling the story again in your own words, in the right order
- **Beginning:** What happens first
- **Middle:** What happens next — the main part of the story
- **End:** What happens last — how the story finishes

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the three story elements.**
Say: "Every story has three important parts we need to know:
- **Who** is in the story? Those are the **characters**.
- **Where** does the story happen? That's the **setting**.
- **What happens** in the story? Those are the **events**."

Hold up three fingers. "Who, where, what happens. Characters, setting, events. Let's practice finding these in a story you already know."

**Step 2: Model with a well-known story.**
Use a story nearly every child knows. Example using "The Three Little Pigs":

Read or summarize the story briefly (or have the student help you tell it, since they know it).

Then model retelling:

"Now I'm going to retell this story. Watch how I do it."

"**Characters:** The three little pigs and the big bad wolf. Those are who the story is about."

"**Setting:** The story happens outside and at the pigs' three houses."

"**Events — Beginning:** The three little pigs each build a house. One builds a straw house, one builds a stick house, and one builds a brick house."

"**Middle:** The wolf comes and blows down the straw house. Then he blows down the stick house. Both pigs run to the brick house."

"**End:** The wolf tries to blow down the brick house but he can't. The pigs are safe. The wolf gives up (or falls in the chimney, depending on the version)."

"See how I told the story in order — first, next, last? That's retelling."

**Step 3: Teach the retelling hand.**
Hold up one hand. Assign each finger a retelling element:
- Thumb: **Characters** (who)
- Pointer: **Setting** (where)
- Middle finger: **Beginning** (what happened first)
- Ring finger: **Middle** (what happened next)
- Pinky: **End** (what happened last)

Say: "Your hand can help you remember. When you retell a story, go through your fingers: Who? Where? Beginning, middle, end."

Practice the retelling hand together with "The Three Little Pigs."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "Let's try another story you might know — 'Goldilocks and the Three Bears.'"
- Characters: Goldilocks and the three bears (Papa, Mama, Baby)
- Setting: The bears' house in the woods
- Beginning: The bears go for a walk. Goldilocks finds their house and goes in.
- Middle: She tries their porridge, chairs, and beds. Baby Bear's are always "just right."
- End: The bears come home and find Goldilocks. She runs away.

**Example 2:** If the student knows "The Very Hungry Caterpillar":
- Characters: The caterpillar
- Setting: Outside, on leaves and food
- Beginning: A tiny caterpillar hatches from an egg. He's very hungry.
- Middle: He eats through lots of food — one apple, two pears, three plums, and more. He gets a stomachache.
- End: He eats a green leaf, feels better, makes a cocoon, and becomes a beautiful butterfly.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice retelling with facilitator support using a picture book.

**Activity: Read and Retell Together**

Read a short picture book aloud to the student (or re-read a familiar one). Choose a book with:
- Clear characters
- A recognizable setting
- 3-5 main events in sequence

After reading, guide the retelling:

**Step 1:** Lay out the three cards labeled Beginning, Middle, End.

**Step 2:** Ask guiding questions:
- "Who was in this story?" (Characters) — Have the student name the characters. Write or draw them on a piece of paper.
- "Where did the story happen?" (Setting) — Have the student describe the place.
- Point to the Beginning card: "What happened first? At the very start of the story?"
- Point to the Middle card: "What happened next? What was the big thing that happened in the middle?"
- Point to the End card: "How did the story end? What happened last?"

**Step 3:** Have the student retell the whole story using the three cards as cues.

*Facilitator prompts:*
- "And then what happened?"
- "Who did that? Which character?"
- "Where were they when that happened?"
- "What happened at the very end?"
- Use the retelling hand if the student gets stuck.

*If the student struggles:*
- Flip through the pictures in the book. Say: "Look at this picture. What's happening here? And this one? And this one?" The pictures serve as visual cues for sequencing.
- If the student lists random events out of order, say: "Those things did happen! Let's put them in order. What happened FIRST? Before everything else?"
- If the student cannot name a character, point to the character in the illustrations: "Who is this? What is their name in the story?"

---

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student retells a story with minimal support.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Student retells the story just read using the Beginning/Middle/End cards as cues. The facilitator asks guiding questions only when the student gets stuck.
- "Who was in the story?"
- "Where did it happen?"
- "What happened at the beginning? In the middle? At the end?"

**Stretch:** Student retells the story WITHOUT looking at the book or the Beginning/Middle/End cards. Uses only the retelling hand for support.

**Challenge:** Student retells a DIFFERENT story entirely — one they know well from past reading (not the one just read). This requires retrieving the story from memory rather than recent exposure.

### Interleaved Review
After retelling, practice reading 2-3 sentences from Lesson 4 to maintain sentence-reading skills.

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment.

**Exit Ticket:**

Using the story read during this lesson:

1. "Who are the characters in the story?" (Student names at least 1 main character)
2. "Where does the story happen?" (Student names or describes the setting)
3. "Tell me what happened in the beginning, the middle, and the end." (Student describes at least 1 event for each part, in approximately correct sequence)

**Mastery = names at least 1 character AND identifies the setting AND provides at least 1 event for each story part (beginning, middle, end) in roughly correct order.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Re-read the same story and pause after each page to ask: "What just happened?"
- Practice retelling with very simple, highly familiar stories (fairy tales the student has heard many times)
- Use the illustrations as a retelling aid — have the student flip through the pictures and tell the story based on what they see
- Act out the story with toys or stuffed animals — physical retelling often helps before verbal retelling

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6 (Reading and Retelling — Putting It All Together) next session.

---

## Extension (Optional)

**For students who retell confidently:**
- Draw a three-panel "comic strip" of the story: one panel for beginning, one for middle, one for end. Write a caption under each panel.
- Retell the story from a different character's point of view: "What would the wolf say happened?"
- Compare two stories: "How are the three pigs and Goldilocks the same? How are they different?"
- Start a "story journal" — after each read-aloud, the student draws and/or writes about the characters, setting, and events.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Why retelling matters at the emergent reading stage:** It is tempting to focus entirely on decoding at this age — but comprehension habits need to start now. A child who can decode words but never thinks about what the story means will struggle with reading comprehension later. Retelling builds the habit of actively processing meaning while reading (or listening).

**Retelling is not memorizing.** The student does not need to repeat the story word-for-word. A retelling in the student's own words, hitting the major characters, setting, and events, is exactly what we want. If the student adds their own commentary ("And then the wolf was mean, but I think the pigs were smart!"), that is excellent — it shows engagement and interpretation.

**Sequence is the hardest part.** Getting the events in order (beginning → middle → end) is cognitively demanding for kindergarteners. Many will describe events out of sequence or focus on the most dramatic moment (usually the middle/climax) while skipping the beginning. This is developmentally normal. The Beginning/Middle/End cards and the retelling hand are scaffolds that help build sequencing skills over time.

**Use the same story multiple times.** There is no pedagogical reason to use a new story every session. Rereading the same book and retelling it each time builds fluency, confidence, and deeper understanding. Students notice new details on each rereading.

**Don't skip picture support.** At this age, illustrations are a critical comprehension tool. Students use pictures to confirm their understanding, recall events, and make inferences. Picture support is not "cheating" — it is developmentally appropriate scaffolding.

## Answer Key

Answers will vary based on the story used. For assessment purposes, look for:
- **Characters:** At least 1 correctly named (or described, if the student cannot remember the name)
- **Setting:** A reasonable description of where the story takes place
- **Beginning:** At least 1 event from the start of the story
- **Middle:** At least 1 event from the main part of the story
- **End:** At least 1 event from the conclusion

Sequence should be roughly correct — beginning events should come before middle events, which should come before ending events. Minor sequence errors within a section (e.g., swapping two middle events) are acceptable at this level.
