# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 2, Lesson 4

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Asking Questions About Informational Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Generate questions before, during, and after reading an informational text
- Explain the purpose of asking questions at each stage (predicting, clarifying, reflecting)
- Use questioning as a strategy to deepen comprehension of informational text
- Identify which of their questions were answered by the text and which were not

## Prerequisites
- Ability to read Grade 2 level informational text independently or with minimal support
- Understanding of informational text features (Lesson 1 of this unit)
- Ability to identify main topic and key details (Lesson 2 of this unit)
- Familiarity with the concept of asking questions about stories (Grade 1 comprehension skill)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "Amazing Sharks" (included below) — initially covered or folded so only the title and first sentence are visible
- Three-column "Question Chart" worksheet (columns: Before Reading / During Reading / After Reading)
- Sticky notes in three colors (e.g., yellow = before, blue = during, pink = after)
- Question word cards: Who? What? Where? When? Why? How? (written on index cards)
- Pencils
- Anchor chart paper or whiteboard

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate the student's natural curiosity and connect questioning to everyday life, establishing that good readers ask questions just like curious people do in daily situations.

**Activity: The Mystery Box**

Place a small object inside a bag or box where the student cannot see it. (If no physical object is available, describe the scenario verbally.)

Say: *"I have something hidden in this bag. You cannot see it. What do you want to know about it?"*

Allow the student to ask questions naturally. They might ask: "What is it? Is it big or small? What color is it? Can I touch it?"

After 4-5 questions, say: *"Notice what you just did! You asked questions because you were curious and wanted to learn more. That is exactly what good readers do when they read. Good readers do not just read the words — they ask questions before, during, and after reading. Questions help us understand what we are reading and remember it better."*

Say: *"Today we are going to learn a special strategy: asking questions before we read, while we read, and after we finish reading."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the three stages of questioning (before, during, after) and explain the purpose of each stage.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Predicting** — Making a guess about what will happen or what you will learn, based on clues you already have
- **Clarifying** — Making something clearer; asking a question to better understand something confusing
- **Reflecting** — Thinking back on what you read; looking at the big picture after finishing
- **Questioning strategy** — A reading tool where you ask questions before, during, and after reading to help you understand the text

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the three-stage questioning strategy (2 minutes)**

Say: *"There are three times you should ask questions when you read an informational text: before you start, while you are reading, and after you finish. Each time you ask questions, you are doing something different. Let me explain."*

Draw three columns on the board or anchor chart: **Before Reading**, **During Reading**, **After Reading**.

**Step 2: Before Reading — Predicting Questions (3 minutes)**

Say: *"Before you read, you look at the title, the headings, and the pictures. Then you ask questions about what you THINK you will learn. This is called predicting. Your questions help get your brain ready — they give you a reason to read."*

Write under "Before Reading": **Purpose: Get your brain ready. Predict what you will learn.**

Say: *"Before-reading questions often start with: 'What will I learn about...?' or 'I wonder...' or 'What do I already know about...?'"*

Write example question starters:
- What will I learn about ___?
- I wonder if...
- What do I already know about ___?

**Step 3: During Reading — Clarifying Questions (3 minutes)**

Say: *"While you are reading, you might come across something confusing, surprising, or interesting. That is when you ask clarifying questions. These questions help you understand the text better. If something does not make sense, asking a question helps you figure it out."*

Write under "During Reading": **Purpose: Understand better. Clear up confusion.**

Say: *"During-reading questions often start with: 'What does ___ mean?' or 'Why did the author say...?' or 'How does ___ work?'"*

Write example question starters:
- What does ___ mean?
- Why did the author say ___?
- How does ___ work?
- I do not understand ___. What does it mean?

**Step 4: After Reading — Reflecting Questions (3 minutes)**

Say: *"After you finish reading, you think back on what you learned. You ask questions to reflect — to look at the big picture. You might also think about what you STILL want to know that the text did not tell you."*

Write under "After Reading": **Purpose: Think about what you learned. Wonder about what you still want to know.**

Say: *"After-reading questions often start with: 'What was the most important thing I learned?' or 'What do I still want to know about...?' or 'How does this connect to what I already knew?'"*

Write example question starters:
- What was the most important thing I learned?
- What do I still want to know about ___?
- How does this connect to something I already knew?
- What surprised me the most?

**Step 5: Quick review (1 minute)**

Say: *"So: before reading, we predict. During reading, we clarify. After reading, we reflect. Three stages, three kinds of questions. Let me show you how this works with a real passage."*

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Before-Reading Questions from a Title**

Show only this title: **"Amazing Sharks"**

Say: *"Let me model asking before-reading questions. I see the title 'Amazing Sharks.' My brain starts wondering right away."*

Think aloud: *"I wonder... what makes sharks amazing? I already know that sharks are big fish that live in the ocean. What will I learn that I do not already know? I predict I will learn about what sharks eat and where they live. Let me write my questions."*

Write on the chart under "Before Reading":
- What makes sharks amazing?
- What do sharks eat?
- Where do sharks live?

Say: *"See how I used the title to get my brain thinking? Now I have reasons to read — I want to find out the answers to my questions!"*

**Worked Example 2: During-Reading Question from a Confusing Sentence**

Read this sentence aloud: *"Sharks have special senses that help them find food in dark water."*

Say: *"Hmm. I understand that sharks find food, but what does 'special senses' mean? I know about the five senses — sight, hearing, touch, taste, and smell. But the text says 'special' senses. Are these different from regular senses? Let me write my during-reading question."*

Write on the chart under "During Reading":
- What are the "special senses" that sharks have? Are they different from our senses?

Say: *"Now I will keep reading to see if the text answers my question. If it does, great! If it does not, that might become an after-reading question — something I still want to know."*

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student applies the three-stage questioning strategy to an informational passage about sharks, with facilitator support at each stage.

**Activity: Question Detectives — "Amazing Sharks"**

This activity unfolds in three phases. Reveal the passage gradually.

---

### Phase 1: Before Reading (3 minutes)

Show ONLY the title and the first sentence of the passage:

> **Amazing Sharks**
>
> Sharks are one of the most incredible animals in the ocean.

Say: *"Before we read the whole passage, let's ask some before-reading questions. Look at the title and the first sentence. What do you wonder? What do you predict you will learn?"*

Guide the student to generate 2-3 before-reading questions. Write them on the chart (or on yellow sticky notes).

If the student struggles, prompt:
- *"What do you already know about sharks?"*
- *"What would you LIKE to know about sharks?"*
- *"The title says 'amazing' — what might be amazing about sharks?"*

Example questions the student might generate:
- How big can sharks get?
- Do all sharks eat people?
- What makes sharks amazing?
- Where do sharks live?

---

### Phase 2: During Reading (5-6 minutes)

Now reveal and read the full passage together. Pause at marked points to generate during-reading questions.

---

### Reading Passage: "Amazing Sharks"

Sharks are one of the most incredible animals in the ocean. There are more than 500 different **species** of sharks, and they come in many shapes and sizes.

**[PAUSE 1]**

The smallest shark is the dwarf lantern shark, which is only about the size of a human hand. The largest shark is the whale shark, which can grow to be as long as a school bus! Even though the whale shark is enormous, it only eats tiny sea creatures called **plankton**.

**[PAUSE 2]**

Sharks have been on Earth for a very long time — even longer than dinosaurs! Scientists believe sharks have lived in the ocean for more than 400 million years. Over all that time, sharks have become excellent hunters.

Sharks have special **senses** that help them find food. They can smell a tiny drop of blood in the water from far away. Some sharks can even feel the heartbeat of a small fish hiding in the sand. These special abilities make sharks some of the best hunters in the sea.

**[PAUSE 3]**

Most sharks are not dangerous to people. In fact, people are much more dangerous to sharks! Many shark species are in trouble because of pollution and overfishing. Scientists are working hard to protect sharks so they can keep living in our oceans for a long time.

Sharks are truly amazing creatures. From the tiny dwarf lantern shark to the giant whale shark, every species plays an important role in keeping the ocean healthy.

---

**At PAUSE 1:**

Say: *"We just learned there are more than 500 species of sharks. Do you have any questions so far?"*

If the student struggles: *"What does the word 'species' mean? Is there anything you are curious about?"*

Guide them to write a during-reading question on a blue sticky note. Example: "What does 'species' mean?" or "What are some different kinds of sharks?"

**At PAUSE 2:**

Say: *"We just learned about the smallest and largest sharks. Did anything surprise you? Do you have questions?"*

Guide toward questions like: "Why does the whale shark only eat tiny plankton if it is so big?" or "How small is a dwarf lantern shark exactly?"

**At PAUSE 3:**

Say: *"We just read about sharks' special senses. Do you have any questions about how sharks find food?"*

Guide toward questions like: "How can a shark feel a heartbeat in the sand?" or "How far away can a shark smell blood?"

Record all during-reading questions on the chart or on blue sticky notes.

---

### Phase 3: After Reading (2-3 minutes)

Say: *"Now we have finished reading. Let's ask some after-reading questions. Think about what you learned. What was the most interesting or surprising thing? Is there anything you still want to know?"*

Guide the student to generate 2-3 after-reading questions. Write them on the chart or on pink sticky notes.

If the student struggles, prompt:
- *"What surprised you the most in this passage?"*
- *"Is there anything the passage did NOT tell you that you wish it did?"*
- *"Did the passage answer all of your before-reading questions?"*

Example after-reading questions:
- Why are sharks important for keeping the ocean healthy?
- What kinds of pollution hurt sharks?
- Did the passage answer my question about whether sharks eat people? (Yes — most sharks are not dangerous to people.)

Say: *"Excellent detective work! Now let's look at your before-reading questions. Were any of them answered by the passage?"*

Go through each before-reading question and check: answered or not answered. This helps the student see that reading often answers our questions — and sometimes leaves us with new ones.

---

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate their ability to generate meaningful questions at all three stages independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):**
Using the "Amazing Sharks" passage and your question chart, answer the following:
1. Write one before-reading question you had. Was it answered by the passage? If yes, what was the answer?
2. Write one during-reading question you had. What part of the text made you ask that question?
3. Write one after-reading question — something you still want to know about sharks that the passage did not tell you.

**★★ (Stretch):**
Complete the On-level tasks, plus:
4. Look at your during-reading questions. Choose one that the passage DID answer. Write the answer in a complete sentence, and write the sentence from the passage that helped you find the answer.
5. Why is it helpful to ask questions DURING reading, not just before or after? Write one or two sentences explaining your thinking.

**★★★ (Challenge):**
Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:
6. Imagine a friend is about to read the "Amazing Sharks" passage. Write three questions they should think about while reading — one before-reading question, one during-reading question, and one after-reading question. Explain why each question would help them understand the passage better.

### Interleaved Review
**Review from Lesson 3 — How Reasons Support Points:**
7. Re-read the last full paragraph of the passage (starting with "Most sharks are not dangerous to people"). The author makes a point that people are more dangerous to sharks than sharks are to people. What reason does the author give to support this point?

---

## Check for Understanding (3-5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to determine whether the student can generate appropriate questions at each reading stage.

**Exit Ticket:**

Imagine you are about to read a passage titled **"All About Honeybees."**

1. Write one question you would ask BEFORE reading. What is the purpose of asking this question? (2 points — 1 for the question, 1 for stating the purpose)
2. While reading, you come across this sentence: *"Bees do a special dance called a waggle dance to tell other bees where to find flowers."* Write one question you would ask DURING reading. (1 point)
3. After reading a passage about honeybees, write one question you might ask to REFLECT on what you learned. (1 point)
4. Which stage of questioning helps you clear up something confusing: before, during, or after? (1 point)

**Mastery = 4 out of 5 points**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the three-column chart and review the purpose of each stage with simple examples
- Use a very short paragraph (3-4 sentences about a familiar topic like cats or pizza) and model all three stages together, with the student generating one question at each stage alongside the facilitator
- Provide the question-starter cards as a physical tool and have the student practice picking a card and forming a question at each stage
- Repeat the assessment the following day before moving to Lesson 5

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready to move to Lesson 5: Introduction to Opinion Writing
- Encourage the student to use the before/during/after questioning strategy every time they read a new informational text — at home, in the library, or in other subjects

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Question Journal:** The student starts a "Wonder Journal" where they write one before-reading, one during-reading, and one after-reading question every time they read a new informational text. They track which questions get answered and which remain unanswered.
- **Research a Question:** The student picks one after-reading question from today's lesson that was NOT answered by the passage. With facilitator guidance, they look up the answer in another book or a safe online resource. This introduces basic research skills.
- **Question Creator:** The student writes 3 questions about the shark passage that they could ask a friend. They write the answers on the back of each card, creating their own quiz.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Pacing:** The three-phase structure of the guided practice is the heart of this lesson. Each phase (before, during, after) should feel distinct. Use the different-colored sticky notes to reinforce the visual separation between stages.
- **Gradual release of the passage:** It is important to physically hide the passage during the before-reading phase so the student genuinely generates predictions based only on the title and first sentence. If the student can see the whole passage, they may skip predicting and jump to answering.
- **Building from Grade 1:** In Grade 1, students practiced asking "who, what, where, when, why, how" questions about texts. This lesson deepens that skill by organizing questions into three purposeful stages. The "when" of asking a question matters as much as the question itself.
- **Common misconception:** Students may think every question must be answered by the text. Normalize unanswered questions: *"Good readers often finish a text with new questions. That is a sign that your brain is working hard and wants to learn more!"*
- **Common misconception:** Students may ask very literal, surface-level questions (e.g., "What is the title?" or "How many paragraphs are there?"). Gently redirect toward content-level questions: *"Try asking a question about what sharks DO or what they are LIKE."*
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson supports RI.2.1 (ask and answer questions about key details in a text) and builds foundational skills for all informational text comprehension in this unit. Questioning is a cross-cutting strategy that supports RI.2.5, RI.2.8, and the upcoming writing standards (W.2.2) as students learn to anticipate what a reader needs to know.
- **The passage "Amazing Sharks"** is an original passage written for this curriculum. It is not taken from any published source.

| Before Reading | During Reading | After Reading |
|---|---|---|
| What makes sharks amazing? | What does "species" mean? (Species means a type or kind of animal.) | Why are sharks important for keeping the ocean healthy? |

| Where do sharks live? | How can a shark feel a heartbeat in the sand? | What can kids do to help protect sharks? |

**Before-reading questions answered by the passage:**
- "What makes sharks amazing?" — Answered: They come in many sizes, have been around for 400 million years, and have amazing senses.
- "What do sharks eat?" — Partially answered: Whale sharks eat plankton; other sharks are described as hunters but specific diets are not listed.

### Independent Practice

**★ On-level:**

1. Accept any reasonable before-reading question with indication of whether it was answered. Example: "I asked 'What do sharks eat?' The passage answered this partly — it said whale sharks eat plankton and other sharks are excellent hunters."
2. Accept any reasonable during-reading question with a reference to the text. Example: "I asked 'How can a shark feel a heartbeat in the sand?' I asked this when I read the sentence about sharks having special senses."
3. Accept any reasonable after-reading question. Example: "I still want to know what the most dangerous type of shark is. The passage did not tell me that."

**★★ Stretch:**

4. Accept any during-reading question with a text-based answer. Example: "I asked 'What does species mean?' The passage says there are more than 500 different species of sharks in many shapes and sizes. So a species must mean a type or kind of shark."
5. Accept any reasonable explanation. Example: "It is helpful to ask questions during reading because if something confuses you, you can try to figure it out right away instead of waiting until the end. It helps you understand the text better as you go."

**★★★ Challenge:**

6. Accept any three well-formed questions (one per stage) with reasonable explanations. Example:
   - Before: "What do you think makes sharks amazing?" — This helps get your brain ready and gives you something to look for.
   - During: "What does 'plankton' mean?" — This helps you understand a new word so you do not get confused.
   - After: "What was the most surprising fact about sharks?" — This helps you think about what you learned and remember it.

**Interleaved Review:**

7. The author's point is that people are more dangerous to sharks than sharks are to people. The reason the author gives is that many shark species are in trouble because of pollution and overfishing. This reason supports the point because it shows that human actions (pollution and overfishing) are hurting sharks, which means people are more of a threat to sharks than sharks are to people.

### Exit Ticket

1. Accept any reasonable before-reading question about honeybees. (1 point) Example: "What do honeybees eat?" Purpose: It gets my brain ready for reading and gives me something to look for. (1 point)
2. Accept any reasonable during-reading question related to the waggle dance sentence. (1 point) Example: "What does the waggle dance look like?" or "How does a dance tell other bees where flowers are?"
3. Accept any reasonable after-reading/reflecting question. (1 point) Example: "What was the most interesting thing I learned about honeybees?" or "What else would I like to learn about bees?"
4. During reading. (1 point)
