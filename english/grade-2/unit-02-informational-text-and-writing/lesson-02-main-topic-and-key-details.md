# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 2, Lesson 2

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Main Topic and Key Details in Informational Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the main topic of an informational passage (what the whole text is mostly about)
- Distinguish between the main topic and individual details
- Locate 3-4 key details that tell more about the main topic
- Organize the main topic and key details using a graphic organizer

## Prerequisites
- Ability to read Grade 2 level informational text independently or with minimal support
- Understanding of informational text features (Lesson 1 of this unit)
- Familiarity with the concept of "main idea" from Grade 1 (identifying what a text is mostly about)
- Basic experience with nonfiction/informational text

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "The Water Cycle" (included below)
- Main Topic and Key Details graphic organizer (one large box at the top labeled "Main Topic," three or four boxes below connected by lines, labeled "Key Detail")
- Highlighters or colored pencils (two colors: one for main topic, one for key details)
- Index cards or sentence strips (6-8 cards)
- Pencils
- Anchor chart paper or whiteboard

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about how information is organized and build the concept that a text is "mostly about" one big thing, supported by smaller pieces of information.

**Activity: Sort the Groceries**

Say to the student: *"Imagine you went to the grocery store and bought these things: apples, bananas, oranges, grapes, strawberries, and blueberries. If someone asked you, 'What did you buy at the store?' would you list every single item? Or would you say something shorter?"*

Allow the student to respond. Guide them toward: *"You might just say, 'I bought fruit.' That one word — fruit — is the BIG idea that covers all those items. The apples, bananas, oranges, and everything else are the details."*

Say: *"Reading works the same way. An informational text has one BIG topic — the main topic — and then lots of smaller pieces of information called details that tell us more about it. Today we are going to practice finding the main topic and the key details in an informational passage."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the student how to identify the main topic of a passage and distinguish it from key details, using clear strategies and modeling.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Main topic** — What the whole text is mostly about; the big subject the author is writing about
- **Key details** — Important pieces of information that tell more about the main topic
- **Graphic organizer** — A visual tool that helps us organize our thinking (like a chart or diagram)
- **Support** — To give more information about something; to hold it up with facts

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Define main topic vs. key details (3 minutes)**

Say: *"The main topic of a text is what the WHOLE text is mostly about. It is the big umbrella that covers everything in the passage. Key details are the important pieces of information under that umbrella. They tell us more about the main topic — they support it."*

Draw a simple umbrella on the board. Write "Main Topic" on the top of the umbrella. Draw raindrops underneath and label them "Detail 1," "Detail 2," "Detail 3."

Say: *"Think of it this way: the umbrella is the big idea, and the raindrops are the details that fall under it. Every detail connects back to the main topic."*

**Step 2: Teach the strategy for finding the main topic (3 minutes)**

Say: *"Here is a trick for finding the main topic. Ask yourself three questions:"*

Write on the board:
1. What is this text mostly about? (One or two words)
2. Does every section or paragraph connect to this subject?
3. Could I use this subject as the title?

Say: *"If you can answer 'yes' to all three questions, you have found the main topic."*

**Step 3: Teach the strategy for finding key details (2 minutes)**

Say: *"Once you know the main topic, you look for details that tell you MORE about it. Key details answer questions like: What? Where? When? How? Why? Not every sentence is a key detail — some sentences are less important. Key details are the ones that give you the most important information about the main topic."*

**Step 4: Introduce the graphic organizer (2 minutes)**

Show the student the Main Topic and Key Details graphic organizer. Point to the top box:

Say: *"The main topic goes up here at the top — that is the big idea. Then each box below holds one key detail. The lines connecting them show that the details support the main topic. We are going to fill one of these out together."*

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: A Short Paragraph — Apples**

Read this paragraph aloud:

> Apples are a popular fruit. They come in many colors, such as red, green, and yellow. Apples can be eaten raw or cooked in pies and sauces. They grow on trees in orchards. Many people pick apples in the fall.

Say: *"Let me find the main topic. I ask myself: What is this whole paragraph mostly about? Every sentence talks about... apples! That is my main topic."*

Write "Apples" in the top box of a sample graphic organizer on the board.

Say: *"Now I look for key details. The second sentence says they come in many colors — red, green, and yellow. That tells me more about apples. That is a key detail."*

Write "Apples come in many colors: red, green, and yellow" in the first detail box.

Say: *"The third sentence says apples can be eaten raw or cooked. That is another key detail about apples."*

Write "Apples can be eaten raw or cooked in pies and sauces" in the second detail box.

Say: *"The fourth sentence says they grow on trees in orchards. That tells me where apples come from — another key detail."*

Write "Apples grow on trees in orchards" in the third detail box.

Say: *"See how every detail connects back to the main topic? They all tell us more about apples."*

**Worked Example 2: Distinguishing a Detail from the Main Topic**

Say: *"Here is something tricky. A student read the apple paragraph and said, 'The main topic is that apples come in red, green, and yellow.' Is that right?"*

Pause. Then say: *"No — that is a detail, not the main topic. The main topic has to cover the WHOLE paragraph. 'Apples come in red, green, and yellow' only covers one sentence. The main topic is 'Apples' because every sentence in the paragraph tells us something about apples."*

Say: *"Remember: the main topic is the big umbrella. Details are the raindrops underneath."*

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student reads an informational passage about the water cycle and works with the facilitator to identify the main topic and key details, using the graphic organizer.

**Activity: Main Topic and Key Details — "The Water Cycle"**

Provide the student with the following passage. Read it aloud together first.

---

### Reading Passage: "The Water Cycle"

**How Water Moves Around the Earth**

Water is always moving around the Earth in a pattern called the **water cycle**. The water cycle has no beginning and no end — it just keeps going around and around.

The water cycle starts when the sun heats up water in oceans, lakes, and rivers. The heat turns the water into a gas called **water vapor**. The water vapor rises up into the sky. This step is called **evaporation**.

As the water vapor goes higher into the sky, it gets cooler. The cool air turns the water vapor back into tiny water drops. These tiny drops come together and form **clouds**. This step is called **condensation**.

When the clouds get full of water drops, the water falls back down to the ground as rain, snow, sleet, or hail. This is called **precipitation**. The water lands in oceans, lakes, rivers, and on the ground.

Then the sun heats the water again, and the whole cycle starts over. The water cycle is what gives us the rain and snow we need to live. Without it, there would be no fresh water to drink, and plants and animals could not survive.

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

**Step 1: Identify the main topic (3-4 minutes)**

Say: *"We just read this passage together. Now let's find the main topic. Remember our three questions. First: What is this text mostly about?"*

Allow the student to respond. If they say "water," guide them to be more specific: *"It is about water, but what about water specifically? The passage describes how water moves around. What is that process called?"*

Guide the student to identify: **The water cycle.**

Say: *"Let's check. Does every section or paragraph connect to the water cycle?"*

Walk through each paragraph briefly:
- Paragraph 1: Introduces the water cycle
- Paragraph 2: Evaporation (part of the water cycle)
- Paragraph 3: Condensation (part of the water cycle)
- Paragraph 4: Precipitation (part of the water cycle)
- Paragraph 5: The cycle starting over again

Say: *"Yes — every paragraph connects to the water cycle. Could 'The Water Cycle' work as a title? Yes, it could! So our main topic is 'The Water Cycle.' Write that in the top box of your graphic organizer."*

**Step 2: Find key details (5-6 minutes)**

Say: *"Now let's find key details. These should be important pieces of information that tell us more about the water cycle. Let's look at each paragraph. What does paragraph 2 teach us about the water cycle?"*

If the student struggles, guide: *"What happens when the sun heats water? What is this step called?"*

Help the student form a key detail: **The sun heats water and turns it into water vapor. This is called evaporation.**

Say: *"Great! Write that in the first detail box. Now what happens in paragraph 3?"*

Guide toward: **Water vapor rises and cools down, forming clouds. This is called condensation.**

Say: *"Excellent! Write that in the second box. What about paragraph 4?"*

Guide toward: **When clouds get full, water falls back as rain, snow, sleet, or hail. This is called precipitation.**

Say: *"Perfect! Write that in the third detail box."*

Optional fourth detail from paragraph 5: **Then the cycle starts all over again. The water cycle gives us the rain and snow we need.**

**Step 3: Review the organizer (1-2 minutes)**

Say: *"Look at your graphic organizer. You have the main topic at the top — the water cycle — and three (or four) key details below it. Every detail tells us more about how the water cycle works. The details support the main topic."*

Point to each detail and trace the line up to the main topic: *"This detail supports the main topic. This detail supports the main topic. They all connect."*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate their ability to identify main topic and key details independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):**
Using the "Water Cycle" passage, answer the following questions in complete sentences:
1. What is the main topic of this passage?
2. Write three key details from the passage that tell more about the main topic.
3. Which of these is a key detail and which is NOT a key detail? Circle the key detail.
   - a) Water vapor rises up into the sky.
   - b) The word "condensation" has five syllables.

**★★ (Stretch):**
Complete the On-level tasks, plus:
4. In your own words, explain why the water cycle is called a "cycle." Use at least one key detail from the passage in your answer.
5. Draw a simple picture of the water cycle with at least three labels (evaporation, condensation, precipitation).

**★★★ (Challenge):**
Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:
6. Read this short paragraph and create a new graphic organizer for it:

> Butterflies go through four stages in their lives. First, a butterfly starts as a tiny egg. Next, the egg hatches into a caterpillar. Then, the caterpillar wraps itself in a covering called a chrysalis. Finally, a beautiful butterfly comes out of the chrysalis. This process is called metamorphosis.

Write the main topic and at least three key details.

### Interleaved Review
**Review from Lesson 1 — Text Features:**
7. Look at the "Water Cycle" passage again. Find two text features from the passage. Name each one and explain what it does. (Hint: Look for bold words and a heading.)

## Check for Understanding (3-5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to determine whether the student can identify main topic and key details.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short paragraph:

> Penguins are amazing birds. They cannot fly, but they are excellent swimmers. Penguins use their wings like flippers to move quickly through the water. Most penguins live in cold places near the ocean. They eat fish, squid, and tiny sea creatures called krill.

Answer the following:

1. What is the main topic of this paragraph? (1 point)
2. Write two key details from the paragraph. (2 points — 1 point each)
3. A student says the main topic is "Penguins eat fish." Is the student correct? Why or why not? (2 points — 1 point for correct answer, 1 point for explanation)

**Mastery = 4 out of 5 points**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the umbrella analogy: draw it again and have the student physically place detail cards under the umbrella
- Re-read the penguin paragraph together, sentence by sentence, and ask after each one: *"Does this tell us about penguins? Is this the BIG idea or a smaller piece of information?"*
- Provide an additional short paragraph about a familiar topic (e.g., dogs or pizza) and practice the three-question strategy together
- Repeat the assessment the following day before moving to Lesson 3

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready to move to Lesson 3: How Reasons Support Points
- Encourage the student to practice finding main topics in books they read at home — they can tell someone the main topic and two details about any book they finish

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Main Topic Match Game:** Write 4-5 main topics on index cards and 12-15 details on separate cards. The student sorts the details under the correct main topic. (Example: Main topics — Dogs, Weather, Schools. Details — "Dogs need exercise," "Rain falls from clouds," "Students learn math.")
- **Write Your Own:** The student writes a 4-5 sentence paragraph about a topic they know well. Then they trade with the facilitator, who must identify the main topic and key details. This reversal deepens understanding.
- **Two-Passage Comparison:** Provide a second informational passage (about a different topic) and have the student complete a graphic organizer for both, then compare: "How are these two passages organized the same way?"

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Pacing:** The graphic organizer is the centerpiece of this lesson. Allow extra time for the student to physically write in each box. The act of writing helps solidify the concept.
- **Common misconception — Topic vs. detail confusion:** Many second graders will identify a detail as the main topic because it is interesting or memorable. Consistently redirect by asking: *"Does this cover the WHOLE passage, or just one part?"*
- **Common misconception — Retelling vs. identifying:** Some students will try to retell the entire passage instead of pulling out key details. Coach them: *"You do not need to say everything. Choose the most important pieces."*
- **Building from Grade 1:** In Grade 1, students identified "what the text is about" in simpler terms. This lesson deepens that work by asking students to separate the main topic from details and organize them visually. The graphic organizer is a new scaffold at this level.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson addresses RI.2.5 (using text features to locate key facts) and builds toward RI.2.8 (describing how reasons support specific points). Understanding main topic and key details is the foundation for later understanding of how reasons support an author's point.
- **The passage "The Water Cycle"** is an original passage written for this curriculum. It is not taken from any published source.

**★ On-level:**

1. The main topic of this passage is the water cycle.
2. Three key details (accept any three of the following):
   - The sun heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers and turns it into water vapor (evaporation).
   - Water vapor rises into the sky, cools down, and forms clouds (condensation).
   - When clouds get full, water falls as rain, snow, sleet, or hail (precipitation).
   - The whole cycle starts over again when the sun heats the water.
   - The water cycle gives us the rain and snow we need to live.
3. The key detail is (a) "Water vapor rises up into the sky." Option (b) is NOT a key detail because it does not give important information about the water cycle — it is about the word itself, not about how water moves.

**★★ Stretch:**

4. Accept any response that explains a cycle is something that repeats or goes around, with at least one detail from the passage. Example: "The water cycle is called a cycle because it keeps happening over and over. The sun heats water, it rises and makes clouds, then it falls as rain, and then the sun heats it again."
5. Accept any drawing that shows evaporation (water going up), condensation (clouds forming), and precipitation (water falling down), with labels.
