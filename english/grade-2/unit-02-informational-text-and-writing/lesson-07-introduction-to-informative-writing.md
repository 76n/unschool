# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 2, Lesson 7

## Topic
Introduction to Informative Writing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain the difference between opinion writing and informative writing
- Identify the parts of an informative piece: topic sentence, facts and details, and closing sentence
- Co-write an informative piece about a familiar animal using the Informative Writing Template
- Use the sentence frames "___ is/are...," "One fact is...," "Another fact is...," "Also,...," and "Now you know about ___" to structure informative writing

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-4: Informational text features, main topic, key details, reasons supporting points, questioning strategies
- Lessons 5-6: Opinion writing structure and practice (opinion, reasons, closing)
- Ability to write complete sentences independently
- Understanding of what a "fact" is (something true that can be checked)

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and lined paper (or writing journal)
- Informative Writing Template (provided in this lesson)
- Model informative piece (provided in this lesson)
- The student's opinion piece from Lesson 6 (for comparison)
- Eraser

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish between opinion writing and informative writing by examining the student's own work and comparing it to a new kind of writing.

**Activity: Opinion or Information?**

Say: "You wrote a wonderful opinion piece in Lesson 6. Let's look at it again."

Have the student read the first sentence of their opinion piece from Lesson 6 aloud.

Say: "That sentence tells us what you THINK. It's your opinion. Now listen to this sentence."

Read aloud: "Penguins live in cold places near the ocean."

Ask: "Is that sentence an opinion or a fact?" (A fact.) "How do you know?" (You can check it. It's true. It doesn't say 'I think.')

Say: "Right! Today we are going to learn about a new kind of writing called informative writing. Instead of telling people what you THINK, you teach people something TRUE. You share facts and details so the reader learns something new."

Play a quick sorting game. Read each sentence and have the student say "OPINION" or "FACT":

1. "Butterflies have four wings." (FACT)
2. "Butterflies are the prettiest insects." (OPINION)
3. "Dogs can learn tricks." (FACT)
4. "I believe pizza is better than tacos." (OPINION)
5. "The sun is a star." (FACT)
6. "Winter is the worst season." (OPINION)

Say: "Great sorting! When we write with facts, we are writing to TEACH. When we write with opinions, we are writing to SHARE WHAT WE THINK. Today we learn to teach!"

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the structure of an informative piece and introduce the Informative Writing Template through a model text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Informative writing:** Writing that teaches the reader facts and details about a topic
- **Topic sentence:** The first sentence that tells the reader what the text is about (e.g., "Frogs are amazing animals.")
- **Fact:** Something true that can be checked or proven
- **Detail:** Extra information that makes a fact more interesting or clear
- **Closing sentence:** The last sentence that wraps up the piece and reminds the reader what they learned
- **Inform:** To teach or give someone information

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Compare opinion writing and informative writing side by side (3 minutes)**

Draw a simple T-chart on paper:

| Opinion Writing | Informative Writing |
|----------------|-------------------|
| Tells what you THINK | Teaches FACTS |
| Uses words like "I think," "I believe," "the best" | Uses words like "is," "has," "can," "lives" |
| Gives REASONS for your opinion | Gives FACTS and DETAILS about the topic |
| Goal: convince the reader | Goal: teach the reader |

Say: "Both kinds of writing have a topic sentence and a closing. Both have sentences in the middle that give more information. But the BIG difference is this: opinion writing shares what you THINK. Informative writing shares what is TRUE."

Point to the chart: "In your opinion piece, you used the word 'because' to give reasons. In informative writing, you'll use facts — things that are real and true — to teach the reader."

**Step 2: Read and study the model informative piece (4 minutes)**

Say: "Let's read an informative piece together. This one teaches us about frogs."

Read aloud together:

---

**All About Frogs**

*Frogs are amazing animals that live all over the world.* One fact about frogs is that they start life as tiny tadpoles that swim in the water. Tadpoles have tails but no legs! Another fact is that frogs can jump very far. Some frogs can jump twenty times the length of their own body. Also, most frogs have smooth, wet skin that helps them breathe. Now you know some cool facts about frogs!

---

After reading, identify each part:

Say: "Let's find the parts of this informative piece, just like we found the parts of an opinion piece."

- **Topic sentence:** "Frogs are amazing animals that live all over the world." Ask: "Does this sentence tell us what the text is about?" (Yes — frogs.) "Does it share an opinion?" (No — it tells us a fact about frogs.)
- **Fact 1:** "They start life as tiny tadpoles that swim in the water." **Detail:** "Tadpoles have tails but no legs!"
- **Fact 2:** "Frogs can jump very far." **Detail:** "Some frogs can jump twenty times the length of their own body."
- **Fact 3:** "Most frogs have smooth, wet skin that helps them breathe."
- **Closing sentence:** "Now you know some cool facts about frogs!"

Say: "Notice the pattern. The writer used these sentence starters: 'One fact about ___ is...' 'Another fact is...' 'Also...' 'Now you know...' These are like a recipe. Let's use this recipe to write our own informative piece!"

**Step 3: Introduce the Informative Writing Template (3 minutes)**

Write the template on paper for the student to see:

**Informative Writing Template:**
1. **Topic sentence:** ___ is/are ___. (Tell the reader what the text is about.)
2. **Fact 1:** One fact is ___. (Share one true thing.)
3. **Fact 2:** Another fact is ___. (Share a second true thing.)
4. **Fact 3:** Also, ___. (Share one more true thing.)
5. **Closing:** Now you know about ___! (Wrap it up.)

Say: "This is your Informative Writing Template. It has five parts. You already know how to do this — it's the same idea as your opinion piece, but with facts instead of reasons."

Compare the two templates side by side:

| Opinion Template | Informative Template |
|-----------------|---------------------|
| I think ___ is the best. | ___ is/are ___. |
| One reason is because ___. | One fact is ___. |
| Another reason is ___. | Another fact is ___. |
| (detail) | Also, ___. |
| That is why ___. | Now you know about ___! |

Say: "See how similar they are? You already have the skills. We're just changing from reasons to facts."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Co-writing about an animal (facilitator and student write together)**

Say: "Let's write an informative piece together. I want to write about an animal we both know well. What animal do you know a lot about?"

Let the student choose an animal they are familiar with. For this example, assume the student picks "cats."

Say: "Great! Let's use our template."

Work through each step together, thinking aloud:

**Topic sentence:** "What can we say about cats to start? We need a sentence that tells the reader what our text is about."

Write together: *Cats are popular pets that many families love.*

**Fact 1:** "What is one true fact about cats? Think about what cats DO, what they LOOK LIKE, or what they EAT."

Student suggests something (e.g., "Cats sleep a lot."). Expand it together: *One fact about cats is that they sleep for about sixteen hours every day.*

Say: "See how we didn't just say 'Cats sleep a lot.' We made it more interesting by adding 'for about sixteen hours every day.' That detail makes the fact come alive!"

**Fact 2:** "What is another fact?"

Student suggests (e.g., "Cats purr."). Expand together: *Another fact is that cats purr when they are happy or comfortable.*

**Fact 3:** "One more fact. Think about something different from your first two facts."

Student suggests (e.g., "Cats can see in the dark."). Write together: *Also, cats have special eyes that help them see well in the dark.*

**Closing:** "Now let's wrap it up."

Write together: *Now you know some interesting facts about cats!*

Read the completed piece aloud together:

*Cats are popular pets that many families love. One fact about cats is that they sleep for about sixteen hours every day. Another fact is that cats purr when they are happy or comfortable. Also, cats have special eyes that help them see well in the dark. Now you know some interesting facts about cats!*

Say: "We did it! We just wrote an informative piece. Every sentence teaches the reader something true."

**Worked Example 2: Identifying what makes informative writing DIFFERENT from a story**

Say: "Watch out for this tricky thing. Sometimes when people write about animals, they accidentally write a story instead. Let me show you."

Read this example aloud:

*One day, a little cat named Whiskers went outside. He climbed a tree and got stuck! "Help!" meowed Whiskers. A nice girl came and helped him down.*

Ask: "Is this informative writing or a story?" (A story.) "How do you know?" (It has a character named Whiskers. It tells what happened. It's made up.)

Say: "Right! A story tells us what happens to made-up characters. Informative writing teaches us real facts. When you write your informative piece, make sure every sentence teaches something TRUE — not a story about a character."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student begins an independent informative piece with facilitator support, choosing a topic and using the template.

**Activity: Plan and Begin Your Informative Piece**

Say: "Now it's your turn to write your own informative piece! Let's pick a topic."

Offer these choices (or let the student suggest their own):
- An animal you know well (dog, cat, fish, bird, rabbit, horse)
- A food you know about (apples, pizza, ice cream, bananas)
- Something you are good at or know about (soccer, swimming, drawing, building with blocks)

Say: "Pick the topic you know the MOST facts about. If you can think of three facts right away, that's a great topic!"

**Planning phase (5 minutes):**

Have the student tell you their three facts orally before writing:

Say: "Before you write, tell me: What is your topic? Now tell me three facts about it."

Listen and coach as needed:
- If the student gives an opinion instead of a fact ("Pizza is the best food"), redirect: "That's an opinion — what you THINK. Can you tell me something TRUE about pizza? Something anyone could check?"
- If the student gives very short facts ("Dogs bark"), prompt for more: "Dogs bark — that's true! Can you add a detail? WHEN do they bark? WHY do they bark?"
- If the student cannot think of three facts, help brainstorm: "Think about what it looks like, what it does, where it lives, what it eats, why it is special."

**Writing phase (7 minutes):**

The student writes using the template. Provide the sentence starters on paper so the student can refer to them:

1. ___ is/are _______________________________________________.
2. One fact is ______________________________________________.
3. Another fact is ___________________________________________.
4. Also, ___________________________________________________.
5. Now you know about _______________________________________!

As the student writes, check in at each step:
- After the topic sentence: "Read it to me. Does it tell the reader what your piece is about?" If it sounds like an opinion, gently redirect.
- After Fact 1: "Is that a fact? Can we check it?" If yes, celebrate. If it's an opinion, help the student rephrase.
- After Fact 2: "Good — is this a DIFFERENT fact from your first one?" Make sure the student isn't repeating themselves.
- After Fact 3 and the closing: "Read the whole thing to me. Does every sentence teach the reader something?"

If the student finishes early, say: "Can you add one more detail sentence after any of your facts? Remember how we made 'Cats sleep a lot' even better by adding 'for about sixteen hours every day'?"

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student strengthens their informative piece and reviews prior skills.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Re-read your informative piece from start to finish.
2. Underline every fact in your piece. You should have at least three.
3. Circle the topic sentence and the closing sentence.
4. Add one more detail sentence anywhere in the piece — something extra that makes a fact more interesting.
5. Read the piece aloud to yourself (or a stuffed animal). Does every sentence teach the reader something? If not, fix it.

**Stretch:**
All of the above, plus:
6. Add a fourth fact using the sentence starter: "Did you know that ___?"
7. Write a title for your informative piece at the top of the page (e.g., "All About Dolphins" or "Cool Facts About Soccer").

**Challenge:**
All of the above, plus:
8. Write a second informative piece about a DIFFERENT topic (choose from the list above or pick your own). It should have all five parts of the template.
9. Compare your two informative pieces. Tell the facilitator: "Which piece teaches the reader more? Why?"

### Interleaved Review

While the student works, do a brief oral review:

**Opinion Writing Review (from Lessons 5-6):**
1. "What is the difference between an opinion and a fact?" (An opinion is what you think or feel; a fact is something true that can be checked.)
2. "In an opinion piece, what do you use to support your opinion?" (Reasons.)
3. "In an informative piece, what do you use instead of reasons?" (Facts and details.)

**Text Features Review (from Lesson 1):**
4. "If you were going to turn your informative piece into a page in a real book, what text features could you add?" (A heading/title, a picture with a caption, bold words for important vocabulary.) This connects reading skills (identifying features) to writing skills (creating features).

## Check for Understanding (3-5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. **What is the goal of informative writing?**
   (Answer: To teach the reader facts and details about a topic.)

2. **Read these two sentences. Which one belongs in an informative piece and which one belongs in an opinion piece?**
   - Sentence A: "Dolphins can swim up to 20 miles per hour."
   - Sentence B: "I think dolphins are the coolest animals in the ocean."
   (Answer: Sentence A is for an informative piece because it is a fact. Sentence B is for an opinion piece because it shares what someone thinks.)

3. **What are the five parts of the Informative Writing Template?**
   (Answer: 1) Topic sentence, 2) Fact 1, 3) Fact 2, 4) Fact 3/Also, 5) Closing sentence. Accept reasonable wording.)

4. **Read your informative piece. Point to one fact in your writing. Is it really a fact, or is it an opinion?**
   (Answer: Student identifies a fact from their piece and confirms it is something true that can be checked, not an opinion.)

**Scoring:**
- Question 1: 1 point
- Question 2: 1 point (must correctly sort BOTH sentences)
- Question 3: 1 point (names all five parts)
- Question 4: 1 point (correctly identifies a fact in their own writing)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 points, AND the student has produced an informative piece with at least a topic sentence, two facts, and a closing.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student confuses opinion and informative writing: Create 10 more sentences on index cards. Have the student physically sort them into two piles: OPINION and FACT. Practice until the student can sort accurately. Then connect back to writing: "Your opinion piece goes in this pile. Your informative piece goes in this pile."
- If the student's piece contains opinions disguised as facts: Go sentence by sentence through the piece. For each sentence, ask: "Can we check this? Is everyone going to agree with this?" If not, help the student rephrase it as a true fact.
- If the student cannot name the template parts: Write each part on a separate strip of paper. Mix them up. Have the student put them in order. Practice this until fluent.
- Re-attempt the exit ticket after remediation.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Celebrate: "You are now an informative writer AND an opinion writer! You can teach people facts AND share what you think. That's two kinds of real writing!"
- Save the informative piece in the student's writing folder for use in Lesson 9 (Revision and Editing) and Lesson 10 (Publishing and Sharing).
- Move to Lesson 8: Writing Informative Pieces with Facts and Details.

## Extension (Optional)
- **Expert Page:** The student creates an "expert page" — a single page that looks like a page from a nonfiction book. It includes: a heading, the informative paragraph, a picture with a caption, and one bold vocabulary word with a definition at the bottom. This connects informative writing to the text features learned in Lesson 1.
- **Teach Someone:** The student reads their informative piece to a family member or friend. After reading, they ask: "What did you learn from my writing?" If the listener can name at least two facts, the writing was successful.
- **Fact Collection:** Over the next few days, the student collects three facts about a new topic by asking family members, looking at books, or observing the world around them. They bring these facts to the next lesson to use as a "fact bank" for writing.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pedagogical context:** This lesson introduces the second major writing genre of the unit. The student has already learned opinion writing (Lessons 5-6), so informative writing is taught by comparison. The explicit side-by-side comparison of the two templates helps students understand that writing has structures, and different purposes call for different structures — but the underlying framework (topic, supporting sentences, closing) remains the same. This is a powerful insight for young writers.

**Pacing notes:** Like Lesson 6, this lesson may take two sessions. The first session should cover the Warm-Up, Direct Instruction, and the beginning of Guided Practice (planning and starting the draft). The second session should allow the student to finish drafting, complete Independent Practice, and do the Exit Ticket. Do not rush — the co-writing in Worked Example 1 is essential. The student needs to see the process of generating informative sentences before doing it alone.

**Common issues:**
- **Writing a story instead of informative text:** This is the most common issue. If the student starts writing "One day, a dolphin named Flipper..." gently redirect: "It sounds like you're starting a story. Remember, informative writing teaches facts that are TRUE for ALL dolphins, not a story about one pretend dolphin. Let's change that sentence to teach a fact."
- **Using opinions as facts:** Students may write "Cats are the best" and believe it's a fact. Use the test: "Would EVERYONE agree with that sentence? Could someone disagree?" If yes, it's an opinion. Rephrase together.
- **Running out of facts:** If a student genuinely doesn't know three facts about a topic, help them brainstorm using these prompts: "What does it look like? What does it do? Where does it live? What does it eat? What is something special about it?" Each answer is a potential fact.
- **Comparing to opinion writing excessively:** Some students may get confused by the comparison. If the T-chart is causing confusion rather than clarifying, simplify: "Informative writing = teaching facts. That's the main idea today."

This lesson aligns with standard **W.2.2** (Write informative/explanatory texts that introduce a topic, use facts and definitions, and provide a closing) and builds on the reading comprehension of informational text from Lessons 1-4.
