# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 2, Lesson 1

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Informational Text Features

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify five informational text features: headings, bold words, captions, table of contents, and glossary
- Explain the purpose of each text feature (what it helps the reader do)
- Locate text features within an informational passage and describe their function

## Prerequisites
- Ability to read Grade 2 level text independently or with minimal support
- Understanding that some books give us information about real things (nonfiction concept from Grade 1)
- Familiarity with basic book parts (cover, title, pages)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "All About Elephants" (included below)
- Text Features Anchor Chart (blank poster or whiteboard space to build together)
- Text Features Scavenger Hunt worksheet (5 rows: Feature / Where I Found It / What It Does)
- Colored pencils or highlighters (5 colors)
- Sticky notes (small size, 5 per student)
- Pencils

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about the difference between storybooks and information books and build curiosity about how information books are organized.

**Activity: Two Books Side by Side**

Hold up (or display images of) two books — one fiction picture book and one nonfiction informational book. If physical books are not available, describe them:
- Book A: A storybook about a bear who goes on an adventure.
- Book B: A book about real bears — what they eat, where they live, and how they grow.

Say to the student:

*"Look at these two books. One tells a made-up story, and one gives us real information. Which one do you think is the information book? How can you tell?"*

Allow the student to respond. Guide them to notice that information books often have photographs instead of drawings, shorter sections with titles above them, and words that are darker or bolder than other words.

Say: *"Today we are going to learn about the special parts that information books use to help readers find and understand information. These special parts are called text features."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce and define five key informational text features, building a shared anchor chart that the student can reference during practice.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Text features** — Special parts of an informational text that help readers find and understand information
- **Heading** — A title at the top of a section that tells the reader what that section is about
- **Bold words** — Words printed in thick, dark letters to show they are important or new vocabulary
- **Caption** — A sentence or phrase near a picture or photograph that explains what the picture shows
- **Table of contents** — A list at the beginning of a book that shows the chapters or sections and their page numbers
- **Glossary** — An alphabetical list at the back of a book that gives definitions of important words

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the concept (2 minutes)**

Say: *"When you read a story, you start at the beginning and read all the way to the end. But information books are different. They have special tools built right into the pages to help you find what you need. These tools are called text features. Today we are going to learn about five text features."*

Write "Text Features" at the top of the anchor chart.

**Step 2: Teach each feature one at a time (6-8 minutes)**

For each feature, follow this pattern: Name it, define it, show an example, and explain why it helps the reader.

**Feature 1 — Heading**

Say: *"A heading is like a sign on a door. It tells you what is inside that section before you read it. If I see a heading that says 'What Elephants Eat,' I already know that section will be about elephant food."*

Write on the anchor chart: **Heading** — Tells what a section is about.

**Feature 2 — Bold Words**

Say: *"Sometimes you see a word in a book that looks thicker and darker than the other words. That is a bold word. Authors make a word bold to say, 'Hey, pay attention! This word is important!' Bold words are usually new vocabulary that the author wants you to learn."*

Write on the anchor chart: **Bold Words** — Show important or new vocabulary.

**Feature 3 — Caption**

Say: *"Have you ever seen a picture in a book with a little sentence underneath it? That sentence is called a caption. A caption explains what the picture is showing. Without the caption, you might not understand the picture fully."*

Write on the anchor chart: **Caption** — Explains a picture or photograph.

**Feature 4 — Table of Contents**

Say: *"A table of contents is at the very front of a book. It is like a map. It lists all the sections or chapters and tells you what page each one is on. If you want to read about a certain topic, you do not have to flip through every page — you can check the table of contents first."*

Write on the anchor chart: **Table of Contents** — Lists sections and page numbers at the front of a book.

**Feature 5 — Glossary**

Say: *"A glossary is at the very back of a book. It is like a mini-dictionary. It lists important words from the book in ABC order and gives their definitions. If you read a word you do not know, you can look it up in the glossary."*

Write on the anchor chart: **Glossary** — Gives definitions of important words at the back of a book.

**Step 3: Quick verbal check (1 minute)**

Ask: *"Can you name one text feature that helps you find what page to turn to?"* (Table of contents.) *"Which text feature tells you what a picture is about?"* (Caption.)

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Identifying a Heading and Explaining Its Purpose**

Display or read this short excerpt:

> **Where Elephants Live**
>
> Elephants live in Africa and Asia. They like warm places with lots of trees and grass.

Say: *"Let me show you how I identify a text feature. I see the words 'Where Elephants Live' at the top. Those words are bigger and bolder than the rest. They come before the paragraph starts. That means they are a heading. The heading tells me this section is going to be about where elephants live. So the heading helps me know what information I will find in this section before I even read it."*

Model writing on the scavenger hunt chart:
- Feature: Heading
- Where I Found It: At the top of the section — "Where Elephants Live"
- What It Does: Tells me this section is about where elephants live

**Worked Example 2: Identifying a Bold Word and Explaining Its Purpose**

Display or read this short excerpt:

> Elephants are **herbivores**. That means they only eat plants.

Say: *"I notice the word 'herbivores' looks different from the other words. It is in bold — thicker and darker. That tells me it is an important vocabulary word. The author wants me to pay special attention to it. And look — the very next sentence explains what it means! Bold words signal that a key term is being introduced."*

Model writing on the scavenger hunt chart:
- Feature: Bold Word
- Where I Found It: The word "herbivores" in the paragraph
- What It Does: Shows me that "herbivores" is an important new word to learn

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply their knowledge of text features by reading an informational passage and identifying features with facilitator support.

**Activity: Text Features Scavenger Hunt — "All About Elephants"**

Provide the student with the following passage. Read it aloud together first, then work through the scavenger hunt.

---

### Reading Passage: "All About Elephants"

**Table of Contents**

| Section | Page |
|---|---|
| What Elephants Look Like | 1 |
| Where Elephants Live | 1 |
| What Elephants Eat | 2 |
| Baby Elephants | 2 |

---

**What Elephants Look Like**

Elephants are the largest land animals on Earth. They have thick, gray skin and big, floppy ears. An elephant's **trunk** is a long nose that can grab food, spray water, and even say hello to other elephants. Elephants also have long, white **tusks** that are made of a hard material called ivory.

**Where Elephants Live**

Elephants live in parts of Africa and Asia. African elephants roam across wide grasslands called **savannas**. Asian elephants prefer thick forests where they can find shade. Both kinds of elephants need large areas to walk and find food.

*[Picture: A group of elephants walking across a flat, grassy plain with trees in the background.]*
*Caption: African elephants travel together in family groups across the savanna.*

**What Elephants Eat**

Elephants are **herbivores**, which means they eat only plants. They munch on grasses, leaves, bark, and fruit. An adult elephant can eat up to 300 pounds of food every single day! They use their trunks to pull leaves from tall trees.

**Baby Elephants**

A baby elephant is called a **calf**. A calf can weigh 250 pounds when it is born. Baby elephants stay close to their mothers for many years. The whole family helps protect the young calves from danger.

**Glossary**

- **Calf** — a baby elephant
- **Herbivore** — an animal that eats only plants
- **Savanna** — a flat, grassy area with few trees, found in warm places
- **Trunk** — the long nose of an elephant, used for grabbing, breathing, and drinking
- **Tusks** — long, pointed teeth made of ivory that stick out from an elephant's mouth

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

After reading the passage together, say: *"Now we are going on a scavenger hunt! We need to find all five text features we learned about. Let's start together."*

**Finding 1 — Table of Contents**

Say: *"Look at the very beginning of the passage. What do you see before the paragraphs start?"*

If the student struggles, point to the table at the top and say: *"This list shows sections and page numbers. What text feature is that?"*

Guide the student to write: Feature: Table of Contents / Where: At the beginning / What it does: Helps me find which page each section is on.

**Finding 2 — Headings**

Say: *"As we read, I noticed some words that stood out at the beginning of each section. Can you find one?"*

If the student struggles, point to "What Elephants Look Like" and ask: *"Is this part of the paragraph, or does it come before the paragraph? What does it tell us about the section?"*

Guide them to record a heading on the chart.

**Finding 3 — Bold Words**

Say: *"Find a word in the passage that looks darker and thicker than the other words."*

If the student struggles, point to "trunk" in the first section: *"Does this word look the same as the others? How is it different?"*

Guide them to record a bold word.

**Finding 4 — Caption**

Say: *"Look at the part where the picture is described. Is there a sentence that explains the picture?"*

If the student struggles, read the caption aloud and ask: *"Does this sentence describe the picture? What text feature is a sentence that describes a picture?"*

Guide them to record the caption.

**Finding 5 — Glossary**

Say: *"Look at the very end of the passage. What do you see there?"*

If the student struggles, ask: *"Are these words in ABC order? Do they have definitions? What text feature gives definitions at the back of a book?"*

Guide them to record the glossary.

Once all five are found, review the chart together. Celebrate the completed scavenger hunt.

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate their ability to identify text features and explain their purposes without direct facilitator guidance.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):**
Using the "All About Elephants" passage, answer the following questions in complete sentences:
1. Name two headings in the passage. What does each heading tell you?
2. Find two bold words. Write each bold word and what it means (use the glossary to help).
3. Read the caption. What does the caption tell you about the picture?

**★★ (Stretch):**
Complete the On-level tasks, plus:
4. Why do you think the author put a table of contents at the beginning? How would it help you if this were a longer book?
5. Pick one bold word. Write a new sentence using that word correctly.

**★★★ (Challenge):**
Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:
6. Imagine you are writing your own information book about dogs. Create a short table of contents with at least 3 sections. Then write one heading and a sentence that would go under it, with one bold word.

### Interleaved Review
**Review from Grade 2 Unit 1 — Story Elements:**
7. Think about the elephants in the passage. If someone wrote a make-believe story about an elephant character, what would be different from this informational text? Write one sentence explaining the difference between a fiction story about elephants and an informational text about elephants.

## Check for Understanding (3-5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to determine whether the student can identify and explain text features.

**Exit Ticket:**

Answer each question:

1. What is a heading? (1 point)
2. Why do authors use bold words? (1 point)
3. Where would you find a glossary — at the beginning or the end of a book? (1 point)
4. Look at this example: *[Picture of a frog sitting on a lily pad.] "The green tree frog rests on a lily pad in the pond."* — What text feature is the sentence underneath the picture? What does it do? (2 points — 1 for naming the feature, 1 for explaining its purpose)

**Mastery = 4 out of 5 points**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the anchor chart with the student, pointing to each feature one at a time
- Use a physical nonfiction book (or another printed passage) and do a hands-on search: *"Can you point to a heading? Can you find a bold word?"*
- Re-teach any features the student missed using concrete, tactile examples (sticky notes labeling parts of a book)
- Repeat the exit ticket or a similar assessment the following day before moving to Lesson 2

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready to move to Lesson 2: Main Topic and Key Details in Informational Text
- Encourage the student to look for text features in any nonfiction books they read at home or in the library

## Extension (Optional)
- **Text Feature Creator:** The student creates their own one-page informational text about a favorite animal. They must include at least three text features: a heading, one bold word with a definition, and a caption for a drawing they make.
- **Text Feature Detective:** Give the student a nonfiction book or magazine and have them use sticky notes to label every text feature they can find. Count how many of each type they discover.
- **Teach a Friend:** The student explains the five text features to a family member or stuffed animal, using the anchor chart as their guide.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Pacing:** This lesson introduces five features, which is a lot of new vocabulary for second graders. If the student seems overwhelmed, split the lesson across two sessions — teach headings, bold words, and captions in session one, then table of contents and glossary in session two.
- **Concrete examples matter:** Whenever possible, supplement the passage with a physical nonfiction book so the student can touch and flip to real tables of contents and glossaries. The tactile experience reinforces understanding.
- **Common misconception:** Students may confuse a heading with a title. Clarify that a title names the whole book or passage, while a heading names just one section within it.
- **Common misconception:** Students may think bold words are bold because they are "louder." Redirect: bold words are important vocabulary words the author wants you to learn.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson addresses RI.2.5 (know and use various text features to locate key facts or information in a text). It also builds foundational skills for RI.2.8 (identifying reasons that support points) since students will need to navigate informational texts efficiently in upcoming lessons.
- **The passage "All About Elephants"** is an original passage written for this curriculum. It is not taken from any published source.

   - **Savannas** — flat, grassy areas with few trees, found in warm places
   - **Herbivores** — animals that eat only plants
   - **Calf** — a baby elephant

**★★ Stretch:**

4. The author put a table of contents at the beginning so readers can quickly find the section they want to read. In a longer book, you would not have to flip through every page — you could just look at the table of contents and go to the right page number.
5. Accept any correctly used sentence. Example: "A cow is an herbivore because it only eats grass and plants."

**★★★ Challenge:**

6. Accept any reasonable table of contents, heading, sentence, and bold word about dogs. Example:
   - Table of Contents: What Dogs Look Like — page 1 / What Dogs Eat — page 2 / How to Train a Dog — page 3
   - Heading: What Dogs Eat
   - Sentence: Dogs are **omnivores**, which means they eat both meat and plants.

**Interleaved Review:**

7. Accept any response that shows understanding of the fiction/nonfiction distinction. Example: "A fiction story about an elephant might have the elephant talking and going to school, but the informational text tells true facts about real elephants."
