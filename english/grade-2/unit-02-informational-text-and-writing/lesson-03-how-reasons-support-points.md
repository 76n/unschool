# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 2, Lesson 3

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
How Reasons Support Points

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between an author's point (what the author thinks or wants the reader to understand) and the reasons the author gives to support that point
- Identify the author's main point in an informational passage
- Locate at least two reasons the author gives to support the main point
- Explain how each reason supports (holds up) the author's point using the "point-reason" connection

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify the main topic of an informational text (Lesson 2 of this unit)
- Ability to identify key details in an informational text (Lesson 2 of this unit)
- Familiarity with informational text features (Lesson 1 of this unit)
- Basic understanding that authors write for a purpose

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "Why Recycling Is Important" (included below)
- Point-Reason graphic organizer (a large rectangle at the top labeled "Author's Point" with three arrow-shaped boxes pointing up to it, each labeled "Reason")
- Building blocks or stacking cups (5-6 blocks/cups) for the physical analogy
- Index cards (6-8 blank cards)
- Markers or colored pencils (two colors)
- Pencils

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build a concrete, physical understanding of how reasons "hold up" a point, using a hands-on analogy before encountering the concept in text.

**Activity: The Block Tower**

Place a flat object (a book, a piece of cardboard, or a large index card) on the table. Place one block or stacking cup underneath it.

Say: *"Look at this. I have a flat card on top and one block holding it up. Is it steady?"*

The card will likely wobble or fall. Then add two or three more blocks underneath.

Say: *"Now I have three blocks holding up the card. Is it steadier now? Why?"*

Guide the student to see: more support = steadier.

Say: *"Authors do the same thing when they write. An author makes a point — that is like the card on top. Then the author gives reasons to hold up that point — those are like the blocks underneath. The more reasons the author gives, the stronger the point becomes. Today we are going to learn how to find the author's point and the reasons that support it."*

Leave the block tower visible during the lesson as a reference.

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the difference between a point and a reason, introduce the "point-reason" connection, and model how to identify both in an informational text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Point** — The main thing an author wants the reader to understand, believe, or think about; what the author is trying to say
- **Reason** — A piece of information or fact that explains WHY the author's point makes sense; reasons hold up the point
- **Support** — To hold up or give strength to; reasons support a point by showing why it is true
- **Point-reason connection** — The link between what an author says (the point) and why the author says it (the reasons)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Define "point" and "reason" (3 minutes)**

Say: *"In Lesson 2, we learned about main topics and key details. Today we are going a step further. Sometimes an author does not just give information — the author makes a POINT. A point is what the author wants you to understand or believe."*

Write on the board: **Point = What the author wants you to think or understand.**

Say: *"Then the author gives REASONS to explain why that point makes sense. Reasons are like the blocks we stacked earlier — they hold up the point."*

Write on the board: **Reasons = Why the point makes sense. They hold up the point.**

Say: *"This is called the point-reason connection. The point and the reasons are connected because the reasons explain the point."*

Draw a simple diagram: a rectangle on top labeled "Point" with three arrows pointing up from boxes labeled "Reason 1," "Reason 2," "Reason 3."

**Step 2: Teach how to find the author's point (3 minutes)**

Say: *"To find the author's point, ask yourself: 'What does the author want me to understand or believe after reading this?' The point is usually stated near the beginning or end of the passage. Sometimes the author says it very clearly. Look for sentences where the author seems to be making a statement about why something matters."*

Write the guiding question on the board: **What does the author want me to think or understand?**

**Step 3: Teach how to find reasons (3 minutes)**

Say: *"Once you find the point, you look for reasons. Reasons answer the question 'Why?' or 'How do you know?' If the author says recycling is important, you ask: 'Why is it important? What reasons does the author give?' Each reason is a piece of information that explains WHY the point makes sense."*

Write on the board: **Reasons answer the question: WHY?**

Say: *"Sometimes authors use signal words to introduce reasons. Listen for words like 'because,' 'first,' 'also,' 'another reason,' and 'for example.' These words often come right before a reason."*

Write signal words on the board: **because, first, also, another reason, for example**

**Step 4: Introduce the graphic organizer (1-2 minutes)**

Show the Point-Reason graphic organizer. Say: *"Today we will use this organizer. The author's point goes in the big box at the top. Then each reason goes in one of the arrow boxes below, pointing up to the point — because the reasons SUPPORT the point. They push it up and hold it steady, just like the blocks in our tower."*

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Finding a Point and Reasons in a Simple Paragraph**

Read this paragraph aloud:

> Dogs make great pets. One reason is that dogs are loyal and love to be with their owners. Another reason is that dogs can learn tricks and follow commands. Dogs also help people feel less lonely.

Say: *"Let me find the author's point. I ask myself: What does the author want me to think? The very first sentence says 'Dogs make great pets.' That is the author's point — the thing the author wants me to believe."*

Write in the top box of the organizer: **Dogs make great pets.**

Say: *"Now I look for reasons. I see the signal words 'One reason is.' What comes after? 'Dogs are loyal and love to be with their owners.' That is Reason 1 — it tells me WHY dogs make great pets."*

Write in Reason 1 box: **Dogs are loyal and love to be with their owners.**

Say: *"I see 'Another reason is' — another signal! 'Dogs can learn tricks and follow commands.' That is Reason 2."*

Write in Reason 2 box: **Dogs can learn tricks and follow commands.**

Say: *"And I see 'also' — that is a signal too. 'Dogs help people feel less lonely.' That is Reason 3."*

Write in Reason 3 box: **Dogs help people feel less lonely.**

Say: *"Now I check the point-reason connection. Does each reason explain WHY dogs make great pets? Loyal and loving — yes, that is why they are great. Can learn tricks — yes, that is why they are great. Help people feel less lonely — yes, that is why they are great. All three reasons hold up the point!"*

**Worked Example 2: A Tricky Example — Telling a Reason Apart from Extra Information**

Read this paragraph aloud:

> Breakfast is the most important meal of the day. Eating breakfast gives your body energy to learn and play. Breakfast also helps you focus in school. Many families eat breakfast together at the kitchen table.

Say: *"The point is: 'Breakfast is the most important meal of the day.' Now let me find reasons. 'Eating breakfast gives your body energy' — that is a reason WHY breakfast is important. 'Breakfast helps you focus in school' — that is another reason WHY. But what about the last sentence: 'Many families eat breakfast together at the kitchen table.' Is that a reason WHY breakfast is important?"*

Pause. Say: *"Not really. That sentence tells us something about breakfast, but it does not explain WHY breakfast is important. It is extra information, not a reason. So I would not put it in my graphic organizer as a reason."*

Say: *"This is important: not every sentence in a passage is a reason. A reason has to explain WHY the point is true."*

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student reads an informational passage about recycling and works with the facilitator to identify the author's point and the reasons that support it.

**Activity: Point-Reason Detective — "Why Recycling Is Important"**

Provide the student with the following passage. Read it aloud together first.

---

### Reading Passage: "Why Recycling Is Important"

Have you ever put a can or a bottle into a recycling bin? When you recycle, you are helping to keep our planet healthy. Recycling is one of the most important things people can do for the Earth.

One reason recycling is important is that it **reduces trash** in our landfills. A **landfill** is a big area where garbage is buried underground. When people do not recycle, landfills get bigger and bigger. Recycling means less trash goes to landfills, so there is more room for parks and forests instead.

Another reason recycling matters is that it **saves natural resources**. **Natural resources** are things from the Earth, like trees, water, and metals. When we recycle paper, we do not need to cut down as many trees. When we recycle cans, we do not need to dig up as much metal from the ground.

Recycling also helps keep the air and water **cleaner**. Making brand-new products from raw materials creates a lot of pollution. But making products from recycled materials creates less pollution. That means recycling helps protect the air we breathe and the water we drink.

Everyone can recycle — even kids! You can start by sorting your trash into recycling bins at home and at school. Every time you recycle a bottle, a can, or a piece of paper, you are doing something good for the Earth.

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

**Step 1: Find the author's point (3-4 minutes)**

Say: *"We just read this passage together. Now let's be detectives. First, we need to find the author's point. Remember our question: What does the author want me to think or understand?"*

Allow the student to respond. If they say "recycling," guide them further: *"Recycling is the topic, but what does the author want you to THINK about recycling?"*

Guide toward: **Recycling is one of the most important things people can do for the Earth.** (Found in the last sentence of the first paragraph.)

Say: *"Great! That is the author's point. The author wants us to understand that recycling is very important. Write that in the top box of your graphic organizer."*

If the student struggles, re-read the first paragraph and ask: *"Which sentence sounds like the author is making a big statement — trying to convince you that something matters?"*

**Step 2: Find the reasons (5-6 minutes)**

Say: *"Now let's find the reasons. The reasons will tell us WHY recycling is important. Look for our signal words: 'one reason,' 'another reason,' 'also.' Let's check paragraph 2. Do you see a signal?"*

Guide the student to find: *"One reason recycling is important is that it reduces trash in our landfills."*

Say: *"That is Reason 1. Write it in the first arrow box: Recycling reduces trash in landfills."*

Say: *"Now look at paragraph 3. Do you see a signal?"*

Guide toward: *"Another reason recycling matters is that it saves natural resources."*

Say: *"That is Reason 2. Write it: Recycling saves natural resources like trees, water, and metals."*

Say: *"What about paragraph 4?"*

Guide toward: *"Recycling also helps keep the air and water cleaner."*

Say: *"That is Reason 3. Write it: Recycling helps keep air and water cleaner by reducing pollution."*

**Step 3: Check the point-reason connection (2 minutes)**

Say: *"Now let's check. Point to each reason and ask: Does this reason explain WHY recycling is important?"*

Go through each one:
- Reduces trash in landfills — does this explain why recycling is important? Yes!
- Saves natural resources — does this explain why recycling is important? Yes!
- Keeps air and water cleaner — does this explain why recycling is important? Yes!

Say: *"All three reasons support the point. They are like the blocks in our tower — each one holds up the point and makes it stronger."*

Point to the block tower from the warm-up: *"See? Three reasons, three blocks. The author's point is solid because it has strong reasons supporting it."*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate their ability to identify points and reasons independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):**
Using the "Why Recycling Is Important" passage, answer the following questions in complete sentences:
1. What is the author's main point?
2. List three reasons the author gives to support the point.
3. Choose one reason. Explain in your own words how it supports the author's point. Start your sentence with: "This reason supports the point because..."

**★★ (Stretch):**
Complete the On-level tasks, plus:
4. Look at the last paragraph of the passage. Does it contain a reason that supports the point, or does it do something else? Explain what the last paragraph does.
5. What signal words did the author use to introduce the reasons? List at least two signal words or phrases you found.

**★★★ (Challenge):**
Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:
6. Read this short paragraph. Identify the author's point and two reasons. Then explain whether the reasons are strong or weak, and why.

> Riding a bike is better than riding in a car. Bikes do not make pollution, so they are better for the air. Riding a bike is also good exercise for your body. My neighbor has a red bike.

Write the point, the two reasons, and explain whether the last sentence is a reason or not.

### Interleaved Review
**Review from Lesson 2 — Main Topic and Key Details:**
7. What is the main topic of the "Why Recycling Is Important" passage? How is the main topic different from the author's point? Write one or two sentences explaining the difference.

## Check for Understanding (3-5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to determine whether the student can identify points and reasons.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short paragraph:

> Drinking water is important for your health. Water helps your body stay cool when it is hot outside. Water also helps your brain think clearly so you can do your best at school. Without enough water, you might get headaches or feel very tired.

Answer the following:

1. What is the author's point? (1 point)
2. Name two reasons the author gives to support the point. (2 points — 1 point each)
3. How does the reason "Water helps your brain think clearly" support the author's point that drinking water is important? Explain the connection. (2 points — 1 point for identifying the connection, 1 point for a clear explanation)

**Mastery = 4 out of 5 points**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the block tower analogy. Write the point on an index card and place it on top. Write each reason on separate cards and place them underneath as supports. Physically show that removing a reason makes the structure weaker.
- Re-read the exit ticket paragraph together. After each sentence, pause and ask: *"Is this the point or a reason? How do you know?"*
- Provide a new simple example with very obvious signal words (e.g., "Pizza is the best lunch. One reason is... Another reason is...") and practice together.
- Repeat the assessment the following day before moving to Lesson 4.

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready to move to Lesson 4: Asking Questions About Informational Text
- Encourage the student to listen for points and reasons in everyday conversation — when someone says "I think ___ because ___," they are making a point and giving a reason!

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Point-Reason Sort:** Write 10 sentences on index cards (mix of points and reasons). The student sorts them into two piles: "Points" and "Reasons." Then the student matches each reason to the point it supports.
- **Build Your Own Argument:** The student picks a topic they feel strongly about (e.g., "Recess should be longer") and writes a point statement and two reasons that support it. They share it with the facilitator, who checks whether the reasons truly support the point.
- **Reason Strength Rater:** Give the student a point with four reasons — two strong and two weak. The student decides which reasons are strong supports and which are weak, explaining their thinking.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Pacing:** The point-reason concept is more abstract than main topic/key details. Be prepared to spend extra time on the physical analogy (block tower) and refer back to it frequently. If the student struggles, extend this lesson over two sessions.
- **Key distinction — Main topic vs. point:** Students may confuse main topic with point. Clarify: the main topic is what the text is ABOUT (recycling). The point is what the author THINKS or wants you to understand ABOUT the topic (recycling is important). A topic is neutral; a point has a position.
- **Common misconception:** Students may identify any detail as a reason. Coach them to ask: *"Does this sentence explain WHY the point is true?"* If it does not answer "why," it is not a reason — it may be extra information.
- **Signal words are scaffolds, not rules:** Signal words like "because" and "one reason" are helpful clues, but not all reasons start with signal words. As the student gains proficiency, wean them off relying solely on signal words and encourage them to think about the logical connection.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson directly addresses RI.2.8 (describe how reasons support specific points the author makes in a text). It also builds toward W.2.1 (write opinion pieces with reasons) by helping students understand the point-reason structure from the reader's perspective before they use it as writers in Lesson 5.
- **The passage "Why Recycling Is Important"** is an original passage written for this curriculum. It is not taken from any published source.
