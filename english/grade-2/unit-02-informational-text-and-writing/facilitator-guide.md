# Facilitator Guide — Grade 2 ELA, Unit 2: Informational Text & Writing

**Grade:** 2
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 2 — Informational Text & Writing
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4-5 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)

---

## Unit Overview

In Unit 1, your student built reading fluency and comprehension with fiction — decoding multisyllable words, reading with expression, asking questions, retelling stories, and comparing texts. This unit shifts focus to informational text and introduces writing as a companion skill to reading. Students learn to navigate nonfiction text features, identify how authors support their points with reasons, and then apply these structures to their own opinion and informative writing.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Identify and use informational text features (headings, bold words, captions, table of contents, glossary)
- Identify the main topic and key details in informational text
- Describe how reasons support specific points the author makes
- Ask and answer who, what, where, when, why, and how questions about informational text
- Write an opinion piece with a topic, opinion statement, supporting reasons, and a closing
- Write an informative piece with a topic, facts and details, and a closing
- Revise and edit their own writing for clarity, spelling, and punctuation

**Why this matters:** Second graders are transitioning from "learning to read" to "reading to learn." Informational text is where that transition becomes real — students encounter nonfiction in science, social studies, and everyday life. Understanding how nonfiction is organized and how authors build arguments with evidence is foundational for all academic reading. Meanwhile, writing cements comprehension: a student who can write an opinion piece with reasons understands what "supporting your point" means. A student who writes an informative text understands how facts and details work together to teach a reader. The reading-writing connection in this unit is deliberate and powerful.

---

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Read grade-level text with accuracy and expression (Unit 1 fluency skills)
- [ ] Ask and answer who, what, where, when, why, how questions about a text
- [ ] Retell a story including characters, setting, central message, and key events
- [ ] Write simple sentences with correct capitalization and end punctuation
- [ ] Spell common high-frequency words correctly and use phonetic spelling for unfamiliar words

If the student struggles with basic sentence writing, spend extra time on writing mechanics before Lesson 5 (opinion writing). Reading comprehension from Unit 1 is essential for the informational text lessons.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Grade-appropriate informational texts:** 4-6 short nonfiction books, articles, or passages about topics that interest the student. Look for texts with clear text features:
  - Headings and subheadings
  - Bold or highlighted vocabulary words
  - Photographs with captions
  - A table of contents or glossary
  - Good options: National Geographic Kids readers, Scholastic News, DK Readers Level 2, or any "all about" nonfiction books

- **Writing supplies:** Lined paper, pencils, erasers, colored pencils or crayons
- **Graphic organizer templates:** The lessons include organizers, but having blank paper to create them is helpful
- **Sticky notes** for marking text features and key details
- **A folder or notebook** to collect the student's writing pieces across the unit
- **Informational passages provided in lessons** — each lesson includes a passage, so published books are supplementary, not required

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Informational Text Features | 1 session |
| 2 | Main Topic and Key Details in Informational Text | 1-2 sessions |
| 3 | How Reasons Support Points | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Asking Questions About Informational Text | 1 session |
| 5 | Introduction to Opinion Writing | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Writing Opinions with Reasons | 1-2 sessions |
| 7 | Introduction to Informative Writing | 1-2 sessions |
| 8 | Writing Informative Pieces with Facts and Details | 1-2 sessions |
| 9 | Revision and Editing | 1-2 sessions |
| 10 | Publishing and Sharing — Putting It All Together | 1 session |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session (30-35 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lessons 1-4 (informational text reading) build the knowledge base for Lessons 5-8 (writing). Do not rush through the reading lessons — the writing lessons will be much easier if the student has a solid understanding of how informational texts work.
- Lessons 5-8 (writing) are the most time-intensive. Plan for at least 2 sessions per writing lesson. First session: instruction and drafting. Second session: finishing the draft and sharing.
- Lesson 9 (revision and editing) requires the student to have completed writing pieces from Lessons 5-8. If the student has not finished those pieces, allow extra time.
- Use a variety of informational texts throughout the unit. The more nonfiction the student reads, the stronger their understanding of how it works — and the better their own informational writing will be.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student treats informational text like fiction — reads straight through without using headings, captions, or bold words | The student is used to reading fiction linearly and has not learned to use nonfiction text features as navigation tools | Before reading, do a "text feature walk": flip through the text and point out each feature. "What does this heading tell us? What does this bold word mean? What does this caption say?" Teach the student to preview before reading. |
| Student cannot distinguish between a fact and an opinion | This distinction is still developing in Grade 2 | Use a simple anchor: "A fact is something you can check and prove. An opinion is what someone thinks or feels." Practice with everyday examples: "Pizza has cheese" (fact) vs. "Pizza is the best food" (opinion). |
| Student writes opinions without reasons ("I like dogs. They are good.") | The student expresses a preference but does not support it with reasoning | Model the difference: "I like dogs" is your opinion. Now tell me WHY. "I like dogs because they play with me and they are soft." The "because" bridges opinion to reason. Practice orally before writing. |
| Student writes informative pieces that are actually stories ("The penguin went to the ice. He was happy.") | The student defaults to narrative mode because story-writing is more familiar | Clarify the difference: "A story tells us what happens to made-up characters. An informative piece teaches us real facts. We're not making up a story about a penguin — we're teaching someone real things about penguins." Use the frame: "Did you know that...?" for each sentence. |
| Student resists revision ("It's already done!") | Revision is a new concept — the student is used to writing being finished when the last word is written | Frame revision positively: "Authors always go back and make their writing even better. Even famous authors do this! We're going to be like real authors today." Start with small, specific revisions rather than asking the student to rewrite everything. |
| Student writes very short pieces (2-3 sentences) despite having more to say | Writing stamina is still developing; the physical act of writing is effortful | Accept short pieces and build gradually. Say: "You wrote three great sentences! Next time, let's try for four." Use oral rehearsal first — have the student SAY everything they want to write before picking up the pencil. This separates composition from transcription. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day:

- "What is one fact you learned from that text that you didn't know before?"
- "How do you know that's true? Where in the text does it say that?"
- "If you could write a book about anything, what would you teach people about?"
- "What is your opinion about ___? Can you give me a reason?"
- "Look at this magazine/book — can you find a heading? A caption? A bold word? What do they tell us?"
- "If you were going to teach someone about ___, what are the three most important things you would say?"
- "Let's read this article together. What do you think it will be about, based on the title and pictures?"
- "You wrote a great sentence. Can you add one more detail to make it even more interesting?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- The student can read informational text fluently but consistently cannot recall any facts or details from what was just read (comprehension gap)
- The student cannot distinguish between fiction and nonfiction even after explicit instruction and repeated practice
- The student is unable to write a complete sentence independently (may need to go back to sentence-level writing instruction before attempting multi-sentence compositions)
- After 3-4 weeks, the student cannot produce any supporting reasons for an opinion, even orally
- The student shows significant frustration or avoidance around writing tasks despite encouragement and scaffolding

If you see these signs, consider:
- Reducing text complexity — use easier informational texts so comprehension can develop without decoding barriers
- Separating reading and writing instruction — work on informational text reading skills first, then layer in writing once the student is comfortable with the text structures
- Using more oral composition — have the student dictate their writing while you transcribe, then have them read it back. This removes the physical writing barrier while building composition skills.
- Consulting with a reading or writing specialist if challenges persist

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 3: Narrative Writing & Language**, where they write their own stories with sequenced events and descriptive details. They also deepen their grammar and language skills with collective nouns, irregular plurals, reflexive pronouns, possessives, and reference materials for spelling. The opinion and informative writing foundations from this unit transfer directly — a student who can organize thoughts into "topic, reasons, closing" can organize a narrative into "beginning, middle, end."

Between units, continue reading both fiction and informational text. Encourage the student to write regularly — even a sentence or two in a daily journal builds writing stamina and confidence. The more the student sees themselves as a writer, the more willing they will be to tackle the narrative writing challenges in Unit 3.
