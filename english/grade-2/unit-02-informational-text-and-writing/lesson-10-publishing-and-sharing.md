# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 2, Lesson 10

## Topic
Publishing and Sharing — Putting It All Together

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Select their best writing piece from this unit and explain why they chose it
- Create a polished final copy with neat handwriting, correct formatting, and an illustration or cover page
- Read their published piece aloud with expression and confidence
- Identify and describe the key skills learned throughout Unit 2: text features, main topic and key details, reasons supporting points, opinion writing, informative writing, and revision/editing

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 5-6: Opinion writing (completed and revised opinion piece)
- Lessons 7-8: Informative writing (completed and revised informative piece)
- Lesson 9: Revision and editing (at least one piece has been revised and edited using the checklists)
- At least one polished writing piece ready for final copy

## Materials Needed
- The student's revised opinion piece (from Lessons 6 and 9)
- The student's revised informative piece (from Lessons 8 and 9)
- Clean lined paper or special "publishing paper" (paper with a blank space at the top for a picture and lines at the bottom for writing)
- Pencil and eraser
- Colored pencils, crayons, or markers for illustrations
- A stapler or paper clip (optional, for binding pages)
- An audience for the reading: facilitator, family member, friend, or stuffed animal

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Celebrate the writing journey and build excitement for publishing.

**Activity: Our Writing Journey**

Say: "Before we start today, let's look back at everything you have learned in this unit. You have learned SO much. Let's see if you remember."

Hold up fingers as you count through each skill. Have the student tell you what they remember about each one:

Say: "Skill number one — we learned about informational text features." Hold up one finger. Ask: "Can you name three text features?" (Headings, bold words, captions, table of contents, glossary — any three.)

Say: "Skill number two — we learned about main topic and key details." Hold up two fingers. Ask: "What is the difference between a main topic and a key detail?" (The main topic is what the whole text is about; key details are specific pieces of information that tell more about the main topic.)

Say: "Skill number three — we learned how reasons support points in a text." Hold up three fingers. Ask: "If an author says 'Exercise is important,' what would be a supporting reason?" (Accept any reasonable reason, e.g., "Because exercise keeps your heart healthy" or "Because exercise makes you stronger.")

Say: "Skill number four — we learned about asking questions before, during, and after reading." Hold up four fingers. Ask: "Name two question words." (Who, what, where, when, why, how — any two.)

Say: "Skill number five — we learned to write opinion pieces." Hold up five fingers. Ask: "What are the parts of an opinion piece?" (Topic sentence with opinion, reasons with details, closing sentence.)

Say: "Skill number six — we learned to write informative pieces." Hold up six fingers (use both hands). Ask: "How is an informative piece different from an opinion piece?" (Informative pieces teach facts; opinion pieces share what you think.)

Say: "Skill number seven — we learned to revise and edit." Hold up seven fingers. Ask: "What is the difference between revision and editing?" (Revision makes writing better — bigger changes to ideas and details. Editing fixes mistakes — spelling, capitalization, punctuation.)

Say: "That is SEVEN big skills you have learned! Today is the best part. Today, you get to PUBLISH your writing. That means you are going to make a clean, beautiful final copy, add a picture, and read it to an audience — just like a real published author!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach what "publishing" means, guide the student in choosing their best piece, and show what a published piece looks like.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Publish:** To make a piece of writing final and share it with an audience
- **Final copy:** The clean, polished version of a piece of writing with no crossed-out words, neat handwriting, and all corrections made
- **Audience:** The people who will read or hear your writing
- **Illustration:** A picture that goes with the writing and helps the reader understand or enjoy it
- **Author:** The person who writes a piece — today, the student is the author

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Choose your best piece (3 minutes)**

Say: "Authors don't publish everything they write. They choose their BEST work. You have two writing pieces: your opinion piece and your informative piece. Let's look at both and decide which one you want to publish."

Have the student take out both pieces. Read each one aloud (the revised and edited versions from Lesson 9).

After reading both, ask these guiding questions:
- "Which piece are you most proud of? Why?"
- "Which piece do you think would be most interesting for someone else to read?"
- "Which piece has stronger details and reasons or facts?"
- "Which piece sounds the most like YOU?"

Say: "There is no wrong answer. Pick the one you like best. That will be the one you publish today."

Let the student choose. If they want to publish both, say: "You can absolutely publish both! Let's start with one today, and you can publish the other as an extension activity."

**Step 2: What does a published piece look like? (3 minutes)**

Say: "When a real author publishes a book, it has certain things. Let's make YOUR piece look like a real published work."

Show the student what their published piece will include:

1. **A cover page or title** — The title of the piece, the author's name ("By [Student's Name]"), and an illustration
2. **The writing** — A clean copy with neat handwriting, correct spelling, capital letters, and punctuation
3. **An illustration** — A picture that matches the writing (either on the cover or next to the text)

Draw a quick sketch of what the layout could look like:

**Option A: One-page layout**
- Title at the top: "The Best Pet" or "All About Dolphins"
- "By [Name]" underneath the title
- The writing in the middle
- A picture at the bottom

**Option B: Two-page layout (like a book)**
- Page 1: Cover — Title, author name, and a big illustration
- Page 2: The complete writing piece

Say: "Which layout do you want to use?" Let the student choose.

**Step 3: Model making a final copy (4 minutes)**

Say: "Let me show you what it looks like to make a clean final copy. Watch what I do."

Take the revised/edited sample piece from Lesson 9 (the dogs piece) and demonstrate writing the first few sentences in neat handwriting on clean paper:

Write slowly, showing:
- Starting with the title centered at the top: **"The Best Pet"**
- Writing "By [Facilitator's Name]" below the title
- Skipping a line
- Writing each sentence neatly, starting at the left margin
- Using your best handwriting
- Making sure every capital letter, punctuation mark, and spelling correction from editing is included

Say: "See how I am writing slowly and carefully? This is not a draft anymore. This is the FINAL version. I am including all the improvements we made during revision and editing. Every sentence starts with a capital letter. Every sentence ends with punctuation. All the words are spelled correctly."

After writing 2-3 sentences, say: "You get the idea. Now it's your turn!"

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Turning a revised piece into a published piece**

Show the student a before-and-after comparison:

**Before (marked-up draft with revision and editing marks):**
A messy page with crossed-out words, carets with additions, circled misspellings, and arrows — the kind of page that results from Lesson 9.

**After (clean final copy):**

---

**The Best Season**
By Maria

I think fall is the best season of all. One reason I love fall is because the leaves change to beautiful colors like red, orange, and yellow. It is like a painting outside my window every morning. Another reason fall is the best is because my family goes apple picking at a farm. We fill big baskets with apples and then make apple pie together. Fall is the most wonderful season, and I hope everyone gets to enjoy it!

---

Say: "See how the final copy is clean and easy to read? All the messy marks from revision and editing are gone. The good ideas stayed, the mistakes are fixed, and it looks like a real piece of writing."

**Worked Example 2: Adding an illustration**

Say: "After you write your final copy, you'll add an illustration. Your picture should match your writing. If your piece is about why dogs are the best pet, what would you draw?" (A dog playing, a dog with its owner, etc.)

"If your piece is about dolphins, what would you draw?" (A dolphin jumping, dolphins swimming in a pod, etc.)

Say: "The picture does not need to be perfect. It just needs to help the reader picture what your writing is about — like a caption and image in an informational book! You already learned about captions in Lesson 1. You could even write a caption under your drawing."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Support the student as they create their final published copy.

**Activity: Write Your Final Copy**

**Step 1: Set up the page (1 minute)**

Help the student set up their clean paper:
- If using a one-page layout: Write the title centered at the top, "By [Name]" underneath, skip a line
- If using a two-page layout: Set aside the first page for the cover (they'll do that last) and start writing on the second page

**Step 2: Write the final copy (7-8 minutes)**

Say: "Now, look at your revised and edited piece. Copy it onto this clean paper in your very best handwriting. Go slowly. Make sure you include all the changes you made during revision and editing."

As the student writes, circulate and check:
- Is the handwriting neat and legible?
- Is the student copying the REVISED version (not the original draft)?
- Are all the editing corrections included (capital letters, punctuation, spelling)?

If the student makes a mistake on the clean copy:
- For a small mistake: Carefully erase and rewrite
- For a larger mistake: "That's okay. Let's start that sentence again on the next line." (It does not need to be absolutely perfect — the goal is significantly cleaner than the draft)

Encourage the student as they write: "You're doing great. Look at that neat handwriting! This is going to look so professional."

**Step 3: Add the illustration (3-4 minutes)**

Say: "Now add your illustration. Draw a picture that goes with your writing. If you want, you can also write a caption underneath your picture — a short sentence that describes what the picture shows."

Let the student draw and color. This is a creative, low-pressure activity after the concentration of writing.

If the student is using the two-page layout, they should also create their cover page now:
- Title in large, neat letters
- "By [Name]"
- A large illustration

Optional: Staple or paperclip the pages together to make it feel like a real book.

**Step 4: Prepare to read aloud (1 minute)**

Say: "Your published piece is almost ready! The last step is the most fun — you get to read it to an audience. But first, let's practice. Read your piece to yourself quietly one time. Practice reading it with expression, like you're telling an important story to a friend."

Give the student a moment to practice silently.

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student reads their piece aloud and completes a unit skills review.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. **Read your piece aloud** to the facilitator (or a family member, friend, or stuffed animal audience). Read with expression and confidence. Hold up your illustration so the audience can see it.
2. After reading, answer this question from your audience: "What is one thing you are most proud of in this piece?"
3. **Unit Skills Review:** Complete the following review questions orally:
   - Name two text features you learned about in this unit.
   - What is the main topic of your published piece?
   - Name one key detail from your published piece.
   - What is the difference between opinion writing and informative writing?

**Stretch:**
All of the above, plus:
4. **Author Q&A:** After reading, the audience (facilitator or family member) asks 2-3 questions about the writing:
   - "Why did you choose this topic?"
   - "What was the hardest part of writing this piece?"
   - "If you could add one more sentence, what would it be?"
5. Write a short "About the Author" sentence on the back of your piece: "The author is [name]. [He/She/They] is [age] years old and loves [something]."

**Challenge:**
All of the above, plus:
6. **Publish the second piece:** Create a final copy of your OTHER writing piece (the one you did not choose to publish first). Write a clean copy, add an illustration, and read it aloud.
7. **Compare and reflect:** After publishing both pieces, tell the facilitator: "My opinion piece and my informative piece are different because ___. The skill I am most proud of learning in this unit is ___."
8. Create a "Table of Contents" for your two published pieces, as if they were chapters in a book. Write:
   - Chapter 1: [Title of Piece 1] .............. page 1
   - Chapter 2: [Title of Piece 2] .............. page 2

### Interleaved Review

This lesson's interleaved review is built into the independent practice through the Unit Skills Review questions. In addition, ask the following questions during or after the reading:

**Reasons Supporting Points Review (from Lesson 3):**
1. "If your piece is an opinion piece: What are your reasons? Do they support your opinion?" OR "If your piece is informative: What are your facts? Do they teach the reader about your topic?"

**Revision and Editing Review (from Lesson 9):**
2. "What is one change you made during revision that made your piece better?"
3. "What is one mistake you caught during editing?"

**Questioning Strategies Review (from Lesson 4):**
4. "If you were going to read YOUR piece using the before/during/after reading strategy, what would you predict before reading based on the title? What question would you ask during reading?"

## Check for Understanding (3-5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment covering both the publishing process and the cumulative unit skills.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. **What does it mean to "publish" a piece of writing?**
   (Answer: To make a clean, final version of your writing and share it with an audience.)

2. **Name three skills you learned in this unit.**
   (Answer: Accept any three of the following: identifying text features, finding main topic and key details, understanding how reasons support points, asking questions about informational text, writing opinion pieces, writing informative pieces, revising writing, editing writing. Must name at least three distinct skills.)

3. **What is the difference between a first draft and a final copy?**
   (Answer: A first draft is the first version — it may have crossed-out words, messy handwriting, and errors. A final copy is clean, neat, corrected, and ready to share.)

4. **Look at your published piece. Read your favorite sentence to me. Why is it your favorite?**
   (Answer: Student reads a sentence and gives a reason — because it has a strong detail, because it sounds good, because it teaches something interesting, etc. Any thoughtful response is acceptable.)

**Scoring:**
- Question 1: 1 point
- Question 2: 1 point (must name at least three skills)
- Question 3: 1 point (must distinguish draft from final copy)
- Question 4: 1 point (reads a sentence and explains a reason for the choice)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 points, AND the student has produced a clean final copy and read it aloud to an audience.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student cannot name three unit skills: Do a guided review. Go back to the Warm-Up activity ("Our Writing Journey") and walk through the seven skills again. Have the student say each one aloud. Then re-ask exit ticket question 2.
- If the student cannot distinguish draft from final copy: Place the student's draft and final copy side by side. Point to specific differences: "Look — this word is crossed out on the draft but not on the final copy. This sentence has neater handwriting on the final copy. The final copy has your illustration." Help the student articulate the differences.
- If the student struggles to read aloud with confidence: Practice the piece two more times. First, read it together (facilitator and student read at the same time). Then the student reads alone. Praise effort and expression.
- Re-attempt the exit ticket after remediation.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Give a big celebration: "You are a published author! You wrote, revised, edited, and published a real piece of writing. That is an incredible accomplishment."
- Display the published piece in a visible place — on the refrigerator, a bulletin board, or a special shelf.
- Prepare for the Unit Assessment: Say: "In our next session, you'll take the Unit 2 assessment. It will cover everything we learned — text features, main topic and key details, reasons supporting points, and both opinion and informative writing. You already know all of this. The assessment is just a chance to show what you know!"
- Encourage the student to re-read their published piece to another person before the assessment — the more they engage with their own writing, the more confident they'll be.

## Extension (Optional)
- **Author's Chair:** Set up a special "Author's Chair" (any chair, made special with a pillow or sign). The student sits in the Author's Chair and reads their piece to family members. After reading, the audience gives one compliment and asks one question. This is a common classroom practice that builds confidence and pride.
- **Class Library:** If multiple students are working through this curriculum, collect all published pieces into a "Class Library" binder or box. Students can read each other's published work.
- **Publish Both Pieces:** If the student only published one piece, create a final copy of the second piece as well. Bind both pieces together into a mini-book with a cover page and table of contents.
- **Letter to the Reader:** The student writes a short letter at the beginning of their published piece: "Dear Reader, I wrote this piece because ___. I hope you enjoy it! From, [Name]." This adds a personal touch and practices letter-writing format.
- **Informational Book Project:** Over the next week, the student chooses a topic they are interested in, collects 5-6 facts (with facilitator help), and writes a multi-page informational book with a cover, table of contents, three pages of facts (one fact per page with an illustration), and a glossary. This is a long-term project that applies all the skills from this unit.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pedagogical context:** This lesson is intentionally celebratory. After nine lessons of skill-building, the student needs a chance to step back and appreciate what they've accomplished. The act of publishing — making a clean copy, adding an illustration, and reading aloud — transforms the student's relationship with their writing. It moves from "schoolwork" to "something I made that I'm proud of." This emotional connection to writing is essential for long-term motivation. The unit skills review embedded in the warm-up and independent practice ensures the student is prepared for the unit assessment without feeling like they are being tested.

**Pacing notes:** This lesson is designed for a single session, though it can stretch to two if the student wants to publish both pieces or create an elaborate illustration. The writing portion (creating the final copy) is the most time-intensive part. If the student's handwriting is slow, consider reducing the illustration time or allowing them to finish the illustration outside of lesson time. The read-aloud should not be rushed — give the student time to feel like a real author sharing their work.

**Common issues:**
- **Perfectionism during final copy:** Some students will want every letter to be perfect and will erase excessively. Set expectations: "We want neat handwriting, but it doesn't have to be perfect. If you make a small mistake, erase it carefully and keep going. The ideas in your writing are more important than perfect handwriting."
- **Reluctance to read aloud:** Some students are shy about sharing. Options: read to a stuffed animal first, read together with the facilitator (echo reading — facilitator reads a sentence, student repeats it), or let the student hold their illustration up and "present" without reading every word. The goal is to build confidence, not create anxiety.
- **Rushing through the final copy:** If the student races through the writing to get to the drawing, gently redirect: "I can see you're excited about the illustration! Let's make sure the writing is just as beautiful as the picture will be. Take your time on these next two sentences."
- **Difficulty choosing between pieces:** If the student cannot decide, ask: "Which piece would you most want someone else to read? Which one makes you feel the proudest?" If they still can't choose, pick the one with stronger revision — it will make a better final product.
- **Unit review gaps:** If the student struggles to remember skills from early in the unit (text features, main topic), this is normal — spaced review is designed to catch these gaps. Use the warm-up review as a diagnostic. If a student cannot recall a skill at all, spend 1-2 minutes re-explaining it briefly. This will help prepare them for the unit assessment.

This lesson aligns with standard **W.2.5** (With guidance and support from adults and peers, focus on a topic and strengthen writing as needed by revising and editing) and serves as a culminating activity for the writing standards covered in this unit: **W.2.1** (opinion writing) and **W.2.2** (informative writing). The unit review component revisits reading standards **RI.2.5** (text features) and **RI.2.8** (reasons supporting points).

*This lesson follows the mastery-based progression and growth mindset principles defined in the [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md).*

## Answer Key

### Warm-Up — Unit Skills Review
1. **Text features (any three):** Headings, bold words, captions, table of contents, glossary, photographs, subheadings
2. **Main topic vs. key detail:** The main topic is what the whole text is about; key details are specific pieces of information that tell more about the main topic.
3. **Supporting reason for "Exercise is important":** Any reasonable reason, e.g., "Because exercise keeps your heart healthy," "Because exercise makes you stronger," "Because exercise helps you sleep better."
4. **Two question words:** Any two of: Who, What, Where, When, Why, How
5. **Parts of an opinion piece:** Topic sentence with opinion, first reason with detail, second reason with detail, closing sentence.
6. **Difference between informative and opinion writing:** Informative pieces teach facts about a topic; opinion pieces share what the author thinks and give reasons.
7. **Difference between revision and editing:** Revision is making writing better (adding details, changing words, improving ideas). Editing is fixing mistakes (spelling, capitalization, punctuation).

### Worked Example 1 — Published Piece Layout
**Cover page:** Title, "By [Name]," illustration
**Writing page:** Clean final copy with all revisions and edits included, neat handwriting, correct formatting

**The Best Season**

I think fall is the best season of all. One reason I love fall is because the leaves change to beautiful colors like red, orange, and yellow. It is like a painting outside my window every morning. Another reason fall is the best is because my family goes apple picking at a farm. We fill big baskets with apples and then make apple pie together. Fall is the most wonderful season, and I hope everyone gets to enjoy it!

### Exit Ticket Answers
1. To publish means to make a clean, final version of your writing and share it with an audience. (1 point)
2. Any three of: identifying text features, finding main topic and key details, understanding how reasons support points, asking questions about informational text, writing opinion pieces, writing informative pieces, revising writing, editing writing. (1 point)
3. A first draft is the first version with possible errors and messy marks. A final copy is clean, neat, corrected, and ready to share. (1 point)
4. Student reads a sentence and gives a thoughtful reason for why it is their favorite. (1 point)

### Interleaved Review Answers
1. Student identifies reasons (opinion piece) or facts (informative piece) from their published work and explains how they support the topic.
2. Student names a specific revision they made (e.g., "I added a detail about..." or "I changed my closing sentence to...").
3. Student names a specific editing correction (e.g., "I fixed the spelling of 'because'" or "I added a capital letter at the beginning of a sentence").
4. Student makes a prediction based on their title and formulates a question they might ask while reading.
