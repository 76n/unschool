# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic
Revision and Editing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain the difference between revision (making writing better) and editing (fixing mistakes)
- Use a Revision Checklist to improve the content and organization of a writing piece
- Use an Editing Checklist to correct capitalization, punctuation, and spelling errors
- Apply revision and editing skills to one of their own writing pieces from this unit

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 6: Writing Opinions with Reasons (completed opinion piece)
- Lesson 8: Writing Informative Pieces with Facts and Details (completed informative piece)
- At least one completed writing piece saved in the student's writing folder
- Understanding of sentence capitalization and end punctuation (periods, question marks, exclamation points)
- Knowledge of common sight word spellings

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and eraser
- The student's opinion piece from Lesson 6
- The student's informative piece from Lesson 8
- Revision Checklist (provided in this lesson)
- Editing Checklist (provided in this lesson)
- A sample writing piece with errors (provided in this lesson)
- Colored pencils or pens (one color for revision marks, a different color for editing marks)
- Clean lined paper (for rewriting revised sentences if needed)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the idea that all writers revise and edit, and build motivation for improving one's own writing.

**Activity: The Author's Secret**

Say: "I want to tell you a secret about authors — the people who write the books you read. Are you ready?"

Pause for effect.

Say: "No author gets it perfect the first time. Not one. Every single author writes a first draft, then goes back and makes it better. They add details, change words, fix mistakes, and sometimes move sentences around. That is what real authors do."

Ask: "When you wrote your opinion piece and your informative piece, did you write them perfectly the first time?" (The student will likely say no.)

Say: "That is totally normal! Today, you are going to do what real authors do. You are going to make your writing EVEN BETTER. We are going to learn two things: REVISION and EDITING. They sound the same, but they are very different. Let me show you."

Write on paper:

**REVISION** = Making your writing BETTER
**EDITING** = Fixing MISTAKES

Say: "Revision is about the big stuff — your ideas, your details, your organization. Editing is about the small stuff — spelling, capital letters, and punctuation. We always revise FIRST, then edit. There is no point in fixing the spelling of a sentence you might change or remove!"

Do a quick physical activity: "When I say REVISION, put your hands out wide — because revision is about the BIG picture." (Student spreads arms.) "When I say EDITING, point to your eye — because editing is about spotting small details." (Student points to eye.)

Call out randomly: "REVISION!" (Arms wide.) "EDITING!" (Point to eye.) "EDITING!" "REVISION!" "REVISION!" "EDITING!"

Say: "Great! You already know the difference. Now let's practice."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach revision and editing strategies using a sample piece with deliberate errors and weak spots.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Revision:** The process of making writing better by adding details, changing words, reorganizing sentences, or removing parts that do not fit
- **Editing:** The process of fixing mistakes in writing, such as spelling errors, missing capital letters, and incorrect punctuation
- **Draft:** A first version of a piece of writing that is not yet finished or polished
- **Final copy:** A clean, corrected version of writing that is ready to share
- **Checklist:** A list of things to look for and check off, used to make sure nothing is missed

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the sample piece (2 minutes)**

Say: "Let's look at a piece of writing that needs both revision AND editing. I wrote this on purpose with some weak spots and some mistakes. Your job is to help me find them."

Read the following sample piece aloud together:

---

**Sample Writing Piece (with deliberate errors and weak spots):**

my favrit animal is dogs. dogs are good. they are nice. one reeson dogs are good is becuz they play with you. dogs are the best animl in the hole world

---

Say: "This piece has some good ideas, but it needs work. Let's revise it first — that means making the ideas better. Then we'll edit it — that means fixing the mistakes."

**Step 2: Model revision using the Revision Checklist (5 minutes)**

Present the Revision Checklist:

**Revision Checklist:**
- [ ] Does my writing have a clear topic? Can the reader tell what my piece is about right away?
- [ ] Do I have reasons (opinion piece) or facts (informative piece) that support my topic?
- [ ] Does my ending wrap things up? Does it connect back to my topic?
- [ ] Could I add more details to make my writing more interesting?
- [ ] Do my sentences make sense? Would a reader understand what I mean?
- [ ] Did I use different sentence starters, or do too many sentences start the same way?

Go through the checklist with the sample piece:

**Checklist item 1 — Clear topic?**
Say: "The first sentence says 'my favrit animal is dogs.' Does it tell us the topic?" (Yes — dogs.) "But is it interesting? Let's make it stronger."
Revise together: "I think dogs are the best animal in the whole world!" or "Dogs are my favorite animal, and I have many reasons why."

**Checklist item 2 — Reasons or facts?**
Say: "The piece says 'dogs are good' and 'they are nice.' Are those reasons?" (Not really — they're vague.) "And there is only one reason: 'they play with you.' Let's add a second reason and make the first one more detailed."
Revise together:
- Reason 1: "One reason dogs are great is because they love to play with you. You can throw a ball and they will bring it back every time!"
- Reason 2: "Another reason is because dogs are loyal. They are always happy to see you when you come home."

**Checklist item 3 — Does the ending wrap things up?**
Say: "The last sentence says 'dogs are the best animl in the hole world.' That's actually pretty good as a closing idea, but let's make it stronger."
Revise together: "That is why I believe dogs are the most wonderful pets anyone could have!"

**Checklist item 4 — Could I add more details?**
Say: "We already added some. But let's check — is there a sentence that could use one more detail?" Look at the piece and add where needed.

**Checklist item 5 — Do sentences make sense?**
Say: "Read each sentence. Does it make sense?" Read through the revised version to check.

**Checklist item 6 — Different sentence starters?**
Say: "Look at the old version. Three sentences started with 'dogs' or 'they.' In our revised version, we used 'One reason,' 'Another reason,' and 'That is why.' Much more varied!"

Show the revised version (still with spelling and punctuation errors):

*I think dogs are the best animal in the whole world! One reeson dogs are great is becuz they love to play with you. You can throw a ball and they will bring it back every time! Another reeson is becuz dogs are loyal. They are always happy to see you when you come home. That is why I beleev dogs are the most wonderful pets anyone could have!*

Say: "Look how much better this is! The ideas are stronger, there are more details, and the ending is powerful. But we still have some mistakes to fix. That is where editing comes in."

**Step 3: Model editing using the Editing Checklist (3 minutes)**

Present the Editing Checklist:

**Editing Checklist:**
- [ ] Does every sentence start with a capital letter?
- [ ] Does every sentence end with punctuation (period, question mark, or exclamation point)?
- [ ] Are my sight words spelled correctly? (the, because, they, said, were, have, one, could, would, etc.)
- [ ] Did I check words I was unsure about and try my best spelling?
- [ ] Are names and the word "I" capitalized?

Go through the editing checklist with the revised sample piece:

**Capital letters:**
Say: "Let's check every sentence. Does each one start with a capital letter?" Read through and check. (All sentences now start correctly in the revised version, but point out that the original started with lowercase "my.")

**End punctuation:**
Say: "Does every sentence end with a period, question mark, or exclamation point?" Check each sentence. (Yes.)

**Sight word spelling:**
Say: "Let's look for sight words. Can you find any that are misspelled?"
- "reeson" should be "reason" (circle it)
- "becuz" should be "because" (circle it)
- "beleev" should be "believe" (circle it)

Fix each one together.

**Final edited version:**

*I think dogs are the best animal in the whole world! One reason dogs are great is because they love to play with you. You can throw a ball and they will bring it back every time! Another reason is because dogs are loyal. They are always happy to see you when you come home. That is why I believe dogs are the most wonderful pets anyone could have!*

Say: "Now THAT is a piece of writing to be proud of. We revised it to make the ideas better, and we edited it to fix the mistakes. Let's compare the before and after."

Read the original and the final version side by side. Ask: "Which one is better?" (The revised and edited version.) "Why?" (It has more details, stronger reasons, better words, correct spelling, and proper punctuation.)

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Revision — Adding a detail to a weak sentence**

Original sentence: "Butterflies are pretty."

Say: "This sentence is true, but it's weak. Let's revise it by adding a detail."

Ask the student: "What makes butterflies pretty? Can you describe what they look like?"

Revised: "Butterflies have colorful wings with bright patterns of orange, blue, and yellow."

Say: "We didn't fix a mistake. We made the sentence BETTER. That's revision."

**Worked Example 2: Editing — Fixing mistakes in a sentence**

Original sentence: "the cat sleped on the sofa all day"

Say: "This sentence has mistakes. Let's find them."

Ask: "What should the first word look like?" (Capital T — "The")
Ask: "Is 'sleped' spelled correctly?" (No — "slept")
Ask: "What is missing at the end?" (A period.)

Edited: "The cat slept on the sofa all day."

Say: "We didn't change the IDEAS. We fixed the MISTAKES. That's editing."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student applies revision and editing skills to one of their own writing pieces from this unit.

**Activity: Revise and Edit Your Writing**

**Step 1: Choose a piece (1 minute)**

Say: "Get out your writing folder. You have two pieces: your opinion piece from Lesson 6 and your informative piece from Lesson 8. Pick the one you want to make better. Which one do you like more? Which one do you think has the most room for improvement?"

Let the student choose. (If one piece is significantly weaker, gently suggest that one: "I think this piece has lots of room to grow. Let's work on this one.")

**Step 2: Revision first (5-6 minutes)**

Say: "First, we revise — the BIG stuff. Let's go through the Revision Checklist together."

Give the student a colored pencil (e.g., blue) for revision marks.

Go through each checklist item:

**Clear topic?**
Say: "Read your first sentence to me. Does it tell the reader what your piece is about? Could you make it stronger or more interesting?"

If the topic sentence is weak, help the student brainstorm a better version. They can write the new version above the original or on a separate line.

**Reasons or facts?**
Say: "Now read your reasons (if opinion) or facts (if informative). Are they strong? Do they have details?"

If a reason or fact is vague (e.g., "Dogs are fun"), prompt: "HOW are dogs fun? Can you add a sentence that explains more?"

Have the student use the blue pencil to add new details between existing sentences (using a caret ^ or drawing an arrow).

**Ending wraps up?**
Say: "Read your closing sentence. Does it remind the reader of your topic? Does it feel like an ending?"

If the ending is abrupt, help the student write a stronger one.

**Different sentence starters?**
Say: "Look at the beginning of each sentence. Do too many start the same way?"

If multiple sentences start with the same word (e.g., "I think... I like... I also..."), help the student vary one or two.

**Step 3: Editing second (4-5 minutes)**

Say: "Now we edit — the SMALL stuff. Take out a different colored pencil" (e.g., red).

Go through each editing checklist item:

**Capital letters:** Say: "Point to the first word of every sentence. Does each one start with a capital letter? If not, circle it in red and write the capital letter above it."

**End punctuation:** Say: "Point to the last word of every sentence. Does each sentence end with a period, question mark, or exclamation point? If not, add the correct punctuation in red."

**Sight word spelling:** Say: "Let's look for sight words. Check these words in your piece: the, because, they, one, another, also, know, have, like, think. Are they all spelled correctly?"

Read through the piece slowly together. The facilitator can help identify misspellings the student misses: "Look at this word. Does it look right to you? Let's sound it out."

Have the student circle misspelled words in red and write the correct spelling above them.

Say: "You just revised AND edited your own work — just like a real author! How does it look now compared to before?"

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student applies revision and editing independently to a second piece or continues improving the first.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Re-read your revised and edited piece one more time. Read it aloud slowly.
2. Find one more place where you could add a detail. Write one new sentence and add it to your piece.
3. Check three more words for spelling. Look at each word carefully — does it look right?
4. Copy one paragraph of your revised piece in your best handwriting on clean paper. This is practice for the "final copy" you'll write in Lesson 10.

**Stretch:**
All of the above, plus:
5. Take out your OTHER writing piece (the one you did NOT revise in Guided Practice). Use the Revision Checklist to find two things you could improve. Mark them with your blue pencil.
6. Use the Editing Checklist on the second piece. Circle any mistakes you find.

**Challenge:**
All of the above, plus:
7. Revise AND edit the second piece completely (both checklists, all items).
8. Write a short paragraph (2-3 sentences) explaining what revision and editing mean and why they are important. This paragraph should be an informative piece — use facts, not opinions!

### Interleaved Review

While the student works, do a quick oral review:

**Informational Text Features Review (from Lesson 1):**
1. "When you publish your final piece, you might want to add text features. Name two text features you could add." (A title/heading, a picture with a caption, bold words for vocabulary.)

**Main Topic and Key Details Review (from Lesson 2):**
2. "Read your piece to me. What is the main topic? What are the key details or reasons?" (Student should be able to identify these clearly in their revised work — if they can't, that signals the revision needs more work.)

**Questioning Review (from Lesson 4):**
3. "If someone read your piece and asked you a question about it, what question do you think they would ask?" (This encourages the student to think about their audience and whether their writing is clear enough.)

## Check for Understanding (3-5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. **What is the difference between revision and editing?**
   (Answer: Revision is making your writing better — adding details, changing words, improving ideas and organization. Editing is fixing mistakes — spelling, capitalization, and punctuation.)

2. **Read this sentence: "i like sumer becuz it is fun" Name TWO editing fixes this sentence needs.**
   (Answer: Any two of the following: (1) "i" should be capitalized to "I"; (2) "sumer" should be spelled "summer"; (3) "becuz" should be spelled "because"; (4) the sentence needs a period at the end.)

3. **Read this sentence: "Dogs are nice." This sentence needs REVISION, not editing. How could you revise it to make it better?**
   (Answer: Accept any revision that adds detail or makes the sentence more interesting. Example: "Dogs are friendly and loyal animals that love to play and cuddle with their owners." The key is that the student changes the IDEAS, not just the spelling or punctuation.)

4. **When you are improving a piece of writing, which do you do FIRST — revision or editing? Why?**
   (Answer: Revision first, then editing. Because there is no point in fixing the spelling of a sentence you might change or remove during revision.)

**Scoring:**
- Question 1: 1 point (must clearly distinguish between the two)
- Question 2: 1 point (must name at least two editing fixes)
- Question 3: 1 point (must revise the content/ideas, not just fix mechanics)
- Question 4: 1 point (must say revision first and give a reasonable explanation)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 points, AND the student has applied both the Revision Checklist and Editing Checklist to at least one of their writing pieces.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student confuses revision and editing: Return to the physical activity. "REVISION — arms wide, big stuff. EDITING — point to your eye, small stuff." Then give three examples and have the student sort each one: "Adding a detail" (revision), "Fixing a capital letter" (editing), "Changing a boring word to a better word" (revision), "Adding a period" (editing), "Adding a new reason" (revision).
- If the student cannot identify editing errors: Practice with five short sentences, each containing one clear error (missing capital, missing period, misspelled sight word). Have the student find and fix each one. Build up to sentences with multiple errors.
- If the student cannot revise a weak sentence: Practice "Boring to Brilliant" from Lesson 8's warm-up. Give three weak sentences and revise each one together, thinking aloud about what details to add.
- Re-attempt the exit ticket after remediation.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Celebrate: "You are now a writer who can REVISE and EDIT. You know how to make your writing better AND how to fix mistakes. That is exactly what professional authors do."
- Make sure both the opinion piece (Lesson 6) and the informative piece (Lesson 8) have been revised and edited. If only one has been done, the student should complete the other before Lesson 10.
- Move to Lesson 10: Publishing and Sharing — Putting It All Together.

## Extension (Optional)
- **Peer Revision:** If there is a sibling, parent, or friend available, have the student swap writing with someone else. Each person reads the other's piece and uses the Revision Checklist to suggest one improvement. Then swap back and discuss. This introduces the concept of peer feedback.
- **Error Hunt Challenge:** The facilitator writes a short paragraph with 5 deliberate errors (a mix of revision-level weak spots and editing-level mistakes). The student has 3 minutes to find all 5. This gamifies the skill.
- **Before-and-After Display:** The student places their original draft and their revised/edited version side by side. They tell a family member: "This is my first draft, and this is my improved version. Here is what I changed and why." This builds metacognitive awareness.
- **Editing Marks Introduction:** Teach the student three simple editing marks used by real editors: the caret (^) for inserting a word, the strikethrough line for deleting a word, and the triple underline (≡) under a letter that should be capitalized. Let the student use these marks on a practice piece.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pedagogical context:** Revision and editing are genuinely difficult for second graders. Most young writers believe their first draft is their final draft — the idea that writing is a process with multiple stages is new and counterintuitive. This lesson makes the distinction between revision and editing explicit and concrete, using physical gestures, color-coded marks, and separate checklists. The key insight for the student is that revision is about IDEAS (the big picture) and editing is about MECHANICS (the small details), and they happen in that order.

**Pacing notes:** This lesson benefits from two sessions. Session 1: Warm-Up, Direct Instruction, and Guided Practice (the student revises and edits one piece with facilitator support). Session 2: Independent Practice (the student works on the second piece more independently) and the Exit Ticket. If time is short, prioritize the Guided Practice — the student doing hands-on revision/editing with support is more valuable than any other part of this lesson.

**Common issues:**
- **"It's already done!"** Resistance to revision is normal. Frame it positively: "Your draft is great — it has wonderful ideas. Now we're going to make it even more amazing, like polishing a diamond." Focus on one improvement at a time so it doesn't feel overwhelming.
- **Confusing revision and editing during practice:** If the student starts fixing spelling when they should be revising ideas, gently redirect: "We'll fix spelling later. Right now, let's focus on making the ideas stronger. Put your red pencil down and pick up the blue one."
- **Difficulty seeing their own errors:** This is developmental. Second graders are still building the metacognitive skills to evaluate their own writing. The checklists help by providing concrete things to look for. Reading aloud is also powerful — the student often catches errors by ear that they miss by eye.
- **Over-editing/erasing everything:** Some students respond to "make it better" by wanting to erase and rewrite the entire piece. Redirect: "We're not starting over. We're finding the good parts and making them even better, and fixing the small mistakes. Most of your writing is already good!"
- **Spelling frustration:** Remind the student that perfect spelling is not the goal of second grade. The Editing Checklist focuses on sight words (high-frequency words they should know) and encourages "best attempt" spelling for everything else. Say: "If you tried to sound it out and did your best, that's great. We'll work on spelling more as we keep learning."

This lesson aligns with standard **W.2.5** (With guidance and support from adults and peers, focus on a topic and strengthen writing as needed by revising and editing) and draws on the writing produced under standards **W.2.1** (opinion) and **W.2.2** (informative).
