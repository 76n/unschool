# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 2, Lesson 8

## Topic
Writing Informative Pieces with Facts and Details

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write a complete informative piece with a topic sentence, at least three facts with details, and a closing sentence
- Turn simple facts into interesting sentences by adding descriptive details
- Choose a topic and use a provided fact bank to compose informative sentences
- Read their writing aloud to check that every sentence teaches the reader something

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 7: Introduction to Informative Writing (informative writing structure, Informative Writing Template, difference between opinion and informative writing)
- Ability to distinguish between a fact and an opinion
- Understanding of the Informative Writing Template: topic sentence, facts, closing
- Ability to write multiple complete sentences independently

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and lined paper (or writing journal)
- Fact Bank sheets (provided in this lesson — one per topic)
- Informative Writing Template from Lesson 7 (for reference)
- Eraser
- Colored pencils or crayons (optional, for illustration)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review informative writing structure and practice turning plain facts into interesting sentences.

**Activity: Boring to Brilliant**

Say: "In Lesson 7, we learned how to write informative pieces that teach the reader facts. Today we are going to write an even longer, even more interesting informative piece. But first, let's practice a skill: turning boring facts into brilliant sentences."

Write these "boring" facts on paper. Read each one aloud. Then work with the student to make each sentence more interesting.

**Boring fact 1:** "Dolphins swim."
Ask: "This is true, but it is not very interesting. Can we add details? HOW do dolphins swim? WHERE? HOW FAST?"
**Brilliant version:** "Dolphins swim fast through the ocean, leaping out of the water and splashing back in."

**Boring fact 2:** "Butterflies are colorful."
Ask: "This is true, but can we say MORE? What colors? What do they look like?"
**Brilliant version:** "Butterflies have bright wings with patterns of orange, blue, black, and yellow that look like tiny paintings."

**Boring fact 3:** "Volcanoes are hot."
Ask: "HOW hot? What makes them hot? What happens because they are hot?"
**Brilliant version:** "Volcanoes are so hot inside that rocks melt into a glowing liquid called lava."

Say: "See the difference? The 'boring' facts are true, but the 'brilliant' ones teach the reader so much more. Today, when you write your informative piece, you are going to turn every fact into a brilliant sentence!"

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model how to select a topic, use a fact bank, and compose a complete informative piece with rich, detailed sentences.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fact bank:** A list of true facts about a topic that a writer can use when composing an informative piece
- **Descriptive details:** Extra words and information that paint a picture in the reader's mind and make writing more interesting
- **Draft:** A first version of a piece of writing — it does not need to be perfect
- **Compose:** To create a piece of writing by putting sentences together in an organized way

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the topic choices and fact banks (3 minutes)**

Say: "Today, you get to choose what to write about. Here are your topic choices."

Write on paper:
- **Choice A:** Dolphins
- **Choice B:** Volcanoes
- **Choice C:** Butterflies
- **Choice D:** Your School

Say: "Each topic comes with a Fact Bank — a list of true facts you can use in your writing. You do not need to research anything. The facts are right here for you. You will pick the facts you want to use and turn them into interesting sentences."

Show the student all four Fact Banks (below) and let them read through the options before choosing:

---

**Fact Bank: Dolphins**
- Dolphins are mammals, not fish.
- Dolphins breathe air through a blowhole on top of their head.
- Dolphins live in groups called pods.
- A pod can have 10 to 30 dolphins in it.
- Dolphins talk to each other using clicks and whistles.
- Baby dolphins are called calves.
- Dolphins can swim up to 20 miles per hour.

**Fact Bank: Volcanoes**
- A volcano is an opening in the Earth's surface.
- Hot melted rock called magma is deep inside the Earth.
- When magma comes out of a volcano, it is called lava.
- Lava can be hotter than 2,000 degrees.
- Some volcanoes are under the ocean.
- The largest volcano on Earth is Mauna Loa in Hawaii.
- When a volcano erupts, it can shoot ash and rocks into the sky.

**Fact Bank: Butterflies**
- Butterflies start life as tiny eggs on a leaf.
- A caterpillar hatches from the egg.
- The caterpillar eats leaves and grows bigger.
- The caterpillar makes a hard shell called a chrysalis.
- Inside the chrysalis, the caterpillar changes into a butterfly.
- Butterflies have four wings covered in tiny scales.
- Butterflies taste with their feet.

**Fact Bank: Your School**
- Your school has a name. (Write the name.)
- Your school has ___ grade levels.
- Students learn subjects like reading, math, science, and art.
- Your school has a place where students eat lunch.
- There are teachers and helpers who work at the school.
- Students go to school ___ days a week.
- Your school has things that make it special. (Think of one.)

---

Say: "Look at all these great facts. Which topic do you want to write about?"

Let the student choose. Once they choose, set the other Fact Banks aside and focus on the chosen one.

**Step 2: Model composing from a fact bank (5-7 minutes)**

Say: "Let me show you how I use a Fact Bank to write an informative piece. I'm going to pick 'Dolphins' and show you how I turn these facts into a complete piece."

Think aloud as you write:

**Topic sentence:**
"First, I need a topic sentence that tells the reader what my piece is about. I'm going to write: *Dolphins are amazing ocean animals that are full of surprises.* See how I didn't just say 'Dolphins are animals'? I added 'amazing ocean animals that are full of surprises' to make the reader want to keep reading."

**Fact 1 — turning a fact into a brilliant sentence:**
"I'll pick this fact from the bank: 'Dolphins are mammals, not fish.' Now let me make it more interesting."

Write: *One fact about dolphins is that they are mammals, not fish. This means they breathe air, just like people do!*

Say: "I took the fact and added a detail that explains what it means. I connected it to something the reader already knows — people breathe air too."

**Fact 2:**
"Next fact: 'Dolphins live in groups called pods.' Let me add a detail."

Write: *Another fact is that dolphins live in family groups called pods. A pod can have ten to thirty dolphins swimming and playing together.*

Say: "I combined two facts from the bank and used the word 'family' and 'swimming and playing together' to paint a picture."

**Fact 3:**
"One more: 'Dolphins talk to each other using clicks and whistles.'"

Write: *Also, dolphins talk to each other using clicks and whistles. Each dolphin has its own special whistle, almost like a name!*

Say: "I added an extra detail that makes it even more interesting. I compared the whistle to a name — that helps the reader understand and remember it."

**Closing:**
Write: *Now you know why dolphins are such special animals!*

Read the complete piece aloud:

*Dolphins are amazing ocean animals that are full of surprises. One fact about dolphins is that they are mammals, not fish. This means they breathe air, just like people do! Another fact is that dolphins live in family groups called pods. A pod can have ten to thirty dolphins swimming and playing together. Also, dolphins talk to each other using clicks and whistles. Each dolphin has its own special whistle, almost like a name! Now you know why dolphins are such special animals!*

Say: "That's eight sentences! And every single one teaches the reader something true about dolphins. Notice what I did: I picked facts from the bank, but I didn't just copy them. I turned them into interesting sentences by adding details and descriptions."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Turning a fact into an interesting sentence (Volcanoes)**

Fact from the bank: "When magma comes out of a volcano, it is called lava."

Say: "This fact is good, but it's a little plain. Let's make it brilliant."

Ask the student: "What could we add? What does lava look like? What does it do?"

Build the sentence together:

Boring version: "When magma comes out of a volcano, it is called lava."
Brilliant version: "When hot magma pushes up and flows out of a volcano, it is called lava. Lava glows bright orange and red and moves slowly down the mountain like a river of melted rock."

Say: "We added what lava looks like (bright orange and red) and what it does (moves slowly down the mountain). Now the reader can picture it!"

**Worked Example 2: Checking — "Does every sentence teach something?"**

Read this example aloud:

*Butterflies are cool. One fact is that butterflies are pretty. Another fact is that I like butterflies. Also, butterflies are awesome. Now you know about butterflies!*

Ask: "What's wrong with this piece?"

Guide the student to notice:
- "Butterflies are cool" — is that a fact or an opinion? (Opinion.)
- "Butterflies are pretty" — fact or opinion? (Opinion.)
- "I like butterflies" — fact or opinion? (Opinion.)
- "Butterflies are awesome" — fact or opinion? (Opinion.)

Say: "This piece has ZERO facts! It's all opinions. An informative piece needs real facts. Let's fix it."

Fix it together using the butterfly fact bank:

*Butterflies are insects that go through an amazing change during their life. One fact is that butterflies start life as tiny eggs on a leaf. A caterpillar hatches from the egg and eats and grows. Another fact is that the caterpillar makes a hard shell called a chrysalis. Inside the chrysalis, it changes into a butterfly! Also, butterflies have four wings covered in tiny scales. Now you know about the amazing life of a butterfly!*

Say: "Every sentence in this version teaches the reader something real. That's informative writing!"

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Support the student step by step as they compose their informative piece using the chosen Fact Bank.

**Activity: Write Your Informative Piece**

**Planning phase (3 minutes):**

Say: "You chose [student's topic]. Let's look at your Fact Bank. Read through the facts. Which ones do you want to use in your piece? You need at least three, but you can use more."

Have the student put a check mark or star next to the facts they want to use (at least 3, up to 5).

Then say: "Now, before you write, tell me your piece out loud. Start with your topic sentence, then tell me the facts, then your closing."

Listen to the student's oral version. Coach as needed:
- If a sentence is just a copied fact: "Good fact! Can you add a detail to make it even more interesting? What does it look like, sound like, or feel like?"
- If the student includes an opinion: "That sounds like what you THINK. For informative writing, we need what is TRUE. Can you change it to a fact?"
- If the student sounds stuck: "Look at your Fact Bank. Pick the next fact and say it in your own words."

**Writing phase (7-9 minutes):**

Say: "Now write it down! Remember the template:"

1. Topic sentence: ___ is/are ___. (Tell the reader what the piece is about.)
2. Fact 1: One fact is ___. (Use a fact from your bank and add a detail.)
3. Fact 2: Another fact is ___. (Different fact, more details.)
4. Fact 3: Also, ___. (One more teaching sentence.)
5. Closing: Now you know about ___!

As the student writes, check in:

**After the topic sentence:** "Read it to me. Does it tell the reader what your piece is about? Does it make the reader want to keep reading?"

If the topic sentence is too plain (e.g., "This is about volcanoes"), help improve it: "Can we make it more interesting? Instead of 'This is about volcanoes,' how about 'Volcanoes are one of the most powerful things on Earth'?"

**After each fact sentence:** Ask: "Is that a real fact? Could we check it? Now, did you add a detail or description to make it interesting?"

If the student writes only the basic fact from the bank without adding anything, say: "You used a great fact! Now, can you add one more sentence after it? Tell me something extra about that fact — what does it mean, what does it look like, or why is it interesting?"

**After the closing:** "Read your whole piece to me from start to finish. After each sentence, ask yourself: 'Does this sentence teach the reader something?' If the answer is YES for every sentence, you've done it!"

Celebrate the student's completed draft: "You just wrote a complete informative piece! Every sentence teaches the reader something true about [topic]. You are an informative writer!"

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student refines their piece and practices previously learned skills.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Re-read your informative piece all the way through.
2. Count your sentences. Write the number at the bottom of your page. You should have at least 5 sentences.
3. Put a star next to your most interesting sentence — the one that you think would surprise the reader the most.
4. Find one "boring" sentence in your piece and make it "brilliant" — add a detail, a description, or a comparison. Cross out the boring version and write the brilliant version above it or on a new line.
5. Draw an illustration at the bottom of your piece that shows one of the facts.

**Stretch:**
All of the above, plus:
6. Add one more fact from your Fact Bank that you did not use yet. Write it as a complete, interesting sentence and insert it into your piece.
7. Write a title for your informative piece at the top (e.g., "All About Dolphins," "The Amazing Butterfly," "Powerful Volcanoes").

**Challenge:**
All of the above, plus:
8. Write a "Did You Know?" box at the end of your piece. This is one bonus fact with an amazing detail, written like this: "Did you know that [amazing fact]? That means [explanation]!"
9. Look at your opinion piece from Lesson 6 and your informative piece from today. Write one sentence explaining how they are different: "My opinion piece is about what I ___, and my informative piece is about what is ___."

### Interleaved Review

While the student works on independent practice, incorporate these review questions:

**Main Topic and Key Details Review (from Lesson 2):**
Say: "Let's pretend someone else wrote your informative piece and you are reading it for the first time."
1. "What is the main topic of your piece?" (The student should name their topic — e.g., "dolphins," "volcanoes.")
2. "What are two key details?" (The student should name two facts from their piece.)

**Opinion Writing Review (from Lesson 6):**
3. "If you were going to write an OPINION piece about your topic instead of an informative piece, what would be different? What would your first sentence say?" (The student should recognize they would need "I think" or "I believe" and would give reasons instead of facts.)

## Check for Understanding (3-5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. **What is a "fact bank" and how does a writer use it?**
   (Answer: A fact bank is a list of true facts about a topic. A writer picks facts from the bank and uses them to write informative sentences.)

2. **Read this sentence: "Volcanoes shoot out hot lava." How could you make this sentence more interesting by adding a detail?**
   (Answer: Accept any reasonable addition, such as: "Volcanoes shoot out hot lava that glows orange and flows down the mountain" or "When volcanoes erupt, they shoot out hot lava that can destroy everything in its path." The student must add at least one descriptive detail.)

3. **Read your informative piece. Pick any sentence and read it aloud. Does that sentence teach the reader something true? How do you know?**
   (Answer: Student reads a sentence and explains that it is a fact — something true that can be checked — not an opinion.)

4. **How is your informative piece different from your opinion piece?**
   (Answer: The informative piece teaches facts; the opinion piece shares what I think and gives reasons. Accept any answer that shows the student understands the difference.)

**Scoring:**
- Question 1: 1 point
- Question 2: 1 point (must add at least one meaningful detail)
- Question 3: 1 point (reads a sentence and correctly identifies it as a fact)
- Question 4: 1 point (explains the difference between informative and opinion writing)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 points, AND the student has produced a complete informative piece with a topic sentence, at least three facts with details, and a closing sentence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student cannot add details to facts: Practice the "Boring to Brilliant" activity with three more facts. For each fact, ask: "What does it look like? What does it sound like? What does it feel like? Why is it interesting?" Write the "brilliant" version together.
- If the piece is too short (fewer than 5 sentences): Go back to the Fact Bank. Have the student pick one more fact and compose a sentence orally, then write it. Repeat until the piece has at least 5 sentences.
- If the student included opinions instead of facts: Go through the piece sentence by sentence. For each sentence, ask: "Is this a fact or an opinion?" Replace opinions with facts from the bank.
- Re-attempt the exit ticket after remediation.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Celebrate: "You wrote an informative piece that teaches real facts! A reader would learn so much from your writing."
- Save this piece in the student's writing folder. The student now has both an opinion piece (Lesson 6) and an informative piece (Lesson 8) available for revision in Lesson 9 and publication in Lesson 10.
- Move to Lesson 9: Revision and Editing.

## Extension (Optional)
- **Two-Page Spread:** The student creates a two-page spread like a nonfiction book: page one has the informative paragraph with a title and heading; page two has an illustration with a caption and a "Fun Fact" box. This connects writing skills to the text features learned in Lesson 1.
- **Fact Expert Presentation:** The student becomes a "Fact Expert" on their topic. They read their piece to a family member and then answer questions about their topic, using facts from the Fact Bank (including ones they did not use in their piece).
- **Compare Topics:** If the student wrote about dolphins, give them the butterfly Fact Bank (or vice versa). Have them write a second informative piece about the new topic. Then compare: "Which piece was easier to write? Why?"
- **Fact vs. Boring Sentence Game:** Write 5 "boring" facts on strips of paper. The student draws a strip and has 30 seconds to say a "brilliant" version out loud. This builds the skill of adding details quickly and fluently.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pedagogical context:** This lesson extends Lesson 7 by moving from co-writing (facilitator-supported) to independent composition with a scaffold (the Fact Bank). The Fact Bank serves two purposes: (1) it removes the need for research, which is beyond most second graders' independent capability, and (2) it gives students raw material to work with so they can focus on the composition skill — turning facts into interesting sentences. This is a deliberate scaffolding decision. In later grades, students will learn to gather their own facts; for now, the composition is the target skill.

**Pacing notes:** Plan for two sessions. Session 1: Warm-Up, Direct Instruction, and Guided Practice (student completes their draft). Session 2: Independent Practice (refining the piece), the Exit Ticket, and any extensions. The "Boring to Brilliant" warm-up is essential — it gives students a concrete before/after model for what "adding details" means. Do not skip it.

**Common issues:**
- **Copying facts word-for-word from the bank:** This is expected for some students and is acceptable for on-level work. For stretch students, encourage them to put the fact in their own words: "The Fact Bank says 'Dolphins are mammals, not fish.' Can you say that in YOUR words? How would YOU explain that to a friend?"
- **Writing too little:** If a student writes only the minimum (topic sentence, three plain facts, closing), celebrate that they met the requirement, then encourage one improvement: "You have five great sentences. Can you pick your favorite fact and add one more detail? Just one extra sentence."
- **Wanting to write about a topic not on the list:** If the student is passionate about a different topic and can provide at least three facts about it from memory, allow it. The skill (composing informative sentences) is more important than the specific topic. Help the student create their own mini Fact Bank orally before writing.
- **Confusing informative writing with opinion writing again:** If this resurfaces, do a quick check: "Read me your first sentence. Does it tell me what you THINK, or does it tell me a FACT?" Correct as needed. The warm-up sorting activity from Lesson 7 can be repeated briefly.

This lesson aligns with standard **W.2.2** (Write informative/explanatory texts introducing a topic, using facts and definitions, and providing a closing statement) and supports **W.2.5** (With guidance and support, focus on a topic and strengthen writing through revision and editing — the groundwork for Lesson 9).
