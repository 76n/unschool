# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
What Makes a Good Story? Elements of Narrative

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the four key elements of a narrative: characters, setting, events, and ending
- Explain what each element contributes to a story
- Find and label narrative elements in a model story

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify characters, settings, and key events in a story (Unit 1, Lessons 6-8)
- Ability to read grade-level text with accuracy (Unit 1 fluency skills)
- Familiarity with retelling a story using beginning, middle, and end (Unit 1, Lesson 6)

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Crayons or colored pencils (four colors for labeling story parts)
- Index cards or sticky notes (optional, for labeling)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about stories from Unit 1 and review informational vs. narrative text from Unit 2.

Say to the student: "In Unit 1, we read stories and talked about characters, settings, and events. In Unit 2, we read informational text that taught us real facts. Today we are going back to stories — but this time, instead of just reading them, we are going to learn how to WRITE them!"

**Quick Review:**

1. "What is the difference between a story and an informational text?" (A story is made up and has characters; informational text teaches real facts.)

2. "Think of your favorite story. Who is in it?" (Student names a character from any story they know.)

3. "Where does that story take place?" (Student names the setting.)

Say: "You already know a lot about stories. Today we are going to look closely at what makes a story GOOD — the special ingredients every story needs."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the four key elements of narrative writing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Narrative:** A story — a piece of writing that tells about something that happens to characters
- **Character:** A person or animal in a story
- **Setting:** Where and when a story takes place
- **Events:** The things that happen in a story — the action
- **Ending:** How the story wraps up — what happens last and how the characters feel

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Every good story has four important ingredients, just like a recipe. If you leave one out, the story does not taste right! Let me show you."

Write this on paper where the student can see it:

**The Four Story Ingredients:**
1. **Characters** — Who is in the story?
2. **Setting** — Where and when does it happen?
3. **Events** — What happens? (beginning, middle)
4. **Ending** — How does it all wrap up?

Say: "Let's learn about each one."

**Characters:** "Characters are the people or animals in your story. A good story helps the reader get to know the characters. We learn what they look like, what they want, and how they feel."

**Setting:** "The setting is where and when the story takes place. It could be a school, a forest, a house, the beach — anywhere! The setting helps the reader picture the story in their mind."

**Events:** "The events are what happens in the story. Something should happen that is interesting or exciting. Maybe the character has a problem to solve, goes on an adventure, or learns something new."

**Ending:** "The ending tells the reader how it all turns out. Does the character solve the problem? How do they feel at the end? A good ending makes the reader feel satisfied — like they got to finish the whole story, not just part of it."

### Worked Examples

Say: "Now let me read you a short story. As I read, let's find all four ingredients."

**Model Story: The Missing Crayon**

> Lily loved to draw. Every day after school, she sat at the kitchen table and drew pictures of animals. Her favorite crayon was the bright blue one because she used it to draw the sky and the ocean.
>
> One afternoon, Lily sat down to draw but she could not find her blue crayon. She looked in her crayon box. Not there. She looked under the table. Not there. She looked in her backpack. Not there either!
>
> Lily felt upset. "I can't draw without my blue crayon," she said.
>
> Then she heard a scratching sound from under the couch. She peeked underneath and found her puppy, Max, chewing on something blue.
>
> "Max! That's my crayon!" Lily laughed. She traded Max a dog treat for the crayon. It was a little chewed up, but it still worked. Lily sat down, picked up her blue crayon, and drew a big blue sky with Max sleeping underneath it.

Say: "Let's find the four ingredients."

Ask the student for each one and fill in together:

- **Characters:** Lily and Max (her puppy)
- **Setting:** Lily's house (kitchen table, couch), after school
- **Events:** Lily cannot find her blue crayon. She looks everywhere. She hears a sound under the couch and finds Max chewing the crayon.
- **Ending:** Lily trades Max a treat for the crayon. She draws a picture of the sky with Max in it. She feels happy.

Say: "See how all four ingredients work together? If we took out the characters, we would not care what happened. If we took out the setting, we could not picture it. If we took out the events, nothing would happen! And if we took out the ending, we would not know how it turned out."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice identifying narrative elements together.

**Activity 1: Label the Story (6 minutes)**

Give the student four crayons or colored pencils (one color for each element). Read this short story together:

> Ben and his sister Rosa walked to the pond behind their grandmother's house. It was a warm Saturday morning.
>
> "Look!" said Rosa. "There's a turtle on that rock!"
>
> Ben wanted to get closer. He tiptoed along the muddy bank, but his foot slipped and — SPLASH — he fell right into the shallow water!
>
> Rosa started laughing. Ben looked down at his muddy clothes and started laughing too. The turtle slid off the rock and swam away.
>
> "I think you scared it," Rosa said.
>
> "At least I cooled off," Ben said, grinning.
>
> They walked back to Grandma's house, dripping and giggling the whole way. Grandma just shook her head and handed them towels.

Say: "Use your four colors to mark the story ingredients."
- Color 1: Underline the **characters** (Ben, Rosa, Grandma, the turtle)
- Color 2: Circle words that show the **setting** (pond, grandmother's house, warm Saturday morning, muddy bank)
- Color 3: Draw a star next to the **events** (walked to pond, saw turtle, Ben slipped and fell in the water, turtle swam away)
- Color 4: Draw a box around the **ending** (walked back to Grandma's house, she gave them towels)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student misses an element, ask: "Is there anything else that tells us about the characters / where this happens / what happens / how it ends?"*

**Activity 2: What's Missing? (6 minutes)**

Say: "Now I am going to read you some story starts. Something important is missing from each one. See if you can figure out what is missing."

**Story A:** "It was a sunny day at the beach. The waves were crashing. There were seashells everywhere."
Ask: "What ingredient is missing?" (Characters — there is no person or animal in the story.)

**Story B:** "Sam wanted to build a treehouse. He found some wood and some nails. He started building."
Ask: "What ingredient is missing?" (Setting — we don't know where or when this is happening.)

**Story C:** "Maria and her dad went to the park. It was a cool fall afternoon. The leaves were red and gold."
Ask: "What ingredient is missing?" (Events — nothing happens! They just went to the park but did not do anything.)

**Story D:** "Jake's dog ran away from the yard. Jake looked all over the neighborhood. He asked his neighbors. He checked the park."
Ask: "What ingredient is missing?" (Ending — we don't know if Jake found his dog or how he felt.)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles, say: "Remember our four ingredients — characters, setting, events, ending. Which one do you NOT see in this story?"*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student works independently to analyze a story and begin thinking like a story writer.

**Activity: Story Map**

Have the student create a Story Map for a story they already know — it could be a book they have read, a movie they have watched, or a story from Unit 1. Draw this organizer on paper:

| Story Ingredient | My Story: _________________ |
|---|---|
| **Characters** (Who?) | |
| **Setting** (Where and when?) | |
| **Events** (What happens?) | |
| **Ending** (How does it end?) | |

### Difficulty Tiers
- ★ Complete the Story Map for a familiar story (write or draw)
- ★★ Complete the Story Map AND explain which ingredient is the most important to the story and why
- ★★★ Complete the Story Map for a familiar story, then create a NEW Story Map for an original story idea of your own

### Interleaved Review
After completing the Story Map, answer these review questions:

4. **Unit 2 Review:** "What is one difference between a narrative (story) and an informative text?" (Write one sentence.)
5. **Unit 1 Review:** Read this word: **adventure**. What strategy did you use to read it? (Multisyllable decoding: ad-ven-ture)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to gauge mastery.

**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer these three questions orally or in writing:

1. Name the four ingredients of a good story. (Characters, setting, events, ending)
2. What do the CHARACTERS in a story tell us? (Who the story is about)
3. Why does a story need an ENDING? (So the reader knows how it turns out; so the story feels finished / complete)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies all four elements and explains at least one of them.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the four elements using the model story (The Missing Crayon) again
- Have the student draw a picture of each element to create a visual anchor
- Before the next lesson, practice identifying elements in a familiar storybook or movie: "Who are the characters? Where does it happen? What happens? How does it end?"

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 2 (Writing a Strong Beginning) next session. Preview: "Now that you know what goes into a good story, next time we are going to learn how to START a story in a way that makes the reader want to keep reading!"

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Story Ingredient Hunt:** Choose a favorite picture book. Go through it page by page and label where each ingredient appears. Are the characters introduced on the first page? When do we learn the setting? Where do the events start getting exciting?
- **Silly Story Fix-It:** The facilitator writes a "story" that is missing TWO ingredients (e.g., no characters and no ending). The student identifies what is missing and adds those elements to fix it.
- **Author Interview:** Pretend you are interviewing a famous author. Ask them: "How do you come up with your characters? How do you decide on a setting?" Then answer the questions yourself as if YOU are the author.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson is designed as a single session. If the student needs more time with the concept, split it into two sessions: Direct Instruction and Guided Practice in session one, Independent Practice and Extension in session two.

**Connection to Unit 1:** Students identified characters, settings, and events as READERS in Unit 1. This lesson explicitly bridges from reading comprehension to writing. Make this connection clear: "Remember when we found the characters and setting in stories we read? Now you are going to create your OWN characters and settings!"

**Common confusion — events vs. ending:** Some students may struggle to separate "events" from "ending" because the ending is technically the last event. Explain it this way: "The events are all the things that happen in the story — the action, the problem, the adventure. The ending is the special last part where everything gets wrapped up and we find out how the characters feel."

**Growth mindset note:** Some students may say "I can't write a story" or "I don't know how." Reassure them: "We are not writing a whole story today — we are just learning the ingredients. It's like learning what goes into a cake before you bake one. By the end of this unit, you WILL write your own story, and it's going to be great."

**Writing vs. drawing:** At this age, some students express ideas more easily through drawing. That is perfectly fine for today's lesson. The goal is understanding story elements, not writing fluency. Drawing will transition to writing in later lessons.
