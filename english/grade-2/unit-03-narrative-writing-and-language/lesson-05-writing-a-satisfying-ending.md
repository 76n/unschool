# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Writing a Satisfying Ending — Resolution, Feelings, Lesson Learned

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write a story ending that wraps up the events and resolves the problem
- Include how the character feels at the end of the story
- Identify what makes an ending satisfying versus unsatisfying
- Use at least one of three ending strategies: resolve the problem, share the character's feelings, or reveal a lesson learned

## Prerequisites
- Identify the four elements of a narrative (Lesson 1)
- Write a story beginning that introduces characters and setting (Lesson 2)
- Use temporal words to sequence events (Lesson 3)
- Add sensory details and show feelings (Lesson 4)

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and lined paper
- The student's writing from previous lessons (story beginnings from Lesson 2, any writing from Lessons 3-4)
- The Temporal Words list from Lesson 3
- Index cards (optional, for matching activity)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review story structure and activate thinking about endings.

Say: "We have been learning about story writing step by step. You know how to write a beginning, put events in order, and add details. Today we are going to learn about the LAST ingredient — the ending!"

**Quick Review:**

1. **Lesson 4 Review:** "What does 'show, don't tell' mean?" (Instead of saying how a character feels, describe what they do so the reader can figure out the feeling.)

2. **Lesson 2 Review:** "Name one hook strategy for beginning a story." (Action, dialogue, or a feeling/thought.)

3. Say: "Think of a story you know well. How does it end?" (Let the student share.) "Did that ending feel satisfying? Did you feel like the story was DONE?"

Say: "A good ending is like the bow on a present — it ties everything together so the reader feels complete."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach three strategies for writing a satisfying ending.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Ending:** The last part of a story where everything gets wrapped up
- **Resolution:** How the problem in the story gets solved
- **Satisfying ending:** An ending that makes the reader feel like the story is truly finished — no loose ends, no confusion
- **Lesson learned:** Something the character (or the reader) understands at the end that they did not understand at the beginning

### Instruction Notes

Say: "An ending is NOT just 'The End.' A good ending does important work. Let me show you three ways to write a satisfying ending."

Write on paper:

**Three Ending Strategies:**
1. **Resolve the problem** — Show how the problem gets solved
2. **Share the character's feelings** — Tell or show how the character feels now
3. **Reveal a lesson learned** — Show what the character discovered or understood

Say: "A great ending often uses TWO or even all THREE of these strategies together."

**Ending Strategy 1: Resolve the Problem**

"Every good story has some kind of problem — the character wants something, or something goes wrong. The ending should show how the problem gets resolved."

**Example story problem:** Mia lost her favorite book.
**Ending that resolves it:** "Mia finally found her book behind the couch cushion, right where she had been reading the night before. She hugged it to her chest and promised herself she would always put it back on the shelf."

**Ending Strategy 2: Share the Character's Feelings**

"At the end of a story, readers want to know: how does the character FEEL now?"

**Example:** "As Mia held her book, she felt relieved and grateful. She had been so worried, but now everything was okay."

Or, using "show, don't tell" from Lesson 4: "Mia let out a big breath she did not even know she had been holding. She grinned and flopped down on the couch to start reading right away."

**Ending Strategy 3: Reveal a Lesson Learned**

"Sometimes the character learns something important by the end of the story."

**Example:** "Mia realized that taking care of your things means always putting them back where they belong. From that day on, she made a special spot on her shelf just for her favorite book."

Say: "See how you can combine these? Mia found her book (resolved the problem), felt relieved (feelings), and learned to put things away (lesson learned). That is a VERY satisfying ending!"

### Worked Examples

Say: "Now let me show you the difference between an UNSATISFYING ending and a SATISFYING ending."

**Story setup:** "Leo tried out for the school soccer team. He practiced every day for two weeks. On the day of tryouts, he was nervous but he did his best."

**Unsatisfying ending:** "Then it was over. The end."

Ask: "How does that make you feel?" (Confused! We do not know if he made the team! It does not wrap up anything.)

**Satisfying ending:** "The next morning, Leo saw his name on the list — he had made the team! He jumped so high he almost hit the ceiling. All that practice had paid off. Leo learned that if you work hard and do not give up, good things can happen."

Ask: "How is this different?" (We know the result — he made the team. We know his feeling — he was so happy he jumped. We know the lesson — hard work pays off. The story feels FINISHED.)

**Another example — not every problem gets solved perfectly:**

Say: "Sometimes the character does NOT get exactly what they wanted, but the ending can still be satisfying."

**Story setup:** "Emma entered the art contest. She worked on her painting for a week."

**Satisfying ending (even though she did not win):** "Emma did not win first place, but the judge said her colors were 'bold and beautiful.' Emma smiled. She had tried something new, and she was proud of what she made. She could not wait to start her next painting."

Say: "Emma did not win, but the ending still feels satisfying because we know how she feels (proud), and she learned something (trying new things is good). Not every story has to have a perfect happy ending — it just has to feel COMPLETE."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice writing endings with support.

**Activity 1: Match the Ending Strategy (4 minutes)**

Read each ending. The student identifies which strategy is used: Resolve the Problem, Share Feelings, or Lesson Learned. (Some may use more than one!)

1. "After searching all morning, Jake finally found his lost puppy hiding under the neighbor's porch."
   Strategy: ___________ (Resolve the problem)

2. "Ava sat on the porch swing and smiled. She felt warm and happy inside."
   Strategy: ___________ (Share feelings)

3. "Noah realized that being honest, even when it is hard, always feels better than keeping a secret."
   Strategy: ___________ (Lesson learned)

4. "Sophie grabbed the rope and pulled the boat to shore. She wiped the sweat from her forehead and laughed. She never knew she was that strong."
   Strategy: ___________ (All three! Resolved the problem — pulled the boat. Feelings — laughed. Lesson — she was stronger than she thought.)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student only identifies one strategy in #4, ask: "Is there anything else happening in this ending? Does Sophie learn something about herself?"*

**Activity 2: Write an Ending Together (8 minutes)**

Say: "Here is a story with a beginning and middle, but no ending. Let's write the ending together."

Read aloud:

> Darius wanted to make a birthday card for his mom. He got out his markers and a big piece of paper. First, he drew a heart. Then he tried to write "Happy Birthday, Mom!" But some of the letters came out backward, and the colors smeared when he touched them.
>
> "This looks terrible," Darius said, staring at the messy card.
>
> His big sister, Jade, walked in. "What are you making?" she asked.
>
> "A card for Mom. But it is all messed up," Darius said.
>
> Jade looked at it. "I think it is perfect," she said. "Mom doesn't care if it is messy. She cares that you made it yourself."

Ask: "What happens next? How does this story end?"

Guide the student to write an ending using at least two of the three strategies:

- Resolve: Does Darius give his mom the card?
- Feelings: How does Darius feel? How does Mom feel?
- Lesson learned: What does Darius learn?

Write together. Example:

> Darius decided to give Mom the card just the way it was. When Mom opened it, her eyes got big and shiny. "Oh, Darius," she said, pulling him into a hug. "This is the best card I have ever gotten." Darius smiled. He learned that something made with love does not have to be perfect.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student writes a very short ending ("He gave it to Mom. She liked it."), ask: "How did Mom react? What did she say? What did she do? How did Darius feel?" Help them expand with details.*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes endings independently.

**Part A: Write Endings for Two Story Starters (7 minutes)**

Read each story start, then write an ending (3-5 sentences) that is satisfying.

**Story 1:**
> Lily and her dad went fishing at the lake. They sat on the dock all morning, but no fish were biting. Lily was getting bored and ready to give up. Then, suddenly, her fishing line tugged hard!

Write the ending: ___

**Story 2:**
> On the first day at his new school, Ravi sat alone at lunch. He did not know anyone. A girl with curly hair sat down next to him. "Hi, I'm Priya. I was new last year. Want to sit with me and my friends?"

Write the ending: ___

### Difficulty Tiers
- ★ Write an ending for ONE story (2-3 sentences) that includes at least one ending strategy
- ★★ Write an ending for BOTH stories (3-4 sentences each) using at least two different strategies
- ★★★ Write an ending for BOTH stories using all three strategies, AND include a sensory detail or "show, don't tell" moment in each ending

**Part B: Interleaved Review (3 minutes)**

3. **Lesson 3 Review:** Rewrite this sentence using a temporal word: "We went to the park. We played on the swings." (Example: "First, we went to the park. Then we played on the swings.")
4. **Unit 1 Review:** Read this word: **resolution**. What does it mean? (How a problem gets solved in a story.)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student can write a satisfying ending.

**Exit Ticket:**

Here is a story with no ending:

> Nadia's class was putting on a play. Nadia wanted the lead part more than anything. She practiced her lines every night for a week. On the day of auditions, her voice shook at first, but then she remembered all her practice and spoke loudly and clearly.

Write an ending (3-4 sentences) that resolves the story and tells how the character feels.

**Mastery = The ending wraps up the story (we learn what happened with the play), includes the character's feelings, and feels complete.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Ask the student to tell the ending orally first, then write it
- Use guiding questions: "What happened after the audition? Did Nadia get the part? How did she feel? Did she learn anything?"
- Review the three ending strategies and have the student pick ONE to focus on

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6 (Collective Nouns and Irregular Plurals). Preview: "You now know how to write a whole story — beginning, middle, and end! For the next few lessons, we are going to learn some special grammar and language rules that will make your writing even better."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Alternate Endings:** Take one of the stories from Guided Practice or Independent Practice and write a DIFFERENT ending. How does changing the ending change the whole feel of the story?
- **Ending Detective:** Look at the endings of two or three picture books. For each one, identify which ending strategy the author used. Which ending is your favorite and why?
- **Personal Narrative Ending:** Think of a real event from your life. Write the ending of that story using all three strategies (what happened, how you felt, what you learned).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson completes the narrative craft sequence (Lessons 1-5). After this lesson, the student has all the building blocks to write a full story. If the student needs extra practice, do a bonus session where they write a short complete story (beginning + 2-3 events + ending) before moving to the grammar lessons.

**"The End" is not an ending:** Many students will write "The End" as their final sentence. Explain that "The End" is a label, not part of the story. The story itself should feel finished before they write "The End." If the reader only knows the story is over because they see "The End," the actual ending needs work.

**Sad or ambiguous endings:** Some students may want to write endings where the character fails or where things do not work out. This is fine! A satisfying ending does not have to be a happy ending — it just has to feel complete. If the student writes a sad ending, ask: "Does the reader know how the character feels? Does the story feel finished?" If yes, it is satisfying.

**Connection to Unit 1:** In Unit 1 (Lesson 6), the student practiced retelling stories with beginning, middle, and end. Remind them: "Remember when you retold stories? You always told what happened at the end. Now YOU get to decide what happens at the end of YOUR story!"

**Growth mindset note:** Writing endings can be hard because the student has to make decisions about how the story turns out. If they are stuck, say: "There is no single right ending. YOU are the author. What do YOU want to happen? There is no wrong answer." Empower the student to make authorial choices.
