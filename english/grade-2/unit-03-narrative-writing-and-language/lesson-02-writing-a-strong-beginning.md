# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 3, Lesson 2

## Topic
Writing a Strong Beginning — Hooking the Reader

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write a story beginning that introduces the characters and setting
- Use at least one "hook" strategy to make the reader want to keep reading
- Distinguish between a weak beginning and a strong beginning

## Prerequisites
- Identify the four elements of a narrative: characters, setting, events, ending (Lesson 1)
- Write complete sentences with capitalization and end punctuation
- Familiarity with what characters and settings are (Unit 1, Lessons 6-7; Unit 3, Lesson 1)

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and lined paper
- The Four Story Ingredients chart from Lesson 1 (or recreate it for reference)
- Crayons or colored pencils (optional, for illustrating the beginning)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the four narrative elements from Lesson 1 and activate thinking about story beginnings.

Say: "Last time, we learned the four ingredients of a good story. Can you name them?"
(Characters, setting, events, ending.)

**Quick Review:**

1. **Lesson 1 Review:** "If a story has events and an ending but no characters, what is wrong?" (We don't know who the story is about — there is no one to care about.)

2. **Unit 2 Review:** "When you wrote an opinion piece, what did you put at the very beginning?" (A topic and my opinion.) "A story beginning works the same way — it tells the reader what this piece of writing is going to be about."

3. **Unit 1 Review:** Read this word aloud: **beginning**. How many syllables? (Three: be-gin-ning.)

Say: "Today we are going to learn how to write the FIRST PART of a story — the beginning. A good beginning makes the reader think, 'Oh! I want to keep reading!'"

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach three strategies for writing a strong story beginning.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Beginning:** The first part of a story where we meet the characters and learn the setting
- **Hook:** Something at the start of a story that grabs the reader's attention and makes them want to read more
- **Introduce:** To tell the reader about someone or something for the first time

### Instruction Notes

Say: "The beginning of a story has two jobs. Job 1: Introduce the characters and setting — tell the reader WHO is in the story and WHERE it takes place. Job 2: Hook the reader — make them curious and excited to keep reading."

Write on paper:

**A strong beginning:**
1. Introduces the characters (who)
2. Introduces the setting (where and when)
3. Hooks the reader (makes them curious)

Say: "Let me show you three ways to hook a reader."

**Hook Strategy 1: Start with Action**

"Jump right into something happening!"

> Marcus raced down the sidewalk as fast as his legs could carry him. His backpack bounced against his shoulders. He could NOT be late for school again!

Say: "See how we are already in the middle of something exciting? We know who (Marcus), where (the sidewalk, heading to school), and we already want to know — will he make it on time?"

**Hook Strategy 2: Start with Dialogue**

"Have a character say something interesting!"

> "Don't open that box!" whispered Grandma, her eyes wide.
>
> Emma stared at the dusty wooden box on the top shelf of the attic. Now she wanted to open it more than ever.

Say: "When a character says something interesting, the reader thinks, 'What is in the box? Why can't she open it?' That is a hook!"

**Hook Strategy 3: Start with a Feeling or Thought**

"Tell the reader what a character is thinking or feeling!"

> Today was going to be the best day of Kai's life. He just knew it. Today was the day his family was getting a puppy.

Say: "We know who (Kai), we can guess where (maybe his house), and we feel excited along with him. We want to keep reading to find out about the puppy!"

### Worked Examples

Say: "Now let me show you the difference between a WEAK beginning and a STRONG beginning."

**Weak beginning:** "This is a story about a girl named Mia. She is seven. She lives in a house."

Ask: "Does that make you want to keep reading?" (Probably not — it is boring. It tells us about Mia but nothing interesting is happening.)

**Strong beginning (same story):** "Mia pressed her nose against the window and watched the rain slide down the glass. Her brand-new red rain boots were sitting by the door, and she could not wait to go outside and splash in every single puddle."

Say: "BOTH beginnings introduce Mia. But the strong one HOOKS us. We can picture Mia. We know she is excited. We want to see what happens when she goes outside."

Ask the student: "What hook strategy did the strong beginning use?" (It started with action — Mia pressing her nose against the window — and showed her feelings.)

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice writing story beginnings together.

**Activity 1: Fix the Weak Beginning (5 minutes)**

Say: "Here is a weak beginning. Let's make it strong together!"

**Weak beginning:** "There was a boy named Tom. He had a dog. They went to the park."

Ask: "What is missing? Does this hook you?" (No — there is no action, no feelings, no interesting detail.)

Together, rewrite it using one of the three hook strategies. Guide the student:

- "What could Tom be DOING when the story starts?" (Maybe he is throwing a ball for his dog.)
- "What could Tom SAY?" (Maybe he calls out to his dog.)
- "What could Tom be FEELING?" (Maybe he is excited because it is the first warm day of spring.)

Help the student write a new beginning. Example they might produce:

> "Come on, Biscuit!" Tom called, tugging on the leash. Biscuit's tail wagged so hard his whole body wiggled. It was the first warm day of spring, and they were finally going to the park!

Say: "Look at that! We used action (Tom calling and tugging the leash), dialogue ('Come on, Biscuit!'), AND feelings (excited about the first warm day). That is a strong beginning!"

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles to get started, offer a choice: "Should we start with what Tom is doing, what he is saying, or what he is feeling?" Let the student pick and go from there.*

**Activity 2: Choose and Write (7 minutes)**

Say: "Now it is your turn to write a beginning. Choose one of these story ideas — or make up your own!"

**Story Idea A:** A girl finds a mysterious key on the playground.
**Story Idea B:** Two friends build a fort out of blankets and pillows.
**Story Idea C:** A cat gets stuck in a tree.
**Story Idea D:** Your own idea!

Say: "Before you write, think about three things: Who are the characters? Where does it take place? And which hook strategy will you use — action, dialogue, or a feeling?"

Have the student plan orally first, then write 2-4 sentences for their story beginning.

*Facilitator prompt: After the student writes, read it together and ask: "Does your beginning introduce the characters? Does it tell us the setting? Does it hook me — do I want to keep reading?" Celebrate what works and suggest one small improvement if needed.*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes story beginnings independently and practices identifying hooks.

**Part A: Identify the Hook (3 minutes)**

Read each beginning below. Write whether the hook strategy is Action, Dialogue, or Feeling.

1. "Look out!" Jasmine shouted as the ball flew toward the window.
   Hook type: ___________

2. Noah felt a knot in his stomach. It was his first day at a brand-new school, and he did not know anyone.
   Hook type: ___________

3. The door to the old barn creaked open, and Ana stepped inside. Dust floated in the sunlight that came through the cracks.
   Hook type: ___________

**Part B: Write Two Beginnings (7 minutes)**

### Difficulty Tiers
- ★ Write one story beginning (2-3 sentences) that introduces characters and setting. Use any hook strategy.
- ★★ Write TWO story beginnings for TWO different story ideas. Each one should use a different hook strategy.
- ★★★ Write TWO story beginnings using different hook strategies, AND write one sentence explaining which beginning you think is stronger and why.

### Interleaved Review
After completing the writing, answer:

4. **Unit 1 Review:** What does it mean to "retell" a story? (To tell the story again in your own words, including the beginning, middle, and end.)
5. **Lesson 1 Review:** What are the four ingredients of a good story? (Characters, setting, events, ending.)

---

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student can write a strong beginning.

**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to write a story beginning (2-3 sentences) for this prompt:

*"A child discovers that their stuffed animal can talk."*

The beginning should:
- Introduce at least one character
- Give a hint about the setting
- Use a hook (action, dialogue, or feeling)

**Mastery = The beginning includes a character, a setting detail, and a recognizable hook strategy.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three hook strategies with examples
- Have the student practice orally: "Tell me the beginning of the story out loud before you write it"
- Work together to write one more beginning using a step-by-step approach: (1) Name your character, (2) Where are they?, (3) Choose a hook — what is the first exciting thing that happens or is said?

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 3 (Sequencing Events). Preview: "You wrote a great beginning! Next time, we are going to learn how to keep the story going by putting events in order using special words like first, next, then, and last."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Three Hooks Challenge:** Take ONE story idea and write THREE different beginnings — one using action, one using dialogue, and one using a feeling. Decide which one is the best hook and explain why.
- **Beginning Detective:** Find the first page of three different picture books or stories. For each one, identify the hook strategy the author used. Which beginning hooks you the most?
- **Illustrate Your Beginning:** Draw a picture that matches the beginning you wrote. Add details in the picture that you did not write about yet — these could become ideas for the rest of your story.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** If the student struggles with the writing component, split this into two sessions. Session 1: Direct Instruction and Guided Practice (focus on understanding hooks). Session 2: Independent Practice and Exit Ticket (focus on writing beginnings).

**Oral rehearsal is essential:** At this age, students should ALWAYS say their story beginning out loud before writing it down. This separates the thinking (composition) from the writing (transcription). If a student stares at a blank page, ask: "Tell me your beginning. Just say it out loud." Then help them write what they said.

**Spelling support:** Do not let spelling anxiety slow down the writing process in this lesson. Tell the student: "Just get your ideas down. Spell the best you can. We will learn more about spelling tools in Lesson 9." If the student asks how to spell a word, give them the spelling quickly so they can keep writing.

**Common confusion — beginning vs. whole story:** Some students will try to write the entire story when asked for a beginning. Gently redirect: "You are writing a great story! For now, let's just focus on the FIRST part — the beginning. We will write the middle and end in the next lessons."

**Celebrating effort:** Display the student's beginnings or read them aloud together. Treat the student like a real author: "I love how you hooked me with that dialogue! I really want to know what happens next." This builds identity as a writer.

3. Action (The door creaks open and Ana steps inside)

**Independent Practice — Part B:**
Answers will vary. Check that each beginning includes a character, a setting detail, and uses a recognizable hook strategy.

**Interleaved Review:**
4. Retelling means telling the story again in your own words, including the beginning, middle, and end.
5. Characters, setting, events, ending.

**Exit Ticket:**
Answers will vary. Example of a mastery-level response:
"Leo was reading in bed when he heard a tiny voice say, 'That book is really boring, you know.' He looked around his dark bedroom. Nobody was there — except his old teddy bear, Button, who was sitting on the pillow and staring right at him."
- Character: Leo (and Button the teddy bear)
- Setting: His bedroom, at night
- Hook: Dialogue ("That book is really boring, you know.")
