# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 3, Lesson 4

## Topic
Adding Details and Description — Showing, Not Telling

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Add sensory details (what characters see, hear, feel, smell, taste) to their writing
- Revise a plain sentence by adding descriptive words and phrases
- Demonstrate the difference between "telling" and "showing" in writing

## Prerequisites
- Write a story beginning that introduces characters and setting (Lesson 2)
- Use temporal words to sequence events (Lesson 3)
- Write complete sentences with correct punctuation

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and lined paper
- Crayons or colored pencils (for the sensory detail activity)
- The Temporal Words list from Lesson 3 (for reference)
- A small object the student can hold and describe (a fruit, a toy, a rock, a flower — anything with interesting texture, color, or smell)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about description and review temporal words.

**Activity: Close Your Eyes and Listen**

Say: "Close your eyes for ten seconds. Listen carefully. What do you hear?"

After ten seconds, the student opens their eyes and names everything they heard (a clock ticking, a bird singing, the refrigerator humming, a car outside).

Say: "You just used one of your SENSES — hearing. Writers use all five senses to help readers feel like they are INSIDE the story. Today we are going to learn how to add details that paint a picture in the reader's mind."

**Review:**

1. **Lesson 3 Review:** "Name three temporal words." (Accept any three: first, next, then, after that, later, finally, last.)

2. **Unit 1 Review:** Read this word aloud: **description**. How many syllables? (Four: de-scrip-tion. Note: the student may say three — "de-scrip-tion" — which is acceptable for this level.)

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the concept of sensory details and "show, don't tell."

### Key Vocabulary
- **Details:** Small pieces of information that make writing more interesting and clear
- **Sensory details:** Words that describe what you can see, hear, feel, smell, or taste
- **Show, don't tell:** Instead of saying how a character feels, describe what the character DOES so the reader can figure out the feeling themselves

### Instruction Notes

**Part 1: The Five Senses in Writing (5 minutes)**

Say: "Good writers use the five senses to help readers experience the story. Let me show you."

Write the five senses on paper:

| Sense | What It Describes | Example |
|-------|------------------|---------|
| **See** | Colors, shapes, sizes, movement | The bright red ball rolled across the green grass |
| **Hear** | Sounds, voices, noises | The thunder rumbled like a drum |
| **Feel** | Textures, temperatures, emotions | The sand was warm and soft between her toes |
| **Smell** | Odors, fragrances | The kitchen smelled like fresh-baked cookies |
| **Taste** | Flavors | The lemonade was sour and cold |

Say: "When you write a story, you do not have to use ALL five senses in every sentence. But sprinkling in sensory details here and there makes the story come alive."

**Demonstration with the small object:**

Hold up the object you gathered (for example, an apple). Say: "Let's describe this apple using our senses."

Together, generate one detail per sense:
- See: "It was shiny and red with a little green spot on top."
- Feel: "The skin was smooth and cool."
- Smell: "It smelled sweet, like fall."
- Hear: "When I bit into it — CRUNCH."
- Taste: "It was juicy and a little bit sour."

Say: "Now imagine a story where a character eats an apple. Which sounds better?"

**Plain:** "Sam ate an apple."
**With details:** "Sam bit into the shiny red apple. CRUNCH! Cold juice ran down his chin. It tasted sweet and a little bit sour."

Ask: "Which one helps you picture it?" (The second one!)

**Part 2: Show, Don't Tell (7 minutes)**

Say: "Now I want to teach you a special writing trick. It is called 'show, don't tell.' Instead of TELLING the reader how someone feels, you SHOW it by describing what the character does, says, or looks like."

Write these examples:

**Telling:** "Anna was scared."
**Showing:** "Anna's hands shook. She pressed her back against the wall and held her breath."

Ask: "In the second one, did I ever say the word 'scared'?" (No!) "But you KNOW she is scared because you can see what she is doing. That is showing!"

**Telling:** "The boy was happy."
**Showing:** "The boy grinned so wide his cheeks hurt. He jumped up and pumped his fist in the air."

**Telling:** "It was a cold day."
**Showing:** "Frost covered the window. Mia could see her breath in the air like tiny clouds."

Say: "You do not have to show EVERY time. Sometimes telling is fine — 'It was a sunny day' is perfectly good. But when you want the reader to really FEEL something, showing is more powerful."

### Worked Examples

Say: "Let's take a plain sentence and add details together."

**Plain sentence:** "The dog ran in the yard."

Ask: "What could we add? What does the dog look like? What sounds does it make? What is the yard like?"

Write together (guide the student to contribute):

**With details:** "The big brown dog raced across the muddy yard, barking and wagging his tail so hard it thumped against the fence. Mud splashed up on his belly."

Say: "Look how much more interesting that is! The reader can SEE the dog, HEAR the barking, and FEEL the energy."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice adding details and showing feelings with support.

**Activity 1: Add Details to Plain Sentences (6 minutes)**

Say: "I am going to give you some plain sentences. Let's make them better by adding sensory details."

Work through these together. The student suggests details and the facilitator helps shape them into sentences.

1. **Plain:** "The girl ate lunch."
   **Goal:** Add details about what the lunch looked like, tasted, or smelled like.
   **Example:** "Maya opened her lunchbox and pulled out a peanut butter and jelly sandwich. The jelly was oozing out the sides. She took a big bite — it tasted sweet and sticky."

2. **Plain:** "It was raining."
   **Goal:** Add sensory details — what did the rain look like, sound like, feel like?
   **Example:** "Rain pattered against the roof like tiny drumsticks. Puddles spread across the sidewalk, and the air smelled like wet dirt."

3. **Plain:** "The classroom was noisy."
   **Goal:** Show, don't tell — describe the specific sounds instead of just saying "noisy."
   **Example:** "Chairs scraped across the floor. Kids were laughing and shouting. Someone dropped a pile of books with a loud BANG."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles, ask guiding questions: "What does it look like? What sounds would you hear? If you were there, what would you feel on your skin?" These prompts activate the senses one at a time.*

**Activity 2: Show the Feeling (6 minutes)**

Say: "Now let's practice showing feelings instead of telling them."

Give the student a feeling. Together, brainstorm what a character might DO, SAY, or LOOK LIKE if they had that feeling. Then write 1-2 showing sentences.

1. **Feeling: Excited**
   Ask: "What does your body do when you are excited?" (Jump up and down, smile really big, clap hands, talk fast)
   Write: "Tia bounced on her toes and clapped her hands. 'We're going to the waterpark! We're going to the waterpark!' she squealed."

2. **Feeling: Sad**
   Ask: "What does someone do when they are sad?" (Cry, look down, talk quietly, shoulders droop)
   Write: "Jake stared at the ground. His eyes stung and his throat felt tight. He blinked hard and stuffed his hands in his pockets."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student writes "he was sad" or "she was excited," say: "That is telling. Can you show me what it looks like instead? What is the character DOING?" Gently guide them to replace the telling sentence with showing details.*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices adding details and showing emotions independently.

**Part A: Revise These Sentences (5 minutes)**

Rewrite each plain sentence by adding at least two sensory details:

1. "The boy walked through the forest."
2. "She opened her birthday present."
3. "The soup was good."

### Difficulty Tiers
- ★ Add one or two descriptive words to each sentence (e.g., "The boy walked through the dark, quiet forest.")
- ★★ Rewrite each sentence with 2-3 added sensory details, using at least two different senses
- ★★★ Rewrite each sentence using the "show, don't tell" technique AND at least two sensory details

**Part B: Show, Don't Tell (5 minutes)**

Write 2-3 sentences that SHOW the following feeling without using the feeling word:

**Feeling: Nervous** (Do NOT write the word "nervous" in your sentences!)

### Interleaved Review

4. **Lesson 1 Review:** What are the four ingredients of a good story? (Characters, setting, events, ending)
5. **Unit 2 Review:** What is the difference between a fact and an opinion? (A fact can be proven true; an opinion is what someone thinks or feels.)

---

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student can add details and show feelings in their writing.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this plain passage:

"Tom went to the beach. He played in the water. He built a sandcastle. He had fun."

Rewrite the passage (or rewrite at least TWO of the sentences) by adding sensory details and showing instead of telling.

**Mastery = Student adds at least two sensory details AND changes "He had fun" from telling to showing (describes what Tom does instead of just saying he had fun).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the five senses chart. Practice describing one familiar object using all five senses.
- Do oral practice first: "Describe the beach to me. What do you see? What do you hear? What do you feel?" Then write down what the student says.
- Focus on one sense at a time rather than all five — add sight details first, then add sound details.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 5 (Writing a Satisfying Ending). Preview: "You can write a beginning, put events in order, and add great details. Next time, we are going to learn how to write an ending that wraps up the story perfectly!"

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Sensory Snapshot:** Go outside (or look out a window). Write a five-sentence paragraph describing what you see, hear, feel, smell, and taste (if applicable) right now. Use one sense per sentence.
- **Revision Challenge:** Take the story beginning you wrote in Lesson 2 and revise it by adding at least three sensory details you did not include before.
- **Feelings Charades:** Write five "showing" sentences on cards (without naming the feeling). Read them to the facilitator and have them guess the feeling. Then switch roles.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson introduces a concept (showing vs. telling) that professional writers spend years refining. Do not expect perfection! If the student can add even one sensory detail to a plain sentence, that is meaningful progress. If needed, split this into two sessions: Part 1 (sensory details) in session one, Part 2 (show, don't tell) in session two.

**The physical object activity matters:** Having the student hold and describe a real object activates concrete thinking. Abstract concepts like "sensory details" become real when the student touches the bumpy surface of an orange, smells a flower, or hears the crinkle of a wrapper. Do not skip this step.

**Overly long descriptions:** Some students will go overboard and describe everything in exhaustive detail. Gently guide them: "That is wonderful detail! In a story, we pick the MOST IMPORTANT details — the ones that help the reader understand what is happening or how the character feels. We do not need to describe every single thing." This skill (selecting relevant details) develops over time.

**Show vs. tell balance:** Reassure the student (and yourself) that "telling" is not wrong. Professional writers use both showing and telling. The goal for second grade is simply to introduce the concept and have the student try it a few times. Saying "Sara was happy" is perfectly acceptable in a second-grade narrative. Saying "Sara grinned and did a little dance" is a stretch goal.

**Connection to reading:** Whenever you read stories together, pause and point out sensory details: "The author said the snow was 'crunchy underfoot.' That is a FEEL detail! And the fire was 'crackling' — that is a SOUND detail!" This helps the student notice detail as a reader, which transfers to their writing.

## Answer Key

**Independent Practice — Part A (Revise These Sentences):**
Answers will vary. Sample responses:

1. "The boy walked through the dark forest. Twigs snapped under his feet, and he could hear an owl hooting somewhere above him." (Hear + see details)
2. "She ripped the shiny paper off her birthday present and gasped. Inside was the bright purple bicycle she had been wishing for!" (See + feel/emotion details)
3. "The hot soup warmed her hands through the bowl. It tasted like tomatoes and cheese, and steam rose up and tickled her nose." (Feel + taste + see details)

**Independent Practice — Part B (Show, Don't Tell: Nervous):**
Answers will vary. Sample: "Zoe's stomach flip-flopped. She wiped her sweaty hands on her jeans and peeked through the curtain at the audience. There were so many people." (Shows nervous without using the word.)

**Interleaved Review:**
4. Characters, setting, events, ending.
5. A fact is something that can be proven true. An opinion is what someone thinks or feels.

**Exit Ticket:**
Answers will vary. Sample mastery-level response:
"Tom ran across the hot sand toward the sparkling blue ocean. He splashed into the cool waves and jumped over each one. After that, he scooped up wet sand and built a sandcastle with a tall tower and a moat around it. When it was time to go, Tom's cheeks were pink from the sun and he could not stop smiling."
- Sensory details: hot sand, sparkling blue ocean, cool waves, wet sand, pink cheeks (see, feel)
- Show not tell: "could not stop smiling" replaces "had fun"
