# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 3, Lesson 3

## Topic
Sequencing Events — First, Next, Then, Last

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Use temporal words (first, next, then, after that, finally, last) to connect events in a story
- Write at least three events in correct sequential order
- Explain why the order of events matters in a narrative

## Prerequisites
- Identify the four elements of a narrative (Lesson 1)
- Write a story beginning that introduces characters and setting (Lesson 2)
- Retell a story using beginning, middle, and end (Unit 1, Lesson 6)

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and lined paper
- Index cards or small strips of paper (for sequencing activity)
- Scissors (optional, for cutting apart sentence strips)
- The Temporal Words list (created during this lesson — keep for future use)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review story beginnings from Lesson 2 and activate thinking about what comes after.

Say: "Last time, you learned how to write a strong story beginning. Let me read you a beginning, and then you tell me — what should come NEXT?"

Read aloud: "Maya ran to the backyard and looked up. Her kite was stuck in the top of the tallest tree."

Ask:
1. "Who is the character?" (Maya)
2. "What is the setting?" (The backyard)
3. "What do you think happens NEXT in this story?" (Let the student suggest ideas — maybe she tries to climb the tree, gets help, etc.)

Say: "You just did something important — you thought about what happens AFTER the beginning. Today we are going to learn how to put events in ORDER and use special words that help the reader follow along."

**Unit 2 Review:** "In an informative piece, you organized your facts in an order that made sense. In a story, the order of events is just as important. If you mix up the order, the story gets confusing!"

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce temporal words and teach how to sequence events.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sequence:** The order in which things happen — what comes first, second, third, and so on
- **Temporal words:** Words that show when things happen and in what order (first, next, then, after that, finally, last)
- **Events:** The things that happen in a story

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When you tell someone what happened during your day, you use ORDER. You do not say, 'I brushed my teeth. I woke up. I ate breakfast.' That does not make sense! You say, 'First I woke up. Then I ate breakfast. After that I brushed my teeth.' The ORDER matters."

Write the Temporal Words list on paper (keep this as a reference for the rest of the unit):

**Temporal Words — Words That Show Order**
| Word | When to Use It |
|------|---------------|
| **First** | The very first event |
| **Next** | The event right after |
| **Then** | Another event after that |
| **After that** | Another event in the chain |
| **Later** | Something that happens after some time passes |
| **Finally** / **Last** | The very last event |

Say: "These words are like road signs for the reader. They tell the reader, 'This happened, and THEN this happened.' Without them, the reader gets lost."

**Demonstration: With and Without Temporal Words**

Write two versions of the same events:

**Without temporal words:**
"Sam found a box. Sam opened the box. A frog jumped out. Sam caught the frog. Sam put the frog outside."

**With temporal words:**
"First, Sam found a box on the front porch. Next, he carefully opened the lid. Then a green frog jumped right out! After that, Sam chased the frog around the living room. Finally, he caught it gently in his hands and put it outside in the garden."

Read both aloud. Ask: "Which one sounds better? Why?" (The second one — the temporal words help us follow the action, and the added details make it more interesting.)

Say: "The temporal words do two things: they put the events in order, AND they make the writing flow smoothly."

### Worked Examples

Say: "Let's practice together. I will give you three events, and we will put them in order using temporal words."

**Events (out of order):**
- She blew out the candles and made a wish.
- Anna's friends came to her house for her birthday party.
- They ate cake and opened presents.

Ask: "What happened first? Next? Last?"

Write together:
> First, Anna's friends came to her house for her birthday party. Then she blew out the candles on her cake and made a wish. Last, they ate cake and opened presents.

Say: "See how the temporal words hold the story together? Without them, the sentences just sit next to each other. With them, the reader can follow the whole event."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice sequencing events and using temporal words with support.

**Activity 1: Put It in Order (5 minutes)**

Write these four sentences on index cards or strips of paper (one sentence per card):

- The caterpillar built a cocoon around itself.
- A tiny caterpillar hatched from an egg on a leaf.
- The caterpillar ate leaves and grew bigger and bigger.
- A beautiful butterfly came out of the cocoon and flew away.

Mix up the cards. Say: "These events are all mixed up! Can you put them in the right order?"

Have the student arrange the cards in sequence. Then, together, rewrite the sequence using temporal words:

> First, a tiny caterpillar hatched from an egg on a leaf. Next, the caterpillar ate leaves and grew bigger and bigger. Then the caterpillar built a cocoon around itself. Finally, a beautiful butterfly came out of the cocoon and flew away!

*Facilitator prompt: If the student puts events in the wrong order, ask: "Could this happen before that? Does it make sense for the butterfly to come out before the cocoon is built?" Help them think about cause and effect.*

**Activity 2: Add Temporal Words to a Story (7 minutes)**

Say: "Here is a story that is missing its temporal words. Let's add them together!"

Read aloud, pausing at each blank:

> _________, Leo woke up early on Saturday morning. He was excited because today was the day of the big soccer game. _________, he put on his soccer uniform and ate a quick breakfast. _________, his mom drove him to the field. _________, the game started! Leo's team played hard. _________, in the last minute, Leo kicked the ball right into the goal. His team won!

Work together to fill in temporal words. Accept reasonable choices — there is more than one right answer:

> **First**, Leo woke up early on Saturday morning. He was excited because today was the day of the big soccer game. **Next**, he put on his soccer uniform and ate a quick breakfast. **Then**, his mom drove him to the field. **After that**, the game started! Leo's team played hard. **Finally**, in the last minute, Leo kicked the ball right into the goal. His team won!

*Facilitator prompt: If the student uses the same word for every blank (e.g., "then, then, then"), say: "Let's try to use DIFFERENT temporal words so the writing doesn't sound repetitive. Look at our list — which words have we not used yet?"*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices sequencing and writing events independently.

**Part A: Sequence the Sentences (4 minutes)**

Number these events in the correct order (1-4):

___ Jada opened her umbrella and jumped over the puddles.
___ Dark clouds filled the sky and rain started to pour.
___ Jada was walking home from school on a cloudy afternoon.
___ She got home, dried off, and drank a cup of hot cocoa.

Then rewrite the events as a short paragraph using temporal words.

**Part B: Write the Middle of a Story (6 minutes)**

Here is a story beginning:

> Ruby and her little brother, Owen, set up a lemonade stand in front of their house on a hot summer day.

Write 3-4 sentences that tell what happens NEXT. Use at least two different temporal words from the list.

### Difficulty Tiers
- ★ Write 3 sentences telling what happens, using at least one temporal word
- ★★ Write 4 sentences using at least two DIFFERENT temporal words from the list
- ★★★ Write 4-5 sentences using at least three different temporal words, AND include at least one detail about how a character feels

### Interleaved Review

6. **Unit 1 Review:** Read this word: **afternoon**. Is it a compound word? What two words make it up? (Yes — after + noon)
7. **Lesson 2 Review:** Name one hook strategy for starting a story. (Action, dialogue, or feeling/thought)

---

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student can sequence events using temporal words.

**Exit Ticket:**

Give the student this prompt: "Write three sentences about what you did this morning. Use a different temporal word to start each sentence."

Example response:
> First, I woke up and got dressed. Then I ate cereal for breakfast. Finally, I brushed my teeth and started my schoolwork.

**Mastery = Student writes three sequential events using at least two different temporal words in the correct order.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice orally: "Tell me three things you did yesterday. What happened first? What happened next? What happened last?"
- Post the Temporal Words list in the student's writing area for constant reference
- Before the next lesson, do a brief sequencing warm-up: give three events on cards, have the student arrange them in order

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 4 (Adding Details and Description). Preview: "You can write a beginning and put events in order. Next, we are going to learn how to add juicy details that help the reader picture your story!"

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Backward Story:** Write a four-event sequence, then rewrite it in REVERSE order. Does it still make sense? Why or why not? (This reinforces understanding that sequence matters.)
- **My Weekend Narrative:** Write a short paragraph (5-6 sentences) about something you did last weekend, using at least four different temporal words.
- **Sequence Illustrator:** Draw four pictures that show a sequence of events, one per box. Write a temporal word and one sentence under each picture to tell the story.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** If the student grasps temporal words quickly, this can be completed in one session. If the student struggles with putting events in order, spend a second session focused on hands-on sequencing (using cards and physical manipulation) before moving to written sequencing.

**"And then" habit:** Many second graders default to connecting every sentence with "and then." This is completely normal. Do not criticize it — instead, frame temporal words as an upgrade: "You know how to use 'and then.' That works! But real authors have even MORE words they can use. Let's try some of these fancy ones." Over time, the student will naturally diversify their connecting words.

**Connecting to real life:** Sequencing is a life skill, not just a writing skill. Reinforce it throughout the day: "Tell me the steps for making a sandwich — use first, next, then, last." "What happened in that TV show? Tell me in order." The more the student practices sequencing orally, the easier it will be in writing.

**Physical sequencing for struggling students:** If the student has difficulty with the written portion, focus on the card-sorting activities. Cut events apart, mix them up, and have the student physically arrange them. This concrete step is the foundation for abstract written sequencing.

**Common confusion — "first" vs. "at first":** Some students may write "At first" when they mean "First." Both are acceptable, but "At first" often implies a contrast ("At first I was scared, but then..."). For now, accept either usage.
