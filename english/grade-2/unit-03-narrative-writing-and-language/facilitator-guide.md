# Facilitator Guide — Grade 2 ELA, Unit 3: Narrative Writing & Language

**Grade:** 2
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 3 — Narrative Writing & Language
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 5 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)
**Standards:** W.2.3, L.2.1a, L.2.1b, L.2.2c, L.2.2e

---

## Unit Overview

In Unit 2, your student learned to read and understand informational text, write opinion pieces with supporting reasons, and write informative pieces with facts and details. This unit builds on those writing foundations by introducing narrative writing — the art of telling a story. Students also deepen their grammar and language skills with collective nouns, irregular plural nouns, reflexive pronouns, possessives with apostrophes, and strategies for consulting reference materials when spelling.

The first five lessons focus on the craft of narrative writing: what makes a good story, how to write a strong beginning, how to sequence events with temporal words, how to add details and description, and how to write a satisfying ending. The next four lessons teach the grammar and language conventions that strengthen student writing. The final lesson brings everything together as students draft a complete narrative from beginning to end using all the skills learned across the unit.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Identify the elements of a narrative (characters, setting, events, ending)
- Write a story beginning that introduces characters and setting and hooks the reader
- Use temporal words (first, next, then, last, after, finally) to sequence events
- Add sensory details and descriptive language to show rather than tell
- Write a satisfying ending that wraps up the story
- Use collective nouns (a flock of birds, a pack of wolves) and irregular plural nouns (children, mice, teeth)
- Use reflexive pronouns correctly (myself, himself, herself, themselves)
- Form possessives using apostrophes (the dog's bone, Sara's book)
- Consult reference materials (word walls, dictionaries, asking an adult) to check spelling
- Write a complete narrative piece with sequenced events, detail, and correct language conventions

**Why this matters:** Narrative writing is one of the three core writing types students need to master in the early grades (along with opinion and informative writing, covered in Unit 2). Telling a story requires students to think about structure, sequence, character, and audience — all skills that transfer to every kind of writing they will do in the future. The grammar and language lessons in this unit are not isolated drills; they are tools that make student writing clearer and more precise. A student who knows how to use possessives, spell tricky words, and choose the right pronoun writes with more confidence and communicates more effectively.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Write complete sentences with correct capitalization and end punctuation
- [ ] Write a multi-sentence piece (opinion or informative) with a topic, supporting details, and a closing (Unit 2)
- [ ] Spell common high-frequency words correctly and attempt phonetic spelling for unfamiliar words
- [ ] Read grade-level text with accuracy and expression (Unit 1)
- [ ] Identify characters, setting, and key events in a story (Unit 1, Lessons 6-8)

If the student struggles with basic sentence writing, spend extra time on sentence mechanics before beginning Lesson 2. The story structure knowledge from Unit 1 (characters, setting, retelling) provides essential background for this unit's writing lessons.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Lined paper and pencils** (the student will write frequently throughout this unit)
- **Crayons or colored pencils** (for illustrating stories and graphic organizers)
- **Index cards or small strips of paper** (for sequencing activities and vocabulary)
- **A folder or writing notebook** to collect drafts and finished pieces
- **A simple children's dictionary** or access to an online dictionary (for Lesson 9; not required for earlier lessons)
- **A word wall or spelling reference sheet** (create one together in Lesson 9, or use any list of common words)
- **Sticky notes** (for marking parts of model texts)
- **Graphic organizer templates** (provided in lessons; blank paper works too)

All model texts and writing samples are included in the lessons. Published storybooks are helpful for additional examples but are not required.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | What Makes a Good Story? (Elements of Narrative) | 1 session |
| 2 | Writing a Strong Beginning | 1-2 sessions |
| 3 | Sequencing Events — First, Next, Then, Last | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Adding Details and Description | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Writing a Satisfying Ending | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Collective Nouns and Irregular Plurals | 1 session |
| 7 | Reflexive Pronouns | 1 session |
| 8 | Possessives and Apostrophes | 1 session |
| 9 | Using Reference Materials for Spelling | 1 session |
| 10 | Writing a Complete Narrative — Putting It All Together | 2 sessions |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session (35-45 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lessons 1-5 (narrative craft) form a progression. Each lesson builds on the previous one, so do not skip ahead. If a student needs more time to internalize story structure, take two sessions for Lessons 2-5.
- Lessons 6-9 (language conventions) are more self-contained. These can be taught at a brisk pace if the student grasps the concepts quickly.
- Lesson 10 (putting it all together) is the most time-intensive. Plan two full sessions: one for planning and drafting, one for revising and sharing. The student should apply skills from all prior lessons.
- If the student finds writing physically tiring, break writing sessions into shorter segments with movement breaks in between. Writing stamina at this age is still developing.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student writes a list of events with no characters or setting ("We went to the park. We played. We went home.") | The student understands sequence but has not yet internalized that a narrative needs characters, a setting, and a problem or interesting event | Go back to Lesson 1 and reread the model narrative together. Point out the characters and setting. Ask: "Who is in your story? Where are they? What is the problem or exciting thing that happens?" Help the student add these elements before continuing. |
| Student writes a strong beginning but the middle and ending trail off | Beginning is the most practiced part; sustaining a story is harder | Use the graphic organizer from Lesson 3 (beginning, middle, end). Have the student plan the whole story before writing. Ask: "What happens after that? And then what? How does it end?" Planning prevents trailing off. |
| Student writes "and then... and then... and then..." to connect every event | The student has not yet learned to vary temporal words | This is very normal for second graders. Teach the temporal word list from Lesson 3 (first, next, then, after that, finally, last). Post the list where the student can see it while writing. Practice replacing "and then" with a variety of words. |
| Student confuses possessives and plurals ("the dog's ran" or "Saras book") | Apostrophe use is new and unfamiliar; the student may not yet hear the difference between "dogs" and "dog's" | Use the oral test from Lesson 8: "Does this mean 'belonging to the dog' or 'more than one dog'?" Practice with concrete examples the student can touch ("This is MY pencil. It is Felix's pencil. The pencil belongs to Felix."). |
| Student writes "hisself" or "theirselves" | These are common non-standard forms that students hear in everyday speech | Do not criticize the student's home language. Simply say: "In our writing, we use 'himself' and 'themselves.' Let me show you the pattern." Use the chart from Lesson 7 as a reference. |
| Student resists looking up words for spelling ("I'll just spell it how it sounds") | Phonetic spelling is efficient and the student may not see the value of checking | Validate phonetic spelling as a great first step: "Sounding it out is smart! Sometimes English words have tricky spellings that don't match the sounds perfectly. Let's check this one." Frame reference materials as a tool that good writers use, not a punishment. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to reinforce narrative thinking:

- "Tell me about something interesting that happened to you today. What happened first? Then what?"
- "If you were going to write a story about our pet / your friend / a superhero, what would happen in the story?"
- "I love how you described that! Can you add one more detail so I can picture it in my mind?"
- "What is the most exciting part of your story? How can we make that part even more exciting?"
- "How does your character feel right now in the story? How could you show that feeling without just saying 'she was sad'?"
- "Read me your favorite sentence. What makes it your favorite?"
- "What temporal word could you use instead of 'and then'?"
- "That is a tricky word to spell. What could we do to find out how to spell it?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- After several sessions, the student cannot write a complete sentence independently (may need additional sentence-level instruction before multi-sentence narratives)
- The student can tell a story orally with vivid detail but produces almost nothing in writing (may indicate a handwriting, motor skills, or processing issue — consider allowing dictation or typing)
- The student consistently cannot sequence three events in the correct order, even orally
- Despite repeated instruction, the student cannot distinguish between a noun and a pronoun (this may affect Lessons 6-8)
- The student shows significant frustration, avoidance, or distress around writing tasks even with scaffolding and encouragement

If you see these signs, consider:
- Allowing the student to dictate stories while you write, then having the student copy or read back the text
- Using drawing as a pre-writing tool — the student draws each scene, then writes about the drawings
- Reducing the length expectations and celebrating small wins ("You wrote four sentences today — that is one more than last time!")
- Consulting with a literacy specialist or occupational therapist if handwriting is a persistent barrier

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 4**, which deepens reading comprehension and introduces more advanced text analysis skills. The narrative writing foundations from this unit carry forward — students will continue to write across genres and apply the grammar and language conventions they learned here. Possessives, irregular plurals, reflexive pronouns, and spelling strategies will appear in warm-up reviews throughout the rest of the year.

Between units, encourage daily writing practice. Even a few sentences in a journal, a short letter to a family member, or a story about the family pet builds writing stamina and confidence. Read stories together and talk about what makes them interesting — the more stories a student hears and reads, the richer their own narrative writing becomes.
