# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 1, Lesson 4

## Topic
Reading Rate and Expression

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read a grade-level passage at a natural, conversational pace (not too fast, not too slow)
- Use punctuation marks (periods, question marks, exclamation points, commas) to guide reading expression
- Demonstrate improved fluency through repeated reading of a passage
- Participate in echo reading and choral reading

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-3 (decoding skills and accuracy strategies)
- Ability to recognize basic punctuation marks (period, question mark, exclamation point, comma)

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Reading passages provided in this lesson (print or display — two copies so facilitator and student can read together)
- Timer or clock (for the optional timed reading)
- Fix-Up Strategies card from Lesson 3

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review decoding and accuracy skills from previous lessons.

**Activity: Punctuation Spotting**

Write these sentences on paper. Ask the student to read each sentence AND name the punctuation mark at the end:

1. The dog ran to the park**.**  (period — tells you to stop)
2. Where are my shoes**?**  (question mark — someone is asking something)
3. Watch out for the ball**!**  (exclamation point — someone is excited or worried)
4. I like apples**,** bananas**,** and grapes**.**  (commas — tell you to pause briefly)

Say: "You know what these punctuation marks look like. Today we are going to learn how they change the way we read — they are like road signs that tell your voice what to do!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how punctuation guides expression and how repeated reading builds fluency.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fluency:** Reading smoothly, at a good pace, with expression — like talking
- **Expression:** Making your voice go up and down to match what is happening in the story
- **Rate:** How fast or slow you read
- **Phrasing:** Grouping words together in a natural way, not reading word... by... word

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: What does fluent reading sound like? (3 minutes)**

Say: "I am going to read the same sentence two different ways. Tell me which one sounds better."

Write: "The little rabbit hopped across the garden and hid behind a big rock."

**Reading 1 (choppy, word by word):** "The... little... rabbit... hopped... across... the... garden... and... hid... behind... a... big... rock." (Read in a flat, robotic voice with long pauses between each word.)

**Reading 2 (fluent):** "The little rabbit / hopped across the garden / and hid behind a big rock." (Read in a natural, conversational voice with smooth phrasing.)

Ask: "Which one sounded better? Why?" (Guide the student to notice that the second one sounded like talking, was smoother, and was easier to understand.)

Say: "The second way is called fluent reading. It means reading smoothly, at a good pace, with expression. It sounds like you are talking, not like a robot!"

**Step 2: Punctuation as voice instructions (4 minutes)**

Say: "Punctuation marks are like instructions for your voice. Let me show you."

Write and model each:

**Period (.)** — "A period means stop. Take a small breath. Your voice goes down a little at the end."
- Model: "The cat sat on the mat." (Voice drops slightly at the end, brief pause.)

**Question mark (?)** — "A question mark means someone is asking something. Your voice goes up at the end."
- Model: "Where is the cat?" (Voice rises at the end.)

**Exclamation point (!)** — "An exclamation point means strong feelings — excitement, surprise, or worry. Your voice gets louder or more intense."
- Model: "The cat is on the roof!" (Voice shows surprise/urgency.)

**Comma (,)** — "A comma means take a tiny pause, then keep going. Do not stop all the way."
- Model: "The cat climbed up the tree, across the branch, and onto the roof." (Brief pauses at each comma.)

**Quotation marks (" ")** — "Quotation marks mean someone is talking. Try to sound like that character."
- Model: "Mom said, 'Come inside for dinner!'" (Change voice slightly for Mom's words.)

**Step 3: Introduce repeated reading (3 minutes)**

Say: "Here is a secret that will make you a better reader: when you read something more than once, you get smoother and faster every time. This is called repeated reading. Even grown-up readers do this! The first time you read something, your brain works on the words. The second time, your brain already knows the words, so it can focus on making it sound great."

### Worked Examples

Write: "Can we go to the park?" asked Sam. "Yes!" said Mom. "Get your shoes, your hat, and your water bottle."

Model reading it with flat, no-expression voice: "Can we go to the park asked Sam. Yes said Mom. Get your shoes your hat and your water bottle."

Then model with proper expression: "'Can we go to the park?' asked Sam. 'Yes!' said Mom. 'Get your shoes, your hat, and your water bottle.'"

Ask: "What was different?" (Guide the student to notice: voice went up for the question, got excited for "Yes!", paused at commas, sounded like the characters were really talking.)

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice fluent reading using echo reading, choral reading, and repeated reading.

**Activity 1: Echo Reading (4 minutes)**

Say: "Echo reading means I read a sentence, and then you read the same sentence right after me, trying to match my voice. Ready?"

Use this passage:

**Passage: The Kite**

> "Look at my new kite!" said Lily. She held it up in the air.
>
> "It is beautiful," said Dad. "The wind is perfect today. Let's go fly it!"
>
> They ran to the top of the hill. Lily held the string tight. Dad threw the kite into the air. It dipped down, then up, then soared high above the trees.
>
> "I did it!" Lily cheered. "My kite is flying!"

Read one sentence at a time. The student echoes each sentence, matching your pace, expression, and phrasing.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student reads in a flat voice, say: "Listen to my voice go up here — 'Look at my new kite!' Can you make your voice do that too?" Exaggerate expression slightly so the student has a clear model to follow.*

**Activity 2: Choral Reading (4 minutes)**

Say: "Now let's read together at the same time. This is called choral reading. Try to stay with me — not ahead and not behind."

Read the same passage together. The facilitator sets the pace and expression; the student reads along simultaneously.

*If the student struggles to keep up: Slow your pace slightly. If the student races ahead: Gently say, "Stay with me — let's match our voices."*

**Activity 3: Repeated Reading — Student Solo (4 minutes)**

Say: "Now it is your turn to read this passage all by yourself. You have already heard it and read it with me, so your brain is ready. Try to make it sound smooth and natural, like you are telling the story."

The student reads the passage independently. Note improvements from the echo and choral reading.

After the student finishes, say: "Let's read it one more time. See if it gets even smoother."

The student reads it a second time.

*Facilitator prompt: After the second reading, give specific praise: "Your voice went up perfectly when Lily asked about her kite!" or "I noticed you paused at the comma — that made it sound so natural!"*

## Independent Practice (8 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student applies fluency skills independently with a new passage.

**Activity: Practice Reading with Expression**

The student reads this new passage independently, focusing on pace and expression:

**Passage: Lost and Found**

> "Where is my red mitten?" asked Nora. She looked under the bed. She looked behind the door. She looked in her backpack.
>
> "Did you check the kitchen?" asked her brother, Max.
>
> Nora ran to the kitchen. There it was, sitting on the table next to her lunch box!
>
> "I found it!" she shouted happily. "Thank you, Max! You are a good helper."
>
> Max smiled. "You always leave things in the kitchen," he said with a laugh.

**Instructions to the student:**
1. Read the passage once silently to yourself (or in a whisper).
2. Read it aloud. Focus on making your voice match the punctuation.
3. Read it aloud one more time and try to make it even smoother.

### Difficulty Tiers
- ★ Read the passage aloud twice with the facilitator nearby for support, using the echo reading technique for tricky sentences
- ★★ Read the passage aloud twice independently, focusing on punctuation and expression
- ★★★ Read the passage aloud three times, and on the third reading, try to sound like the characters are really talking (different voices for Nora and Max)

### Interleaved Review
After the passage, have the student read these review words:

1. **cheerful** (Lessons 1-2: ee vowel team + suffix -ful)
2. **outside** (Lesson 2: compound word)
3. **turned** (Lesson 1: ur r-controlled + Lesson 2: suffix -ed)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student reads with appropriate rate and expression.

**Exit Ticket:**

Have the student read this short text aloud:

> "Stop!" yelled the crossing guard. The children waited at the curb. "Now you may cross," she said calmly. They walked across the street, looked both ways, and hurried to school.

Assess using this rubric:

| Skill | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Rate** | Reads at a natural, conversational pace | Reads mostly smoothly but with some hesitations or rushes | Reads word by word or much too fast to understand |
| **Expression** | Voice changes for questions, exclamations, and dialogue; pauses at commas | Some expression present but inconsistent | Reads in a flat, monotone voice with no variation |
| **Phrasing** | Groups words in natural phrases | Groups some words but breaks in odd places | Reads each word as a separate unit |

**Mastery = Score of at least 2 in all three areas (Rate, Expression, Phrasing).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on the specific area of need:
  - **Rate:** Continue repeated reading practice. Read the same short passage 3-4 times per session until it sounds natural.
  - **Expression:** Practice echo reading daily. Exaggerate expression so the student has a clear model.
  - **Phrasing:** Use a pencil or finger to scoop under phrases (groups of words that go together). Read in phrases, not word by word.
- Spend an extra session on fluency before moving to Lesson 5

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 5 (Asking and Answering Questions) next session. Continue to include fluency practice as part of warm-ups in all future lessons.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate strong fluency:**
- **Reader's Theater:** Find or write a short dialogue between two characters. The student reads one character's lines; the facilitator reads the other. Focus on expression and character voice.
- **Timed Reading (optional — only if the student enjoys it):** Time the student reading a passage. Then have them read it again and see if they read it more smoothly (not just faster — smoother). Celebrate improvement in smoothness, not just speed.
- **Record and Listen:** If you have a recording device, let the student record themselves reading and then listen back. They often notice things about their own reading that they do not notice in the moment.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** Fluency develops gradually. This lesson introduces the concepts and gives initial practice, but fluency work should continue in every lesson going forward. Five minutes of fluency practice per session (through warm-up reading or repeated reading) is more effective than one long session.

**Critical note on speed vs. fluency:** Fluency is NOT just reading fast. Some students (and adults) equate fluent reading with speed. If the student starts racing through text, say: "You are reading fast, which is great! But I want to make sure you are also reading smoothly and with expression. Pretend you are telling this story to a friend — how would it sound?"

**Common issue — monotone reading:** Many second graders read in a flat voice because all their mental energy is going to decoding. This is normal and improves as decoding becomes more automatic. Echo reading is the most effective remedy — when the student hears fluent reading modeled sentence by sentence, they naturally mimic the expression.

**Common issue — ignoring punctuation:** If the student runs through periods and question marks without pausing or changing expression, point to the punctuation mark and ask: "What is this mark? What does it tell your voice to do?" Making punctuation visible and meaningful is a gradual process.

**Do not use timed reading punitively:** If you time readings, the goal is always "smoother and more expressive," not just "faster." Some students become anxious about being timed. If your student does not respond well to timing, skip it entirely and focus on repeated reading without a timer.

**Growth mindset note:** Say: "Reading the same passage more than once is not going backwards — it is practicing, just like athletes practice the same moves over and over. Every time you read it, your brain gets better at it."
