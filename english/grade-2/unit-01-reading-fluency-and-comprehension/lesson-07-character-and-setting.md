# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 1, Lesson 7

## Topic
Character and Setting

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the characters in a story (who is in the story?)
- Identify the setting of a story (where and when does it take place?)
- Describe a character using evidence from the text (what they say, do, and feel)
- Explain how the setting affects what happens in the story

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 5: Asking and Answering Questions
- Lesson 6: Retelling and Central Message
- Ability to read a short passage and answer comprehension questions

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Crayons or colored pencils (for the character/setting sketch, optional)
- Reading passage provided in this lesson (print or display)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review retelling skills from Lesson 6 and activate thinking about characters and settings.

**Activity: Think About Characters You Know**

Say: "Think about a character from a story you know — it could be a character from a book, a movie, or a story we have read together. Tell me two things about that character."

Let the student share. Then say: "You just described a character! A character is who the story is about. Today we are going to learn how to look closely at characters and settings in the stories we read."

**Decoding/Fluency Review:** Have the student read these sentences with expression:

1. "The old farmer walked slowly through the muddy field." (r-controlled vowels: farmer, walked)
2. "Come quickly!" shouted the girl. "The boat is leaving!" (Lesson 2: suffix -ly; Lesson 4: expression)

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to identify and describe characters and settings using text evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Character:** A person, animal, or creature in a story
- **Setting:** Where and when a story takes place
- **Text evidence:** Words or details from the story that support your answer — proof from the text
- **Trait:** A word that describes what a character is like (kind, brave, shy, funny, etc.)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Characters — Who is in the story? (4 minutes)**

Say: "Characters are the people or animals in a story. Sometimes a story has one main character. Sometimes it has several."

Say: "We can learn a lot about a character by paying attention to three things:"

Write on paper:

**How to Learn About a Character:**
1. **What the character SAYS** — their words tell us what they think and feel
2. **What the character DOES** — their actions show us what kind of person they are
3. **What the character FEELS** — their emotions help us understand them

Model with a quick example:

"Imagine a story says: 'Ella shared her lunch with the new kid who forgot his.' What does that tell us about Ella? She shared — that is what she DID. It shows us she is kind and generous."

"Now imagine it says: 'Ella whispered to her friend, I feel bad that he has no lunch.' What she SAID tells us she cares about other people."

**Step 2: Setting — Where and when? (3 minutes)**

Say: "The setting is where and when a story takes place. Sometimes the author tells you directly: 'One summer day at the beach.' Other times, you have to look for clues."

Write:

**Setting includes:**
- **Where:** A place — a school, a forest, a house, a city, the ocean
- **When:** A time — morning, winter, long ago, present day, at night

Say: "The setting is important because it affects what can happen in the story. A story set in a snowy forest will be very different from a story set at a sunny beach!"

**Step 3: How setting affects the story (3 minutes)**

Say: "Let me show you how setting matters."

Quick example: "Imagine a character wants to play outside. If the setting is a sunny park — great, they can play! But if the setting is a house during a thunderstorm — they cannot play outside. The setting changed what could happen."

"In the story 'The Big Storm' from Lesson 5, the setting changed from the backyard (sunny) to inside the house (during a storm). That change in setting caused Anna and her dad to go inside and read instead of playing catch."

### Worked Examples

Read this mini-passage aloud:

> Late at night, deep in the dark forest, a small fox crept through the bushes. His ears turned this way and that, listening for danger. He sniffed the cold air carefully.

Ask: "Who is the character?" (A small fox.)

Ask: "What is the setting?" (A dark forest, late at night.)

Ask: "What can we tell about the fox from what he does?" (He crept through bushes — he is being careful/sneaky. His ears listened for danger — he is alert and maybe nervous. He sniffed the cold air carefully — he is cautious.)

Say: "The dark forest at night makes this story feel mysterious and a little scary. If this fox were in a sunny meadow, the story would feel very different! That is how setting affects the mood and events of a story."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice identifying characters and setting together using a passage with vivid details.

**Activity: Read and Analyze Together**

**Passage: The Lighthouse Keeper's Morning**

> Clara lived in a small stone cottage at the top of a rocky cliff. Every morning, she woke up before the sun to climb the winding stairs of the lighthouse. It was her job to make sure the light was working so that ships could see the shore.
>
> The wind howled outside and waves crashed against the rocks below. Clara pulled her warm coat tight and pushed open the heavy door. Salt spray hit her face.
>
> "Good morning, ocean!" she called out. She was not afraid of the wind or the waves. She loved living by the sea, even on the wildest mornings.
>
> Clara checked the big light at the top of the lighthouse. She cleaned the glass and made sure the lamp was bright. Then she looked out at the gray water. Far away, she could see a fishing boat heading home.
>
> "Safe travels," she whispered. She smiled, knowing her light had helped guide them through the dark night.

**Step 1: Identify the character**

Ask: "Who is the main character?" (Clara.)

Ask: "What is Clara's job?" (She is the lighthouse keeper — she makes sure the light works so ships can see.)

**Step 2: Describe the character using evidence**

Draw a simple character web on paper:

```
         [What Clara SAYS]
              |
[What Clara DOES] — CLARA — [What Clara FEELS]
```

Fill in together:

| What Clara SAYS | What Clara DOES | What Clara FEELS |
|----------------|-----------------|------------------|
| "Good morning, ocean!" | Wakes up before the sun | Loves living by the sea |
| "Safe travels" | Climbs winding stairs | Not afraid of wind or waves |
| | Cleans the glass, checks the lamp | Smiled knowing she helped |
| | Pulls her coat tight, pushes open heavy door | |

Ask: "Based on what Clara says, does, and feels, what words would you use to describe her?" (Guide toward: brave, hardworking, caring, cheerful, loves her job, responsible.)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student gives vague answers like "nice," ask: "What did Clara DO that shows us she is nice? Can you find it in the story?" This teaches the student to support descriptions with evidence.*

**Step 3: Identify and analyze the setting**

Ask: "Where does this story take place?" (A rocky cliff by the sea, a lighthouse, a stone cottage.)

Ask: "When does it take place?" (Early morning, before the sun comes up.)

Ask: "How does the setting affect the story? What details tell us about the setting?" (The wind howled, waves crashed, salt spray, gray water — the setting is wild and stormy, which makes Clara's job important and shows how brave she is.)

*If the student struggles: Point to specific setting details in the text: "What do 'wind howled' and 'waves crashed' tell us about this place? Is it calm or wild?"*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student identifies characters and setting independently.

**Activity: Read and Respond**

The student reads the following passage independently and completes the activities below.

**Passage: The Snow Day**

> Marcus pressed his nose against the cold window. Outside, the whole world was white. Snow covered the yard, the trees, and the rooftop of the neighbor's house. Fat snowflakes were still falling from the gray sky.
>
> "School is canceled!" his mom called from the kitchen. "It's a snow day!"
>
> Marcus jumped up and pumped his fist. "Yes!" he shouted. He ran to the closet and pulled on his boots, his coat, his hat, and his thickest gloves. He burst out the back door into the fluffy snow.
>
> The cold air stung his cheeks, but Marcus did not care. He scooped up snow and packed it into a ball. Then another. Then another. He stacked them into a snowman, using a carrot for the nose and two rocks for the eyes.
>
> His neighbor, Aisha, came outside too. "That is a great snowman!" she said. "Can I help?"
>
> "Sure!" said Marcus. "Let's build a whole snow family!"
>
> They worked together all morning, laughing and building. By lunchtime, five snow people stood in a row in Marcus's yard.
>
> Marcus looked at Aisha and grinned. "This is the best snow day ever."

**Part 1: Character Analysis**

1. Who is the main character? ___
2. Name one other character in the story. ___
3. Write two things Marcus DOES in the story. ___
4. Write one thing Marcus SAYS in the story. ___
5. What word would you use to describe Marcus? Why? ___

**Part 2: Setting Analysis**

6. Where does this story take place? ___
7. When does this story take place? (Think about time of day AND season.) ___
8. Name two details from the story that help you picture the setting. ___
9. How does the setting make this story possible? (Hint: could this story happen on a hot summer day?) ___

### Difficulty Tiers
- ★ Answer questions 1-4 and 6-7 orally or in writing. (Focus on basic identification.)
- ★★ Answer all 9 questions in writing. Use words or short phrases.
- ★★★ Answer all 9 questions in complete sentences. For question 5, give two describing words and support each with evidence from the text.

### Interleaved Review
After completing the questions, the student reads this sentence aloud for fluency practice:

"Marcus jumped up, pulled on his boots, and burst out the back door into the fluffy snow!"

Ask: "Where did your voice pause?" (At the commas.) "How did your voice sound at the end?" (Excited — because of the exclamation point.) (Review of Lesson 4.)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student can identify characters and setting and describe them with evidence.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short text to the student (or have them read it):

> On a hot summer afternoon, Jade sat under the big oak tree in her grandmother's garden. She was reading her favorite book. A ladybug landed on the page.
>
> "Hello, little friend," Jade said gently. She let it crawl onto her finger, then carefully placed it on a leaf.

Ask:
1. Who is the character? (Jade)
2. Where and when does the story take place? (In her grandmother's garden, on a hot summer afternoon)
3. Tell me one thing Jade does and what it shows about her. (She gently let the ladybug crawl on her finger and carefully placed it on a leaf — this shows she is gentle/kind/careful with living things.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the character and setting AND gives at least one character description supported by something from the text.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- For character identification struggles: Practice with very simple texts. Ask "Who is this story about?" first, before analyzing traits.
- For setting struggles: Practice noticing setting details by looking at pictures in books — "Where are the characters? What time of day is it? How do you know?"
- For "describing with evidence" struggles: Provide sentence frames: "I think [character] is [trait] because in the story, [evidence]."
- Revisit this lesson's passage and work through the analysis together before moving on

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 8 (Connecting Events in a Story) next session. Continue to ask about characters and settings in all future reading.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate strong character and setting analysis:**
- **Character Comparison:** Think about two characters from stories we have read — Clara (lighthouse keeper) and Marcus (snow day). How are they similar? How are they different?
- **Setting Swap:** Imagine the snow day story happened at the beach instead. How would the story change? What would Marcus do instead of building a snowman?
- **Character Sketch:** Draw the main character and write 3-4 describing words around the drawing. Under each word, write the evidence from the story.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** Character and setting are foundational literary concepts. Most students will grasp identification (who and where) quickly. Describing characters with text evidence and understanding how setting affects the story are more advanced skills that develop over time. Focus on identification first, then evidence-based description.

**Common issue — listing events instead of traits:** When asked "What is the character like?", students often respond with events: "He built a snowman." Redirect: "That is what Marcus DID. But what does that tell us about what kind of person he is?" Help bridge from action to trait: "Someone who jumps up excited and runs outside to build things — what word describes that kind of person? Energetic? Enthusiastic?"

**Common issue — not noticing the setting:** Students often focus on characters and actions and overlook setting details. Before reading, say: "As you read, pay attention to WHERE the story happens. Look for clues about the place and the time." After reading, ask: "Close your eyes. Can you picture where this story happens? What do you see?"

**Common issue — "I don't know" when asked to describe a character:** Offer choices: "Do you think Marcus is more excited or bored in this story? How do you know?" Multiple-choice questions help build the skill of matching traits to evidence.

**Growth mindset note:** Say: "When you pay attention to characters and settings, you are reading like a detective. You are finding clues in the story to understand the people in it and the world they live in. That is a very advanced reading skill!"

**Connection to future lessons:** Understanding characters and settings is essential for Lesson 8 (connecting events — how characters respond to events) and Lesson 9 (comparing texts — comparing characters across stories).

## Answer Key

**Independent Practice:**

1. Marcus
2. Aisha (or Mom)
3. Any two: pressed his nose against the window, jumped up and pumped his fist, pulled on boots/coat/hat/gloves, burst out the back door, scooped up snow, built a snowman, invited Aisha to help, worked together all morning
4. Any one: "Yes!" / "Sure!" / "Let's build a whole snow family!" / "This is the best snow day ever."
5. Accept any reasonable trait supported by evidence. Examples: excited/enthusiastic (he jumped up and shouted "Yes!"), friendly (he invited Aisha to help), creative (he built a snowman and then a whole snow family), energetic (he ran to the closet, burst out the door, worked all morning)
6. Marcus's yard/backyard (and neighbor's yard)
7. A winter morning on a snow day (school was canceled, snow was falling, they worked until lunchtime)
8. Any two details: whole world was white, snow covered the yard/trees/rooftop, fat snowflakes falling, gray sky, cold air stung his cheeks, fluffy snow
9. The story needs snow — if it were a hot summer day, there would be no snow day, no snowman, and no snow family. The winter/snow setting makes everything in the story possible.

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Jade
2. In her grandmother's garden, on a hot summer afternoon
3. She gently let the ladybug crawl on her finger and carefully placed it on a leaf — shows she is gentle, kind, and careful with nature/animals.
