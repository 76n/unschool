# English Language Arts — Grade 2 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Comparing Two Texts on a Similar Theme

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare two stories that share a similar theme
- Identify similarities and differences in characters, events, and messages between two texts
- Use a Venn diagram as a thinking and organizing tool
- Explain how two different stories can teach a similar lesson

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 5: Asking and Answering Questions
- Lesson 6: Retelling and Central Message
- Lesson 7: Character and Setting
- Lesson 8: Connecting Events in a Story

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Crayons or colored pencils (optional)
- Reading passages provided in this lesson (print or display — two stories)
- Large paper for Venn diagram (or draw on regular paper)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review comprehension skills from previous lessons and introduce the idea of comparing.

**Activity: Quick Compare — Everyday Objects**

Say: "Today we are going to learn how to compare two stories. But first, let's practice comparing with something easy."

Hold up (or describe) two everyday items, such as an apple and an orange.

Ask: "How are an apple and an orange the SAME?" (Both are fruit, both are round, both are healthy, both have seeds.)

Ask: "How are they DIFFERENT?" (Different colors, different taste, different texture.)

Say: "When we compare, we look for what is the same AND what is different. We are going to do the same thing with two stories today!"

**Decoding/Fluency Review:** Have the student read these words:
- **comparing** (Lesson 2: suffix -ing)
- **different** (Lesson 2: VCCV pattern — dif|fer|ent)
- **similar** (Lesson 2: multisyllable decoding)

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to compare two texts and introduce the Venn diagram.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Compare:** To look at two or more things and find what is the same and what is different
- **Similar:** Almost the same, alike
- **Different:** Not the same, unlike
- **Venn diagram:** Two overlapping circles used to show similarities and differences
- **Theme:** The big idea or lesson of a story (similar to central message)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Why compare stories? (2 minutes)**

Say: "Different stories can teach the same lesson, even if the characters and events are completely different. When we compare stories, we discover the big ideas that connect them. This makes us deeper readers and thinkers."

**Step 2: Introduce the Venn diagram (3 minutes)**

Draw a large Venn diagram on paper — two overlapping circles.

```
     Story A          Both         Story B
   ___________    ___________    ___________
  /           \  /           \  /           \
 |   Only in   ||  In Both   ||   Only in   |
 |   Story A   ||  Stories   ||   Story B   |
  \           /  \           /  \           /
   ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾    ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
```

Say: "This is called a Venn diagram. The left circle is for things only in Story A. The right circle is for things only in Story B. The middle — where the circles overlap — is for things that are in BOTH stories."

Model with the apple and orange example:

- Left (Apple only): Red or green, crunchy, thin skin
- Middle (Both): Fruit, round, healthy, have seeds
- Right (Orange only): Orange, juicy, thick skin you peel

**Step 3: What to compare in stories (5 minutes)**

Say: "When we compare two stories, we can look at:"

Write on paper:

1. **Characters:** Who is in each story? Are they similar or different?
2. **Setting:** Where does each story happen? Same place or different?
3. **Events:** What happens in each story? Are the problems similar?
4. **Central message:** What lesson does each story teach? Is it the same message?

Say: "Let me show you with two stories you already know from this unit."

Quick comparison of "Two Cats and a Blanket" (Lesson 6) and the cookie story from the exit ticket in Lesson 6:

- **Characters:** Two cats vs. Sam and Mia — different characters!
- **Events:** Cats fought over a blanket, it ripped; Sam and Mia argued over a cookie, Sam broke it in half — different events but similar problem (two want one thing).
- **Central message:** BOTH stories teach that sharing solves problems. Same message!

Say: "Two completely different stories — one about cats and a blanket, one about kids and a cookie — but the same big lesson. That is what comparing helps us find."

### Worked Examples

Say: "When I compared those stories, I asked three questions:"
1. "What is similar about the characters or events?"
2. "What is different?"
3. "Do they teach the same lesson?"

"Those three questions will guide you every time you compare two stories."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Read two stories together and practice comparing them.

Say: "Now we are going to read two new stories. Both stories are about a similar topic. After we read them, we will compare them using a Venn diagram."

**Story A: The Little Ant**

> A little ant found a big crumb of bread on the sidewalk. She tried to carry it home by herself, but it was too heavy. She pushed and pushed, but the crumb did not move.
>
> "I need help," the ant said. She went back to her ant hill and asked her friends. Ten ants came to help. Together, they lifted the crumb and carried it home easily.
>
> "That was too hard for one ant," she said. "But it was easy for all of us together!"

**Story B: The Stuck Kite**

> Owen's kite got stuck in a tall tree at the park. He jumped and reached as high as he could, but the tree was too tall. He tried shaking the trunk, but the kite did not come down.
>
> Owen sat on the ground, feeling frustrated. Then his friend Sophia walked by.
>
> "What happened?" she asked.
>
> "My kite is stuck and I can't reach it," Owen said.
>
> Sophia looked at the tree. "I have an idea. If you lift me up on your shoulders, I think I can reach it!"
>
> Owen crouched down and Sophia climbed on his shoulders. She reached up and grabbed the kite string. "Got it!" she said.
>
> "We make a great team!" Owen said, laughing.

**Step 1: Read both stories** (5 minutes)

Read Story A together (choral or echo reading). Then read Story B together. After each story, do a quick retelling: "What happened in the beginning, middle, and end?"

**Step 2: Compare using a Venn diagram** (7 minutes)

Draw a Venn diagram. Label the left circle "The Little Ant" and the right circle "The Stuck Kite." Label the middle "Both Stories."

Work through it together:

Ask: "Who are the characters in Story A?" (The little ant and her ant friends.)
Ask: "Who are the characters in Story B?" (Owen and Sophia.)
Ask: "How are the characters different?" (One is about ants, one is about kids.)

Ask: "What is the problem in Story A?" (The crumb is too heavy to carry alone.)
Ask: "What is the problem in Story B?" (The kite is stuck too high to reach alone.)
Ask: "How are the problems similar?" (Both characters cannot solve the problem by themselves.)

Ask: "How does Story A end?" (The ants work together to carry the crumb.)
Ask: "How does Story B end?" (Owen and Sophia work together to get the kite.)
Ask: "How are the endings similar?" (Both problems are solved by working together.)

Ask: "What is the central message of Story A?" (Working together makes hard things easier.)
Ask: "What is the central message of Story B?" (Working together solves problems.)
Ask: "Are the messages similar?" (Yes! Both teach that teamwork/cooperation helps.)

Fill in the Venn diagram together:

**The Little Ant (only):**
- Characters are ants
- Problem: crumb too heavy
- Setting: sidewalk and ant hill
- Asked many friends to help

**Both Stories:**
- Character has a problem they cannot solve alone
- Character asks for help
- Teamwork solves the problem
- Central message: working together makes hard things easier
- Characters feel happy at the end

**The Stuck Kite (only):**
- Characters are kids (Owen and Sophia)
- Problem: kite stuck in a tree
- Setting: park
- Only one friend helped
- Sophia had a clever idea (shoulders)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles to identify similarities, ask: "What was hard for the ant? What was hard for Owen? Were those problems kind of the same?"*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student compares two texts independently.

**Activity: Compare Two Stories**

The student re-reads both Story A and Story B (from guided practice above) and completes the following independently:

**Part 1: Answer these comparison questions**

1. How is the ant's problem similar to Owen's problem?
2. How is the ant's solution similar to Owen's solution?
3. How are the characters different?
4. How are the settings different?
5. What is the same message in both stories?

**Part 2: Venn Diagram**

Draw your own Venn diagram and add at least 2 items in each section (left only, middle/both, right only).

**Part 3: Your Opinion**

Which story did you like better? Why? Write 1-2 sentences explaining your choice.

### Difficulty Tiers
- ★ Answer questions 1, 2, and 5 from Part 1 orally. The facilitator helps draw and fill in the Venn diagram.
- ★★ Answer all 5 questions in writing. Complete the Venn diagram independently. Write 1 sentence for Part 3.
- ★★★ Answer all 5 questions in complete sentences. Complete the Venn diagram with at least 3 items in each section. Write 2-3 sentences for Part 3 explaining your opinion with a reason.

### Interleaved Review
After completing the comparison, ask:
- **Cause and effect (Lesson 8):** In Story A, what caused the ant to ask for help? (The crumb was too heavy to carry alone.)
- **Character (Lesson 7):** What word would you use to describe Sophia in Story B? What evidence supports that? (Clever/helpful — she came up with the shoulder idea.)
- **Decoding (Lesson 1):** Read the word "frustrated" from Story B. What strategy did you use? (Multisyllable: frus|tra|ted)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student can compare two texts.

**Exit Ticket:**

Tell the student these two very short stories (or have them read them):

**Story 1:** "Emma tried to open a jar, but the lid was stuck. She asked her brother Liam for help. Together, they twisted the lid and it popped open."

**Story 2:** "A mouse tried to move a heavy rock that was blocking the path. He could not do it alone. He called his friends, and together they pushed the rock out of the way."

Ask:
1. What is the same about these two stories? (Both characters have a problem they cannot solve alone; both ask for help; teamwork solves the problem.)
2. What is different? (Characters are different — kids vs. animals; settings/problems are different — jar lid vs. rock.)
3. What message do both stories share? (Working together helps solve problems.)

**Mastery = Student identifies at least one similarity AND one difference AND states a shared message.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Simplify by focusing on one comparison dimension at a time: "Let's just talk about the characters. How are the characters the same? How are they different?" Then move to events, then message.
- Use visual supports — draw pictures of the two stories side by side and point to similarities
- Return to the Venn diagram from guided practice and review it together
- Practice comparing two familiar stories (from earlier in this unit) before trying new ones

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 10 (Independent Reading and Response) next session. The student has now learned all the core comprehension strategies for this unit.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate strong comparison skills:**
- **Three-Story Comparison:** Think about "Two Cats and a Blanket" (Lesson 6), "The Little Ant," and "The Stuck Kite." All three stories have a similar theme. How are they all connected? What is the shared message?
- **Create a Comparison Story:** Write your own very short story (3-5 sentences) that teaches the same lesson as "The Little Ant" but with different characters and a different setting.
- **Different Message, Similar Characters:** Can you think of (or make up) two stories where the characters are similar but the message is different? Example: Two stories about kids at school — one about teamwork and one about honesty.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson requires the student to hold two stories in mind simultaneously, which is cognitively demanding. If the student seems fatigued, split the lesson: read Story A one day and Story B the next, then compare on the second day.

**Common issue — difficulty holding two stories in mind:** If the student forgets details from Story A while reading Story B, have them do a quick retelling of Story A before starting the comparison. A brief B-M-E summary written on paper can serve as a reference during comparison.

**Common issue — only finding differences:** Some students focus exclusively on differences ("these characters are different, these events are different"). Prompt for similarities: "I notice the stories are different in many ways. But can you find something that is the SAME? Think about the problem... the solution... the message."

**Common issue — only finding one type of similarity:** Students may say "both stories have a problem" but not go deeper. Push for specificity: "What KIND of problem? Is there something similar about both problems?" Guide toward: "Both characters have a problem they cannot solve alone."

**About the Venn diagram:** The Venn diagram is a thinking tool, not a test. The point is not to fill every section perfectly — it is to make similarities and differences visible. Some students love Venn diagrams and fill them eagerly. Others find them confusing. If the diagram is not working, switch to a simple T-chart (Same / Different) instead.

**Growth mindset note:** Say: "Comparing stories is something that even grown-up readers do! When you notice that two different stories teach the same lesson, you are seeing the big ideas that connect all stories together. That is really powerful thinking."

## Answer Key

**Independent Practice:**

**Part 1:**
1. Both characters have a problem they cannot solve by themselves — the ant cannot carry the crumb alone, and Owen cannot reach the kite alone.
2. Both characters solve their problems by getting help from others — the ant asks her friends, and Owen works with Sophia.
3. Story A has ant characters; Story B has human characters (kids). The ant asked many friends; Owen had help from one friend.
4. Story A is set on a sidewalk/ant hill; Story B is set at a park.
5. Both stories teach that working together (teamwork/cooperation) makes it easier to solve problems that are too hard to solve alone.

**Part 2:** Venn diagram answers will vary. See the guided practice Venn diagram for expected content.

**Part 3:** Answers will vary. Accept any opinion supported with a reason. Example: "I liked Story B better because Sophia's idea to climb on Owen's shoulders was really clever."

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Both characters have a problem they cannot solve alone and both get help to solve it.
2. Different characters (kids vs. animals), different problems (jar lid vs. rock), different settings.
3. Working together helps solve problems.
