# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 9

## Topic
Revision and Editing Strategies

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between revision (improving ideas, organization, evidence, and development) and editing (correcting grammar, punctuation, and spelling)
- Apply a structured revision protocol to strengthen argumentative, informative, and narrative writing
- Identify and correct common Grade 7 writing errors including run-on sentences, comma splices, vague pronoun references, and inconsistent tone

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-8: Drafts of argumentative, informative, and/or narrative writing from earlier in the unit
- W.6.1-W.6.3: Experience writing in all three modes
- Basic understanding of grammar, punctuation, and sentence structure
- Understanding of formal academic style

## Materials Needed
- Student writing folders with drafts from Lessons 1-8
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Revision Checklist (described below)
- Editing Checklist (described below)
- Model texts with errors (provided below for editing practice)
- Colored pens or pencils (two colors — one for revision, one for editing)
- Highlighters

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish between revision and editing and establish that revision comes FIRST.

**Activity: The Two-Step Process**

Display the following two lists side by side:

**Revision (Big Picture):**
- Is my claim clear and debatable?
- Is my evidence specific and credible?
- Does every paragraph have a purpose?
- Is my reasoning chain complete?
- Did I address a counterclaim?
- Is my organizational strategy clear?
- Does my dialogue reveal character?
- Did I expand the right moments?

**Editing (Surface Level):**
- Are my sentences complete (no fragments or run-ons)?
- Is dialogue punctuated correctly?
- Are there spelling errors?
- Do subjects and verbs agree?
- Are verb tenses consistent?
- Did I avoid contractions in formal writing?

Say: "Revision and editing are both essential, but they are not the same thing, and the order matters. Revision is like renovating a house — you are tearing down walls, moving rooms, and rebuilding the structure. Editing is like painting the walls and fixing the light switches. If you paint the walls before you renovate, you will just have to paint them again."

Say: "Today we work in this order: revise FIRST (fix the big stuff), THEN edit (fix the small stuff). Most students want to start by fixing spelling because it is easier. Resist that urge. A perfectly spelled paragraph that says nothing is still a bad paragraph."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach specific revision strategies for each writing mode and common editing errors for Grade 7 writers.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Revision:** The process of improving the content, organization, development, and clarity of a piece of writing; revision changes what the writing says and how it is structured
- **Editing:** The process of correcting surface-level errors in grammar, punctuation, spelling, and formatting; editing changes how the writing looks on the page
- **Comma splice:** An error in which two independent clauses are joined by only a comma without a coordinating conjunction ("She studied all night, she still failed the test" — incorrect)
- **Run-on sentence:** Two or more independent clauses joined without proper punctuation or conjunction ("She studied all night she still failed the test" — incorrect)

### Instruction Notes

**Revision Strategies by Writing Mode:**

Say: "Each type of writing has its own revision priorities. I am going to give you a focused revision checklist for each mode."

Write on chart paper:

**Argumentative Writing Revision Checklist:**
1. Is my claim clear, debatable, and stated in the introduction?
2. Does each body paragraph have a reason, evidence from a credible source, and a complete reasoning chain (what does it mean? how does it connect? why does it matter)?
3. Have I acknowledged at least one counterclaim and responded with evidence?
4. Do my transitions clarify relationships (not just "first," "second," "third")?
5. Does my conclusion synthesize rather than summarize?
6. Is my style consistently formal (no "I think," no contractions, no slang)?

**Informative Writing Revision Checklist:**
1. Does my introduction include a hook, context, and a focus statement that previews the organizational strategy?
2. Is each body paragraph developed with at least three development tools (fact, definition, detail, quotation, example)?
3. Have I used and defined at least three domain-specific vocabulary terms in context?
4. Do transitions connect ideas within and between paragraphs?
5. Does my conclusion synthesize information and offer an insight or significance?
6. Is my tone consistently objective (no opinions, no first person)?

**Narrative Writing Revision Checklist:**
1. Does my opening establish context, point of view, and character without telling?
2. Is my point of view consistent throughout?
3. Have I used indirect characterization (SAID: Speech, Actions, Internal thoughts, Details)?
4. Is there at least one expanded moment with sensory details?
5. Is there at least one compressed section?
6. Does my dialogue reveal character and advance the plot?
7. Does my conclusion follow from the narrated events and reflect on their meaning?

**Common Editing Errors (Grade 7):**

Say: "Now let me show you the four editing errors I see most often in seventh-grade writing."

Write on chart paper with examples:

**1. Run-on Sentences:**
- Wrong: "The experiment failed the students had to start over."
- Fixed: "The experiment failed, so the students had to start over." (Add comma + coordinating conjunction.)
- Fixed: "The experiment failed. The students had to start over." (Separate into two sentences.)

**2. Comma Splices:**
- Wrong: "The data was clear, the program was effective."
- Fixed: "The data was clear; the program was effective." (Semicolon.)
- Fixed: "The data was clear, and the program was effective." (Comma + coordinating conjunction.)
- Fixed: "The data was clear. The program was effective." (Two sentences.)

**3. Vague Pronoun References:**
- Wrong: "When Marcus told Jordan about the test, he was confused." (Who was confused — Marcus or Jordan?)
- Fixed: "When Marcus told Jordan about the test, Jordan was confused."

**4. Inconsistent Tone (Formal/Informal Shifts):**
- Wrong: "The research demonstrates significant academic improvements. Basically, the kids just did way better."
- Fixed: "The research demonstrates significant academic improvements. Students who participated in the program showed measurably higher performance on standardized assessments."

### Model Text / Worked Example

Display the following draft paragraph with multiple revision and editing issues:

> Schools should have longer lunch periods. Studies show lunch is important. When students dont have enough time to eat they rush through their food and this is bad for their health. I think that if schools gave students at least 35 minutes for lunch everyone would be healthier and happier. Some people say that extending lunch takes away class time, but they are wrong because lunch is important too. Furthermore more time would let students actually enjoy their meals and talk to their friends which is good for mental health.

Say: "Let's revise first, then edit. Remember: big picture before small picture."

**Revision pass (what to change about content and structure):**
1. "Schools should have longer lunch periods" — vague claim. Needs specifics and previewed reasons.
2. "Studies show lunch is important" — no specific evidence. Which studies? What did they find?
3. "I think that if schools gave students..." — first person breaks formal style. Remove "I think."
4. Counterclaim response ("they are wrong because lunch is important too") — weak refutation. Needs evidence.
5. Reasoning chain is missing — no explanation of WHY the evidence matters.

Draft a revised version together:

> All middle schools should extend lunch periods to a minimum of 35 minutes because adequate meal times improve student nutrition, reduce afternoon behavioral incidents, and support adolescent mental health. According to a study published in the Journal of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, students who had at least 30 minutes to eat consumed 26% more fruits and vegetables than students with shorter lunch periods. This finding is significant because it demonstrates that time — not just food quality — determines whether students make healthy choices. When lunch is rushed, students discard nutritious foods in favor of items they can consume quickly, reinforcing habits that contribute to childhood obesity and poor concentration in afternoon classes.

**Editing pass (what to fix on the surface):**
In the original: "dont" (missing apostrophe — but also, contractions should not appear in formal writing: "do not"), run-on ("they rush through their food and this is bad" — needs a comma or restructuring), comma splice potential in the last sentence.

Say: "See the difference? The revision changed the QUALITY of the argument. The editing fixed the mechanics. Both matter, but revision made the bigger impact."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply the revision protocol to the student's own writing from earlier in the unit.

**Activity: Revision Conference**

Say: "Take out the strongest draft from your writing folder — whichever piece you are most proud of from Lessons 1-8. We are going to revise it together using the appropriate checklist."

**Step 1 — Identify the writing mode** and select the corresponding revision checklist.

**Step 2 — Read the piece aloud.** Say: "Reading aloud is the single most powerful revision tool. Your ear catches problems your eye misses. Read your piece out loud, slowly, and stop every time something sounds wrong, unclear, or boring."

**Step 3 — Work through the checklist together.** For each item:
- If the answer is "yes," put a check mark.
- If the answer is "no" or "partially," circle the area that needs work and write a specific note about what to change.

**Step 4 — Choose the TWO most important revisions** (not all of them — two) and make those changes now.

**Step 5 — Then do a quick editing pass** focused on the four common errors: run-ons, comma splices, vague pronouns, and tone consistency.

If the student does not have a draft from earlier lessons, use this practice paragraph for revision:

> "Social media is bad for kids. Lots of studies show this. It makes them feel sad and they compare themselves to other people online. I believe schools should ban social media because it causes problems. However, some people think social media is fine, but I disagree."

Guide the student through revising this into a strong argumentative paragraph with a claim, evidence, reasoning, and a counterclaim response.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently revise and edit a piece of their own writing.

**Task:** Select ONE piece of writing from your portfolio (argumentative, informative, or narrative). Apply the full revision-then-editing process:

1. **Read aloud** — mark any sentences that sound wrong, unclear, or flat.
2. **Apply the revision checklist** for your writing mode. Identify at least THREE specific revisions.
3. **Make the revisions** — rewrite sections as needed. Use a different color pen or pencil so you can see your changes.
4. **Apply the editing checklist** — check for run-ons, comma splices, vague pronouns, tone shifts, and spelling.
5. **Correct at least THREE editing errors.**

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Revise one piece with three revisions and three editing corrections.
**Stretch:** Revise one piece with five revisions, rewriting at least one full paragraph from scratch based on checklist feedback. Correct all identifiable editing errors.
**Challenge:** Revise two pieces from different writing modes. For each, write a brief "revision reflection" (three to four sentences) explaining what you changed and why.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1) Edit the following sentence for formal style by removing all informal elements: "I totally think that homework is basically pointless because, like, kids already spend a ton of time at school and they shouldn't have to do even more work at home." (Expected: "Homework is an unnecessary burden because students already spend approximately seven hours per day in academic instruction, and additional assignments encroach on time needed for rest, physical activity, and family engagement.")
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.3e) Read this narrative ending and explain ONE specific way to improve it: "And then everything was fine and she learned a lot from the experience. The end." (Expected: The ending is vague and tells rather than shows. Improve by showing the character's realization through a specific moment, action, or piece of dialogue rather than summarizing with "she learned a lot." Remove "The end.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following sentence and identify TWO errors (one revision-level issue and one editing-level issue). Then fix both.

"Students should exercise more, studies show it helps them focus better and they do better in there classes."

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**

Revision issue: The evidence is vague — "studies show" with no specific source, data, or reasoning chain. Fix: "According to a 2020 study by the Centers for Disease Control, students who engage in at least 60 minutes of daily physical activity score an average of 10% higher on assessments measuring sustained attention."

Editing issue: "there" should be "their" (homophone error). Also: comma splice — "Students should exercise more" and "studies show..." are two independent clauses joined only by a comma. Fix with a semicolon, a period, or a comma + conjunction.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies at least one revision-level issue (content/evidence quality) AND one editing-level issue (grammar/mechanics), and provides reasonable fixes for both.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the distinction between revision and editing from the warm-up. Ask: "Is this a 'renovating' problem or a 'painting' problem?"
- Practice with two simpler sentences — one with only a revision issue ("Dogs are cool pets because they are fun") and one with only an editing issue ("The students was ready for the test") — so the student can isolate each type
- Provide the frame: "Revision: The CONTENT is weak because ___. Editing: The MECHANICS are wrong because ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10, the Integrated Writing Workshop
- Encourage the student to complete revisions on their portfolio piece and prepare it for the workshop

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Before-and-After Gallery:** Create a side-by-side display of your original draft and your revised version. Write a brief annotation explaining the three most significant changes you made and why each one improved the writing.
2. **Peer Editing Protocol:** Exchange drafts with a partner. Use the appropriate revision checklist to evaluate their writing. Write three specific compliments ("Your counterclaim paragraph uses Strategy 2 effectively because...") and two specific suggestions ("Your reasoning chain after the evidence about ___ could be strengthened by explaining why this matters for...").
3. **Error Pattern Analysis:** Review all the editing corrections you made across your portfolio. Do you notice a pattern? (Most students have one or two recurring errors.) Write a "personal editing priority" note that identifies your most common errors and a strategy for catching them.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most important shift in this lesson is getting students to understand that revision is not editing. Many students believe "revising" means fixing spelling and adding a comma. The house renovation analogy is powerful: you do not paint the walls before you knock down the ones you are moving. Reinforce this distinction throughout the lesson.
- Reading aloud is the most underused revision tool. Students are self-conscious about reading their work aloud, but it is remarkably effective at exposing awkward sentences, missing transitions, and underdeveloped sections. If the student is reluctant, offer to read their work aloud while they listen.
- During the Guided Practice revision conference, resist the urge to point out every issue. Let the checklist guide the student to discover problems themselves. When they identify an issue, ask: "What do you think would fix this?" before offering solutions.
- The revision checklist is designed to be mode-specific because each writing type has different priorities. An argument needs evidence and counterclaims; a narrative needs pacing and dialogue. A generic "make it better" instruction is not helpful.
- Some students may discover during revision that a section of their writing needs to be completely rewritten. This is not a sign of failure — it is a sign of developing critical judgment. Celebrate the willingness to tear something down and rebuild it.
- Common editing errors in this lesson (run-ons, comma splices, vague pronouns, tone shifts) are the highest-priority errors for seventh grade because they affect clarity. Spelling errors, while worth correcting, rarely impede comprehension.

## Rubric / Answer Key

### Independent Practice — Revision Quality Rubric

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Revision Identification** | Student identifies at least three meaningful revision needs using the checklist; revisions address content, organization, or development | Student identifies two revision needs; at least one addresses content | Student identifies only surface-level issues or fewer than two revisions |
| **Revision Execution** | Revisions are substantive — paragraphs are rewritten, evidence is strengthened, reasoning is added, structure is improved | Revisions are partially effective — some improvement in content but some issues remain | Revisions are cosmetic — word substitutions or minor rewording without substantive improvement |
| **Editing Identification** | Student identifies and corrects at least three editing errors; corrections are accurate | Student identifies and corrects two errors; most corrections are accurate | Student identifies fewer than two errors or corrections are inaccurate |
| **Process** | Student followed the revision-first, editing-second protocol; changes are visible (different color or marks) | Student generally followed the protocol but mixed revision and editing | Student did not distinguish between revision and editing |
