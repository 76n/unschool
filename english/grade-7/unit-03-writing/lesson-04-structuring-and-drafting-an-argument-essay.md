# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 4

## Topic
Structuring and Drafting an Argument Essay

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Organize a complete argument essay with an introduction, body paragraphs with evidence, a counterclaim paragraph, and a conclusion
- Write an introduction that hooks the reader, provides context, and states the claim
- Write a concluding section that follows from and supports the argument presented

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 1: Claims, reasons, evidence, counterclaims
- Lesson 2: Evidence selection, reasoning chains, cohesive transitions
- Lesson 3: Counterclaim strategies (concede and refute, expose the flaw, reframe)
- W.6.1: Experience writing complete argument essays at the Grade 6 level

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Argument Essay Outline template (described below)
- Model argument essay (provided below)
- Conclusion Strategy Reference (described below)
- Highlighters (three colors)
- Writing folders (for collecting all argument planning from Lessons 1-3)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the full argument structure and introduce the role of introductions and conclusions as framing devices.

**Activity: First and Last Impressions**

Say: "Think about the last time you met someone new. What did you notice first? Now think about the last conversation you had with a friend before a long trip. What did they say as you were leaving?"

After brief discussion, say: "Introductions and conclusions work the same way in an essay. Your introduction is your reader's first impression — it sets the tone, establishes what you are writing about, and makes the reader want to continue. Your conclusion is the last thing the reader remembers — it should leave them thinking. Today we are going to put everything from Lessons 1-3 together into a complete argument essay, with special attention to writing strong introductions and conclusions."

Display the argument essay structure on the board:

```
ARGUMENT ESSAY STRUCTURE (Grade 7)

1. INTRODUCTION
   - Hook (question, statistic, scenario, bold statement)
   - Context (background on the issue)
   - Claim (debatable position + previewed reasons)

2. BODY PARAGRAPH 1 — Reason 1 + Evidence + Reasoning
3. BODY PARAGRAPH 2 — Reason 2 + Evidence + Reasoning
4. COUNTERCLAIM PARAGRAPH — Acknowledge + Respond
5. CONCLUSION
   - Restate claim (new words)
   - Synthesize (connect reasons to a bigger idea)
   - Final thought (call to action, question, forward look)
```

Say: "You already know how to write body paragraphs and counterclaim paragraphs. Today we are going to focus on the bookends — the introduction and conclusion — and then draft a complete essay."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach introduction and conclusion techniques for argumentative essays, emphasizing formal style and cohesion throughout.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Hook:** An opening sentence or sentences designed to capture the reader's attention and create interest in the topic
- **Context:** Background information that helps the reader understand why the issue matters and what is at stake
- **Synthesis:** Combining ideas from different parts of the essay into a unified, big-picture conclusion (not just repeating what was said)
- **Call to action:** A concluding strategy that urges the reader to do something specific in response to the argument

### Instruction Notes

**Introduction Techniques:**

Say: "A strong introduction does three things: hooks, contextualizes, and claims. Let me show you four hook strategies for arguments."

Write on chart paper:

| Hook Type | Example (Topic: year-round school) |
|-----------|-------------------------------------|
| Surprising statistic | "American students lose an average of two months of academic progress every summer — a loss that disproportionately affects students from low-income families." |
| Thought-provoking question | "What if the single most effective reform in education required no new technology, no additional funding, and no teacher training — just a different calendar?" |
| Vivid scenario | "Every September, teachers across the country spend the first three weeks of school reteaching material their students mastered in May. By October, the class has finally returned to where it was in June." |
| Bold, direct statement | "The traditional school calendar was designed for an agricultural society that no longer exists. It is time for the calendar to catch up with the century." |

Say: "After the hook, provide context — one to three sentences of background information that explains the issue. Then state your claim with previewed reasons. The introduction should flow from general (hook) to specific (claim)."

**Conclusion Techniques:**

Say: "A conclusion is NOT a summary. It is a synthesis — you take the pieces of your argument and combine them into something bigger. Then you leave the reader with a final impression."

Write on chart paper:

| Conclusion Strategy | Description |
|---------------------|-------------|
| Call to action | Urge the reader to take a specific step |
| Forward look | Describe what will happen if the argument is (or is not) followed |
| Return to the hook | Circle back to the opening image, question, or scenario and answer it |
| Broader significance | Connect the specific argument to a larger principle or value |

Say: "The worst conclusion starts with 'In conclusion' and repeats the introduction word for word. The best conclusion makes the reader feel that everything in the essay was building toward this moment."

### Model Text / Worked Example

Display the following complete model argument essay:

> **Should Schools Teach Media Literacy as a Required Subject?**
>
> Every day, the average American teenager encounters more than 5,000 advertisements, news articles, social media posts, and digital messages — yet fewer than one in five can reliably distinguish a factual news report from a sponsored advertisement. This gap between information exposure and information literacy is not merely an academic problem; it is a threat to informed citizenship. All middle and high schools should require a semester-long media literacy course because students need systematic instruction in evaluating sources, because media manipulation tactics are becoming increasingly sophisticated, and because an informed citizenry is the foundation of a functioning democracy.
>
> The most urgent reason to require media literacy education is that students currently lack the skills to evaluate the information they consume daily. A landmark study by the Stanford History Education Group tested 7,804 middle school, high school, and college students on their ability to assess online sources and found that more than 80% of middle schoolers could not distinguish between a news article and a sponsored advertisement, and 96% failed to consider why a source might be biased. These findings are alarming because they reveal that the generation most immersed in digital media is also the least equipped to navigate it critically. Without deliberate instruction, students absorb information passively, accepting claims at face value simply because they appear on a screen. A required media literacy course would transform students from passive consumers into active, questioning readers.
>
> Furthermore, the tactics used to manipulate public opinion through media are growing more sophisticated each year, making self-taught media literacy increasingly inadequate. Deepfake technology — artificial intelligence that generates realistic but entirely fabricated video and audio — has advanced to the point where a fabricated video of a public figure speaking words they never said is nearly indistinguishable from authentic footage. According to a report by the Brookings Institution, the volume of deepfake content online doubled every six months between 2019 and 2023. Students who do not learn to identify manipulated media in a structured educational setting will encounter it in the unstructured chaos of social media, where no teacher is present to guide their analysis. The sophistication of modern misinformation demands an equally sophisticated educational response.
>
> Opponents of required media literacy courses argue that this skill should be integrated into existing English language arts and social studies classes rather than taught as a separate subject, and that a new requirement would strain already limited scheduling capacity. This concern reflects a legitimate desire for curricular efficiency. However, decades of attempting to embed media literacy into other subjects have produced the dismal results documented by the Stanford study. When media literacy is treated as an add-on to an existing course, it receives inconsistent attention — a day here, a lesson there — rather than the sustained, sequential instruction that complex skills require. Mathematics is not taught as an occasional addition to science class; it has its own dedicated time because the skill demands it. Media literacy, given its importance and complexity, deserves the same.
>
> The evidence is unambiguous: students cannot evaluate the information they encounter, manipulation tactics are outpacing their defenses, and integrated approaches have failed to close the gap. Requiring a dedicated media literacy course is not a matter of adding one more subject to a crowded schedule — it is a matter of equipping the next generation with the single most important skill for participating in a democratic society. Every day that schools delay, students scroll past thousands of messages they cannot evaluate, forming opinions on foundations they cannot examine. The time for media literacy education is not next year. It is now.

Say: "Let's analyze the introduction and conclusion specifically."

Use three highlighter colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** The hook (first two sentences — surprising statistic about information exposure and literacy gap).
- **Color 2 (green):** The context (third sentence — frames it as a threat to informed citizenship).
- **Color 3 (blue):** The conclusion paragraph — notice how it synthesizes rather than summarizes, connects to democratic citizenship (broader significance), and uses a forward look ("every day that schools delay") combined with urgency.

Ask: "Does the conclusion simply repeat the introduction?" (No — it takes the ideas from the entire essay and connects them to a bigger principle about democratic participation.)

Ask: "How does the last sentence create impact?" (Short, direct, urgent — the rhythm shifts from long, complex sentences to two punchy fragments.)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively outline and draft an introduction and conclusion for a complete argument essay.

**Activity: Bookending an Argument**

Say: "Let's build an introduction and conclusion together. We will use this claim from Lesson 1: 'All middle schools should require students to complete a semester-long financial literacy course.'"

**Step 1 — Draft the introduction together:**

Guide through each component:
1. **Choose a hook type:** Surprising statistic. "By the time most Americans turn 25, they will have signed a lease, opened a credit card account, and possibly taken on student loans — yet fewer than one in three will have received any formal instruction in managing money."
2. **Add context:** "This disconnect between financial responsibility and financial education leaves young adults vulnerable to debt, predatory lending, and poor decision-making that can take decades to recover from."
3. **State the claim:** "All middle schools should require students to complete a semester-long financial literacy course because early financial education builds lifelong money-management habits, because financially literate young people are less likely to fall into debt as adults, and because the middle school years represent a developmentally ideal window for introducing abstract economic concepts."

Read the complete introduction aloud.

**Step 2 — Draft the conclusion together:**

Say: "Let's use the 'return to the hook + broader significance' strategy."

Guide through:
1. **Restate the claim (new words):** "The case for required financial literacy education is supported by research, common sense, and the lived experiences of millions of adults who wish someone had taught them sooner."
2. **Synthesize:** "When schools teach students to budget, save, and evaluate financial decisions during the critical middle school years, they do more than prevent future debt — they empower young people to build the stable, secure lives that financial stress so often disrupts."
3. **Final thought (return to hook):** "The 25-year-old signing that first lease will face the same financial system whether or not they were prepared for it. The only question is whether schools will send them in equipped or unprotected."

Read the complete conclusion aloud and evaluate: "Does this conclusion feel like a summary, or does it feel like the argument is building to something?"

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students outline and draft a complete argument essay (or key sections of one).

Using all the work from Lessons 1-3 (claim, evidence, counterclaim paragraph), complete an Argument Essay Outline and draft your essay.

**Argument Essay Outline:**
```
1. Introduction
   - Hook type: ___
   - Context: ___
   - Claim: ___

2. Body Paragraph 1
   - Reason: ___
   - Evidence + Source: ___
   - Reasoning chain: ___

3. Body Paragraph 2
   - Reason: ___
   - Evidence + Source: ___
   - Reasoning chain: ___

4. Counterclaim Paragraph
   - Strategy: ___
   - Counterclaim: ___
   - Response with evidence: ___

5. Conclusion
   - Strategy: ___
   - Synthesis: ___
   - Final thought: ___
```

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete the outline and draft the introduction and conclusion (body paragraphs and counterclaim can come from Lessons 2-3).
**Stretch:** Complete the outline and draft the full five-paragraph argument essay.
**Challenge:** Draft the full essay and include TWO counterclaims (either in one paragraph or two), each handled with a different strategy from Lesson 3.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1d) Read the following conclusion and identify ONE specific problem with it: "In conclusion, I have shown that schools should start later. Starting later is good because students will sleep more and get better grades. That is why I think schools should start later." (Expected: Problems include — uses "I" (breaks formal style), uses "in conclusion" (weak opening), simply restates the introduction instead of synthesizing, vague language ("good"), and uses "I think" instead of letting the evidence speak.)
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.3a) Write a one-sentence narrative hook using the "action opening" technique for this situation: A student discovers they left their science project on the kitchen table on the morning it is due. (Expected: Something like "The bus had already pulled away from the curb when Jamal's hand shot into his empty backpack and found nothing but a crumpled sweatshirt where his science project should have been.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Write a concluding sentence (not a full paragraph — just one or two sentences) for the following argument:

Claim: "Schools should provide free lunch to all students, regardless of family income."

Your concluding sentence should synthesize the argument and leave the reader with a final thought. Do NOT begin with "In conclusion."

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
Answers will vary. Strong examples:
- "When no student has to worry about whether they can afford to eat, every student can focus on what school was designed for: learning."
- "A school that feeds every student is a school that has decided hunger is not an acceptable barrier to education — and that decision benefits not just the students who are hungry today but the community they will build tomorrow."

**Mastery = The concluding sentence (1) does not begin with "In conclusion" or simply restate the claim, (2) connects the specific argument to a bigger idea or consequence, and (3) uses formal language.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the four conclusion strategies (call to action, forward look, return to hook, broader significance) and have the student choose one
- Provide the sentence frame: "When [the argument is implemented], [broader consequence or benefit]."
- Read two sample conclusions aloud — one weak (restates the claim) and one strong (synthesizes) — and ask the student to identify which is which and why

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 5, which transitions to informative writing
- Encourage the student to complete and revise their full argument essay before the next session, keeping it in their writing portfolio

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Peer Review:** Exchange argument essays with a partner. Use the following checklist: (a) Is the claim clear and debatable? (b) Are reasons supported with credible evidence and reasoning? (c) Is a counterclaim acknowledged and responded to? (d) Does the conclusion synthesize rather than summarize? Write two specific compliments and one specific suggestion for improvement.
2. **Conclusion Comparison:** Find three argument essays online (editorials, op-eds, or student essays). Read only the conclusions. Which one is most effective? Write a brief analysis explaining why.
3. **Full Revision:** Take your complete argument essay and revise it using the following lens: Read each paragraph and ask, "Does this paragraph earn its place in the essay? Does every sentence contribute to the argument?" Cut any sentences that do not advance the argument, and strengthen any sentences that are vague or unsupported.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This is the synthesis lesson for the argument sequence (Lessons 1-4). Students should draw on all the skills they have built: claim writing, evidence with reasoning, counterclaim acknowledgment, cohesive transitions, and formal style. If a student struggles to pull it all together, identify the weakest link and reteach that specific skill rather than trying to address everything at once.
- The outline is a critical step. Students who skip outlining often produce disorganized essays. Insist on the outline before drafting, but assure students that the outline can change as they write.
- Conclusions are notoriously difficult for middle schoolers. The most common error is the "In conclusion, I have shown that..." summary. Counter this by banning the phrase "In conclusion" entirely and requiring students to use one of the four strategies.
- Some students may not finish a complete essay in one session. This is expected. The outline and at least the introduction and conclusion should be complete; body paragraphs can be drafted or revised in the student's writing time between sessions.
- Celebrate strong writing publicly. Read effective introductions, evidence paragraphs, or conclusions aloud (with student permission) and ask the class to identify what makes them work.

## Rubric / Answer Key

### Independent Practice — Essay Rubric

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Introduction** | Hook engages the reader; context frames the issue; claim is clear, debatable, and previews reasons | Introduction is present but missing one element (hook, context, or previewed reasons) | No clear introduction, or the claim is vague / not debatable |
| **Evidence and Reasoning** | At least two reasons supported by credible evidence with complete reasoning chains | One reason has evidence and reasoning; the other is underdeveloped | Reasons are unsupported or evidence lacks reasoning |
| **Counterclaim** | Counterclaim is acknowledged fairly and responded to with evidence using a clear strategy | Counterclaim is mentioned but response is weak or unsupported | No counterclaim is addressed |
| **Conclusion** | Conclusion synthesizes the argument and leaves a final impression; does not simply restate | Conclusion restates the claim but adds limited synthesis | No conclusion, or it is a single sentence that restates the claim |
| **Formal Style and Cohesion** | Formal tone throughout; cohesive transitions clarify relationships; no contractions or first person | Mostly formal but with occasional lapses; some transitions present | Informal tone; few or no transitions; reads as disconnected paragraphs |

### Interleaved Review
1. Problems: uses "I" (informal), starts with "In conclusion" (weak), restates instead of synthesizing, vague language ("good"), "I think" breaks formal style.
2. Answers will vary. Must use action to drop the reader into the moment without telling (e.g., no "Jamal was scared").

### Exit Ticket
See expected answers above.
