# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 8

## Topic
Dialogue, Pacing, and Description

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write dialogue that reveals character, advances the plot, and sounds natural
- Control pacing by expanding important moments with sensory detail and compressing less important events
- Use precise words, relevant descriptive details, and sensory language to create vivid scenes

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 7: Understanding of point of view, indirect characterization, and narrative transitions
- W.6.3b: Experience using show-don't-tell, dialogue, and pacing techniques
- W.6.3d: Familiarity with sensory details and figurative language
- Understanding of basic dialogue punctuation (quotation marks, commas, new speaker/new paragraph)

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model narrative scenes (provided below)
- Dialogue Dos and Don'ts Reference (described below)
- Pacing Toolkit (described below)
- Highlighters (three colors)
- Writing folders (for narrative drafts from Lesson 7)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review dialogue punctuation and introduce the concept that dialogue has a purpose beyond filling silence.

**Activity: Real Talk vs. Story Talk**

Display the following two dialogue exchanges:

**Exchange A:**
> "Hi," said Jordan.
> "Hi," said Keisha.
> "What are you doing?" asked Jordan.
> "Nothing," said Keisha.
> "Cool," said Jordan.

**Exchange B:**
> "You were not at practice today." Jordan dropped his backpack on the bench and sat down without looking at her.
> Keisha pulled her sleeves over her hands. "I know."
> "Coach asked about you."
> "What did you tell him?"
> Jordan hesitated. "I told him you were sick."
> Keisha exhaled — a long, slow breath that seemed to carry something heavy with it. "Thanks," she said, and for the first time all day, she almost meant it.

Say: "Both exchanges are technically dialogue. But Exchange A tells us nothing except that two people said hello. Exchange B tells us a story. Something happened. There is tension. We learn about both characters without a single description of their appearance or a single 'she felt' sentence."

Ask: "What do we learn about Jordan and Keisha from Exchange B?" (Something happened that kept Keisha from practice. Jordan covered for her. There is a secret between them. Keisha is struggling — the pulled sleeves, the exhale. Jordan is loyal but uncomfortable — the hesitation.)

Say: "In seventh grade, every line of dialogue needs to earn its place. If dialogue does not reveal character, create tension, or move the story forward, cut it. Today we are going to learn how to write dialogue that works hard, and how to combine it with pacing and description to create scenes the reader can see, hear, and feel."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach purposeful dialogue techniques, pacing through expansion and compression, and descriptive precision with sensory language.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Dialogue tag:** The phrase that identifies who is speaking ("said Jordan," "she whispered," "he asked"); should be unobtrusive — "said" and "asked" are almost always sufficient
- **Action beat:** A brief description of a character's action placed near dialogue to identify the speaker and add visual detail, replacing a dialogue tag ("Jordan dropped his backpack on the bench.")
- **Expansion:** The narrative technique of slowing down time to describe an important moment in detail, stretching seconds into paragraphs
- **Compression:** The narrative technique of speeding up time by summarizing less important events in a sentence or two

### Instruction Notes

**Dialogue Dos and Don'ts:**

Write on chart paper:

| DO | DON'T |
|----|-------|
| Use "said" and "asked" — they are invisible to the reader | Overuse fancy dialogue tags ("exclaimed," "retorted," "queried," "opined") |
| Use action beats to break up dialogue and show character | Have characters talk in a vacuum — anchor dialogue in the physical world |
| Make each character sound different — speech patterns, word choice, sentence length | Make all characters sound the same |
| Let subtext do the work — what characters do NOT say is powerful | Have characters state their emotions in dialogue ("I am so angry right now!") |
| Start dialogue in the middle of the conversation — skip the greetings | Begin every conversation with "Hi" and end it with "Bye" |
| Use dialogue to create tension, reveal secrets, or change the direction of the scene | Use dialogue just to fill space or deliver information the reader already knows |

**Punctuation Refresher:**

Say: "Let me remind you of the three rules for dialogue punctuation that trip students up most often."

Write on chart paper:

1. **Comma before the tag:** "I already told you," she said. (Comma inside the quotation marks.)
2. **New speaker, new paragraph:** Every time the speaker changes, start a new paragraph — even if the dialogue is only one word.
3. **Action beats use periods, not commas:** Jordan dropped his backpack. "You were not at practice today." (Period after the action beat, not a comma, because "Jordan dropped his backpack" is not a dialogue tag.)

**The Pacing Toolkit:**

Write on chart paper:

| Pacing Move | When to Use It | How It Sounds |
|-------------|---------------|---------------|
| **Expand** (slow motion) | The most important moment in the scene — the climax, the decision, the emotional turning point | Long sentences, sensory details from multiple senses, internal thoughts, moment-by-moment description |
| **Compress** (fast forward) | Travel, routine events, transitions between important scenes | Short sentences, summary, time markers ("Three days later," "The rest of the afternoon passed") |
| **Real time** (normal pace) | Dialogue scenes, action sequences | Mix of dialogue, action beats, and brief description; sentences match the speed of events |

Say: "Think of pacing like a camera in a movie. Expansion is the slow-motion close-up. Compression is the montage. Real time is the regular shot. The best stories use all three."

### Model Text / Worked Example

Display the following model narrative scene:

> **The Audition**
>
> The drama room smelled like sawdust and old velvet. Ava sat in the third row of folding chairs, script balanced on her knee, mouthing the words she had memorized weeks ago. Around her, other students flipped through their pages with the frantic energy of people who had started preparing that morning.
>
> "Number fourteen. Ava Chen."
>
> She stood. The script slid off her knee and hit the floor with a slap that seemed to echo off every wall in the room. She picked it up, smoothed the pages against her thigh, and walked to the front. The stage was just a rectangle of tape on the linoleum floor — no lights, no curtain, no audience except Ms. Torres sitting behind a folding table with a pencil and a clipboard.
>
> "Whenever you are ready," Ms. Torres said, not looking up.
>
> Ava opened her mouth. Nothing came out.
>
> Not silence, exactly — she could hear her own breathing, the hum of the fluorescent lights, the distant percussion of a basketball in the gymnasium two hallways over. But the words that had lived in her throat for weeks, the words she had whispered to her bedroom ceiling at midnight and shouted into the wind on her walk home from school, had vanished. Her mind was a blank stage.
>
> Three seconds. That was all. Three seconds of emptiness.
>
> Then something shifted — not in the room, but in her. She stopped trying to remember the first line and instead remembered why she had chosen this monologue in the first place. It was a speech about a girl who refuses to be invisible. Ava had read it for the first time in the school library on a Tuesday in October, and something in the character's voice had reached through the page and pressed against the inside of her ribs like a second heartbeat.
>
> She did not start with the first line. She started with the third — the one that mattered.
>
> "You think I came here to ask permission?"
>
> Her voice cracked on the word "permission," and she did not care. The words were coming now, and they were not recited; they were spoken. Ms. Torres looked up from her clipboard. The pencil stopped moving.
>
> The rest of the monologue took two minutes. It felt like ten seconds.
>
> When she finished, the room was quiet. Ms. Torres wrote something on her clipboard, then set the pencil down and folded her hands. "Thank you, Ava. That was not what I expected."
>
> Ava did not know if that was good or bad. She walked back to her seat, sat down, and realized her hands were shaking. She folded them in her lap and held them still.

Say: "Let's analyze pacing, dialogue, and description in this scene."

Use three highlighter colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow) — Expansion:** Highlight the expanded moment — the three seconds of silence before Ava speaks. Notice how the writer stretches three seconds across an entire paragraph with sensory detail (breathing, fluorescent hum, distant basketball) and internal thought (blank stage metaphor).
- **Color 2 (green) — Compression:** Highlight the compressed moment — "The rest of the monologue took two minutes. It felt like ten seconds." The actual performance is compressed into one sentence because the climax was the moment of deciding to speak, not the performance itself.
- **Color 3 (blue) — Dialogue and action beats:** Highlight the dialogue and note: Ms. Torres's dialogue is sparse and professional (reveals her character). Ava's one line — "You think I came here to ask permission?" — is the most important sentence in the story. Action beats replace most dialogue tags ("Ms. Torres said, not looking up" — the action beat tells us she is businesslike; "Ms. Torres looked up from her clipboard" — this tells us Ava has captured her attention).

Ask: "Why does the writer expand the three seconds of silence but compress the two-minute performance?" (Because the story is about the courage to start, not the performance itself. The turning point — the decision — deserves expansion. The outcome is less important.)

Ask: "How does dialogue work in this scene?" (It is minimal but every line matters. Ms. Torres's "Whenever you are ready" creates pressure. Ava's "You think I came here to ask permission?" is the character breaking through her fear. Ms. Torres's "That was not what I expected" creates ambiguity — the reader does not know if it is praise or criticism, just like Ava does not know.)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively write a scene that combines dialogue, pacing (expansion and compression), and sensory description.

**Activity: Expanding a Moment**

Say: "Let's write an expanded moment together. The scenario: A student is about to open an envelope that contains the results of whether they were accepted to a competitive summer program. The entire scene covers about 15 seconds."

**Step 1 — Identify what to expand and what to compress:**

- Compress: How the student got the envelope (one sentence).
- Expand: The moment of opening it (multiple sentences with sensory detail and internal thought).
- Real time: Any dialogue before or after.

**Step 2 — Draft the compressed setup:**

> The envelope had been sitting on the kitchen counter since 3:15, when Dayo's mother had set it there without a word and gone back to washing dishes.

**Step 3 — Add dialogue (real time):**

> "You can open it whenever you want," her mother said, still facing the sink. "No pressure."
>
> Dayo picked it up. "Then why are you washing the same plate for the fourth time?"
>
> Her mother laughed — a short, nervous sound — and set the plate in the drying rack. "Fair point."

**Step 4 — Expand the opening of the envelope:**

> Dayo turned the envelope over. Her name was printed in the center, perfectly aligned, and in the upper left corner was the program's logo — a small blue compass rose that she had stared at on the website so many times she could draw it from memory. The paper was thick and cream-colored, the kind of paper people use when the contents matter. She slid her finger under the flap, feeling the resistance of the glue, hearing the small ripping sound that seemed unreasonably loud in the quiet kitchen. The folded letter inside was a single page. She pulled it out, and before she could unfold it, she noticed that her hands had begun to tremble.
>
> She unfolded the page. The first word was "Congratulations."

Say: "Count the senses in the expanded moment." (Sight: envelope details, compass logo, cream paper. Touch: thick paper, resistance of glue, trembling hands. Sound: ripping sound. The reader experiences the moment as slowly and intensely as Dayo does.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write a scene that combines dialogue, pacing, and sensory description.

Using the narrative you began in Lesson 7 (or choose a new prompt), write ONE complete scene (not the whole story — one scene) that includes:

New prompts (if needed):
1. A character confronts a friend about a broken promise.
2. A character performs in front of an audience for the first time.
3. A character gets lost in an unfamiliar place and must find their way.

**Task:** Write a scene of three to five paragraphs that includes:
- At least one exchange of dialogue (correctly punctuated) using action beats
- One expanded moment (slow down an important moment with sensory details from at least two senses)
- One compressed section (summarize less important events in one or two sentences)
- Precise descriptive language — no vague words like "nice," "good," "bad," or "stuff"

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write a scene with dialogue, one expanded moment, and one compressed section as described.
**Stretch:** Write a scene that meets on-level requirements AND includes internal thought from the point-of-view character, at least one metaphor or simile, and sensory details from at least three senses.
**Challenge:** Write a scene that meets stretch requirements AND uses subtext in the dialogue (characters say one thing but mean another — the reader can infer the true meaning from context and action beats).

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.3b) Identify the pacing technique in this sentence and explain its effect: "The summer before seventh grade passed the way summers do when you are not paying attention — in a blur of late mornings, sprinkler water, and popsicle-stained afternoons." (Expected: Compression. The writer summarizes an entire summer in one sentence, creating a feeling of time moving quickly and pleasantly. The sensory details [sprinkler water, popsicle stains] make the compression feel rich despite its brevity.)
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1e) Write a concluding sentence for an argument about whether schools should have therapy dogs. The sentence should synthesize the argument, not summarize it. (Expected: "A school that welcomes a therapy dog is a school that recognizes what research has proven: learning does not begin with a textbook — it begins with a student who feels safe.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Write a two-sentence expanded moment for the following situation: A student reaches the top of a climbing wall and looks down for the first time.

Your two sentences must include at least one sensory detail and must slow down the moment (not just report what happened).

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
Answers will vary. Strong example: "Her fingers found the final handhold — a rough, chalky lip of plastic bolted to the plywood — and she pulled herself level with the top of the wall. Below, the gym floor stretched out like a map drawn at the wrong scale, the crash mats impossibly small, and the faces looking up at her were bright, open circles that she could not quite resolve into people she recognized."

**Mastery = The response (1) slows down the moment rather than summarizing it, (2) includes at least one sensory detail, and (3) uses precise language (not "it was really high" or "she felt scared").**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the expanded moment in the model text (Ava's three seconds of silence) and count the sensory details together
- Practice with a simpler moment: "A student takes a bite of something they have never tasted before." Model: "The flavor hit the back of her tongue first — sharp, almost electric, like biting into a lemon that had been soaked in ginger." Then have the student try another moment
- Provide the frame: "She felt/heard/saw ___, and the detail that struck her most was ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9, which focuses on revision and editing across all three writing types
- Encourage the student to continue drafting their narrative, incorporating expanded moments, compression, and purposeful dialogue

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Dialogue-Only Story:** Write a one-page story using ONLY dialogue — no narration, no description, no action beats. The reader must be able to understand the characters, conflict, and resolution entirely from what the characters say. After writing, reflect: What was hardest about this constraint? What does it reveal about the power of dialogue?
2. **Pacing Analysis:** Choose a chapter or scene from a novel you are currently reading. Identify one expanded moment and one compressed section. Write a brief analysis: Why did the author choose to slow down here and speed up there? How do these pacing choices affect the reader's experience?
3. **Sensory Revision:** Take a page of your narrative draft and highlight every sensory detail. Which senses are represented? Which are missing? Add at least two details from underrepresented senses (most students rely heavily on sight — try adding sound, touch, taste, or smell).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Dialogue is where many seventh graders' writing either comes alive or falls flat. The most common errors: (1) Characters who all sound the same — coach students to give each character distinct speech patterns, vocabulary, and rhythm. (2) Dialogue that delivers information the reader already knows — remind students that dialogue should reveal, not repeat. (3) Overuse of fancy dialogue tags — "said" is almost always the right choice because it is invisible. Action beats can replace tags entirely.
- Pacing is a sophisticated skill that takes time to develop. The key concept is that importance determines pace, not chronology. The most important three seconds of a story might take a full paragraph; the least important three days might take a sentence. When conferencing, ask: "What is the most important moment in your scene? Is it getting the most words?"
- Sensory language should feel natural, not forced. Discourage the "checklist" approach (one detail from each sense per paragraph). Instead, ask: "What would your character notice in this moment? What would stand out?" A nervous character might notice small sounds; a hungry character might notice smells; a character entering an unfamiliar place might notice visual details.
- The model text about Ava's audition is intentionally literary. Some students may feel intimidated by it. Reassure them that they are working toward this level of craft, not expected to achieve it immediately. Celebrate any attempt at expansion, subtext, or purposeful pacing.
- Some students may struggle with dialogue punctuation. This is a mechanics issue, not a writing issue — address it during editing (Lesson 9) rather than interrupting the creative flow during drafting.

## Rubric / Answer Key

### Independent Practice — Scene Rubric

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Dialogue** | Dialogue sounds natural; advances plot or reveals character; uses action beats; correctly punctuated | Dialogue present but feels stiff or purposeless; some punctuation errors | No dialogue, or dialogue is "Hi"/"Bye" exchanges that add nothing |
| **Pacing (Expansion)** | One moment is clearly expanded with sensory detail and/or internal thought; time slows effectively | An attempt at expansion but lacks sensory detail or does not slow the pace effectively | No expansion; events are listed at uniform speed |
| **Pacing (Compression)** | Less important events are compressed into one or two sentences; the compression feels deliberate | Some compression but it is unclear whether it was deliberate or the writer simply ran out of ideas | No compression; every event receives equal attention |
| **Description** | Precise language throughout; sensory details from at least two senses; no vague words | Some precise language but mixed with vague descriptions; sensory details present but limited | Vague, generic language ("it was nice," "she felt bad"); no sensory details |
| **Overall Craft** | Scene has a clear arc; techniques work together to create an immersive reading experience | Scene has direction but techniques feel disconnected or forced | Scene lacks direction; techniques are absent or minimal |
