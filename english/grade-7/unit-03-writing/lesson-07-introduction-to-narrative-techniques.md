# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 7

## Topic
Introduction to Narrative Techniques

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Establish context and point of view (first person or third person limited) to engage and orient the reader
- Introduce a narrator and/or characters using indirect characterization (showing through actions, thoughts, and dialogue rather than telling)
- Use transition words, phrases, and clauses to convey sequence and signal shifts in time, setting, or mood

## Prerequisites
- W.6.3: Experience writing narratives with engaging openings, descriptive details, and meaningful conclusions
- W.6.3a: Ability to establish a narrator, characters, and situation using a narrative hook
- W.6.3b: Familiarity with show-don't-tell, dialogue, and pacing techniques
- Understanding of story elements: character, setting, conflict, rising action, climax, resolution

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model narrative text (provided below)
- Narrative Planning Map template (described below)
- Point of View Reference (described below)
- Highlighters (three colors)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review Grade 6 narrative skills and introduce the concept of point of view as a deliberate authorial choice.

**Activity: Whose Story Is This?**

Display the following two openings for the same story:

**Version A (First Person):** "I pressed my back against the cold metal of the locker and tried to make myself invisible. The hallway was loud — sneakers squeaking, voices overlapping, someone's phone blasting music two aisles over — but all I could hear was the drumbeat of my own heart."

**Version B (Third Person Limited):** "Mina pressed her back against the cold metal of the locker and tried to make herself invisible. The hallway was loud — sneakers squeaking, voices overlapping, someone's phone blasting music two aisles over — but all she could hear was the drumbeat of her own heart."

Say: "These two versions describe the same moment with the same details. The only difference is point of view. Version A uses first person — the narrator IS the character, using 'I.' Version B uses third person limited — a narrator outside the story who can see into ONE character's thoughts and feelings, using 'she.'"

Ask: "Which version feels more immediate and personal? Which feels more like a movie camera following the character?" (First person feels more personal and intimate; third person limited creates a slight distance that can make the reader feel like an observer.)

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned how to start stories with hooks and establish characters and settings. In seventh grade, you are going to make deliberate choices about point of view, and you are going to develop your characters with more sophisticated techniques. Today we focus on two big skills: choosing and maintaining a point of view, and introducing characters through their actions, thoughts, and dialogue — not through descriptions that read like a police report."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach point of view selection, indirect characterization, and narrative transitions for sequence and shifts.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Point of view:** The perspective from which a story is told; determines what the reader can see, hear, know, and feel
- **First person:** The narrator is a character in the story and uses "I" and "me"; the reader experiences events through this character's consciousness
- **Third person limited:** The narrator is outside the story but has access to one character's thoughts and feelings; uses "he," "she," or "they"
- **Indirect characterization:** Revealing a character's personality through their actions, dialogue, thoughts, and reactions rather than directly stating traits

### Instruction Notes

**Point of View Reference:**

Write on chart paper:

| Point of View | Pronouns | Reader Access | Best For |
|---------------|----------|---------------|----------|
| **First Person** | I, me, my, we | Only what the narrator sees, thinks, feels, and knows | Emotional intimacy; unreliable narrators; stories where the narrator's internal experience is central |
| **Third Person Limited** | He, she, they | One character's thoughts and feelings, plus external observations | Maintaining some distance while still allowing internal access; stories with multiple scenes |

Say: "The most important rule of point of view is consistency. If you start in first person, stay in first person. If you are writing third person limited focused on one character, do not suddenly jump into another character's thoughts. Shifting point of view without purpose confuses the reader."

**Indirect Characterization:**

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned to show instead of tell. In seventh grade, we are going to sharpen that skill specifically for introducing characters. A common mistake is to introduce a character with a description that reads like a dating profile: 'Marcus was thirteen years old, tall for his age, with brown eyes and a friendly smile. He was nervous but brave.' That is telling. Let me show you the difference."

Write on chart paper:

| TELLING (Weak) | SHOWING (Strong) |
|----------------|-----------------|
| Marcus was nervous. | Marcus wiped his palms on his jeans for the third time and checked his phone — 2:47, same as it had been thirty seconds ago. |
| She was kind and caring. | She slid her own granola bar across the table without a word, pretending not to notice when the younger boy's hands shook as he unwrapped it. |
| He was angry. | The classroom door hit the wall hard enough to rattle the whiteboard markers in their tray. Jamal did not sit down. He stood at his desk, jaw clenched, staring at the space above the teacher's head. |

Say: "Notice: in every 'showing' example, the reader FEELS the emotion because they SEE the character's behavior. You never have to say 'nervous,' 'kind,' or 'angry' — the details do the work."

**Four Methods of Indirect Characterization (SAID):**

Write on chart paper:

- **S — Speech:** What the character says and how they say it reveals personality.
- **A — Actions:** What the character does — especially small, specific actions — reveals who they are.
- **I — Internal thoughts:** What the character thinks (accessible in first person and third person limited) reveals their values, fears, and desires.
- **D — Details of appearance and environment:** What the character wears, carries, or surrounds themselves with can reveal personality without stating it.

**Narrative Transitions for Sequence and Shifts:**

Say: "In seventh-grade narratives, you need to control time. You need to speed up, slow down, jump forward, and flash back. Transitions tell the reader when these shifts are happening."

Write on chart paper:

| Purpose | Transition Examples |
|---------|-------------------|
| Moving forward in time | Later that afternoon, the next morning, three days later, by the time the sun set |
| Slowing down (expanding a moment) | In that instant, time seemed to stretch, the next few seconds felt like hours |
| Speeding up (compressing time) | The rest of the week passed in a blur, the hours bled together, before she knew it |
| Flashback | She remembered the last time, years ago, it was the same feeling she had felt when |
| Shifting setting | Across town, meanwhile back at, when they finally reached |
| Shifting mood | But something had changed, the air felt different now, without warning |

### Model Text / Worked Example

Read the following model narrative opening aloud. Display it so the student can follow along.

> **The Cartographer's Daughter**
>
> The map was wrong, and Lucia knew it.
>
> She stood at the edge of the canyon rim with the hand-drawn survey spread across a flat rock, pinned at the corners by four small stones, and traced the contour lines with her index finger. According to her father's map — the one he had spent three weeks hiking, measuring, and sketching by hand — the trail should descend gradually along the north face, switch back twice, and emerge at the river crossing a half mile downstream. But the trail she was looking at did none of those things. It dropped straight down a near-vertical wall of red sandstone, with no switchbacks, no gradual descent, and no obvious path to the river at all.
>
> Lucia pulled her water bottle from the side pocket of her backpack and took a long drink, buying herself time to think. Her father would have known what to do. He had spent twenty years mapping backcountry trails for the Forest Service, carrying nothing but a compass, a sketchpad, and an inexplicable confidence that the landscape would reveal its secrets to anyone patient enough to look. He had taught Lucia to read contour lines before she could read chapter books, to orient a compass before she learned to ride a bicycle. "The map is a conversation," he used to say, tapping the paper with his pencil. "The land talks. You listen. Then you draw what it told you."
>
> But her father was not here. He was in a hospital bed forty miles east, with a broken leg and a collection of monitors beeping in steady rhythm, and Lucia was standing at a canyon rim with a map she did not trust and a decision she was not sure she could make.
>
> She folded the map carefully — the way he had taught her, along the original creases, so the paper would not weaken — and slid it into the waterproof sleeve in her pack. Then she pulled out her own sketchpad and a freshly sharpened pencil. If the map was wrong, she would make a new one.

Say: "Let's analyze the techniques this writer uses."

Use three highlighter colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** Point of view markers — third person limited focused on Lucia. The reader can access Lucia's thoughts ("Lucia knew it," "buying herself time to think," "a decision she was not sure she could make") but no one else's.
- **Color 2 (green):** Indirect characterization — What do we learn about Lucia's father without being told directly? He is meticulous (three weeks of work), experienced (twenty years), patient ("the land talks, you listen"), and loving (taught Lucia skills from a young age). What do we learn about Lucia? She is observant (notices the map is wrong), resourceful (decides to make a new map), and emotionally connected to her father (folds the map the way he taught her).
- **Color 3 (blue):** Transitions that signal shifts — "But" signals the contrast between the map and reality. "Her father would have known" shifts to flashback. "But her father was not here" shifts back to the present. "Then she pulled out her own sketchpad" signals a turning point.

Ask: "Does the writer ever say 'Lucia was brave' or 'Her father was experienced'?" (No — the reader figures this out from actions, thoughts, and details.)

Ask: "How does the writer use time?" (The present moment at the canyon is expanded with detail. The father's twenty years of experience is compressed into a few sentences. The memory of his teaching is a brief flashback.)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively plan a narrative and draft an opening that establishes context, point of view, and character through indirect characterization.

**Activity: Planning and Drafting a Narrative Opening**

Say: "Let's plan a narrative together. Our prompt is: Write a story about a character who must make a quick decision in an unexpected situation."

**Step 1 — Complete the Narrative Planning Map:**

```
NARRATIVE PLANNING MAP

Point of View: First Person
Main Character: Deshawn, a seventh grader who plays trumpet in the school jazz band
Key Character Traits (to show, not tell): Cautious, loyal, self-doubting
Setting: Behind the school auditorium, 15 minutes before the winter concert
Conflict: Deshawn discovers that the sheet music for the trumpet solo has been
  rearranged — someone changed the melody — and he must decide whether to play the
  altered version or improvise from memory

SEQUENCE:
Beginning: Deshawn warms up behind the auditorium; discovers the altered sheet music
Rising Action Event 1: Tries to figure out who changed it and why
Rising Action Event 2: Considers telling the band director (but the concert starts in minutes)
Climax: Steps onto stage; decides to play from memory, trusting what he knows
Resolution: The performance is imperfect but genuine; afterward, he learns the truth
  about who changed the music and why
```

**Step 2 — Draft the opening together (first person):**

Guide collaboratively:

> I heard the wrong note before I played it.
>
> That probably does not make sense, so let me explain. I was sitting on the concrete steps behind the auditorium, trumpet in my lap, running through the solo one last time before the winter concert. The December air was sharp enough to make my lips sting, and my breath came out in small white clouds that dissolved before they reached the music stand. I had played this solo a hundred times — in my bedroom, in the practice room, in the shower humming along with the water — and my fingers knew the notes the way they knew the combination to my locker. So when I glanced down at the sheet music propped on the stand and saw a B-flat where a C-natural should have been, it was like seeing a stranger's face in my bathroom mirror. Wrong. Just wrong.

Analyze together: "What do we know about Deshawn from this opening?" (He is careful and well-prepared — practiced a hundred times. He is observant — noticed the wrong note immediately. He has a sense of humor — "that probably does not make sense." He feels a personal connection to the music — compared knowing the notes to knowing his locker combination.)

"What point of view is this?" (First person — "I heard," "my lap," "I had played.") "What transitions signal time and sequence?" ("Before the winter concert" establishes when. "So when I glanced down" signals a shift from the routine to the discovery.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently plan a narrative and draft an opening that establishes context, point of view, and character through indirect characterization.

Choose ONE of the following prompts (or propose your own):

1. Write a story about a character who finds something that does not belong to them and must decide what to do with it.
2. Write a story about a character whose plan goes completely wrong and who must adapt.
3. Write a story about a character who overhears a conversation they were not meant to hear.
4. Write a story about a character who takes on a challenge everyone else has given up on.

**Task:**
1. Complete a Narrative Planning Map (point of view, character, traits to show, setting, conflict, and sequence).
2. Choose first person or third person limited and explain your choice in one sentence.
3. Draft your narrative opening (two to three paragraphs) that establishes context and point of view, introduces the character through indirect characterization (at least two SAID techniques), and uses at least two transitions to control time or signal shifts.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete the planning map and draft a two-paragraph opening using one SAID technique and one transition.
**Stretch:** Complete the planning map and draft a three-paragraph opening using at least two SAID techniques, two transitions, and at least one "showing" detail that reveals a character trait without stating it.
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task, then continue drafting through the first rising action event. Maintain consistent point of view throughout and use at least one flashback or time compression.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.3d) Rewrite this telling sentence as a showing sentence: "The room was old and creepy." Use at least two sensory details from different senses. (Expected: Something like "Dust motes drifted through the single shaft of light from the cracked window, and the floorboards groaned under her weight like an old man settling into a chair. The air tasted stale, as if the room had been holding its breath for years.")
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.2a) Write an informative focus statement for this topic: "How thunderstorms form." Include a preview of the organizational approach. (Expected: "Thunderstorms develop through a three-stage process — the cumulus stage, the mature stage, and the dissipating stage — each driven by the interaction of rising warm air, moisture, and atmospheric instability.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following sentence and identify which SAID technique (Speech, Actions, Internal thoughts, or Details) is being used to characterize the character. Then state what trait or quality the sentence reveals.

"Priya arrived at the group meeting ten minutes early, her color-coded notes arranged in a binder with tabbed dividers, a backup pen clipped to the front pocket of her backpack."

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
Technique: **D — Details** (of appearance and environment). The organized notes, color coding, tabbed dividers, and backup pen reveal that Priya is highly organized, prepared, and possibly perfectionistic or anxious about being caught unprepared.

(Also acceptable: **A — Actions** — arriving early is an action that reveals preparedness. If the student identifies either A or D with a valid explanation, award mastery.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the SAID technique AND names a character trait that the details reveal, with a brief explanation of how the details convey that trait.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the SAID chart and review each technique with the examples from Direct Instruction
- Provide three sentences, each using a different technique, and have the student label them
- Ask: "What does this character DO, SAY, THINK, or CARRY that tells us something about who they are?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8, which focuses on dialogue, pacing, and description
- Encourage the student to continue drafting their narrative, applying indirect characterization and consistent point of view

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Character Study:** Choose a character from a novel or short story you have read. Find three examples of indirect characterization (one speech, one action, one detail). For each, explain what trait is being revealed and how.
2. **Point of View Experiment:** Take your narrative opening and rewrite it in the other point of view (if you wrote in first person, rewrite in third person limited, or vice versa). Write a brief reflection: How did the change affect the tone and emotional impact of the opening?
3. **The Invisible Backstory:** Write a one-paragraph backstory for your main character that the reader will never see. Include three details about the character's past that explain why they behave the way they do in the story. Then check: Does your narrative SHOW these backstory elements without explicitly stating them?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Point of view consistency is a major challenge for seventh graders. Students writing in first person may accidentally shift to third person mid-paragraph, or students writing in third person limited may slip into omniscience (knowing what multiple characters are thinking). During conferencing, simply ask: "Whose head are we in right now?" If the answer shifts, the student needs to revise.
- Indirect characterization is harder than it sounds. Students are accustomed to telling ("She was brave") because it is faster and requires less craft. Celebrate every instance of showing you see in student writing, and use the specific SAID framework to give concrete feedback: "I see you used the A technique here — her actions show me she is determined. Can you add a D — a detail about what she is carrying or wearing — that reinforces that trait?"
- The model text about Lucia is intentionally longer and more literary than the Grade 6 narrative models. Seventh graders should be stretching toward more complex, layered narratives. But do not penalize students who write shorter openings — quality of technique matters more than length.
- Some students may want to write in second person ("You walk into the room") or omniscient third person. For this unit, limit choices to first person and third person limited, which are the most common and most developmentally appropriate for Grade 7.
- The Narrative Planning Map is a scaffold, not a rigid blueprint. Stories evolve as they are written. If a student's narrative diverges from the map, that is healthy — it means they are responding to the story's internal logic. The map's purpose is to prevent the "I started writing and then I did not know what happened next" problem.

## Rubric / Answer Key

### Independent Practice
**Sample Narrative Planning Map (Prompt 2 — plan goes wrong):**
- **Point of View:** Third person limited (focused on Elena)
- **Main Character:** Elena, a seventh grader who is organizing a surprise party for her best friend
- **Traits to show:** Determined, creative under pressure, secretly insecure about whether her friendships are solid
- **Setting:** Elena's apartment, Saturday afternoon, two hours before the party
- **Conflict:** Everything that can go wrong does: the cake collapses, the decorations fall, and two key friends cancel at the last minute
- **Sequence:** Beginning (Elena setting up, everything looks perfect), Rising Action 1 (cake disaster), Rising Action 2 (friends cancel, Elena questions whether anyone cares), Climax (considers calling the whole thing off, then hears a knock at the door), Resolution (the party is smaller and messier than planned but genuine; Elena realizes that the imperfect version was more honest than the perfect one would have been)

**Sample opening:**
> Elena had taped the last streamer to the ceiling when the cake made a sound that cakes are not supposed to make. A soft, wet sigh — like air escaping from a tire — followed by a slow, decisive lean to the left. She watched, one hand still pressed against the ceiling, as the top layer of the double-decker chocolate cake she had spent three hours assembling slid sideways, paused at a forty-five-degree angle as if considering its options, and then collapsed onto the kitchen counter in a landslide of frosting and crumbled sponge.
>
> For a full five seconds, Elena did not move. Then she lowered her hand from the ceiling, pulled a single candle from the wreckage, and set it upright on the counter like a flag planted on a defeated battlefield.
