# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 2

## Topic
Building Claims with Evidence and Reasoning

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Select evidence from credible sources and evaluate its relevance to a claim
- Use logical reasoning to explain how evidence supports a claim (the "so what?" connection)
- Apply cohesive devices (transitional words, phrases, and clauses) to clarify relationships among claims, reasons, and evidence

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 1: Understanding of claims, reasons, evidence, and counterclaims
- W.6.1b: Experience supporting claims with reasons and relevant evidence using the ICE pattern
- W.6.1c: Familiarity with transitional words and phrases for connecting ideas
- Ability to distinguish between fact and opinion

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Copy of the model body paragraphs (provided below)
- Evidence Evaluation checklist (described below)
- Transition Reference Chart (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the concept of evidence from Lesson 1 and introduce the idea that not all evidence is equally strong or credible.

**Activity: Strong or Weak?**

Display the following three pieces of "evidence" for the claim: "Schools should start later in the morning."

1. "My friend told me she is always tired in first period."
2. "A 2019 study by the American Academy of Pediatrics found that adolescents who started school after 8:30 a.m. slept an average of 34 additional minutes per night and showed a 4.5% improvement in grades."
3. "Everyone knows that teenagers need more sleep."

Say: "Rank these from strongest to weakest evidence. Then explain your ranking to a partner."

After 90 seconds, discuss: Evidence 2 is strongest because it comes from a credible, named source, includes specific data, and directly connects to the claim. Evidence 1 is weak because it is anecdotal — one person's experience does not prove a pattern. Evidence 3 is weakest because it is a vague generalization with no source ("everyone knows" is not evidence).

Say: "In seventh grade, the quality of your evidence matters as much as having evidence at all. Today we are going to learn how to choose strong, credible evidence and — just as importantly — how to explain exactly WHY your evidence proves your claim."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the criteria for credible evidence, the reasoning chain (evidence to claim connection), and cohesive transitions for argumentative writing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Credible source:** A source that is trustworthy, authoritative, and recognized in its field (e.g., research institutions, peer-reviewed studies, government agencies, established news organizations)
- **Reasoning:** The logical explanation that connects evidence to a claim; the "so what?" or "this matters because" part of the argument
- **Cohesion:** The quality of writing in which ideas flow logically and connect smoothly; achieved through transitions, repeated key terms, and clear logical relationships
- **Warrant:** The underlying assumption or principle that explains why the evidence supports the claim

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned the ICE pattern — Introduce, Cite, Explain. In seventh grade, we are going to strengthen the E — the Explain part. We call this the reasoning chain. It is not enough to drop a piece of evidence into your paragraph and expect the reader to figure out what it means. YOU have to do the thinking for the reader."

Write on chart paper:

**The Evidence-Reasoning Chain:**
1. **Introduce:** Set up the evidence with context. ("According to a study by...")
2. **Cite:** Present the specific evidence. ("...researchers found that students who...")
3. **Explain (Reasoning):** Answer THREE questions:
   - What does this evidence mean? (Interpretation)
   - How does it connect to my reason? (Connection)
   - Why does it matter for my claim? (Significance)

Say: "Watch me model this."

**Weak example (Grade 6 level):**
"Schools should start later. A study found that students who start school after 8:30 a.m. get more sleep. This is important because sleep is good."

**Strong example (Grade 7 level):**
"A 2019 study by the American Academy of Pediatrics found that adolescents who started school after 8:30 a.m. slept an average of 34 additional minutes per night and showed a 4.5% improvement in grades. This finding is significant because it demonstrates a direct, measurable link between start time and academic performance — not a vague correlation, but a specific outcome that benefits every student in the building. When schools adjust their schedules to align with adolescent biology, the result is not merely better-rested students but demonstrably higher achievement."

Ask: "What makes the strong example better?" Guide students to notice: specific data, named source, three layers of explanation (what it means, how it connects, why it matters), and no vague phrases like "this is important."

**Transition Reference Chart:**

Write on chart paper:

| Purpose | Transition Words, Phrases, and Clauses |
|---------|----------------------------------------|
| Adding evidence | Furthermore, additionally, in addition, equally important, another compelling reason |
| Introducing evidence | According to, as demonstrated by, research conducted by ___ reveals, data from ___ shows |
| Explaining evidence | This finding is significant because, this demonstrates that, this evidence reveals, the implication is clear |
| Contrasting / counterclaims | However, on the other hand, while some argue that, despite this objection, although opponents contend |
| Concluding | Therefore, consequently, as a result, the evidence makes clear that, taken together |

Say: "These are not just decorations. Transitions tell the reader HOW your ideas connect. 'Furthermore' signals you are adding another reason. 'However' signals you are about to address an opposing view. Use them deliberately."

### Model Text / Worked Example

Display the following two model body paragraphs:

> **Body Paragraph 1 (Reason + Evidence + Reasoning):**
>
> The most compelling reason to extend the school year is that a longer academic calendar closes achievement gaps between students from different socioeconomic backgrounds. Research conducted by the RAND Corporation examined 30 school districts that added 25 or more instructional days to their calendars and found that students from low-income families gained an average of 20 additional percentile points on standardized reading assessments over a three-year period, compared to a 7-point gain for their higher-income peers. This disparity in gains is critical because it reveals that the students who benefit most from additional learning time are precisely those who lose the most during long summer breaks — a phenomenon researchers call "summer slide." When low-income students lack access to books, academic programs, and enrichment activities during the summer, they fall behind their peers at a rate of approximately two months of learning per year. A longer school year does not just add days to the calendar; it interrupts the cycle of inequity that summer break perpetuates.
>
> **Body Paragraph 2 (Counterclaim + Response):**
>
> Opponents of an extended school year frequently argue that students and teachers need the summer break to rest, recharge, and pursue interests outside of academics. While this concern reflects a genuine value — rest is essential for both physical and mental health — it rests on a false assumption: that the only alternative to the current 180-day calendar is year-round school with no breaks at all. In reality, most extended-year models redistribute breaks throughout the calendar rather than eliminating them. The Wake County, North Carolina school district, for example, operates a 210-day calendar with four three-week intersession breaks spaced evenly through the year. Teachers in the district report similar levels of job satisfaction to their peers on traditional calendars, and students still receive ample time for rest and non-academic activities. The question is not whether students deserve breaks — they do — but whether concentrating all of that break time into a single ten-week summer serves students well. The evidence suggests it does not.

Ask: "In the first paragraph, how many layers of reasoning follow the evidence?" (Three: interpretation of the disparity, explanation of summer slide, and significance for equity.)

Ask: "In the second paragraph, how does the writer treat the counterclaim? Is it dismissed or taken seriously?" (Taken seriously — the writer calls it "a genuine value" before explaining why it rests on a "false assumption" and providing counter-evidence from Wake County.)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively strengthen a weak body paragraph by adding credible evidence, reasoning, and cohesive transitions.

**Activity: Paragraph Surgery**

Display the following weak body paragraph:

> Schools should have more recess time. Kids need to move around. Studies show that recess is good for students. When kids get more recess, they do better. This proves that recess is important.

Say: "This paragraph has the right idea, but it is weak. Let's perform surgery on it — together, we are going to make it a strong seventh-grade argument paragraph."

Work through these steps collaboratively:

1. **Identify the reason:** "Physical activity during the school day improves academic focus." (Restate it precisely.)
2. **Add credible evidence:** "A study published in the journal Pediatrics followed 11,000 third through sixth graders and found that students who received at least 15 minutes of daily recess scored an average of 8 points higher on classroom behavior assessments and showed significantly better attention during afternoon instructional periods."
3. **Add reasoning (three layers):** "This finding demonstrates that recess is not merely a break from learning — it is a catalyst for it. When students are confined to their desks for extended periods, their capacity for sustained attention diminishes, leading to restlessness and off-task behavior. By providing regular physical movement, schools create the neurological conditions necessary for students to absorb and retain new information."
4. **Add transitions:** Review where transitions connect ideas and add them.

Draft the revised paragraph together:

> The most important reason schools should increase recess time is that physical activity during the school day directly improves students' ability to focus and learn. A study published in the journal Pediatrics followed 11,000 third through sixth graders and found that students who received at least 15 minutes of daily recess scored an average of 8 points higher on classroom behavior assessments and showed significantly better attention during afternoon instructional periods. This finding demonstrates that recess is not merely a break from learning — it is a catalyst for it. When students are confined to their desks for extended periods, their capacity for sustained attention diminishes, leading to restlessness and off-task behavior. By providing regular opportunities for physical movement, schools create the neurological conditions necessary for students to absorb and retain new information effectively.

Ask: "How many improvements did we make?" Count them together: precise reason statement, credible source, specific data, three layers of reasoning, transitions, formal style.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write a body paragraph with credible evidence, a reasoning chain, and cohesive transitions.

Using the claim and Claim-Counterclaim-Evidence chart you developed in Lesson 1 (or choose a new topic below), write ONE complete body paragraph for your argument.

If you need a new topic, choose from:
1. Should schools require uniforms for all students?
2. Should the voting age be lowered to 16?
3. Should plastic water bottles be banned in schools?

**Task:** Write a body paragraph that includes:
- A clear topic sentence stating the reason
- At least one piece of evidence from a credible source (real or plausible)
- A reasoning chain that answers: What does it mean? How does it connect? Why does it matter?
- At least two cohesive transitions

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write one body paragraph with one piece of evidence and a reasoning chain as described.
**Stretch:** Write one body paragraph with two pieces of evidence, each with its own reasoning chain, connected by a transition.
**Challenge:** Write two body paragraphs — one developing a reason with evidence and reasoning, and one acknowledging a counterclaim and responding with evidence.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1c) Add an appropriate transition to connect these two sentences: "Regular exercise improves cardiovascular health. ___ it reduces stress and improves mood." (Expected: "Furthermore," "Additionally," "In addition," or "Equally important,")
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.3) Write two sentences that SHOW a character is excited without using the word "excited." Use at least one sensory detail. (Expected: Something like "Marcus burst through the front door and dropped his backpack on the floor without breaking stride. His words tumbled out so fast they overlapped, and his hands gestured wildly toward the letter clutched in his right fist.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following evidence and write a two-sentence reasoning chain that explains what it means and why it matters for the claim "Schools should offer free breakfast to all students."

Evidence: "A study by the Food Research and Action Center found that schools implementing universal free breakfast programs saw a 17% decrease in chronic absenteeism."

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
Answers will vary. Strong example: "This 17% decrease in chronic absenteeism demonstrates that hunger is not merely an inconvenience — it is a barrier that prevents students from physically being present in school. When schools remove that barrier by providing free breakfast, they ensure that students have the opportunity to learn every day, regardless of their family's financial situation."

**Mastery = Student provides a reasoning chain that (1) interprets what the evidence means and (2) connects it to the claim with a "why it matters" explanation. A response that simply restates the evidence ("This shows that free breakfast reduces absenteeism") does NOT meet mastery.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the three reasoning questions: What does it mean? How does it connect? Why does it matter?
- Practice with a simpler example: "Evidence: 90% of dentists recommend brushing twice a day. Claim: Students should brush their teeth before school." Walk through each reasoning question together
- Provide the sentence frame: "This finding is significant because it demonstrates that ___. The implication for [topic] is clear: ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3, where they will focus specifically on counterclaim strategies
- Encourage the student to draft a second body paragraph for their argument using the techniques learned today

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Source Credibility Investigation:** Choose a debatable topic and find three online sources that provide evidence related to it. Evaluate each source: Who wrote it? What organization published it? Is it biased? Would you use it in a formal argument? Write a brief credibility report for each.
2. **Reasoning Chain Challenge:** Take a body paragraph from a classmate (or from a published essay) and highlight the evidence in one color and the reasoning in another. Is the reasoning chain complete? If not, write the missing reasoning.
3. **Transition Transformation:** Take a paragraph you wrote in Lesson 1 and revise it by adding at least three cohesive transitions from the Transition Reference Chart. Read both versions aloud and describe how the transitions change the flow.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The reasoning chain is the most challenging concept in this lesson. Many students will write evidence and then immediately move to the next point without explaining what the evidence means. The "so what?" question is the key coaching move: after a student writes a piece of evidence, ask "So what? Why does this matter for your claim?" repeatedly until the reasoning is fully developed.
- Credible sources can be a sticking point because seventh graders may not have access to research databases. Teach them to look for: named organizations (universities, government agencies, established nonprofits), specific data (not vague claims), and publication dates. For classroom purposes, plausible evidence with logical reasoning is acceptable, but encourage specificity.
- The Paragraph Surgery activity in Guided Practice is designed to make the revision process concrete and visible. Students often cannot see what is wrong with weak writing until they watch it being transformed step by step.
- Formal style continues to be important. Watch for creeping informality — "kids" instead of "students," "a lot" instead of "significantly," "stuff" instead of specific nouns.
- The Transition Reference Chart should be posted or kept in students' writing folders for the remainder of the unit. Transitions are a tool, not a checklist — students should use them deliberately, not mechanically.

### Independent Practice
**Sample Body Paragraph (Topic 3 — plastic water bottles):**

"The primary reason schools should ban plastic water bottles is that single-use plastics create an enormous and unnecessary waste problem within school communities. According to the Container Recycling Institute, the average American middle school generates approximately 18,000 plastic water bottles per year, and fewer than 30% of those bottles are recycled. The remaining 70% — roughly 12,600 bottles per school — end up in landfills, where they will take an estimated 450 years to decompose. This volume of waste is staggering, particularly when the solution is straightforward: reusable water bottles and refilling stations eliminate the need for single-use plastic entirely. Furthermore, when students witness the daily accumulation of plastic waste in their own school, they internalize the message that disposability is acceptable — a lesson that contradicts the environmental stewardship most schools claim to value. Banning plastic bottles is not merely an environmental measure; it is an educational one, teaching students that convenience must be weighed against consequence."
