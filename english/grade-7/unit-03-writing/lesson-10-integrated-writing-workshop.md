# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 10

## Topic
Integrated Writing Workshop

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write about a single topic in all three modes: argumentative, informative, and narrative
- Identify how purpose and audience shape the structure, tone, evidence, and techniques of each writing mode
- Demonstrate control of the key skills from W.7.1, W.7.2, and W.7.3 in an integrated writing task

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-4: Argumentative writing (claims, evidence, counterclaims, essay structure)
- Lessons 5-6: Informative writing (organizational strategies, development, domain-specific vocabulary)
- Lessons 7-8: Narrative writing (point of view, characterization, dialogue, pacing, description)
- Lesson 9: Revision and editing strategies
- Writing portfolio with drafts from the unit

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Writing folders with all unit drafts
- Mode Comparison Reference (described below)
- Timer or clock
- Highlighters (three colors)
- Peer feedback forms (optional — described in Guided Practice)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate that the same topic can be approached through different writing modes, and that the mode changes everything about how the writing sounds and what it accomplishes.

**Activity: One Topic, Three Voices**

Display the following three passages, all about the same topic: school lunch.

**Argumentative voice:** "School districts should allocate a minimum of $5.00 per student meal because the current average of $1.30 is insufficient to provide nutritionally adequate lunches. Research by the School Nutrition Association demonstrates that districts spending less than $2.00 per meal serve lunches that fail to meet federal dietary guidelines for fruits, vegetables, and whole grains."

**Informative voice:** "The cost of a public school lunch in the United States varies significantly by district, ranging from $1.00 to $6.50 per meal. Factors that influence meal cost include geographic location, the size of the district, local food sourcing agreements, and whether the district operates its own kitchen facilities or contracts with external food service providers."

**Narrative voice:** "The line moved slowly, and by the time Marcus reached the counter, the only option left was a rectangle of pizza that had been sitting under the heat lamp long enough to develop a waxy sheen. He slid it onto his tray next to a bruised apple and a carton of milk, then carried the whole sad arrangement to the table by the window where his friends were already complaining about the same meal."

Say: "Same topic — school lunch. But each passage has a completely different purpose, tone, and structure. The argument takes a position and pushes for change. The informative text examines the facts without taking sides. The narrative puts you in a specific moment with a specific character."

Ask: "Could you combine all three in one piece of writing?" (No — each mode has a different purpose. But a writer who can work in all three modes has the flexibility to approach any topic from any angle.)

Say: "Today is your writing workshop. You are going to take a single topic and write about it in all three modes, demonstrating everything you have learned in this unit."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to approach a single topic from three perspectives and review the key structural elements of each mode.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Mode:** The type or form of writing, defined by its purpose — argumentative (to persuade), informative (to explain), narrative (to tell a story)
- **Purpose:** The reason a writer is writing — what they want to accomplish and how they want the reader to respond
- **Audience awareness:** The writer's understanding of who will read the writing and how that knowledge shapes choices about tone, vocabulary, structure, and detail
- **Versatility:** A writer's ability to shift effectively between different modes, tones, and styles depending on the task

### Instruction Notes

**Mode Comparison Reference:**

Write on chart paper:

| Element | Argumentative (W.7.1) | Informative (W.7.2) | Narrative (W.7.3) |
|---------|----------------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| **Purpose** | Persuade the reader to accept a claim | Help the reader understand a topic | Engage the reader in an experience |
| **Tone** | Confident, formal, authoritative | Objective, formal, explanatory | Varies — can be intimate, suspenseful, reflective |
| **Structure** | Claim, reasons, evidence, counterclaim, conclusion | Focus statement, organized body (definition/ classification/ comparison/ cause-effect), conclusion | Setting, character, conflict, rising action, climax, resolution |
| **Evidence** | Facts, statistics, expert opinions to support a position | Facts, definitions, details, examples to explain a topic | Sensory details, dialogue, actions, internal thoughts to create an experience |
| **Voice** | Third person, formal ("The evidence demonstrates...") | Third person, objective ("Research indicates...") | First or third person limited; personal, descriptive |
| **Key Grade 7 Skills** | Counterclaim acknowledgment, reasoning chains, cohesion | Organizational strategy, domain-specific vocabulary, development | Indirect characterization (SAID), expansion/compression, purposeful dialogue |

Say: "When you write about the same topic in three modes, the information might overlap, but the purpose, structure, and voice change completely. An argument about space exploration argues that we SHOULD fund it. An informative text about space exploration explains HOW it works. A narrative about space exploration puts you inside the experience of an astronaut."

**Integrated Writing Task Structure:**

Say: "Here is how today's workshop works. You will choose one topic from the list I provide. Then you will write three short pieces — one in each mode — about that topic."

Write on chart paper:

```
INTEGRATED WRITING WORKSHOP

Step 1: Choose your topic from the list.
Step 2: Write a short ARGUMENT about the topic (one strong paragraph
   with claim, evidence, reasoning, and a counterclaim sentence).
   Time: ~8 minutes.
Step 3: Write a short INFORMATIVE text about the topic (one paragraph
   with a focus statement, facts/details, and domain-specific vocabulary).
   Time: ~8 minutes.
Step 4: Write a short NARRATIVE connected to the topic (one to two
   paragraphs with character, setting, dialogue or sensory detail).
   Time: ~8 minutes.
Step 5: Write a brief reflection (3-4 sentences) comparing how the
   three modes changed your writing about the same topic.
   Time: ~3 minutes.
```

**Topic Options:**

Write on the board:
1. Technology in schools (tablets, AI, coding, etc.)
2. The environment and climate (pollution, conservation, renewable energy, etc.)
3. Sports and competition (youth sports, sportsmanship, team vs. individual, etc.)
4. Food and nutrition (school food, food deserts, cultural cuisine, healthy eating, etc.)
5. Student choice (propose your own topic — must be approved)

### Model Text / Worked Example

Say: "Let me model this quickly with the topic: public libraries."

**Argument (one paragraph):**
> Public libraries should receive increased municipal funding because they serve as essential community resources that extend far beyond book lending. According to the American Library Association, libraries provided over 300 million free educational programs in 2023, including literacy classes, job training, and technology access for underserved populations. This figure demonstrates that libraries function as de facto community centers that fill gaps left by other public services. While some critics argue that digital resources have made physical libraries obsolete, this position ignores the 26 million Americans who lack home internet access and depend on library computers for job applications, school assignments, and government services.

**Informative (one paragraph):**
> Public libraries in the United States operate under a tiered funding model that draws from three primary sources: local taxes, state allocations, and federal grants. Local property taxes account for approximately 85% of most public library budgets, which means that library funding varies dramatically by municipality — a library in a wealthy suburb may receive ten times the per-capita funding of a library in a rural or low-income urban area. State funding, which typically accounts for 7-10% of a library's budget, often comes with requirements tied to service hours, collection size, or participation in statewide lending networks. Federal funding through programs such as the Library Services and Technology Act (LSTA) provides targeted grants for specific initiatives, including digital literacy, early childhood reading, and services for people with disabilities.

**Narrative (one paragraph):**
> The library closed at eight, and Tomás knew he had eleven minutes left. He pulled his headphones down around his neck and saved the document — a history essay that was due at midnight and still did not have a conclusion — then glanced at the battery icon on the library computer. Four percent. At home, there was no computer and no internet, just a phone with a cracked screen and a data plan that ran out every month like clockwork. He hit save again, just in case, and began typing the conclusion with the kind of focused speed that only a deadline and a dying battery can produce.

Say: "Notice: same topic, three completely different pieces. The argument advocates for more funding. The informative text explains how funding works. The narrative puts you in the shoes of someone who depends on the library. Each one is true to its mode."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively plan the three-mode integrated writing task and draft one of the three pieces together.

**Activity: Planning the Triple Write**

Say: "Let's plan together using the topic: technology in schools."

**Step 1 — Brainstorm angles for each mode:**

- **Argument angle:** Should schools provide every student with a personal tablet or laptop? (Take a position.)
- **Informative angle:** How do schools integrate technology into the classroom? (Explain the systems, programs, and approaches without taking a side.)
- **Narrative angle:** What does it feel like to be a student whose school just received new laptops for the first time? (Tell a story.)

**Step 2 — Draft the informative paragraph together** (since this mode has received the least practice time):

> Schools across the United States integrate technology into instruction through three primary approaches: one-to-one device programs, shared device models, and blended learning platforms. In a one-to-one program, each student is assigned a personal laptop or tablet — typically a Chromebook or iPad — that they use throughout the school day and, in some districts, take home. Shared device models, by contrast, maintain classroom sets of devices that students access during specific instructional periods, reducing hardware costs but limiting the consistency of student access. Blended learning platforms, such as Google Classroom and Canvas, combine in-person instruction with digital assignments, assessments, and communication tools, creating a hybrid environment in which students interact with course material both face-to-face and online. According to the National Center for Education Statistics, 94% of American public schools reported having at least one computer available for every three students in 2023, though the ratio varies significantly by district wealth.

Analyze: "What organizational strategy did we use?" (Classification — three types of technology integration.) "What domain-specific vocabulary did we define?" (One-to-one, blended learning, Chromebook.) "Is there any opinion in this paragraph?" (No — purely informative.)

**Step 3 — Briefly outline the argument and narrative:**

- **Argument:** Claim: Schools should adopt one-to-one device programs because... Evidence: Studies on digital access and academic performance. Counterclaim: Cost concerns. Response: Long-term cost savings.
- **Narrative:** Character: Amira, who has never had a device of her own. Moment: The first time she opens the laptop and sees her name on the login screen. Expand: the moment she types her name. Compress: the rest of the school day.

Say: "Now you are ready to do this on your own."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently complete the integrated writing task — three short pieces on one topic in three modes.

Choose ONE topic from the list (or propose your own with facilitator approval):
1. Technology in schools
2. The environment and climate
3. Sports and competition
4. Food and nutrition
5. Student choice (approved topic: ___)

**Task:** Write three short pieces about your chosen topic:

**Piece 1 — Argument (one strong paragraph):**
- Include a claim, at least one piece of evidence with reasoning, and one counterclaim sentence
- Maintain formal style

**Piece 2 — Informative (one paragraph):**
- Include a focus statement, at least two facts or details, and at least one domain-specific term defined in context
- Maintain objective tone

**Piece 3 — Narrative (one to two paragraphs):**
- Include a character, setting, and at least one narrative technique (dialogue, sensory detail, expansion, or internal thought)
- Maintain consistent point of view

**Reflection (3-4 sentences):** How did switching between modes change your writing? Which mode felt most natural? Which was most challenging? What did you learn about the same topic by approaching it from three angles?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write all three pieces as described (one paragraph each) plus the reflection.
**Stretch:** Write all three pieces, making each at least two paragraphs. Include at least two evidence-based sentences in the argument, three development tools in the informative piece, and two narrative techniques in the narrative.
**Challenge:** Write all three pieces at the stretch level. Then write a fourth piece: a paragraph that intentionally BLENDS two modes (e.g., an argument that uses a narrative anecdote as evidence, or an informative text that opens with a brief narrative scenario). Explain in your reflection how and why you blended the modes.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1) Identify the counterclaim in this argument paragraph and evaluate whether the writer responds to it effectively: "Schools should offer art classes because art develops creativity. Some people say art is not as important as math, but I disagree. Art is really important for students." (Expected: The counterclaim is "Some people say art is not as important as math." The response is NOT effective because it simply states "I disagree" and "art is really important" without evidence or reasoning. An effective response would acknowledge the counterclaim, then refute with specific evidence — e.g., "However, a study by the NEA found that students enrolled in arts programs demonstrate 20% higher performance on creative problem-solving tasks, a skill increasingly valued in STEM careers.")
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.2c) Add transitions to connect these three sentences into a cohesive paragraph: "The Great Barrier Reef is the largest coral reef system in the world. It supports over 1,500 species of fish. Rising ocean temperatures have caused significant coral bleaching in recent years." (Expected: "The Great Barrier Reef is the largest coral reef system in the world, stretching over 2,300 kilometers along the northeastern coast of Australia. This vast ecosystem supports over 1,500 species of fish, making it one of the most biodiverse marine habitats on the planet. However, rising ocean temperatures have caused significant coral bleaching in recent years, threatening the very foundation on which this biodiversity depends.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following sentence and identify which writing mode it belongs to (argumentative, informative, or narrative). Explain your reasoning in one sentence.

"The rain had stopped, but the gutters still ran with brown water, and somewhere down the block a dog was barking at the silence."

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
Narrative. The sentence uses sensory details (brown water, dog barking), creates a specific scene and mood, and employs literary language ("barking at the silence") that serves an experiential rather than persuasive or explanatory purpose. There is no claim, no evidence, no objective information delivery — only an evoked experience.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the mode AND provides reasoning that references at least one feature of that mode (e.g., sensory detail, scene-setting, literary language, lack of claim or data).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the Mode Comparison Reference chart and review the key features of each mode
- Provide three sentences (one per mode) and have the student sort them: "Schools must increase funding for art programs" (argument), "Art education encompasses visual arts, music, dance, and theater" (informative), "The paintbrush trembled in her hand as she touched the first stroke of blue to the canvas" (narrative)
- Ask: "Is this sentence trying to persuade, explain, or make me experience something?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student has demonstrated integrated understanding of all three writing modes
- Encourage the student to review their writing portfolio and select their strongest piece for final revision before the unit assessment

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Portfolio Reflection:** Review all the writing you have done in this unit — arguments, informative texts, narratives, and today's integrated pieces. Write a one-page reflection answering: What is the strongest piece of writing in your portfolio? What makes it strong? What skill improved the most over the course of this unit? What do you still want to work on?
2. **Mode Blending in the Real World:** Find three examples of writing that blend modes — an argument that uses a narrative anecdote, a news article that uses both informative and argumentative elements, or a personal essay that blends narrative with argument. For each, identify where the modes shift and explain why the writer made that choice.
3. **Writing for Different Audiences:** Take one of your three workshop pieces and rewrite it for a different audience. If you wrote your argument for a school board, rewrite it for fellow students. If you wrote your narrative for a general audience, rewrite it as a letter to a friend. How does the audience change your choices about vocabulary, tone, and detail?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This is the culminating lesson of the unit before the assessment, and it requires students to demonstrate fluency across all three writing modes. The integrated task is deliberately challenging — writing about the same topic in three different modes forces students to think about PURPOSE and AUDIENCE rather than just following a template.
- Time management is critical. The three pieces are short by design — one paragraph each — to keep the task manageable within a single session. If students struggle to finish, prioritize quality over quantity: a strong one-paragraph argument is better than a rushed three-paragraph attempt.
- The reflection is an important metacognitive exercise. It asks students to think about their own writing process, not just their writing product. Encourage honest, specific reflections ("I found the informative paragraph hardest because I kept wanting to state my opinion") rather than vague ones ("They were all good").
- Some students may find it difficult to approach the same topic from three angles. Help them brainstorm by asking: "What SHOULD happen about this topic? (That is your argument.) What IS true about this topic? (That is your informative piece.) What DOES it feel like to experience this topic? (That is your narrative.)"
- The Mode Comparison Reference chart should remain visible throughout the session. Students may need to refer to it frequently as they switch between modes.
- Use this lesson as a diagnostic opportunity. Observe which mode each student is strongest and weakest in, and use that information to target your review before the unit assessment.
- If peer feedback is used, provide a structured form: "For the argument: Is there a clear claim? For the informative: Is there a focus statement? For the narrative: Can you picture the scene?" Simple, mode-specific questions are more helpful than general feedback.

## Rubric / Answer Key

### Independent Practice — Integrated Writing Rubric

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Argument** | Clear claim with evidence and reasoning; counterclaim acknowledged; formal style | Claim present but evidence or counterclaim weak; mostly formal | No clear claim, or missing evidence and counterclaim |
| **Informative** | Focus statement present; developed with facts/details; domain-specific vocabulary defined; objective tone | Some development but lacks specificity or vocabulary; mostly objective | Thin development; no domain-specific vocabulary; opinion creeps in |
| **Narrative** | Character and setting established; at least one technique (dialogue, sensory detail, expansion) used effectively; consistent POV | Character present but techniques underdeveloped; POV mostly consistent | No clear character or scene; narrative techniques absent |
| **Mode Distinction** | Each piece is clearly in its mode — distinct purpose, tone, and structure; no mode blending unless intentional | Modes are mostly distinct but some blurring (e.g., opinion in informative, or flat reporting in narrative) | Modes are not distinct; pieces feel interchangeable |
| **Reflection** | Specific, honest, and insightful; identifies strengths and challenges with concrete examples | Generally reflective but vague; lacks specific examples | No reflection, or reflection is a single generic sentence |

### Interleaved Review
1. The counterclaim is identified ("Some people say art is not as important as math"). The response is ineffective — "I disagree" with no evidence. Must include specific evidence and reasoning in a revised response.
2. Must add transitions that create cohesion. See expected example above.
