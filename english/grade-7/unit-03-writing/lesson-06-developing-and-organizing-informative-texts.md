# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 6

## Topic
Developing and Organizing Informative Texts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Develop body paragraphs with relevant facts, definitions, concrete details, and examples that examine a topic thoroughly
- Use precise language and domain-specific vocabulary defined in context
- Write a complete informative text with an introduction, developed body paragraphs with transitions, and a concluding section

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 5: Understanding of organizational strategies, informative focus statements, and informative introductions
- W.6.2b: Experience developing topics with facts, definitions, and concrete details
- W.6.2c: Familiarity with transitions that connect ideas within and between paragraphs
- Understanding of formal, objective academic style

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model informative body paragraphs (provided below)
- Informative Transition Reference Chart (described below)
- Informative Outline from Lesson 5

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the concept of "developing" a topic versus simply mentioning it, and activate prior knowledge about using facts and details.

**Activity: Thin vs. Thick**

Display two versions of the same body paragraph about the same topic (types of clouds):

**Thin version:** "Cumulus clouds are fluffy and white. They are common on nice days. They can sometimes grow into storm clouds."

**Thick version:** "Cumulus clouds, identifiable by their flat bases and rounded, cauliflower-like tops, typically form on clear days when warm air rises from the Earth's surface and cools at higher altitudes, causing water vapor to condense into visible droplets. While most cumulus clouds are associated with fair weather — meteorologists classify them as cumulus humilis, meaning 'humble cumulus' — they can develop vertically under certain atmospheric conditions, growing into towering cumulonimbus formations that produce thunderstorms, heavy rainfall, and in severe cases, hail and tornadoes."

Say: "Both paragraphs are about cumulus clouds. But the thin version tells me almost nothing I could not guess from looking at the sky. The thick version teaches me something. It uses precise vocabulary — cumulus humilis, cumulonimbus — defines terms in context, and includes specific details about HOW and WHY clouds form and change."

Ask: "What specific strategies does the thick version use that the thin version does not?" (Domain-specific vocabulary defined in context, explanation of the process, specific details, precise language.)

Say: "Today you are going to learn how to 'thicken' your informative writing — to develop your ideas with the depth and precision that seventh-grade informative writing demands."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach development strategies for informative body paragraphs, precise language and vocabulary techniques, and informative transitions and conclusions.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Development:** The process of elaborating on a topic with sufficient facts, details, definitions, examples, and explanations to give the reader a thorough understanding
- **Precise language:** Word choices that are specific and exact rather than vague or general ("approximately 2.5 centimeters" rather than "about an inch"; "accelerate" rather than "go faster")
- **Domain-specific vocabulary:** Words and phrases that belong to a particular subject area and have specialized meanings within that field
- **Appositive definition:** A technique for defining a term within a sentence by placing the definition next to the term, set off by commas or dashes

### Instruction Notes

**Development Strategies:**

Say: "There are five tools you can use to develop an informative paragraph. Think of them as ingredients — the more you use, the richer your writing."

Write on chart paper:

| Development Tool | What It Does | Example |
|------------------|-------------|---------|
| **Fact** | States something objectively true and verifiable | "The human brain contains approximately 86 billion neurons." |
| **Definition** | Explains what a term or concept means | "Neurons — specialized cells that transmit electrical and chemical signals — are the fundamental units of the nervous system." |
| **Concrete detail** | Provides specific, observable information | "Each neuron can form up to 10,000 connections with other neurons, creating a network more complex than any computer ever built." |
| **Quotation** | Uses an expert's exact words for authority | "Neuroscientist Dr. Suzanne Corkin described the brain as 'the most complicated object in the known universe.'" |
| **Example** | Illustrates an abstract concept with a specific case | "For instance, when a person touches a hot stove, sensory neurons transmit a pain signal to the spinal cord at a speed of approximately 120 meters per second." |

Say: "A well-developed paragraph uses at least three of these five tools. The goal is to give the reader enough information to truly understand the topic, not just recognize it."

**Defining Vocabulary in Context:**

Say: "Domain-specific vocabulary makes your writing precise, but you cannot assume the reader knows these terms. Seventh-grade writers define terms inside their sentences, not in a separate glossary."

Write three techniques on chart paper:

1. **Appositive definition:** "Photosynthesis — the process by which plants convert sunlight into chemical energy — is the foundation of nearly all food chains on Earth."
2. **Parenthetical definition:** "The tectonic plates (massive slabs of Earth's crust and upper mantle) move at an average rate of 2.5 centimeters per year."
3. **Explanatory clause:** "Coral bleaching occurs when rising water temperatures cause coral polyps to expel the symbiotic algae that provide them with both color and nutrients, leaving behind the white calcium carbonate skeleton."

**Informative Transition Reference Chart:**

Write on chart paper:

| Purpose | Transitions |
|---------|------------|
| Adding information | Additionally, furthermore, in addition, another key factor, equally significant |
| Defining or explaining | In other words, that is, specifically, this means that, to clarify |
| Giving examples | For instance, for example, one illustration of this, a notable case is |
| Showing cause/effect | As a result, consequently, therefore, this leads to, because of this |
| Comparing | Similarly, in the same way, likewise, both...and |
| Contrasting | However, in contrast, unlike, conversely, on the other hand, whereas |
| Sequencing | First, next, subsequently, following this, finally |

### Model Text / Worked Example

Display the following two model body paragraphs from an informative text about how the human memory system works:

> **Body Paragraph 1 (Sensory Memory and Short-Term Memory):**
>
> The first stage of the human memory system is sensory memory, a brief holding area where incoming information from the five senses is retained for fractions of a second before the brain decides whether to process it further. Visual sensory memory, known as iconic memory, lasts approximately 250 milliseconds — roughly one quarter of a second — while auditory sensory memory, called echoic memory, persists for three to four seconds. The vast majority of sensory information is discarded almost immediately; the brain simply cannot process every sight, sound, and sensation it encounters. However, when the brain identifies incoming information as important — because it is novel, relevant to a current task, or emotionally charged — that information is transferred to short-term memory, also called working memory. Short-term memory has a limited capacity: research by cognitive psychologist George Miller established that most people can hold approximately seven items (plus or minus two) in short-term memory at any given time, a finding so influential that it is commonly referred to as "Miller's Law." Information in short-term memory is fragile; without active rehearsal — the deliberate repetition or manipulation of information — it fades within 15 to 30 seconds.
>
> **Body Paragraph 2 (Long-Term Memory):**
>
> Information that survives the short-term memory bottleneck enters long-term memory, a vast storage system with a capacity that, as far as neuroscientists can determine, has no practical limit. Long-term memories are formed through a process called consolidation, during which the hippocampus — a seahorse-shaped structure deep within the temporal lobe — strengthens the neural connections associated with a particular memory until they become stable enough to exist independently. Consolidation is not instantaneous; it occurs primarily during sleep, which is one reason that sleep deprivation impairs the ability to retain new information. Long-term memory itself is not a single system but a collection of distinct subtypes. Declarative memory stores facts and events ("The capital of France is Paris" or "I ate pizza for dinner last Tuesday"), while procedural memory stores skills and habits (riding a bicycle, typing on a keyboard). A particularly powerful form of long-term memory is emotional memory, which is processed through the amygdala and tends to be stronger and more persistent than other types. This is why people often remember exactly where they were during a frightening or joyful experience, even years later, while forgetting routine events from the same period.

Ask: "Let's count the development tools in paragraph 1." (Fact: 250 milliseconds, 3-4 seconds. Definition: iconic memory, echoic memory, working memory, rehearsal. Concrete detail: seven items plus or minus two. Quotation: reference to Miller's Law. Example: implied through the description of the process.)

Ask: "How does the writer define domain-specific vocabulary?" (Appositive definitions: "sensory memory, a brief holding area"; "iconic memory"; "echoic memory"; "working memory"; "rehearsal — the deliberate repetition or manipulation of information." Also parenthetical and explanatory definitions throughout.)

Ask: "What transitions connect ideas within and between paragraphs?" ("However," "also called," "which is one reason that," "while.")

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively develop a "thin" body paragraph into a "thick," well-developed informative paragraph.

**Activity: Thickening a Paragraph**

Display this thin paragraph:

> "Bees are important for plants. They carry pollen from one flower to another. Without bees, many crops would not grow. Scientists are worried because bee populations are declining."

Say: "This paragraph has good ideas, but it is thin — it lacks facts, definitions, precise vocabulary, and detailed explanations. Let's thicken it together."

Work through each sentence, adding development:

1. **First sentence — add precision and a definition:** "Bees play a critical role in agriculture through a process called pollination — the transfer of pollen grains from the male anther of a flower to the female stigma, which enables the plant to produce seeds and fruit."

2. **Second sentence — add concrete detail and an example:** "As a honeybee forages for nectar, microscopic pollen grains adhere to the tiny branched hairs covering its body. When the bee visits the next flower, some of these grains rub off onto the stigma, completing the fertilization process. A single honeybee colony can pollinate approximately 300 million flowers per day, making bees among the most efficient pollinators in the natural world."

3. **Third sentence — add a fact and domain-specific vocabulary:** "According to the United States Department of Agriculture, pollinators — primarily bees — are responsible for one out of every three bites of food Americans eat. Crops valued at more than $15 billion annually in the United States alone, including almonds, blueberries, and apples, depend on bee pollination."

4. **Fourth sentence — add cause/effect detail:** "Since 2006, beekeepers have reported average annual colony losses of approximately 30%, a phenomenon initially termed Colony Collapse Disorder (CCD). Researchers have identified several contributing causes, including pesticide exposure — particularly a class of chemicals called neonicotinoids — habitat loss due to agricultural expansion, and parasitic mites that weaken bee immune systems."

Read the complete thickened paragraph aloud.

Say: "Count with me: How many development tools did we use? Facts, definitions, concrete details, and examples. How many domain-specific terms did we define in context?" (Pollination, anther, stigma, pollinators, Colony Collapse Disorder, neonicotinoids.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently draft a complete informative text (or substantial sections of one) using their outline from Lesson 5.

Using the outline and introduction you developed in Lesson 5, draft your informative text.

If you did not complete an outline in Lesson 5, choose a new topic:
1. How does the human digestive system break down food?
2. What are the major types of ecosystems on Earth?
3. How has the technology of animation changed from hand-drawn to computer-generated?

**Task:** Draft a complete informative text that includes:
- An introduction with a hook, context, and focus statement
- At least two body paragraphs, each developed with facts, definitions, concrete details, and/or examples
- At least three domain-specific vocabulary terms defined in context
- Transitions that connect ideas within and between paragraphs
- A concluding section that synthesizes key information

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Draft the introduction and two body paragraphs with at least three domain-specific terms and development using at least three of the five tools.
**Stretch:** Draft the complete text (introduction, two or more body paragraphs, conclusion) with four or more domain-specific terms and varied development tools.
**Challenge:** Draft the complete text using a combination of two organizational strategies (e.g., classification in the body with cause/effect in one paragraph). Include at least five domain-specific terms and a conclusion that connects the topic to a broader significance.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.2e) Rewrite the following sentence to replace informal language with formal, objective style: "Scientists think bees are basically disappearing because of these chemicals that are super bad for them, and it's honestly kind of scary." (Expected: "Scientists have identified neonicotinoid pesticides as a significant contributor to declining bee populations, a trend that poses serious risks to global food production.")
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1c) Write a transition sentence that connects these two argument paragraphs: Paragraph 1 argues that school gardens teach science. Paragraph 2 argues that school gardens improve student nutrition. (Expected: "Beyond their academic benefits, school gardens also serve a critical role in improving student nutrition and reshaping attitudes toward healthy eating.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this informative sentence and improve it by adding ONE domain-specific vocabulary term defined in context:

Original: "The outer layer of the Earth is broken into large pieces that move slowly over time."

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
Improved: "The outer layer of the Earth, known as the lithosphere, is divided into large segments called tectonic plates — rigid slabs of crust and upper mantle that drift across the underlying asthenosphere at a rate of approximately 2 to 10 centimeters per year."

(Accept any response that adds at least one domain-specific term with a contextual definition and maintains formal, informative style.)

**Mastery = Student adds at least one domain-specific vocabulary term AND defines it in context (using an appositive, parenthetical, or explanatory clause). The sentence must remain informative in tone.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three techniques for defining vocabulary in context (appositive, parenthetical, explanatory clause) with simple examples
- Provide a sentence and a vocabulary term and have the student insert the term with a definition: "The process that turns a caterpillar into a butterfly is remarkable." (Add: metamorphosis) Expected: "The process that turns a caterpillar into a butterfly — a transformation called metamorphosis — is one of the most remarkable phenomena in the natural world."
- Practice with two more sentences before moving on

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7, which transitions to narrative writing
- Encourage the student to complete and revise their informative text, keeping it in their writing portfolio

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Infographic Translation:** Take one of your informative body paragraphs and redesign the information as a simple infographic or diagram with labels. Then write a brief reflection: What information was easy to show visually? What was hard? What does this reveal about the relationship between text and visual information?
2. **Cross-Text Comparison:** Find a published informative article on a topic similar to yours (an encyclopedia entry, a science magazine article, or a textbook passage). Compare: What organizational strategy does the published text use? How does it define domain-specific vocabulary? What development tools does it employ? Write a one-paragraph analysis.
3. **Peer Review for Development:** Exchange informative texts with a partner. For each body paragraph, count the number of development tools used (fact, definition, concrete detail, quotation, example). Identify any paragraphs that are "thin" and suggest at least one specific addition that would strengthen development.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Development is the most common weakness in seventh-grade informative writing. Students tend to make broad, general statements without supporting them with specific facts, details, or examples. The "thin vs. thick" comparison from the warm-up is a powerful reference point — when conferencing, you can simply ask, "Is this paragraph thin or thick? What could you add to thicken it?"
- Domain-specific vocabulary should be a natural part of the text, not forced. If a student is writing about volcanoes, terms like "magma," "tectonic plates," and "pyroclastic flow" should appear because the topic demands them, not because the assignment requires a specific number of terms. The numerical requirement (three terms minimum) is a scaffold; the real goal is precision.
- The informative conclusion is often the weakest section because students default to summarizing. Coach them toward synthesis: "Do not tell me what you already said. Tell me why it matters. What is the big takeaway? Why should someone care about this topic?"
- Some students may struggle to distinguish between informative and argumentative conclusions. An informative conclusion can note significance or implications without advocating for a position. "Understanding ocean currents is essential for predicting climate change" is informative; "We must take action to prevent climate change" crosses into argumentation.
- Encourage students to read their informative texts aloud. Awkward sentences, missing transitions, and underdeveloped sections become much more obvious when heard than when silently read.

## Rubric / Answer Key

### Independent Practice — Informative Text Rubric

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| **Introduction and Focus** | Hook engages; context frames the topic; focus statement previews organizational strategy | Introduction present but missing one element; organizational strategy unclear | No introduction, or the focus statement is vague or takes an argumentative position |
| **Development** | Body paragraphs use at least three development tools; information is specific and accurate; paragraphs are "thick" | Some development but paragraphs remain thin; fewer than three tools used; some statements are vague | Body paragraphs contain only general statements without specific facts, details, or examples |
| **Domain-Specific Vocabulary** | At least three terms are used and defined in context naturally | One or two terms present; definitions may be missing or awkward | No domain-specific vocabulary, or terms are used without definition |
| **Transitions and Organization** | Transitions connect ideas within and between paragraphs purposefully; organizational strategy is clear | Some transitions present but inconsistent; organization is partially clear | Few or no transitions; paragraphs feel disconnected |
| **Conclusion and Style** | Conclusion synthesizes information; formal, objective tone throughout | Conclusion summarizes rather than synthesizes; tone is mostly formal | No conclusion; informal or opinionated tone |
