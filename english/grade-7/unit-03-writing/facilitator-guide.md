# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3: Writing

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview

Unit 3 advances seventh-grade students through the three core modes of academic writing: argumentative, informative/explanatory, and narrative. Building on the foundational writing skills developed in Grade 6 — where students learned to construct claims with reasons and evidence, examine topics through informative texts, and craft narratives with descriptive details — this unit raises expectations significantly. In argumentative writing, students must now acknowledge and respond to opposing claims (counterclaims), use more sophisticated cohesive devices, and maintain a consistently formal style. In informative writing, students employ organizational strategies such as definition, classification, comparison/contrast, and cause/effect while integrating precise, domain-specific vocabulary. In narrative writing, students deepen their command of dialogue, pacing, and description to develop experiences and characters with greater complexity. The unit culminates with a revision workshop and an integrated writing experience where students apply all three modes to a single topic, demonstrating versatility and control across writing types. Each lesson includes original model texts, graphic organizers, sentence frames for scaffolding, and structured practice so that students internalize the qualities of effective seventh-grade writing through both analysis and production.

### Facilitation Tips

- **Emphasize the transition from Grade 6 to Grade 7 argumentation.** In Grade 6, students learned to construct claims with reasons and evidence. In Grade 7, the critical addition is counterclaims: students must now acknowledge what someone who disagrees would argue and explain why their own position is stronger. This is a significant cognitive leap. Return to this distinction frequently throughout Lessons 1-4 and celebrate students who engage thoughtfully with opposing views.
- **Model the writing process visibly and repeatedly.** Seventh graders benefit from watching the facilitator compose in real time. Think aloud as you draft on chart paper or a whiteboard, narrating your decisions about claim placement, counterclaim acknowledgment, evidence selection, transitions, vocabulary choices, and pacing. Make your revision process visible too — cross things out, move sentences, try alternative word choices.
- **Use graphic organizers as scaffolds that fade.** Introduce the Claim-Counterclaim-Evidence chart, Informative Outline, and Narrative Planning Map explicitly in early lessons, but encourage students to internalize these structures. By Lessons 9-10, students should be planning independently with minimal scaffolding.
- **Build a writing portfolio throughout the unit.** Have students keep all drafts, plans, and revised pieces in a writing folder. The portfolio becomes a tool for reflection in Lessons 9-10 and provides evidence of growth for assessment.
- **Conference strategically during independent practice.** Circulate and hold focused 2-minute conferences with individual students. Address one skill per conference (e.g., strengthening a counterclaim response, adding a transition, improving dialogue punctuation) rather than trying to fix everything at once.
- **Protect writing time.** Once students begin independent practice, minimize interruptions. Use a quiet signal or timer to mark transitions. Writing stamina is a skill that develops through sustained, uninterrupted practice.
- **Normalize revision as the heart of good writing.** Emphasize that professional writers revise extensively. First drafts are for getting ideas down; quality comes from rewriting. Lesson 9 is entirely devoted to revision strategies.
- **Connect writing modes explicitly.** Help students see that skills transfer across modes: strong evidence supports arguments AND informative writing; vivid details enhance narratives AND informative texts; clear organization matters in all three modes. Lesson 10 makes this connection explicit by having students write about a single topic in all three modes.

### Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| Acknowledging a counterclaim weakens my argument | The opposite is true. Acknowledging a counterclaim shows the writer has considered multiple perspectives, which builds credibility. The key is to acknowledge the counterclaim and then refute it with evidence — this actually strengthens the argument. |
| A counterclaim is the same as a weakness in my argument | A counterclaim is a different position, not a flaw. It is what someone who disagrees with you would argue. Addressing it means explaining why, despite that opposing view, your position is still stronger. |
| Formal style means using big words | Formal style means avoiding slang, contractions, and first-person pronouns ("I think"); using precise academic vocabulary; and maintaining an objective, professional tone. It does not mean using unnecessarily complex words that obscure meaning. |
| Informative writing and argumentative writing are the same | Argumentative writing takes a position and tries to persuade. Informative writing examines a topic objectively without taking sides. The organizational strategies, tone, and purpose are fundamentally different. |
| More details always make narrative writing better | Details must be purposeful. A detail that develops character, advances the plot, or creates mood is valuable. A detail that is merely decorative slows the story down. Seventh graders should learn to choose details strategically. |
| Dialogue means having characters say everything they think | Effective dialogue is selective — it reveals character, creates tension, or advances the plot. It should sound natural, not like a transcript of every thought. What characters do NOT say is often as powerful as what they do say. |
| Revision means fixing spelling and grammar | Revision and editing are different processes. Revision addresses the big picture — ideas, organization, evidence, clarity, and development. Editing addresses surface-level conventions — spelling, punctuation, grammar. Lesson 9 teaches both, in that order. |

### Assessment Guidance

**Formative Assessment (ongoing):**
- Exit tickets at the end of each lesson provide a quick snapshot of skill acquisition
- Writing conferences during independent practice allow for individualized feedback and targeted instruction
- Graphic organizers and planning documents reveal students' thinking processes before they draft
- Peer feedback activities build critical reading skills while providing writers with reader perspective

**Summative Assessment (Unit Assessment):**
- The unit assessment includes four sections: argumentative writing (W.7.1), informative writing (W.7.2), narrative writing (W.7.3), and revision/editing (all standards)
- Each section is worth 15 points, for a total of 60 points
- Mastery requires 42/60 (70%) overall
- Rubrics are provided for each writing task; the revision/editing section uses item-level scoring

**Portfolio Assessment (Lesson 10):**
- Students select their strongest piece from the unit, revise it thoroughly, and reflect on their growth
- The portfolio provides valuable information about student self-assessment, revision skills, and development over the unit

### Differentiation at a Glance

**Below Grade Level:**
- Provide sentence starters and paragraph frames for each writing type (included in lessons)
- Offer pre-filled graphic organizers with some sections completed as models
- Reduce the number of required body paragraphs (e.g., one counterclaim paragraph instead of two for arguments)
- Allow students to dictate ideas before writing or use speech-to-text tools
- Provide word banks for academic vocabulary (claim, counterclaim, evidence, transition, cohesion)
- Pair with a peer partner for collaborative planning before independent writing
- Use the On-level tier in independent practice

**On Grade Level:**
- Follow lesson plans as written with the standard graphic organizers and model texts
- Encourage use of transition reference resources and peer conferencing protocols
- Expect counterclaim acknowledgment in arguments, organizational strategy use in informative writing, and purposeful narrative techniques
- Use the On-level and Stretch tiers in independent practice

**Above Grade Level:**
- Challenge students to address and refute multiple counterclaims in argumentative writing
- Encourage the integration of multiple organizational strategies in informative writing
- Ask narrative writers to experiment with nonlinear structure, unreliable narrators, or extended metaphor
- Have students write across modes on the same topic independently (anticipating Lesson 10)
- Invite students to serve as peer writing coaches during the revision workshop
- Use the Stretch and Challenge tiers in independent practice
