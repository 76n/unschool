# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Introduction to Informative Writing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between informative and argumentative writing by identifying differences in purpose, tone, and structure
- Write an informative introduction that clearly previews the topic and organizational strategy
- Select and apply an organizational strategy (definition, classification, comparison/contrast, or cause/effect) appropriate to the topic

## Prerequisites
- W.6.2: Experience writing informative/explanatory texts that examine a topic with facts and details
- W.6.2a: Ability to introduce a topic with a focus statement and organize ideas logically
- Lessons 1-4: Understanding of formal style, evidence, and structured writing (transfers across modes)
- Familiarity with objective, third-person academic tone

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model informative text (provided below)
- Organizational Strategy Reference (described below)
- Informative Outline template (described below)
- Highlighters (three colors)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish between informative and argumentative writing purposes and activate prior knowledge from Grade 6 informative writing.

**Activity: Argue or Inform?**

Display the following two passages and ask the student to identify which is argumentative and which is informative, and to explain how they know:

**Passage A:** "Sleep deprivation affects the adolescent brain in three primary ways: it reduces the prefrontal cortex's ability to regulate impulses, it impairs the hippocampus's capacity to consolidate new memories, and it disrupts the production of serotonin, a neurotransmitter essential for emotional stability."

**Passage B:** "Schools must address the epidemic of adolescent sleep deprivation by pushing start times to no earlier than 8:30 a.m. because sleep loss damages impulse control, memory formation, and emotional health."

After the student responds, discuss: Passage A is informative — it examines how sleep deprivation affects the brain without advocating for a particular action. Passage B is argumentative — it takes a position ("schools must") and argues for a specific change.

Say: "Notice that both passages use facts. Both are formal. Both are specific. The difference is purpose. Argumentative writing says 'this is what should happen.' Informative writing says 'this is how it works' or 'this is what it is.' In the next two lessons, we are shifting from persuading to explaining."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the four organizational strategies for informative writing and model how the choice of strategy shapes the entire text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Organizational strategy:** The method used to arrange information logically within an informative text; the structure that helps the reader understand relationships among ideas
- **Definition:** An organizational strategy that explains what something is by describing its characteristics, features, and boundaries
- **Classification:** An organizational strategy that groups related items into categories and explains the categories
- **Comparison/contrast:** An organizational strategy that examines similarities and differences between two or more subjects
- **Cause/effect:** An organizational strategy that explains why something happens (causes) and what results from it (effects)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned to organize informative writing logically. In seventh grade, you are going to be deliberate about your organizational strategy. Before you write a single sentence, you need to ask: What is the best way to organize this information so the reader understands it clearly?"

Write on chart paper:

**Four Organizational Strategies:**

| Strategy | Best For | Signal Words | Example Topic |
|----------|----------|-------------|---------------|
| **Definition** | Explaining what something is | is defined as, refers to, is characterized by, means | "What is artificial intelligence?" |
| **Classification** | Grouping items into categories | can be divided into, one type is, another category, falls into the group | "Types of renewable energy" |
| **Comparison/Contrast** | Showing similarities and differences | similarly, in contrast, unlike, both, however, whereas, on the other hand | "Traditional vs. online learning" |
| **Cause/Effect** | Explaining why something happens | because, as a result, consequently, leads to, is caused by, the effect of | "How deforestation affects biodiversity" |

Say: "Sometimes you will use one strategy for the entire text. Other times, you might combine strategies — for example, you might define a concept in your introduction and then use cause/effect in the body. The key is choosing deliberately, not accidentally."

**Informative Introduction Structure:**

Say: "An informative introduction has three jobs: hook the reader, provide context, and preview the organizational approach."

Write on chart paper:

```
INFORMATIVE INTRODUCTION FORMULA:
1. Hook — A surprising fact, question, or vivid detail that makes the reader curious
2. Context — Background information on the topic
3. Focus Statement — What the text will examine AND how it will be organized
   (Preview your categories, causes, points of comparison, etc.)
```

Say: "The focus statement in informative writing is like the claim in argumentative writing — but it does not take a position. It announces what the reader will learn and how the information is organized."

**Example focus statements:**

- Definition: "Artificial intelligence, broadly defined, refers to computer systems designed to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, including learning, problem-solving, and pattern recognition."
- Classification: "Renewable energy sources can be divided into five primary categories: solar, wind, hydroelectric, geothermal, and biomass, each with distinct advantages and limitations."
- Comparison/Contrast: "While traditional classroom learning and online learning share the fundamental goal of educating students, they differ significantly in structure, social interaction, and the skills they demand from learners."
- Cause/Effect: "Deforestation in tropical regions triggers a chain of ecological consequences that begins with habitat loss and extends to soil erosion, disrupted water cycles, and accelerated climate change."

### Model Text / Worked Example

Read the following model informative text aloud. Display it so the student can follow along.

> **How Ocean Currents Shape Global Climate**
>
> If the world's oceans stopped moving, life on Earth would be unrecognizable within a generation. Coastal cities that enjoy mild winters would plunge into arctic cold. Tropical regions would overheat. Rainfall patterns that sustain agriculture across entire continents would collapse. The oceans are not simply vast bodies of water sitting passively between landmasses — they are a dynamic, planet-spanning conveyor system that distributes heat, regulates temperature, and drives the weather patterns on which human civilization depends. Understanding how ocean currents shape global climate requires examining three interconnected processes: thermohaline circulation, the redistribution of equatorial heat, and the influence of currents on regional weather patterns.
>
> The engine that drives global ocean circulation is a process called thermohaline circulation, a term derived from the Greek words for heat (thermo) and salt (haline). In the North Atlantic, cold, dense, salty water near the Arctic sinks to the ocean floor and begins a slow journey southward along the seabed. As this deep water moves toward the equator and beyond, warmer surface water from the tropics flows northward to replace it, creating a continuous loop that oceanographers often call the "global conveyor belt." This cycle moves an estimated 17 million cubic meters of water per second — roughly 100 times the flow of the Amazon River. The entire loop takes approximately 1,000 years to complete, meaning that a molecule of water sinking near Greenland today will not return to the surface until the year 3000.
>
> The most significant climatic effect of this conveyor belt is the redistribution of heat from the equator toward the poles. Solar energy strikes the Earth most intensely at the equator, where the sun's rays hit the surface at a direct angle. Without ocean currents, this heat would remain concentrated in the tropics, making equatorial regions far hotter and polar regions far colder than they are today. The Gulf Stream, a powerful warm current that flows from the Gulf of Mexico across the Atlantic to northwestern Europe, is perhaps the most dramatic example of heat redistribution. The Gulf Stream carries approximately 1.4 petawatts of thermal energy — equivalent to the output of roughly one million nuclear power plants — northward across the Atlantic. As a result, cities like London and Dublin enjoy average winter temperatures 5 to 10 degrees Celsius warmer than cities at the same latitude in eastern Canada, such as St. John's, Newfoundland. Without the Gulf Stream, the climate of the British Isles would more closely resemble that of Labrador.
>
> Ocean currents also shape regional weather patterns, particularly precipitation. When warm ocean currents flow along a coastline, they increase evaporation, which adds moisture to the air and fuels rainfall. The Kuroshio Current, a warm Pacific current that flows past Japan, contributes to the heavy monsoon rains that sustain rice cultivation across East Asia. Conversely, cold currents can suppress rainfall: the Benguela Current, which carries frigid Antarctic water northward along the coast of southwestern Africa, chills the air above it and prevents evaporation, contributing to the extreme aridity of the Namib Desert — one of the driest places on Earth, despite sitting directly adjacent to the ocean.
>
> From the massive thermohaline conveyor belt to the regional currents that determine whether a coastline receives monsoon rains or desert winds, ocean circulation is one of the most powerful forces shaping life on Earth. As global temperatures rise and polar ice melts, scientists warn that the influx of fresh water into the North Atlantic could slow or disrupt thermohaline circulation, with consequences that would ripple across every continent. Understanding these currents is not merely an exercise in oceanography — it is essential to understanding the climate system on which all human activity depends.

Say: "Let's analyze this text." Use three highlighter colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** The hook and focus statement in the introduction. Note the organizational strategy: cause/effect (how currents cause climate effects), organized into three categories (thermohaline circulation, heat redistribution, regional weather).
- **Color 2 (green):** Domain-specific vocabulary defined in context (thermohaline, global conveyor belt, Gulf Stream, Kuroshio Current, Benguela Current, petawatts).
- **Color 3 (blue):** Transitions that signal organizational structure ("The engine that drives...," "The most significant climatic effect...," "Ocean currents also...").

Ask: "What organizational strategy does this text primarily use?" (Cause/effect — it explains how ocean currents cause climate effects — combined with classification of three types of effects.)

Ask: "Does the writer express an opinion anywhere?" (No — the tone remains objective and explanatory throughout, even in the conclusion where it mentions climate change.)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively select an organizational strategy, write a focus statement, and outline an informative text.

**Activity: Planning an Informative Text Together**

Say: "Let's plan an informative text together. Our topic is: How does social media affect the way teenagers communicate?"

**Step 1 — Choose an organizational strategy:**

Say: "Which strategy fits this topic best? Let's consider our options."
- Definition? We could define social media, but that is only one piece.
- Classification? We could classify types of social media communication.
- Comparison/Contrast? We could compare communication before and after social media.
- Cause/Effect? We could explain how social media causes changes in teen communication patterns.

Guide: "Cause/effect works well because we are examining HOW social media AFFECTS communication — cause and effect is built into the question itself."

**Step 2 — Write the focus statement:**

Draft together: "Social media platforms have fundamentally altered the way teenagers communicate by shifting conversations from face-to-face interactions to digital exchanges, by creating new forms of expression such as memes and shorthand, and by changing the speed and permanence of interpersonal communication."

**Step 3 — Create an informative outline:**

```
INFORMATIVE OUTLINE

Topic: How social media affects teen communication
Organizational Strategy: Cause/Effect

Introduction:
  Hook: Statistic about teen social media usage (e.g., "The average American teenager
    sends 67 text messages per day and spends 3.5 hours on social media platforms")
  Context: Social media has transformed communication in less than two decades
  Focus Statement: [Written above]

Body Paragraph 1 — Effect 1: Shift from face-to-face to digital
  Fact: Pew Research reports that only 32% of teens prefer communicating in person
  Detail: This shift affects nonverbal communication skills, emotional recognition
  Domain-specific vocabulary: "digital native," "synchronous vs. asynchronous communication"

Body Paragraph 2 — Effect 2: New forms of expression
  Fact: Linguists at Georgetown University study how memes function as a visual language
  Detail: Abbreviations, emoji, GIF reactions serve communicative purposes
  Domain-specific vocabulary: "visual literacy," "multimodal communication"

Body Paragraph 3 — Effect 3: Speed and permanence
  Fact: Messages are instant but also permanent — screenshots, digital footprints
  Detail: This creates both convenience and risk; the paradox of fast but permanent
  Domain-specific vocabulary: "digital footprint," "ephemeral content"

Conclusion: Synthesize the three effects; note that the changes are neither entirely
  positive nor negative but represent a fundamental shift in human communication
```

Say: "Notice that our outline uses an objective tone — we are examining effects, not arguing whether social media is good or bad. That is the informative stance."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently select an organizational strategy, write a focus statement, and draft an informative introduction.

Choose ONE of the following topics (or propose your own):

1. How does the human body respond to extreme cold?
2. What are the different types of volcanoes and how do they form?
3. How has the role of robots in everyday life changed over the past 20 years?
4. What causes food allergies and how do they affect daily life?

**Task:**
1. Select the organizational strategy that best fits your topic (definition, classification, comparison/contrast, or cause/effect). Explain your choice in one sentence.
2. Write a focus statement that previews the categories or structure of your text.
3. Draft an introductory paragraph (hook + context + focus statement) in formal, objective style.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Select a strategy, write a focus statement, and draft an introduction as described.
**Stretch:** Complete the on-level task, then create a complete informative outline with three body paragraph plans, each including at least one fact and one domain-specific vocabulary term.
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task, then draft one body paragraph using precise language, domain-specific vocabulary defined in context, and at least two transitions.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.2d) Rewrite the following sentence using precise, domain-specific vocabulary: "The weather got really bad and a lot of rain fell, which caused the river to overflow." (Expected: "Severe thunderstorms produced approximately four inches of precipitation within six hours, causing the river to exceed its floodplain and inundate the surrounding lowlands.")
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1a) Write a debatable claim for this topic: "Whether schools should teach cursive handwriting." Include at least two previewed reasons. (Expected: "Schools should continue teaching cursive handwriting because it strengthens fine motor development, improves reading comprehension of historical documents, and activates brain regions associated with memory and learning.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following focus statement and identify the organizational strategy being used. Then explain your reasoning in one sentence.

"While traditional libraries and digital libraries both aim to provide public access to information, they differ significantly in their physical accessibility, the types of resources they offer, and the way users interact with their collections."

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
Comparison/Contrast. The focus statement signals this strategy through the language "while both... they differ" and previews three specific points of comparison (physical accessibility, resource types, user interaction).

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies comparison/contrast AND provides reasoning that references specific language from the focus statement (e.g., "while both... they differ" or the three comparison categories).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the Organizational Strategy Reference chart and review the signal words for each strategy
- Provide four sample focus statements (one per strategy) and have the student match each one to its strategy
- Ask: "Does this focus statement take a position? Or does it announce what the text will examine?" If the student confuses it with a claim, revisit the difference between argumentative and informative purpose

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 6, where they will develop and organize a complete informative text
- Encourage the student to complete the outline for their informative text and begin drafting body paragraphs

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Strategy Experiment:** Take the topic you chose for independent practice and write a focus statement using a DIFFERENT organizational strategy. How does the change in strategy shift the focus of the text?
2. **Informative Text Analysis:** Find a nonfiction article in a magazine, textbook, or news site. Identify the organizational strategy used. Highlight the signal words that reveal the strategy. Could the writer have chosen a different strategy? How would the text change?
3. **Domain Vocabulary Builder:** Choose a topic you find interesting and list five domain-specific vocabulary terms related to that topic. Write a student-friendly definition for each. Then write a paragraph about the topic that uses at least three of the terms, defining each in context.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The biggest challenge in this lesson is helping students understand the difference between informative and argumentative writing when both use evidence and formal style. The key distinction is purpose: argumentative writing advocates for a position; informative writing examines a topic objectively. When students slip into opinion in their informative writing (e.g., "Social media is bad for teenagers"), redirect: "Is that a fact or a judgment? How would you restate it objectively?" ("Research suggests that excessive social media use correlates with increased rates of adolescent anxiety.")
- Organizational strategies should be chosen deliberately, not randomly. When students struggle to select a strategy, ask: "Are you explaining what something is (definition)? Grouping things into categories (classification)? Comparing two things (comparison/contrast)? Or explaining why something happens (cause/effect)?" The question embedded in the topic often points to the best strategy.
- Domain-specific vocabulary is a key Grade 7 expectation (W.7.2d). Encourage students to define terms in context (appositive phrases, parenthetical definitions, or explanatory clauses) rather than listing definitions separately.
- The model text about ocean currents is deliberately content-rich. It demonstrates that informative writing can be both rigorous and engaging — it does not have to be dry or textbook-like. Point out the vivid hook and specific data as features that make informative writing compelling.
- Students who excelled at argumentative writing may find the shift to informative writing challenging because they are accustomed to taking a position. Reinforce that being objective does not mean being passive — it means letting the facts speak for themselves.
