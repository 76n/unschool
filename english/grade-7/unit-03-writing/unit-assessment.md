# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3: Writing

## Unit Assessment

### Overview

This assessment evaluates student mastery of the three core writing standards and revision skills addressed in Unit 3: argumentative writing (W.7.1), informative/explanatory writing (W.7.2), narrative writing (W.7.3), and revision/editing (all standards). The assessment has four sections:

- **Section 1 — Argumentative Writing (W.7.1):** Write an argument with a claim, evidence, reasoning, and counterclaim acknowledgment (15 points)
- **Section 2 — Informative Writing (W.7.2):** Write an informative text examining a topic with an organizational strategy, development, and domain-specific vocabulary (15 points)
- **Section 3 — Narrative Writing (W.7.3):** Write a narrative with context, point of view, characterization, dialogue, pacing, and description (15 points)
- **Section 4 — Revision and Editing (W.7.1-3):** Identify and correct errors in a provided draft (15 points)

**Time:** 75-90 minutes (recommended: 15-20 minutes per section)

**Total Points:** 60 points

**Mastery Criteria:** 42/60 (70%) overall.

---

## Section 1 — Argumentative Writing (W.7.1)

**15 points**

**Prompt:**

Your school district is considering a new policy that would limit students to no more than 90 minutes of homework per night across all classes combined. Teachers would be required to coordinate assignments so that the total does not exceed this limit. Supporters say the policy will reduce student stress and allow time for extracurricular activities, family, and rest. Opponents say it will lower academic standards and prevent teachers from assigning the work necessary for students to master challenging material.

Write an argumentative essay in which you take a clear position on this issue. Your essay must include:

- An introduction with a hook, context, and a clearly stated claim that previews your reasons
- At least two body paragraphs, each developing a reason with specific, relevant evidence from credible sources and a reasoning chain (What does it mean? How does it connect? Why does it matter?)
- A counterclaim paragraph that acknowledges the strongest opposing argument fairly and responds with evidence or reasoning
- Cohesive transitions that clarify relationships among claims, counterclaims, reasons, and evidence
- A conclusion that synthesizes the argument (does not simply restate it)
- A consistently formal style throughout (no contractions, no first person, no slang)

Write at least five paragraphs (introduction, two or more body paragraphs, counterclaim paragraph, conclusion).

---

### Argumentative Writing Rubric (15 points: 5 criteria x 3 points at Proficient)

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Claim and Introduction (W.7.1a)** | No claim is stated, or the essay argues multiple positions without committing; no introduction structure | A position is stated but is vague, not debatable, or does not preview reasons; introduction is incomplete | A clear, debatable claim is stated in a well-structured introduction with a hook, context, and at least two previewed reasons | A compelling, precisely worded claim is embedded in an engaging introduction that builds urgency and clearly frames the argument |
| **Evidence and Reasoning (W.7.1b)** | No evidence or reasoning; the essay consists of unsupported opinions | One reason with vague evidence; reasoning chain is incomplete (evidence is dropped without explanation) | At least two reasons are supported by specific evidence from credible sources with complete reasoning chains (interpretation, connection, significance) | Multiple reasons are strategically ordered with well-chosen evidence; reasoning is thorough and insightful; evidence from different types of sources |
| **Counterclaim (W.7.1a, W.7.1c)** | No counterclaim is acknowledged | A counterclaim is mentioned but dismissed without evidence or reasoning ("they are wrong") | A counterclaim is acknowledged fairly (steel man) and responded to with evidence or reasoning using a clear strategy (concede/refute, expose the flaw, or reframe) | Multiple counterclaims are addressed, or a single counterclaim is handled with exceptional nuance; response includes specific evidence and demonstrates genuine engagement with the opposing view |
| **Cohesion and Transitions (W.7.1c)** | No transitions; paragraphs are disconnected; ideas do not flow | Simple transitions only ("first," "also," "next"); relationships among ideas are unclear | Varied transitions clarify relationships among claims, reasons, evidence, and counterclaims; the essay reads as a unified argument | Transitions are seamlessly integrated and varied; cohesive devices include repeated key terms, parallel structure, and transitional clauses; the essay feels like a single, building argument |
| **Conclusion and Style (W.7.1d, W.7.1e)** | No conclusion; style is informal with contractions, first person, and/or slang | Conclusion restates the claim without synthesis; style is mostly formal but with lapses | Conclusion synthesizes the argument using a clear strategy (call to action, forward look, broader significance); formal style is maintained throughout | Conclusion is powerful and resonant, connecting the specific argument to broader implications; formal style is polished and authoritative throughout |

**Scoring:** Rate each criterion 1-4. Calculate average and convert:
- Advanced (avg 3.5-4.0): 14-15 points
- Proficient (avg 2.5-3.4): 10-13 points
- Developing (avg 1.5-2.4): 6-9 points
- Beginning (avg 1.0-1.4): 1-5 points

### Sample Proficient Argumentative Response

> **The Case for a Homework Limit**
>
> At 11:47 on a Wednesday night, a seventh grader sits at a kitchen table surrounded by three open textbooks, a half-finished math worksheet, a science lab report, and an English essay due in nine hours. The student has been working since 4:00 p.m., with only a short break for dinner. This is not an unusual scenario — it is, for millions of middle school students, a routine one. School districts should implement a 90-minute nightly homework cap because excessive homework has been shown to increase adolescent stress without proportional academic benefit, and because a coordinated limit ensures that students retain time for the physical activity, sleep, and family interaction essential to healthy development.
>
> The most compelling reason to cap homework at 90 minutes is that research consistently shows diminishing academic returns beyond that threshold. A landmark study by Dr. Harris Cooper at Duke University, which synthesized data from more than 60 studies conducted over a 25-year period, found that for middle school students, the correlation between homework and academic achievement plateaus at approximately 60 to 90 minutes per night. Beyond that point, additional homework does not produce measurable gains in test scores or grades and begins to generate negative effects, including increased anxiety, sleep deprivation, and a growing resentment toward schoolwork itself. This finding is critical because it dismantles the assumption that more homework always equals more learning. In reality, the quality and purposefulness of homework matter far more than the quantity, and a 90-minute cap forces teachers to assign only the most valuable, targeted practice rather than piling on busywork.
>
> Furthermore, a homework limit protects the time students need for activities that contribute to their overall well-being. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that adolescents get at least 60 minutes of physical activity daily and 8 to 10 hours of sleep per night. When homework routinely consumes three or more hours of a student's evening — as surveys by the National Education Association indicate it does for approximately 40% of middle school students — those recommendations become impossible to meet. Sleep deprivation alone has been linked to a 30% increase in symptoms of depression and anxiety among adolescents, according to research published in the Journal of Youth and Adolescence. A homework cap does not eliminate homework; it creates space for the sleep, exercise, and human connection that research identifies as essential to the academic performance homework is supposed to support.
>
> Opponents of a homework limit argue that capping assignments would lower academic standards, particularly in rigorous subjects like mathematics and science, where repetitive practice is essential for skill mastery. This concern reflects a legitimate commitment to academic excellence, and it deserves serious consideration. However, it rests on the assumption that more practice always means better outcomes, which Cooper's research directly contradicts. Moreover, a 90-minute cap does not prohibit challenging assignments — it requires teachers to be strategic about what they assign. The Wake County, North Carolina school district implemented a homework coordination policy in 2019 that limits total nightly homework to 70 minutes for middle schoolers, and after two years, the district reported no decline in standardized test scores and a 15% reduction in student-reported stress. Strategic limitation, not elimination, is the goal.
>
> The evidence points in a single direction: excessive homework harms students without helping them academically, and a coordinated 90-minute cap protects both learning and well-being. Schools exist to educate the whole student, not just the student who sits at a desk. When districts implement reasonable homework limits, they do not lower standards — they raise the standard for what homework should accomplish. The student at the kitchen table at midnight deserves a policy that values quality over quantity, and the research makes clear that such a policy would benefit every student in the building.

## Section 2 — Informative Writing (W.7.2)

**15 points**

**Prompt:**

Write an informative text that examines ONE of the following topics:

1. How the human immune system defends the body against illness
2. The major types of natural disasters and how they occur
3. How music affects the human brain

Your informative text must include:

- An introduction with a hook, context, and a focus statement that previews the organizational strategy (definition, classification, comparison/contrast, or cause/effect)
- At least two body paragraphs, each developed with relevant facts, definitions, concrete details, and/or examples
- At least three domain-specific vocabulary terms, each defined in context (using appositive definitions, parenthetical definitions, or explanatory clauses)
- Transitions that connect ideas within and between paragraphs and clarify the organizational structure
- A conclusion that synthesizes key information and provides a final insight or reflection
- A consistently formal, objective tone throughout (no personal opinions)

Write at least four paragraphs (introduction, two or more body paragraphs, conclusion).

---

### Informative Writing Rubric (15 points: 5 criteria x 3 points at Proficient)

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Introduction and Focus (W.7.2a)** | No introduction, or the text begins without a focus statement; no organizational strategy is apparent | An introduction is present but missing a hook, context, or focus statement; organizational strategy is vague | The introduction includes a hook, context, and a clear focus statement that previews the organizational strategy and categories/subtopics | The introduction is polished and engaging; the focus statement builds curiosity and provides a clear roadmap; organizational strategy is named or strongly implied |
| **Development (W.7.2b, W.7.2d)** | Body paragraphs are vague; no facts, definitions, or details; fewer than two domain-specific terms | Some facts and details present but development is thin; domain-specific terms are used but not defined in context | Each body paragraph is developed with multiple facts and concrete details; at least three domain-specific terms are defined in context; development tools (fact, definition, detail, quotation, example) are varied | Body paragraphs are richly developed with precise, accurate information; domain-specific vocabulary is used fluently; multiple development tools create thorough examination of the topic |
| **Organization and Transitions (W.7.2a, W.7.2c)** | No organization; paragraphs are disconnected; no transitions | Some structure apparent; simple transitions used but relationships among ideas are unclear | Information is grouped logically by the chosen organizational strategy; transitions connect ideas within and between paragraphs purposefully | Organization is seamless; transitions are varied and contribute to coherence and flow; the organizational strategy enhances the reader's understanding |
| **Conclusion (W.7.2f)** | No conclusion, or the text ends abruptly | The conclusion restates the introduction without synthesizing or offering insight | The conclusion synthesizes key information and provides a final insight, reflection, or statement of significance | The conclusion is powerful, connecting the topic to broader implications; it leaves the reader with a clear understanding of why the topic matters |
| **Tone and Conventions (W.7.2e)** | Tone is informal or opinionated; frequent errors interfere with readability | Tone is mostly objective but occasionally slips into opinion; several convention errors | Tone is consistently objective and formal; minor convention errors do not interfere with meaning | Tone is authoritative and engaging while remaining objective; conventions are flawless |

**Scoring:** Same scale as Section 1.

### Sample Proficient Informative Response

> **How Music Affects the Human Brain**
>
> A surgeon performs a delicate operation while classical music plays softly in the background. A student slips on headphones before a final exam, letting a familiar song calm her nerves. A physical therapist asks a stroke patient to tap along to a rhythmic beat as part of a rehabilitation exercise. In each of these scenarios, music is not simply entertainment — it is a tool that alters how the brain functions. Music affects the human brain through three primary mechanisms: by activating the brain's reward system, by enhancing memory consolidation, and by stimulating neuroplasticity in damaged or developing brains.
>
> The most immediate effect of music on the brain is the activation of the mesolimbic reward system — the same neural circuit that responds to food, social bonding, and other pleasurable experiences. When a person listens to music they enjoy, neurons in a region called the nucleus accumbens release dopamine, a neurotransmitter associated with pleasure, motivation, and emotional regulation. A landmark study by neuroscientist Valorie Salimpoor at McGill University used PET scans to measure dopamine release in subjects listening to their favorite music and found that peak dopamine levels occurred not only during the most emotionally intense moments of a piece but also in the seconds of anticipation before those moments. This finding reveals that music engages the brain's reward system in a uniquely dynamic way: the brain derives pleasure both from the experience of hearing a musical passage and from the prediction of what is coming next. This anticipatory pleasure is one reason music can be so emotionally powerful — the brain is, in a sense, composing its own expectations alongside the composer.
>
> Music also enhances the brain's ability to encode and retrieve memories, a process known as memory consolidation. The hippocampus — a seahorse-shaped structure in the temporal lobe responsible for forming new long-term memories — responds strongly to musical stimuli, particularly when music is paired with verbal or visual information. Research published in the journal Memory and Cognition found that students who studied vocabulary words while listening to background music recalled 14% more words after a one-week delay than students who studied in silence. Clinicians have applied this finding in therapeutic settings: music therapy programs for patients with Alzheimer's disease use familiar songs to activate autobiographical memories that would otherwise be inaccessible. A patient who cannot remember a family member's name may still be able to sing every word of a song from their adolescence, because musical memories are stored across multiple brain regions and are therefore more resistant to the neural degradation that characterizes dementia.
>
> Perhaps the most remarkable effect of music on the brain is its ability to stimulate neuroplasticity — the brain's capacity to form new neural connections and reorganize existing ones. Studies of professional musicians have found that regions of the motor cortex responsible for fine finger movement are significantly larger and more densely connected in musicians than in non-musicians, a structural difference that develops in direct response to years of practice. This principle extends beyond professional musicians: research at Northwestern University demonstrated that children who received as little as two years of group music instruction showed measurable improvements in auditory processing, reading ability, and speech perception in noisy environments. The brain literally rewires itself in response to musical training, strengthening circuits that are useful far beyond the practice room.
>
> From the dopamine surge of a favorite chorus to the neural reorganization produced by years of practice, music's effects on the brain are profound, measurable, and far-reaching. As neuroscience continues to map the pathways through which sound shapes cognition, emotion, and recovery, music is increasingly recognized not as a cultural luxury but as a biological necessity — a force that does not merely accompany human life but actively shapes the organ responsible for experiencing it.

## Section 3 — Narrative Writing (W.7.3)

**15 points**

**Prompt:**

Write a narrative about a character who discovers that something they believed to be true is actually wrong — and must decide what to do with that knowledge. The story may be realistic or entirely imagined.

Your narrative must include:

- An opening that engages the reader and establishes context, point of view (first person or third person limited), and at least one character
- A clear sequence of events with rising action, a turning point, and a resolution
- Indirect characterization using at least two SAID techniques (Speech, Actions, Internal thoughts, Details)
- At least one expanded moment with sensory details from at least two senses
- At least one compressed section that summarizes less important events
- At least one exchange of dialogue that reveals character or advances the plot (correctly punctuated)
- A variety of transition words, phrases, and clauses to convey sequence and signal shifts
- Precise words and phrases and relevant descriptive details — no vague language
- A conclusion that follows from the narrated events and reflects on their meaning

Write at least five paragraphs.

---

### Narrative Writing Rubric (15 points: 5 criteria x 3 points at Proficient)

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Context, POV, and Character (W.7.3a)** | No clear character, setting, or context; point of view shifts or is unclear | One or two elements present but underdeveloped; point of view is inconsistent | Opening engages the reader and establishes context, consistent point of view, and at least one character through indirect characterization (two or more SAID techniques) | Opening is immediately compelling; character is vivid and multi-dimensional; context is woven seamlessly into the action; POV is used strategically |
| **Pacing and Structure (W.7.3b, W.7.3c)** | Events are listed at uniform speed; no expansion or compression; no clear structure | Some pacing variation attempted; structure is loosely present but lacks a clear turning point | At least one moment is expanded with sensory detail; at least one section is compressed; the story has a clear rising action, turning point, and resolution; transitions signal shifts | Pacing is deliberately controlled throughout; expansion and compression serve the story's emotional arc; transitions are varied and sophisticated |
| **Dialogue and Technique (W.7.3b)** | No dialogue; narrative techniques are absent | Dialogue attempted but feels unnatural or is incorrectly punctuated; limited technique | Dialogue sounds natural, reveals character or advances plot, and is correctly punctuated; narrative techniques (showing, internal thought, action beats) are present | Dialogue is distinctive and memorable; subtext is present; techniques are varied and confident |
| **Description and Language (W.7.3d)** | Language is vague and generic; no sensory details | Some specific language but mixed with vague descriptions; sensory details from one sense only | Precise words and phrases throughout; sensory details from at least two senses; descriptive details are relevant and purposeful | Language is vivid and original; sensory details are immersive; every descriptive choice serves mood, character, or theme |
| **Conclusion and Conventions (W.7.3e)** | No conclusion; story ends abruptly; frequent convention errors | A conclusion is present but rushed or disconnected from events; several errors | The conclusion follows naturally from the events, provides closure, and reflects on meaning; minor convention errors do not interfere | The conclusion is resonant and satisfying; it illuminates something the reader did not expect; conventions are flawless |

**Scoring:** Same scale as Sections 1 and 2.

---

### Sample Proficient Narrative Response

> **The Map on the Wall**
>
> There was a map of the world on the wall of Mr. Okafor's history classroom, and for the first three months of seventh grade, Zara had never given it more than a glance. It was just a map — countries in different colors, oceans in pale blue, a few faded push pins marking places someone had cared about once. But on a Tuesday in November, during a lesson on the Mercator projection, Mr. Okafor said something that made Zara sit up in her chair.
>
> "This map," he said, tapping the familiar rectangle with the back of his dry-erase marker, "is lying to you."
>
> Zara looked at the map. It was the same map she had seen in every classroom since kindergarten. North America was in the center. Greenland looked almost as big as Africa. Everything seemed normal.
>
> "Greenland," Mr. Okafor continued, "appears to be roughly the same size as Africa on this projection. In reality, Africa is fourteen times larger." He pulled up a second image on the projector — a Peters projection, where Africa stretched long and enormous down the center of the frame, and Greenland shrank to a modest island near the top. "The Mercator projection was designed in 1569 for navigation, not for accuracy. It distorts the size of landmasses near the poles and shrinks those near the equator. The result is that regions in the Global North — Europe, North America, Russia — look far larger than they actually are, while regions near the equator — Africa, South America, Southeast Asia — appear far smaller."
>
> The rest of the class moved on, but Zara did not. She sat with her notebook open, pen unmoving, staring at the two projections side by side on the screen. Fourteen times larger. She thought about the flat, colorful map in her grandmother's kitchen — the one her grandmother had pointed to when telling stories about Lagos, about the market near the Third Mainland Bridge, about the house with the blue gate. On that kitchen map, Nigeria was a small wedge on the western coast of a continent that looked no bigger than a generous-sized island. But it was not a generous-sized island. It was a continent so large that the United States, China, and most of Europe could fit inside it with room to spare.
>
> She raised her hand. Mr. Okafor nodded.
>
> "If the map is wrong," she said, and she could hear the tightness in her own voice, "why is it still on the wall?"
>
> Mr. Okafor looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked at the map. "That," he said, "is the best question anyone has asked me all year."
>
> He did not take the map down. But that afternoon, Zara stayed after class and asked if she could put the Peters projection next to it — not to replace the Mercator, but to let people see both. Mr. Okafor handed her a roll of tape.
>
> She taped the printout to the wall beside the old map and stepped back. The two projections hung side by side — one familiar, one strange, both claiming to represent the same world. Zara stood there for a while, looking at the space between them. She thought about all the things she had accepted without question simply because they had been on a wall, in a textbook, in a voice she trusted. She thought about the blue-gated house in Lagos and the continent that held it — a continent so vast it could swallow countries whole, reduced on a flat sheet of paper to something that looked like it could fit in her backpack.
>
> She pulled her notebook from her bag and wrote a single sentence at the top of a blank page: *What else have I been looking at without seeing?*
>
> It was not an answer. But it was, she decided, the right question.

## Section 4 — Revision and Editing (W.7.1-3)

**15 points**

**Directions:** The following draft contains errors in content, organization, evidence, reasoning, style, grammar, and mechanics. Read the draft carefully, then complete the tasks that follow.

### Draft to Revise and Edit

> **(1)** Schools should get rid of standardized tests. **(2)** Standardized tests are bad for students because they cause stress and they don't really show what students know. **(3)** I think that if schools stopped giving these tests everyone would be happier and learn more.
>
> **(4)** First of all, tests make kids stressed out. **(5)** A lot of students get really nervous before tests and some of them even get sick. **(6)** This proves that tests are harmful. **(7)** Furthermore, studies show that tests are not a good way to measure learning. **(8)** Students who are bad at taking tests might actually be really smart but the test doesn't show it.
>
> **(9)** Some people say that tests are necessary because teachers need to know how students are doing, but I disagree because tests are still bad even if teachers need them.
>
> **(10)** In conclusion, standardized tests should be eliminated because they are stressful and inaccurate. **(11)** I have shown that tests hurt students and don't work. **(12)** Schools should find a better way to assess students.

---

### Task A — Revision (8 points)

Answer the following questions about the draft's content and structure. For questions that ask you to rewrite, write your revision on the lines provided.

**A1. (2 points)** Sentence 2 contains the draft's claim. Rewrite it as a strong, formal, seventh-grade claim that previews at least two specific reasons and avoids informal language.

**Expected answer:** "School districts should replace standardized testing with portfolio-based assessment because standardized tests generate disproportionate anxiety without measuring the full range of student abilities, and because alternative assessment methods provide teachers with more actionable information about student learning."

**Scoring:** 2 points for a claim that is debatable, formal (no contractions, no "I think," no slang), and previews at least two specific reasons. 1 point if the claim is improved but still has one informal element or previews only one reason. 0 points if the claim is unchanged or remains vague.

---

**A2. (2 points)** Sentences 4-6 form a body paragraph, but the evidence is vague and the reasoning chain is incomplete. Rewrite this paragraph with specific evidence from a credible source and a complete reasoning chain (What does it mean? How does it connect? Why does it matter?).

**Expected answer:** "The most significant concern about standardized testing is its measurable impact on student mental health. According to a study published in the Journal of Educational Psychology, 61% of students reported moderate to severe anxiety specifically related to high-stakes standardized tests, and students in the highest anxiety group scored an average of 12 percentile points lower than their classroom performance would predict. This discrepancy reveals that standardized tests do not merely measure knowledge — they measure a student's ability to perform under artificial psychological pressure. When a student who consistently demonstrates mastery in the classroom receives a low test score because anxiety impaired their performance, the test has failed to capture what it was designed to measure. The result is an assessment system that penalizes the most vulnerable students rather than accurately representing their abilities."

**Scoring:** 2 points for a paragraph with specific evidence from a named/plausible source, data or detail, and a reasoning chain with interpretation, connection, and significance. 1 point if evidence is present but vague, or reasoning chain is incomplete. 0 points if the paragraph remains vague or unsupported.

---

**A3. (2 points)** Sentence 9 is the counterclaim paragraph. It acknowledges a counterclaim but dismisses it without evidence. Rewrite it as a full counterclaim response using one of the three strategies (Concede and Refute, Expose the Flaw, or Reframe the Issue). Include evidence in your response.

**Expected answer (Concede and Refute):** "Proponents of standardized testing argue that these assessments provide essential data that teachers and administrators need to track student progress and identify achievement gaps across schools and districts. This function is genuinely important — without some form of common measurement, districts would have no way to compare outcomes or allocate resources where they are most needed. However, standardized tests are not the only tool capable of providing this data. Portfolio assessments, which collect samples of student work over time, allow teachers to evaluate growth, skill application, and depth of understanding in ways that a single test cannot. A pilot program in the New Hampshire Performance Assessment Network demonstrated that portfolio-based assessment provided district-level data that was as reliable as standardized test scores while also giving teachers specific, actionable information about individual students' strengths and areas for growth. The question is not whether schools need assessment data — they do — but whether a single high-stakes test is the best way to collect it."

**Scoring:** 2 points for a counterclaim paragraph that acknowledges the counterclaim fairly, uses a clear strategy, and includes evidence in the response. 1 point if the counterclaim is acknowledged but the response lacks evidence or the strategy is unclear. 0 points if the counterclaim is still dismissed without reasoning or evidence.

---

**A4. (2 points)** Sentences 10-12 form the conclusion. Identify TWO specific problems with this conclusion and rewrite it using a conclusion strategy (call to action, forward look, return to the hook, or broader significance).

**Expected answer — Problems:** (1) Begins with "In conclusion" (weak, formulaic opening). (2) Uses first person "I have shown" (breaks formal style). (3) Simply restates the claim instead of synthesizing. (4) Does not connect to broader significance.

**Rewritten:** "The evidence is clear: standardized tests impose measurable psychological harm on students while producing scores that fail to capture the depth and complexity of real learning. Schools exist to develop capable, confident, independent thinkers — and an assessment system that reduces a year of growth to a single number on a single morning undermines that mission. When districts replace standardized testing with assessments that value process, growth, and authentic demonstration of skill, they send a powerful message: what matters is not how a student performs under pressure but what a student truly knows and can do."

**Scoring:** 2 points for correctly identifying at least two problems AND providing a rewritten conclusion that synthesizes, uses a clear strategy, and maintains formal style. 1 point if only one problem is identified or the rewritten conclusion still summarizes rather than synthesizes. 0 points if no problems are identified or the conclusion is unchanged.

---

### Task B — Editing (7 points)

Identify and correct the following errors in the original draft. Write the corrected version of each sentence.

**B1. (1 point)** Sentence 2 contains a contraction. Identify it and write the corrected version using formal language.

**Expected answer:** "don't" should be "do not." Corrected: "...they do not really show what students know." (Note: this sentence should also be revised for content per A1, but B1 assesses only the contraction.)

---

**B2. (1 point)** Sentence 3 contains a run-on (missing comma before a subordinate clause or between independent clauses). Identify and correct it.

**Expected answer:** "I think that if schools stopped giving these tests everyone would be happier" — needs a comma after "tests": "if schools stopped giving these tests, everyone would be happier and learn more."

---

**B3. (1 point)** Sentence 3 uses first person ("I think"). Rewrite the sentence in formal third person, removing the opinion marker.

**Expected answer:** "If schools eliminated standardized testing, students would experience reduced anxiety and greater engagement in learning." (Or similar — must remove "I think" and maintain formal tone.)

---

**B4. (1 point)** Sentence 5 is a run-on sentence. Correct it using one of the three methods: (a) separate into two sentences, (b) add a comma and coordinating conjunction, or (c) use a semicolon.

**Expected answer:** "A lot of students get really nervous before tests, and some of them even get sick." Or: "A lot of students get really nervous before tests. Some of them even get sick." Or: "A lot of students get really nervous before tests; some of them even get sick."

---

**B5. (1 point)** Sentence 8 contains a run-on sentence. Correct it.

**Expected answer:** "Students who are bad at taking tests might actually be really smart, but the test doesn't show it." (Add comma before "but.") — Or better yet, with formal style: "Students who perform poorly on standardized tests may possess strong academic abilities that the test format fails to capture."

---

**B6. (1 point)** Sentences 2, 8, and 11 all contain contractions that violate formal style. List all three contractions and their formal replacements.

**Expected answer:**
- Sentence 2: "don't" → "do not"
- Sentence 8: "doesn't" → "does not"
- Sentence 11: "don't" → "do not"

---

**B7. (1 point)** The draft uses informal language throughout. Identify TWO examples of informal word choice (not contractions — those are covered in B6) and provide formal replacements.

**Expected answer (any two):**
- "get rid of" (Sentence 1) → "eliminate" or "replace"
- "bad" (Sentence 2) → "harmful" or "detrimental"
- "kids" (Sentence 4) → "students"
- "stressed out" (Sentence 4) → "experiencing significant anxiety"
- "really nervous" (Sentence 5) → "severe test anxiety"
- "bad at taking tests" (Sentence 8) → "perform poorly on standardized assessments"
- "really smart" (Sentence 8) → "demonstrate strong academic abilities"
- "still bad" (Sentence 9) → "remain ineffective"

**Scoring:** 1 point for identifying two informal examples with appropriate formal replacements.

## Administration Notes

- **Timing:** Allow 75-90 minutes total. Students may work at their own pace within that window. Recommended allocation: 15-20 minutes per section. If students need more time, consider administering the assessment across two sessions (Sections 1-2 in one session, Sections 3-4 in another).
- **Reference materials:** Students may use the Transition Reference Chart from Lessons 2 and 6, the Concession-Refutation Sentence Frames from Lesson 3, and the Revision/Editing Checklists from Lesson 9. They should NOT use their previous drafts, graphic organizers, or model texts — this assessment measures independent application.
- **Section 4 draft:** The revision and editing draft should be available for reference throughout Section 4. Students should annotate the draft before writing corrections.
- **Accommodations:** Students with IEPs or 504 plans should receive their documented accommodations (extended time, reduced quantity, dictation, etc.). For students who struggle with writing stamina, consider administering one section per session across four days.
- **Scoring priorities:** When scoring writing sections (1-3), read the entire piece before assigning scores on any criterion. Focus on what the student CAN do, not just what is missing. Look for evidence of the specific Grade 7 skills taught in the unit:
  - Argumentative: counterclaim acknowledgment (not just reasons and evidence), reasoning chains, cohesive transitions, formal style
  - Informative: organizational strategy, domain-specific vocabulary defined in context, development with multiple tools, objective tone
  - Narrative: consistent point of view, indirect characterization (SAID), expansion and compression, purposeful dialogue, meaningful conclusion
- **Re-assessment:** Students who do not meet mastery may be re-assessed after targeted reteaching. Focus reteaching on the specific standard(s) where the student scored Developing or Beginning. A re-assessment should use different prompts but assess the same skills.
