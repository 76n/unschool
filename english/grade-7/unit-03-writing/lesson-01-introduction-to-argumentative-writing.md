# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Introduction to Argumentative Writing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify and explain the components of an argument: claim, reasons, evidence, and counterclaim
- Write a clear, debatable claim that previews reasons and takes a definitive position
- Distinguish between Grade 6 argumentation (claim + reasons + evidence) and Grade 7 argumentation (adding counterclaim acknowledgment, cohesive devices, and formal style)

## Prerequisites
- W.6.1: Experience writing arguments with claims supported by reasons and evidence
- W.6.1a: Ability to introduce a claim and organize reasons logically
- W.6.1b: Experience using the Introduce-Cite-Explain (ICE) pattern for evidence
- Familiarity with the difference between opinion, claim, and evidence from Grade 6

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Copy of the model argumentative text (provided below)
- Graphic organizer: Claim-Counterclaim-Evidence chart (described below)
- Highlighters (four colors)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge from Grade 6 argumentative writing and introduce the concept of counterclaims.

**Activity: Two Sides of Every Argument**

Display the following claim on the board:

"Schools should replace all paper textbooks with digital tablets."

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned how to write a claim and back it up with reasons and evidence. Let's see if you remember. Take 60 seconds and jot down one reason that supports this claim and one piece of evidence that could prove it."

After 60 seconds, take a response. Then say: "Good. Now here is what is new this year. I want you to think about someone who disagrees with this claim. What would they argue? Take 30 seconds and write down the strongest argument AGAINST replacing textbooks with tablets."

Take two or three responses. Write one on the board.

Say: "That argument against your position is called a counterclaim. In sixth grade, you built arguments. In seventh grade, you build arguments AND deal with the other side. A strong argument does not pretend the other side does not exist — it faces the opposition head-on and explains why your position is still stronger. That is the big leap we are making this year."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the full structure of a Grade 7 argument, emphasizing counterclaim acknowledgment as the key new skill.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Claim:** A debatable statement that takes a clear position on an issue and can be supported with evidence
- **Counterclaim:** The opposing position; what someone who disagrees with your claim would argue
- **Evidence:** Specific facts, statistics, expert opinions, or examples from credible sources that support a reason
- **Cohesion:** The quality of writing that makes ideas flow logically and connect smoothly through transitions, repeated key terms, and clear relationships between sentences

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Let me show you how seventh-grade argumentative writing builds on what you already know."

Write on chart paper:

| Grade 6 Argument | Grade 7 Argument (NEW) |
|---|---|
| Claim with previewed reasons | Same — but more precisely worded |
| Reasons supported by evidence (ICE) | Same — but evidence must come from credible sources |
| Transitions between paragraphs | Cohesive devices that clarify relationships among claims, counterclaims, reasons, and evidence |
| Concluding statement | Concluding section that follows from and supports the argument |
| — | **Acknowledge and respond to counterclaims** |
| — | **Maintain a consistently formal style** |

Say: "The two biggest additions are counterclaims and formal style. A counterclaim is the strongest argument the other side could make. You do not ignore it. You do not pretend it does not exist. You bring it into your essay, treat it fairly, and then explain why your evidence is stronger. This is what makes your argument credible — it shows you have thought about the issue from multiple angles."

Say: "Formal style means you write like a scholar, not like you are texting a friend. No slang. No contractions. No 'I think' or 'in my opinion.' Your evidence speaks for itself."

### Model Text / Worked Example

Read the following model argumentative text aloud. Display it so the student can follow along.

> **Should Middle Schools Require Students to Take a Financial Literacy Course?**
>
> By the time most Americans reach adulthood, they are expected to manage bank accounts, pay taxes, budget monthly expenses, and make decisions about credit cards and student loans — yet the vast majority have never received a single hour of formal instruction on any of these topics. This gap between expectation and preparation is both avoidable and harmful. All middle schools should require students to complete a semester-long financial literacy course because early financial education builds lifelong money-management habits, because financially literate young people are less likely to fall into debt as adults, and because the middle school years represent a developmentally ideal window for introducing abstract economic concepts.
>
> The most compelling reason to require financial literacy education in middle school is that habits formed during adolescence tend to persist into adulthood. A longitudinal study published in the Journal of Financial Planning tracked 1,200 students who received financial education between ages 11 and 14 and found that, ten years later, these individuals were 32% more likely to maintain a household budget and 28% more likely to have an emergency savings fund compared to peers who did not receive such education. These figures are significant because they demonstrate that financial habits are not simply learned in adulthood through trial and error — they are shaped by deliberate instruction during the formative years. Teaching students to budget, save, and distinguish between needs and wants at age twelve gives them a decade of practice before they face the financial responsibilities of adulthood.
>
> Furthermore, financial literacy education directly reduces the likelihood of future debt. According to the National Endowment for Financial Education, adults who received financial education as adolescents carry, on average, 23% less credit card debt and are significantly less likely to take out predatory loans. The connection is straightforward: students who understand how interest compounds, how credit scores work, and how to evaluate the true cost of a purchase are equipped to make informed financial decisions rather than impulsive ones. Without this education, young adults enter a marketplace designed to exploit their inexperience.
>
> Some opponents argue that financial literacy should be taught at home, not at school, and that adding another required course overburdens an already crowded curriculum. This counterclaim has surface appeal, but it does not withstand scrutiny. A survey by the Council for Economic Education found that only 17% of parents feel confident teaching their children about personal finance, and in low-income households, where financial stress is highest, parents are least likely to have the knowledge or resources to provide this instruction. Relying on home-based financial education guarantees that the students who need it most will be the least likely to receive it. A school-based requirement ensures equity — every student, regardless of household income, receives the same foundational knowledge.
>
> The evidence is clear: middle school financial literacy courses build lasting habits, reduce future debt, and close an equity gap that home-based instruction cannot address. Schools have a responsibility to prepare students not only for academic success but for the practical realities of adult life. Financial literacy is not a luxury — it is a necessity, and the time to teach it is now.

Say: "Let's annotate this together." Use four highlighter colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** The claim (last sentence of paragraph 1).
- **Color 2 (green):** The reasons (first or second sentence of each body paragraph — paragraphs 2 and 3).
- **Color 3 (blue):** The evidence in each body paragraph (statistics, study results, expert sources).
- **Color 4 (pink):** The counterclaim paragraph (paragraph 4) — highlight both the counterclaim itself and the response.

Ask: "How does this essay handle the counterclaim? Does it dismiss it or take it seriously?" Guide students to notice that the writer acknowledges the counterclaim has "surface appeal" but then provides specific evidence (the 17% statistic) to refute it.

Ask: "What makes this essay's style formal?" Guide students to notice: no contractions, no first person ("I"), precise vocabulary ("longitudinal study," "predatory loans," "foundational knowledge"), and confident, authoritative tone.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively develop a claim with previewed reasons and begin identifying potential counterclaims.

**Activity: Building Claims and Anticipating Counterclaims**

Draw the following graphic organizer on chart paper:

```
CLAIM-COUNTERCLAIM-EVIDENCE CHART

CLAIM: _______________________________________________

| Reason | Evidence (Source) | How It Supports the Claim |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| 1.     |                   |                           |
| 2.     |                   |                           |

COUNTERCLAIM: _______________________________________________
Why someone might believe the counterclaim: _______________
My response to the counterclaim (with evidence): _______________
```

Say: "Our topic is: Should schools require all students to learn computer programming? Let's build an argument together."

Guide the student through these steps:

1. **Decide a position:** Take a stand — for or against required programming courses.
2. **Write the claim:** Use the formula: Topic + Position + Preview of Reasons. Example: "All middle schools should require students to take at least one computer programming course because coding develops logical thinking skills that transfer to every academic subject, because technology careers are among the fastest-growing job sectors, and because early exposure reduces the demographic gaps that currently exist in the technology workforce."
3. **Fill in Reason 1:** "Develops logical thinking." Evidence: "A study by the College Board found that students who took computer science courses scored an average of 17% higher on standardized math assessments, suggesting that the problem-solving strategies used in coding strengthen mathematical reasoning." How it supports: "This shows that programming is not just a vocational skill — it makes students stronger thinkers across subjects."
4. **Fill in Reason 2:** Work with the student to generate a reason and evidence.
5. **Identify the counterclaim:** Ask: "What would someone who disagrees with us say?" Example: "Opponents might argue that the curriculum is already overcrowded and that requiring another course would take time away from core subjects like reading and math."
6. **Draft a response:** "While it is true that scheduling is limited, programming can be integrated into existing math and science classes rather than added as a separate course. Schools in Finland have done this successfully since 2016, with no negative impact on core subject performance."

If the student struggles with generating counterclaims, prompt: "Think about what a parent, teacher, or student who disagrees might say. What are their strongest reasons? Remember — pick their BEST argument, not their weakest one."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write a claim with previewed reasons and identify a counterclaim with a response.

Choose ONE of the following topics (or propose your own):

1. Should middle school students be allowed to have personal cell phones during the school day?
2. Should schools eliminate letter grades and switch to a pass/fail system?
3. Should all students be required to participate in at least one extracurricular activity?

**Task:** Write a claim statement on your chosen topic that previews at least two reasons. Then complete a Claim-Counterclaim-Evidence chart with at least two reasons (with evidence for each) and one counterclaim with your response to it.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write a claim, complete the chart with two reasons and evidence, and identify one counterclaim with a brief response.
**Stretch:** Complete the on-level task, then write the introductory paragraph of your argument in formal style, including a hook, context, and your claim statement.
**Challenge:** Complete the stretch task, then write a full counterclaim paragraph that acknowledges the opposing view fairly and refutes it with specific evidence.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1) Evaluate the following claim and explain what makes it effective or ineffective: "Video games are bad and parents should not let kids play them." (Expected: This is ineffective because it is vague ("bad"), uses informal language, and does not preview specific reasons. A stronger version: "Parents should limit children's daily screen time for video games to one hour because excessive gaming is linked to reduced physical activity, lower academic performance, and disrupted sleep patterns.")
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.2) Write two sentences about the water cycle using a formal, informative tone. Avoid first person and contractions. (Expected: "The water cycle, also known as the hydrological cycle, describes the continuous movement of water through Earth's atmosphere, surface, and underground systems. Evaporation, condensation, and precipitation are the three primary stages that drive this cycle.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following claim and answer the questions:

"Schools should require all students to eat lunch in the cafeteria rather than allowing them to eat in classrooms."

1. Write one counterclaim — the strongest argument someone who disagrees could make.
2. In one sentence, explain how you would respond to that counterclaim.

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
1. Answers will vary. Strong examples: "Some students with social anxiety or sensory sensitivities find the noisy, crowded cafeteria overwhelming and need a quieter environment to eat." Or: "Students who eat in classrooms can use that time to study or complete homework, making their day more efficient."
2. The response must address the counterclaim directly with reasoning or evidence, not simply dismiss it. Example: "While some students may prefer quieter spaces, schools could designate a separate quiet dining area rather than allowing food in classrooms, which creates hygiene and pest problems."

**Mastery = Student writes a relevant counterclaim AND provides a response that addresses it with reasoning or evidence (not a dismissal).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model text and re-read paragraph 4 together, analyzing how the writer acknowledges the counterclaim before responding
- Practice with a simpler topic: "Should students have homework?" Have the student state a claim, then role-play the opposing side in a verbal debate before writing
- Provide the sentence frame: "Some people argue that ___. While this may seem reasonable, the evidence shows that ___ because ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 2, where they will learn to select credible evidence and build logical reasoning chains
- Encourage the student to begin drafting body paragraphs for the argument they planned today

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Counterclaim Debate:** Choose a debatable topic and argue BOTH sides in writing — one paragraph supporting the claim and one paragraph supporting the counterclaim. Then write a brief reflection: Which side was harder to argue? Why?
2. **Credibility Audit:** Find an argument in a newspaper editorial, blog post, or online article. Identify the claim, reasons, and evidence. Does the writer address counterclaims? Rate the argument's credibility on a scale of 1-5 and explain your rating.
3. **Formal Style Transformation:** Take an informal text message or social media post that expresses an opinion and rewrite it as a formal claim with one supporting reason. Notice how the tone and word choice change.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The transition from Grade 6 to Grade 7 argumentation centers on counterclaims. Many students will resist this — they may feel that acknowledging the other side weakens their argument. Use the analogy of a courtroom: a good lawyer anticipates what the opposing lawyer will say and prepares a response. Ignoring the opposition does not make it go away; addressing it shows confidence and credibility.
- Some students may confuse a counterclaim with a weakness in their own argument. Clarify: a counterclaim is a different position, not a flaw. It is what someone who genuinely disagrees would argue based on their own evidence and reasoning.
- Formal style is an ongoing challenge. Students are accustomed to informal writing in texts, social media, and casual conversation. Watch for and gently redirect: contractions ("don't" to "do not"), first person ("I think" to "The evidence suggests"), and slang.
- The model text uses specific statistics and named sources. Seventh graders should work toward this level of specificity, but accept plausible evidence and logical reasoning in early lessons. By Lesson 4, push for greater precision and source credibility.
- If working with multiple students, the warm-up debate can be energizing. Let students take opposing sides and argue verbally before writing — this builds the muscle of anticipating counterclaims.

## Rubric / Answer Key

### Independent Practice
**Sample Claim (Topic 1 — cell phones):** "Middle school students should not be permitted to use personal cell phones during the school day because phones are a documented source of distraction in the classroom, because unrestricted phone access increases cyberbullying incidents during school hours, and because students who are constantly connected to social media report higher levels of anxiety and lower academic engagement."

**Sample Claim-Counterclaim-Evidence Chart:**

| Reason | Evidence (Source) | How It Supports the Claim |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| Phones distract from learning | A study by the London School of Economics found that schools that banned phones saw student test scores increase by an average of 6.4%, with the largest gains among low-performing students | This demonstrates that simply removing phones from the classroom environment leads to measurable academic improvement, proving that phones are a barrier to learning |
| Unrestricted phone access increases cyberbullying | The Cyberbullying Research Center reports that 37% of students who experience cyberbullying at school say it occurs through personal devices during the school day | This shows that phones are not just distracting — they are actively enabling harm between students during hours when the school is responsible for student safety |

**Counterclaim:** "Parents argue that students need phones for safety — to contact family in emergencies or after school."
**Response:** "While parent-student communication is important, schools already have office phones and emergency notification systems. Additionally, schools could implement a phone storage policy where phones are collected at the start of the day and returned at dismissal, preserving safety communication while eliminating classroom distraction."

### Interleaved Review
1. The claim is ineffective because it is vague, uses informal language, and does not preview reasons. A stronger version would specify the position, provide a measurable recommendation, and preview at least two supporting reasons.
2. Answers will vary but must use formal, informative tone with no first person or contractions. See expected example above.
