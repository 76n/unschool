# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 3, Lesson 3

## Topic
Acknowledging and Responding to Opposing Claims

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the strongest counterclaim for a given argument and acknowledge it fairly
- Write a counterclaim paragraph that concedes a point, then refutes it with evidence and reasoning
- Use concession-refutation transitions to create cohesion between opposing ideas

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 1: Understanding of claims, reasons, evidence, and counterclaims
- Lesson 2: Ability to write body paragraphs with evidence and reasoning chains
- W.6.1: Experience writing arguments with claims, reasons, and evidence
- Understanding of formal academic style

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model counterclaim paragraphs (provided below)
- Concession-Refutation sentence frames (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate that engaging with opposing views is a strength, not a weakness, and activate prior knowledge about counterclaims from Lessons 1-2.

**Activity: The Steel Man Challenge**

Say: "In debates, there is a dishonest strategy called a 'straw man' — that is when you misrepresent the other side's argument to make it easier to knock down. The opposite — and the honest strategy — is called a 'steel man.' A steel man is when you represent the other side's argument as STRONGLY as possible, even stronger than they might make it themselves, and THEN respond to it."

Display this claim: "Schools should eliminate homework for middle school students."

Say: "Even if you agree with this claim, I want you to steel man the OTHER side. Write the strongest possible argument IN FAVOR of homework. Make it so good that someone reading it would be convinced."

After 90 seconds, take two or three responses. Praise the strongest, most specific counterarguments.

Say: "Notice what just happened. By making the other side's argument as strong as possible, you actually showed that you understand the full complexity of the issue. That is exactly what the best argumentative writers do. They do not hide from the other side — they face it head-on."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the concession-refutation strategy for counterclaim paragraphs and model three approaches to handling opposing claims.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Concession:** Acknowledging that the opposing side has a valid point or a reasonable concern before explaining why your position is still stronger
- **Refutation:** The part of your counterclaim paragraph where you present evidence or reasoning that disproves or weakens the opposing claim
- **Steel man:** Representing an opposing argument in its strongest possible form before responding to it
- **Qualify:** To limit or modify a claim; acknowledging that your position may not apply in every situation (e.g., "While there may be exceptions, the overall evidence shows...")

### Instruction Notes

Say: "There are three strategies for handling counterclaims in your argument essays. All three are valid, but they work differently."

Write on chart paper:

**Strategy 1: Concede and Refute**
- Acknowledge the counterclaim has some validity, then explain why your position is still stronger.
- Signal words: "While it is true that... / Although... / Admittedly... HOWEVER / the evidence shows / nevertheless"
- Best for: When the other side has a reasonable point but your evidence outweighs it.

**Strategy 2: Expose the Flaw**
- Show that the counterclaim is based on a false assumption, incomplete information, or flawed logic.
- Signal words: "This argument rests on the assumption that... / However, this overlooks... / In reality..."
- Best for: When the other side's reasoning has a specific weakness you can identify.

**Strategy 3: Reframe the Issue**
- Show that even if the counterclaim is valid, it does not outweigh the benefits of your position.
- Signal words: "Even if we accept that... / Granted that... / While this concern is valid, it must be weighed against..."
- Best for: When both sides have legitimate points but yours carries more weight.

Say: "In all three strategies, you FIRST present the counterclaim fairly — the steel man — and THEN respond. Never start by attacking. Start by showing you understand."

### Model Text / Worked Example

Display the following three model counterclaim paragraphs, each using a different strategy. The claim for all three is: "Schools should require students to take a foreign language starting in fifth grade."

> **Strategy 1 — Concede and Refute:**
>
> Critics of mandatory foreign language education argue that the elementary and middle school curriculum is already overcrowded and that adding a language requirement would force schools to cut time from core subjects like mathematics and reading. This concern is understandable — instructional time is finite, and core academic skills must remain the priority. However, research consistently demonstrates that foreign language study does not detract from core academic performance; it enhances it. A meta-analysis published in the Review of Educational Research examined 34 studies involving over 20,000 students and found that children enrolled in foreign language programs scored significantly higher on standardized reading and mathematics assessments than monolingual peers. Far from competing with core subjects, language study strengthens the cognitive skills — pattern recognition, working memory, and analytical reasoning — that underlie performance across all academic areas.

> **Strategy 2 — Expose the Flaw:**
>
> Opponents frequently claim that learning a foreign language is unnecessary because English is the dominant global language and most international communication already occurs in English. This argument rests on an assumption that is both outdated and inaccurate. According to the United States Census Bureau, more than 67 million Americans speak a language other than English at home, a number that has tripled since 1980 and continues to grow. Globally, only 20% of the world's population speaks English, meaning that four out of five people on Earth communicate primarily in another language. The claim that English sufficiency eliminates the need for multilingualism ignores the reality that the United States is becoming more linguistically diverse, not less, and that students who speak only English will find themselves at a professional and social disadvantage in an increasingly interconnected world.

> **Strategy 3 — Reframe the Issue:**
>
> Some parents and educators express concern that requiring a foreign language places undue pressure on students who already struggle with English language arts, arguing that these students should focus on mastering English before taking on a second language. This concern comes from a genuine desire to support struggling learners, and it deserves serious consideration. However, even if we accept that some students find language study challenging, the long-term benefits of bilingualism far outweigh the short-term difficulty. Research by Dr. Ellen Bialystok at York University has shown that bilingual individuals demonstrate superior executive function — the ability to focus, switch between tasks, and filter irrelevant information — advantages that persist throughout life and even delay the onset of cognitive decline in old age. The question is not whether language study is demanding; it is whether we are willing to let short-term difficulty prevent students from gaining lifelong cognitive advantages.

Say: "Let's compare the three strategies."

Use two highlighter colors:
- **Color 1 (yellow):** Highlight the concession (where the writer acknowledges the counterclaim) in each paragraph.
- **Color 2 (green):** Highlight the refutation (where the writer responds with evidence and reasoning).

Ask: "Which strategy treats the opposing side most generously?" (Strategy 3 — it grants that the concern is valid.) "Which one is most aggressive?" (Strategy 2 — it identifies a specific flaw.) "Is any of them disrespectful to the opposing side?" (None — all three take the counterclaim seriously.)

**Concession-Refutation Sentence Frames:**

Write on chart paper for student reference:

- "While it is true that ___, the evidence demonstrates that ___."
- "Opponents argue that ___. Although this concern is understandable, ___."
- "Some people contend that ___. However, this argument overlooks ___."
- "Admittedly, ___. Nevertheless, when weighed against ___, the benefits clearly outweigh ___."
- "Critics may point out that ___. Even if we accept this claim, it does not change the fact that ___."
- "This objection rests on the assumption that ___. In reality, ___."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively write a counterclaim paragraph using one of the three strategies.

**Activity: Building a Counterclaim Paragraph Together**

Say: "Let's practice. Our claim is: 'Middle schools should dedicate at least 30 minutes per day to independent reading time.' What counterclaim would the other side make?"

Guide the student to identify a strong counterclaim. Example: "Some educators argue that 30 minutes of independent reading takes valuable time away from direct instruction in subjects like math and science, where teacher-led lessons are essential."

Say: "Good — that is a steel man. Now let's choose our strategy. I think Strategy 1 — Concede and Refute — works well here because the other side has a legitimate concern about instructional time. Let's write this paragraph together."

Draft collaboratively, step by step:

1. **Introduce the counterclaim fairly:**
   "Some educators argue that dedicating 30 minutes per day to independent reading would significantly reduce the time available for direct instruction in core subjects, particularly mathematics and science, where structured teacher-led lessons are critical for student understanding."

2. **Concede the valid point:**
   "This concern reflects a genuine tension in curriculum planning — instructional time is limited, and every minute allocated to one activity is a minute not spent on another."

3. **Transition to refutation:**
   "However, this argument assumes that independent reading and core subject instruction are competing priorities, when in fact they are complementary."

4. **Present refuting evidence:**
   "A three-year study by the Renaissance Learning Institute found that students who engaged in 30 minutes of daily independent reading not only improved their reading comprehension by an average of 22% but also showed measurable gains in science and social studies assessments, because stronger reading skills allowed them to process and retain content-area information more effectively."

5. **Add reasoning:**
   "In other words, time spent reading is not time taken from other subjects — it is an investment in the foundational skill that makes learning in every subject possible."

Read the complete paragraph aloud and evaluate: "Did we treat the other side fairly? Did we refute with evidence? Is our reasoning clear?"

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write a counterclaim paragraph using one of the three strategies.

Using the argument you have been developing in Lessons 1-2, or choose a new topic below, write ONE counterclaim paragraph.

New topics (if needed):
1. Should schools replace traditional physical education with yoga and mindfulness programs?
2. Should students be allowed to listen to music through headphones during independent work time?
3. Should schools provide free menstrual products in all restrooms?

**Task:** Write a counterclaim paragraph that:
- Identifies the strongest counterargument (steel man)
- Uses one of the three strategies: Concede and Refute, Expose the Flaw, or Reframe the Issue
- Includes at least one piece of evidence in the refutation
- Uses at least one concession-refutation transition from the sentence frames

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write one counterclaim paragraph using Strategy 1 (Concede and Refute) with the sentence frame support.
**Stretch:** Write one counterclaim paragraph using any strategy, without relying on sentence frames, and include evidence from a credible source.
**Challenge:** Write two counterclaim paragraphs addressing two different opposing arguments, using two different strategies.

### Interleaved Review
1. (Grade 6 review — W.6.1b) Write one sentence of evidence using the Introduce-Cite-Explain pattern for this claim: "Schools should offer healthier lunch options." (Expected: "According to a report by the USDA, students who eat balanced school lunches perform 12% better on afternoon assessments than students who eat high-sugar meals [Introduce-Cite], demonstrating that nutrition directly affects the cognitive function schools depend on [Explain].")
2. (Grade 6 review — W.6.2) In two sentences, explain the difference between argumentative writing and informative writing. Use a formal tone. (Expected: "Argumentative writing takes a definitive position on a debatable issue and seeks to persuade the reader using claims, evidence, and reasoning. Informative writing, by contrast, examines a topic objectively, presenting facts and information without advocating for a particular point of view.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this counterclaim paragraph and identify which strategy the writer used (Concede and Refute, Expose the Flaw, or Reframe the Issue). Then explain your reasoning in one sentence.

> "Opponents of year-round schooling argue that children need a long summer break to relax, play, and enjoy their childhood. This argument assumes that a traditional summer break is the only way to provide rest and recreation. In reality, year-round school calendars include frequent breaks of two to three weeks throughout the year, giving students multiple opportunities to rest without the learning loss associated with a ten-week summer gap."

**Expected Answer / Rubric:**
Strategy 2 — Expose the Flaw. The writer identifies a false assumption in the opposing argument ("This argument assumes that a traditional summer break is the only way to provide rest") and then presents factual information that corrects that assumption.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the strategy AND provides a valid explanation that references specific language from the paragraph.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the three strategy descriptions on the anchor chart and re-read each one
- Re-read the model counterclaim paragraphs from Direct Instruction and label each strategy together
- Provide a simpler example: "Some people say dogs are better pets. While dogs are loyal and active [concession], cats are better for small apartments because they require less space and no outdoor walks [refutation]." Ask: "Is this Concede and Refute, Expose the Flaw, or Reframe?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 4, where they will structure and draft a complete argument essay
- Encourage the student to revise their counterclaim paragraph and integrate it into the argument they have been building across Lessons 1-3

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Strategy Switch:** Take the counterclaim paragraph you wrote in independent practice and rewrite it using a different strategy. Compare the two versions: How does the tone and effect change?
2. **Counterclaim Hunt:** Read an editorial or opinion piece from a credible news source. Does the writer address counterclaims? If so, which strategy do they use? If not, write a counterclaim paragraph that the writer should have included.
3. **Debate Preparation:** Choose a debatable topic and prepare to argue BOTH sides. Write a one-paragraph argument for each side, then decide which side has stronger evidence. Write a brief reflection explaining your decision.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often want to dismiss counterclaims quickly ("Some people say homework is good, but they are wrong"). Redirect by insisting on the steel man: "Make their argument so strong that it is hard to refute. THEN refute it."
- The three strategies give students options, but most seventh graders will gravitate toward Strategy 1 (Concede and Refute) because it feels safest. This is perfectly fine — mastering one strategy well is more valuable than attempting all three poorly.
- Watch for the common error of presenting the counterclaim and then failing to respond to it. Some students will write "Opponents argue that..." and then move on to a new reason without refutation. The paragraph must include both the acknowledgment AND the response.
- The concession-refutation sentence frames are scaffolds designed to fade. Use them in this lesson, but encourage students to develop their own transitional language by Lesson 4.
- If students struggle to identify counterclaims, try role-playing: "Pretend you are a parent who disagrees with your claim. What would you say at a school board meeting?" This makes the abstract task concrete and personal.

## Rubric / Answer Key

### Independent Practice
**Sample Counterclaim Paragraph (Topic 2 — music during independent work):**

Strategy 1 — Concede and Refute:

"Teachers who oppose allowing students to listen to music during independent work time argue that music is a distraction that prevents students from fully concentrating on academic tasks, and that headphones create a barrier between the student and the learning environment. This concern is not unfounded — certain types of music, particularly songs with lyrics, can compete with language-processing tasks like reading and writing for the brain's attention. However, research distinguishes between music that distracts and music that enhances focus. A study published in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of America found that students who listened to instrumental music at moderate volume during independent work completed tasks 15% faster and with 9% fewer errors than students who worked in a typical classroom environment with ambient noise. The key variable is not whether music is present but what kind of music is played and at what volume. Rather than banning headphones entirely, schools could establish guidelines — instrumental only, moderate volume, during independent work periods only — that harness the cognitive benefits of music while addressing legitimate concerns about distraction."

### Interleaved Review
1. Answers will vary but must follow the Introduce-Cite-Explain pattern with a specific source, data, and analysis.
2. Must distinguish between argumentative (persuasive, position-taking) and informative (objective, fact-presenting) writing in formal tone.
