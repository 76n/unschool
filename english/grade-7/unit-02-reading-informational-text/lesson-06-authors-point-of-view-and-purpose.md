# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 6

## Topic
Determining Author's Point of View and Purpose

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Determine an author's point of view (what the author believes) and purpose (why the author wrote the text) in an informational text
- Identify specific textual evidence that reveals point of view and purpose, including word choice, detail selection, and organizational decisions
- Distinguish between purpose and point of view as related but separate concepts

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine an author's point of view or purpose and explain how it is conveyed (RI.6.6)
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence (Lessons 1-2, RI.7.1)
- Ability to identify central ideas in informational text (Lessons 3-5, RI.7.2)
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Purpose and Point of View Analysis Frame graphic organizer (sections: Author's Purpose, Evidence of Purpose, Author's Point of View, Evidence of Point of View)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (one for purpose clues, one for point of view clues)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Clarify the distinction between purpose and point of view and show how both are present in informational texts.

**Activity: Purpose vs. Point of View**

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned to determine an author's purpose -- the reason the author wrote the text. In seventh grade, we go deeper. You now need to determine both the purpose and the point of view, and you need to understand how they are different."

Write on the board:
- **Purpose:** Why did the author write this? (to inform, to persuade, to raise awareness, to argue, to explain)
- **Point of view:** What does the author believe about the topic? (the author's position, stance, or perspective)

Say: "Here is an example. Imagine a passage about zoos."

Write on the board:
- Purpose: To argue that zoos should be reformed
- Point of view: The author believes that most zoos prioritize entertainment over animal welfare and need to shift their focus to conservation

Say: "Notice: the purpose tells us why the author wrote. The point of view tells us what the author thinks. They are related -- the author's belief drives the reason for writing -- but they are not the same thing. Today, you will learn to identify both."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify an author's point of view and purpose using three types of textual evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Author's purpose:** The reason an author writes a text -- to inform, persuade, argue, explain, raise awareness, or entertain
- **Author's point of view:** The author's position, belief, or perspective on the topic being discussed
- **Word choice (diction):** The specific words an author selects, which can reveal bias, emphasis, or attitude
- **Detail selection:** The information an author chooses to include or omit, which reveals priorities and perspectives

### Instruction Notes

Say: "To uncover an author's purpose and point of view, I use three detectors."

Write on the board:
1. **Word Choice Detector:** What adjectives, adverbs, and loaded words does the author use? Neutral language suggests informing. Persuasive language suggests arguing.
2. **Detail Selection Detector:** What information does the author emphasize? What does the author leave out? Authors include details that support their perspective and may understate or omit details that challenge it.
3. **Structure Detector:** How does the author organize the text? Does the author build toward a conclusion? Does the author present one side more favorably than another?

Say: "Let me demonstrate with a passage where the author takes a clear position."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Case for Later School Start Times"**

> Every morning in communities across the United States, millions of teenagers drag themselves out of bed hours before their bodies are ready, board buses in the dark, and arrive at school buildings where they are expected to learn at peak capacity despite being chronically sleep-deprived. This is not a matter of lazy teenagers staying up too late -- it is a matter of biology colliding with bad policy. Research from the National Sleep Foundation confirms that the adolescent brain does not begin producing melatonin until around 11:00 PM, making it biologically impossible for most teenagers to fall asleep before midnight. Yet the average high school in the United States starts at 8:00 AM.
>
> The evidence in favor of later start times is overwhelming. A landmark study by the University of Minnesota tracked 9,000 high school students and found that when schools shifted their start time to 8:30 AM or later, students reported better sleep, improved mood, higher attendance, and significantly reduced rates of car crashes among teen drivers. Schools in districts that adopted later start times also saw average improvement of several percentile points on standardized test scores. The American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Medical Association, and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention have all issued recommendations that middle and high schools begin no earlier than 8:30 AM.
>
> Opponents of later start times point to logistical challenges: busing schedules that serve multiple schools, conflicts with after-school sports and jobs, and the preferences of parents accustomed to the current system. These are real considerations, but they are solvable problems -- matters of scheduling and habit, not fundamental barriers. The health and academic performance of an entire generation of students should not be sacrificed because adults find it inconvenient to adjust a bus route.

Say: "Let me apply the three detectors."

Write on the board:
- **Word Choice Detector:** "Drag themselves out of bed" (sympathetic to students); "chronically sleep-deprived" (medical urgency); "lazy teenagers" immediately countered with "it is a matter of biology" (dismissing counterargument); "overwhelming" (strong emphasis on evidence); "sacrificed because adults find it inconvenient" (emotional, positions opponents as selfish).
- **Detail Selection Detector:** The author includes extensive evidence supporting later start times (multiple studies, three major medical organizations) but presents opposing arguments in only three brief phrases. The detail balance strongly favors one side.
- **Structure Detector:** Paragraph 1 establishes the problem, paragraph 2 presents extensive evidence for the author's position, and paragraph 3 dismisses the opposition. This is a classic persuasive structure: problem, solution, rebuttal.

Write on the board:
- **Author's Purpose:** To argue that schools should adopt later start times for teenagers
- **Author's Point of View:** The author believes that early school start times are harmful to adolescent health and academic performance, and that the logistical objections to changing start times are solvable and do not justify continuing a practice that harms students

Say: "Notice how the word choice ('overwhelming,' 'sacrificed'), the detail imbalance (extensive pro-evidence versus minimal con-evidence), and the persuasive structure all point in the same direction. The author is not neutrally informing -- the author is actively arguing for a position."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying purpose and point of view with facilitator support.

**Passage B: "The Silent Crisis of Loneliness Among Young People"**

> In 2023, the United States Surgeon General issued a rare public advisory declaring loneliness and social isolation an epidemic with health consequences as severe as smoking fifteen cigarettes a day. The advisory focused particular attention on young people, noting that despite being the most digitally connected generation in human history, adolescents and young adults report higher rates of loneliness than any other age group. A survey by the American Psychological Association found that 61 percent of young adults aged 18 to 25 reported feeling "seriously lonely" -- a number that has risen steadily over the past decade.
>
> The rise of social media appears to be a major contributing factor, though the relationship is more nuanced than headlines suggest. Heavy social media use -- defined as more than three hours per day -- correlates with doubled rates of anxiety and depression in adolescents. However, moderate use of social media does not show the same harmful effects and may actually help shy or isolated young people maintain social connections they would otherwise lack. The problem is not social media itself but the way it has replaced rather than supplemented in-person interaction. Time spent on screens has steadily crowded out time spent in face-to-face activities: team sports participation has declined, in-person socializing among teens has dropped by nearly 40 percent since 2003, and the average teenager now spends more hours interacting through screens than in direct human contact. Rebuilding the social fabric will require not the elimination of technology but the deliberate restoration of spaces, habits, and institutions that bring young people together in person.

Say: "Apply the three detectors. What is the author's purpose, and what is the author's point of view? Use specific textual evidence."

Allow the student to work for three minutes.

Expected response:
- **Word Choice Detector:** "Silent crisis" in the title suggests urgency and hidden harm. "Epidemic" carries medical weight. "Seriously lonely" emphasizes severity. "Deliberate restoration" suggests intentional action is needed.
- **Detail Selection Detector:** The author includes strong evidence of the problem (Surgeon General advisory, 61% loneliness statistic, doubled anxiety rates) but also includes a nuanced view of social media (moderate use not harmful, may help shy people). This balanced detail selection suggests the author is informing more than persuading, though with a clear concern.
- **Structure Detector:** Paragraph 1 establishes the severity of the problem. Paragraph 2 explores causes with nuance and ends with a recommended direction ("deliberate restoration of spaces"). The structure is informative-analytical with a position at the end.

- **Author's Purpose:** To raise awareness about the loneliness epidemic among young people and argue that the solution involves restoring in-person interaction rather than eliminating technology
- **Author's Point of View:** The author believes loneliness among young people is a serious health crisis driven primarily by the replacement of face-to-face interaction with screen-based interaction, and that solving it requires deliberately rebuilding opportunities for in-person connection

Say: "Good work. You noticed that this author is more nuanced than the author in Passage A -- this author acknowledges that social media is not entirely harmful. But the author still has a clear point of view: the replacement of in-person interaction is the core problem, and the solution involves restoration, not elimination."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently determine author's point of view and purpose in a new passage.

**Passage C: "Rethinking Homework"**

> The homework debate is not new, but the evidence has shifted dramatically over the past twenty years. For decades, educators assumed that assigning homework was inherently beneficial -- that any additional practice would reinforce learning and build discipline. Research tells a different story. A comprehensive review by Duke University professor Harris Cooper, which analyzed data from hundreds of studies, found that homework in elementary school has virtually no measurable academic benefit. In middle school, the benefits are modest and diminish sharply when assignments exceed sixty to ninety minutes per night. Only in high school does homework show a consistent positive correlation with academic achievement, and even then, the benefits plateau beyond two hours per night.
>
> What the research does show consistently is that excessive homework carries real costs. Students who spend more than two hours per night on homework report higher levels of stress, anxiety, and physical health complaints. Family time shrinks as evenings become dominated by assignments, and the pressure falls disproportionately on students from lower-income families who may lack quiet study spaces, reliable internet, and adults available to help. Meanwhile, wealthy families can hire tutors and provide resources that make homework manageable, meaning that homework may actually widen achievement gaps rather than close them.
>
> None of this means that homework should be abandoned entirely. Targeted, meaningful practice -- especially in subjects like math and foreign language where repetition builds fluency -- has clear value. The problem is not homework itself but the culture of excessive, undifferentiated homework that values quantity over quality. Schools that have redesigned their homework policies to emphasize purpose over volume report that students are less stressed, more engaged, and no less academically prepared than their counterparts buried under hours of busywork each night.

**Directions:** Use the three detectors (word choice, detail selection, structure) to analyze this passage.

1. **(On-level)** What is the author's purpose in writing this passage? Cite at least two specific textual clues that reveal this purpose.
2. **(On-level)** What is the author's point of view about homework? State it as a complete sentence and cite at least two pieces of evidence that reveal this belief.
3. **(Stretch)** How does the third paragraph affect your understanding of the author's point of view? Does it change, complicate, or strengthen the author's position? Explain with specific evidence.
4. **(Challenge)** Compare the author's approach in this passage to the approach in Passage A (school start times). Both authors argue for change, but one is more balanced than the other. Which one, and how do you know? Cite evidence from both passages.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.2) Identify two central ideas in this passage. State each as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.1) Cite two pieces of evidence that support the claim that excessive homework is harmful. Use signal phrases and quotation marks.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Community gardens are one of the most effective tools for revitalizing struggling neighborhoods. Research from the University of Pennsylvania found that properties within one-quarter mile of a community garden saw an average increase in value of nearly 10 percent. Gardens reduce crime by transforming vacant lots into active, monitored spaces, and they improve nutrition by giving residents in food deserts access to fresh fruits and vegetables that would otherwise require a long trip to a distant grocery store.

1. What is the author's purpose? Cite one specific textual clue.
2. What is the author's point of view? State it in one sentence.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The author's purpose is to argue (or persuade the reader) that community gardens are valuable for struggling neighborhoods. Textual clue: The opening sentence makes a strong evaluative claim -- "one of the most effective tools for revitalizing struggling neighborhoods" -- which is persuasive language, not neutral reporting. (Also accept: the author selects only positive evidence -- property values, crime reduction, nutrition -- with no counterarguments mentioned.)
2. The author believes that community gardens provide significant, measurable benefits to struggling neighborhoods -- including economic, safety, and health improvements -- and should be supported as a revitalization strategy.

**Mastery = correctly identifies both purpose and point of view with at least one supporting textual clue**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three detectors using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Practice with two very short paragraphs: one written to inform and one written to persuade on the same topic; have the student identify the purpose and point of view of each
- Use the Purpose and Point of View Analysis Frame to scaffold the process
- Clarify the distinction: purpose = why the author wrote; point of view = what the author believes

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7 on how authors distinguish their position from others
- Encourage the student to identify purpose and point of view when reading news articles or opinion pieces

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Rewrite the Perspective:** Take one of the lesson passages and rewrite the first paragraph from the opposite point of view. Keep the same topic but reverse the author's position. How does word choice change?
2. **Purpose Spectrum:** Arrange the three lesson passages on a spectrum from "purely informational" to "strongly persuasive." Explain where each falls and what evidence supports the placement.
3. **Hidden Purposes:** Some texts have a stated purpose and a hidden purpose. A passage might claim to inform the reader about a product while actually trying to sell it. Write a short passage (4-5 sentences) with a hidden persuasive purpose disguised as neutral information.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students frequently default to the three basic purposes they learned in earlier grades: inform, persuade, entertain. While these are useful starting points, RI.7.6 demands more nuance. Push students toward specific purposes: "to argue for later school start times," "to raise awareness about loneliness among young people," "to advocate for reforming homework policies." The more specific the purpose statement, the better.
- Point of view is the concept students confuse most. In narrative text, "point of view" means first-person or third-person narrator. In informational text for RI.7.6, "point of view" means the author's position or belief about the topic. Clarify this distinction explicitly.
- The three detectors (word choice, detail selection, structure) give students concrete tools for analysis. Without these tools, students tend to make vague claims like "the author seems to think this is important." With the detectors, they can cite specific words, detail choices, and organizational patterns.
- Passage A in this lesson is deliberately one-sided to make the author's purpose and point of view obvious. Passage B is more balanced, and Passage C falls in between. This progression from obvious to nuanced mirrors the range of real-world texts students will encounter.
- This lesson introduces RI.7.6 concepts. Lesson 7 will deepen the analysis by focusing specifically on how authors distinguish their position from the positions of others.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The author's purpose is to argue that schools should reform homework policies to emphasize quality over quantity. Textual clue 1: The opening signals that conventional wisdom is wrong -- "educators assumed that assigning homework was inherently beneficial" is immediately countered with "research tells a different story," positioning the author as challenging the status quo. Textual clue 2: The author uses evaluative language in the final paragraph -- "busywork" to describe excessive homework is a dismissive word choice that reveals the author's negative judgment of current practices.

2. The author's point of view is that excessive, undifferentiated homework is harmful to students and families, widens achievement gaps, and should be replaced with targeted, purposeful assignments that prioritize quality over volume. Evidence 1: The author states that "homework in elementary school has virtually no measurable academic benefit" and that benefits diminish sharply beyond sixty to ninety minutes in middle school, suggesting the author believes much current homework is unnecessary. Evidence 2: The author argues that "homework may actually widen achievement gaps rather than close them" because wealthy families can provide resources that poorer families cannot, revealing a concern about equity.

3. The third paragraph complicates and strengthens the author's point of view by adding nuance. The author concedes that "targeted, meaningful practice has clear value" and that "none of this means that homework should be abandoned entirely." This prevents the reader from dismissing the author as anti-homework. The author then refines the argument: "the problem is not homework itself but the culture of excessive, undifferentiated homework that values quantity over quality." By acknowledging homework's legitimate uses, the author strengthens the credibility of the overall argument against excess. The final sentence provides evidence of successful reform, ending on a constructive note rather than mere criticism.

4. Both authors argue for change, but the homework author (Passage C) is more balanced. The school start time author (Passage A) uses strongly emotional language ("sacrificed because adults find it inconvenient"), dismisses opponents in a single sentence, and presents evidence almost entirely from one side. The homework author acknowledges the legitimate value of homework (paragraph 3), presents a nuanced view of when homework does and does not help (citing specific grade-level findings), and frames the problem as "quantity over quality" rather than homework being universally bad. The homework passage presents a more complete picture that addresses potential counterarguments fairly, while the school start time passage is designed primarily to convince.

### Interleaved Review
5. Central Idea 1: Research shows that homework provides limited or no measurable academic benefit in elementary and middle school, with positive effects appearing consistently only in high school. Central Idea 2: Excessive homework creates real harm, including student stress, reduced family time, and widened achievement gaps between students from different economic backgrounds.
6. Evidence 1: According to the passage, "students who spend more than two hours per night on homework report higher levels of stress, anxiety, and physical health complaints." Evidence 2: The author notes that "the pressure falls disproportionately on students from lower-income families who may lack quiet study spaces, reliable internet, and adults available to help."
