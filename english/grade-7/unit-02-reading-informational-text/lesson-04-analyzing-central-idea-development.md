# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 4

## Topic
Analyzing How Central Ideas Develop

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Trace how a central idea is introduced, developed, and refined over the course of an informational text
- Identify the specific techniques authors use to develop central ideas, including details, examples, facts, statistics, and connections
- Explain how the development of one central idea relates to or supports the development of another central idea within the same text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify two or more central ideas in an informational text (Lesson 3, RI.7.2)
- Ability to distinguish between topic, central idea, and supporting detail (Lesson 3)
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence (Lessons 1-2, RI.7.1)
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Central Idea Development Tracker graphic organizer (columns: Central Idea, Where Introduced, How Developed (details/examples/facts/connections), Where Refined or Deepened)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (one per central idea)
- Colored pencils for marking development stages (optional)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect Lesson 3's identification skill to this lesson's analysis of how central ideas are developed.

**Activity: Plant Analogy**

Say: "In Lesson 3, you learned to find two or more central ideas in a text. That was like identifying two different plants growing in the same garden. Today, you are going to trace how each plant grows -- where the seed was planted, how it was watered and fed, and how it matured."

Write on the board:
- **Introduced:** Where does the author first present the central idea?
- **Developed:** What details, examples, and evidence does the author add to build the idea?
- **Refined:** Does the central idea become more specific, more complex, or more nuanced by the end of the text?

Say: "An author does not just state a central idea and leave it. The author develops it across the text, adding layers of meaning. Your job today is to trace that development -- to see the architecture behind the ideas."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to trace how authors develop central ideas using specific techniques.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Development (of a central idea):** The process by which an author builds, deepens, and supports a central idea across the text through the addition of details, examples, evidence, and reasoning
- **Introduce:** The point in the text where the author first presents or signals a central idea
- **Refine:** The process of making a central idea more specific, nuanced, or complex as the text progresses
- **Development techniques:** The tools authors use to build central ideas, including facts, statistics, examples, anecdotes, cause-and-effect reasoning, comparison, and expert testimony

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When you trace the development of a central idea, you are answering three questions: Where does it start? How does the author build it? Where does it end up? Let me model this with a passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Microbiome Revolution"**

> For most of the history of modern medicine, bacteria were seen almost exclusively as enemies -- invaders to be destroyed with antibiotics and antiseptic practices. This view, while it saved millions of lives, turned out to be dangerously incomplete. Scientists now know that the human body is home to roughly 38 trillion bacteria, most of them living in the digestive system, and that these microorganisms are not freeloaders but active participants in human health. Collectively called the human microbiome, these bacteria help digest food that the human stomach cannot break down on its own, produce vitamins that the body needs, and train the immune system to distinguish between harmful invaders and harmless substances.
>
> Research into the microbiome has revealed connections that earlier generations of doctors could never have imagined. Studies have linked the composition of gut bacteria to conditions as varied as obesity, depression, allergies, and autoimmune diseases. In experiments with laboratory mice, transplanting gut bacteria from an obese mouse into a lean mouse caused the lean mouse to gain weight, even when both mice ate the same diet. Other studies have shown that patients who take broad-spectrum antibiotics, which kill beneficial bacteria along with harmful ones, experience higher rates of digestive disorders and are more vulnerable to secondary infections. These findings have led researchers to reconsider the aggressive use of antibiotics and to explore treatments that work with the microbiome rather than against it, including probiotics, dietary changes, and even fecal microbiota transplants.

Say: "Let me trace the development of two central ideas."

Write on the board:

**Central Idea 1: The traditional view of bacteria as purely harmful is incomplete; bacteria play essential roles in human health.**
- **Introduced:** First two sentences -- the author establishes the old view ("bacteria were seen almost exclusively as enemies") and signals its inadequacy ("turned out to be dangerously incomplete").
- **Developed:** The author adds specific facts: 38 trillion bacteria live in the body. Then adds specific functions: digesting food, producing vitamins, training the immune system.
- **Refined:** By the end of paragraph 1, the central idea has grown from "the old view is wrong" to "bacteria are active, essential participants in human health." The idea has become more specific and more positive.

**Central Idea 2: Research on the microbiome is transforming medicine and challenging long-held medical practices.**
- **Introduced:** Opening of paragraph 2 -- "Research into the microbiome has revealed connections that earlier generations of doctors could never have imagined."
- **Developed:** The author provides examples: gut bacteria linked to obesity, depression, allergies; mouse transplant experiment; antibiotics killing beneficial bacteria.
- **Refined:** The final sentence expands the idea from "research is revealing connections" to "these findings are changing medical practice," with specific alternatives to antibiotics listed (probiotics, dietary changes, fecal transplants).

Say: "Notice how each central idea starts as a general claim, gets built up with evidence and examples, and ends up more specific and nuanced than where it began. That is development."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice tracing central idea development with facilitator support.

**Passage B: "Light Pollution"**

> On a clear night far from city lights, the naked eye can see roughly 4,500 stars. From the center of a major city, that number drops to fewer than 200. The difference is light pollution -- the excessive and misdirected artificial light that washes out the night sky and disrupts ecosystems adapted to natural cycles of light and dark. Light pollution affects an estimated 80 percent of the world's population and 99 percent of Americans and Europeans.
>
> The consequences extend far beyond an obscured view of the stars. Migrating birds that navigate by starlight become disoriented by the glow of cities, sometimes circling brightly lit buildings until they collapse from exhaustion. Sea turtle hatchlings, which instinctively crawl toward the brightest horizon to reach the ocean, are drawn inland toward streetlights and parking lot lights, where they die of dehydration or predation. Insects, which are attracted to artificial light in enormous numbers, cluster around street lamps instead of performing their ecological roles as pollinators and food sources for other animals. Studies have also linked nighttime light exposure in humans to disrupted sleep patterns, suppressed melatonin production, and increased risks of certain health conditions.
>
> The encouraging news is that light pollution is one of the most reversible forms of environmental damage. Unlike chemical contamination or habitat destruction, light pollution stops the moment the lights are turned off or redirected. Cities such as Tucson, Arizona, and the island of Bortle in Denmark have adopted "dark sky" ordinances that require shielded outdoor lighting, reduced wattage, and curfews on unnecessary illumination. These measures have restored visible stars, reduced bird and turtle mortality, and lowered municipal energy costs by 30 to 50 percent. The challenge is convincing communities that darkness is not a problem to be solved with more light but a natural resource worth protecting.

Say: "Using the Central Idea Development Tracker, trace two central ideas through this passage. For each, identify where it is introduced, how it is developed, and where it is refined."

Allow the student to work for three minutes. Guide as needed.

Expected response:
- **Central Idea 1: Light pollution is a widespread environmental problem with serious consequences for wildlife and human health.**
  - Introduced: Paragraph 1 -- the contrast between 4,500 stars and fewer than 200 defines the problem, and the statistic about 80% of the world's population establishes its scale.
  - Developed: Paragraph 2 provides four specific consequences: migrating birds disoriented, sea turtle hatchlings drawn inland, insects clustering around lamps instead of pollinating, and humans experiencing sleep disruption and health risks.
  - Refined: By the end of paragraph 2, the idea has expanded from "light pollution blocks stars" to "light pollution disrupts entire ecosystems and human biology," making it more serious and multidimensional than the opening suggested.

- **Central Idea 2: Light pollution is uniquely reversible among environmental problems, and practical solutions already exist.**
  - Introduced: Paragraph 3 opens with "light pollution is one of the most reversible forms of environmental damage."
  - Developed: The author explains why it is reversible ("stops the moment the lights are turned off"), then provides real examples of cities implementing dark sky ordinances with specific measures (shielded lighting, reduced wattage, curfews).
  - Refined: The results are quantified (restored stars, reduced bird and turtle mortality, 30-50% energy savings), and the final sentence reframes the issue philosophically -- darkness is "a natural resource worth protecting." The idea has developed from "it can be fixed" to "here are proven solutions" to "we need to rethink our relationship with darkness."

Say: "You traced both ideas from introduction through development to refinement. Notice how the second central idea does not just repeat itself -- it grows from a claim, to evidence, to a philosophical reframing."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently trace the development of central ideas in a new passage.

**Passage C: "The History and Future of Vaccination"**

> In 1796, English physician Edward Jenner performed an experiment that would save more lives than perhaps any other medical advance in history. Jenner observed that milkmaids who had contracted cowpox, a mild disease caught from cattle, seemed to be immune to smallpox, one of the deadliest diseases in human history. He deliberately exposed a young boy to cowpox and then, weeks later, to smallpox. The boy did not develop the disease. Jenner called his technique "vaccination," from the Latin word vacca, meaning cow, and his discovery launched a revolution in preventive medicine that would eventually eliminate smallpox entirely -- the only human disease ever eradicated by medical intervention.
>
> Over the following two centuries, vaccines were developed against dozens of deadly and disabling diseases, including polio, measles, tetanus, and diphtheria. In the United States alone, vaccines prevent an estimated 2.5 million deaths per year. The development of each new vaccine followed a similar pattern: scientists identified the pathogen causing the disease, developed a weakened or inactivated form of it that could train the immune system to recognize and fight the real thing, and then conducted extensive trials to ensure safety and effectiveness. This process, while remarkably successful, was also slow -- developing a new vaccine typically took ten to fifteen years from initial research to widespread distribution.
>
> The arrival of mRNA vaccine technology represented a fundamental shift in this timeline. Unlike traditional vaccines, which use weakened or inactivated pathogens, mRNA vaccines deliver a set of genetic instructions that teach human cells to produce a specific protein found on the surface of a virus. The immune system recognizes this protein as foreign and mounts a defense against it. Because the mRNA approach does not require growing live pathogens in laboratories, new vaccines can be designed and manufactured far more rapidly. The first mRNA vaccines were developed and authorized for emergency use within less than a year of a new virus being identified -- a process that would have been unimaginable using older methods. Scientists are now exploring mRNA technology as a platform for vaccines against influenza, certain cancers, and other diseases that have resisted traditional approaches.

**Directions:** Use the Central Idea Development Tracker for each question.

1. **(On-level)** Identify two central ideas in this passage. For each, trace where it is introduced, how it is developed (cite at least two development techniques), and how it is refined by the end of the text.
2. **(On-level)** How does the development of Central Idea 1 set the stage for Central Idea 2? Explain the relationship in 2-3 sentences.
3. **(Stretch)** The passage develops its central ideas chronologically -- 1796, two centuries of progress, and then mRNA technology. How does this chronological structure help the reader understand the significance of mRNA vaccines? Cite specific evidence.
4. **(Challenge)** Write a paragraph that could serve as a fourth section of this passage, further developing one of the central ideas. Include at least two development techniques (facts, examples, statistics, cause-and-effect, expert testimony, comparison).

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.2) Write an objective summary of this passage in 3-4 sentences. Do not include personal opinions or judgments.
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.6) What is the author's purpose in writing this passage? How is this purpose conveyed through the author's selection of details?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Coral reefs cover less than 1 percent of the ocean floor, yet they support approximately 25 percent of all marine species. These underwater ecosystems provide food and income for more than 500 million people worldwide, particularly in developing coastal nations. However, rising ocean temperatures have triggered mass coral bleaching events, in which stressed corals expel the algae that give them color and provide most of their nutrition. Scientists estimate that 14 percent of the world's coral was lost between 2009 and 2018 alone.

1. Identify one central idea of this passage and trace how it is developed from beginning to end. Name at least two development techniques the author uses.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Central Idea: Coral reefs are extraordinarily important ecosystems that are rapidly declining due to rising ocean temperatures. Introduced: The opening sentence establishes the paradox of coral's small size but enormous ecological importance (less than 1% of ocean floor, 25% of marine species). Developed: The author adds economic importance (food and income for 500 million people) and then shifts to the threat -- rising temperatures causing bleaching events. The author explains the mechanism (corals expel algae that provide nutrition). Refined: The final statistic (14% of coral lost in just nine years) makes the threat concrete and urgent. Development techniques: statistics (1%, 25%, 500 million, 14%), cause-and-effect (rising temperatures cause bleaching, which causes algae expulsion, which threatens coral survival), and contrast (enormous importance vs. rapid decline).

**Mastery = identifies a central idea and traces its development through at least two stages, naming at least two development techniques**

- Review the Development Tracker using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Give the student a pre-filled tracker with one central idea's development already mapped, and have them map the second
- Focus on just one central idea and trace it sentence by sentence through the text
- Use color-coding: highlight where the idea is introduced in one color, where it is developed in another, and where it is refined in a third

### If Mastery Is Met

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often confuse "development" with "repetition." If an author states the same idea three times in slightly different words, that is repetition, not development. True development adds new information: a new example, a new statistic, a deeper explanation, a connection to something else. Teach students to ask: "Does this sentence add something I did not already know about the central idea?"
- The concept of "refinement" may be new to students. Refinement means the central idea becomes more precise, qualified, or complex by the end of the text. In Passage A, Central Idea 1 refines from "bacteria are not all bad" to "bacteria are essential health partners." Help students see this shift in specificity.
- Chronological structure (as in Passage C) is a common development pattern in informational text. Help students recognize that time progression is not just storytelling -- it is a development technique that shows how understanding evolved.
- If a student can identify central ideas but struggles with tracing development, provide a paragraph-by-paragraph framework: "In paragraph 1, the author introduces the idea by ___. In paragraph 2, the author develops the idea by adding ___. In paragraph 3, the author refines the idea by ___."
- This lesson builds directly on Lesson 3. If a student struggled with identifying multiple central ideas in Lesson 3, review that skill before attempting the development analysis. You cannot trace the development of an idea you have not identified.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Central Idea 1: Vaccination is one of the most significant medical advances in human history, saving millions of lives over more than two centuries.**
   - Introduced: Paragraph 1 -- Jenner's 1796 experiment is described as leading to a discovery that "would save more lives than perhaps any other medical advance in history."
   - Developed: Paragraph 1 adds the historical example of the cowpox-to-smallpox experiment and the result (smallpox eradicated). Paragraph 2 expands the idea with scope -- vaccines developed against "dozens of deadly and disabling diseases" and prevent "2.5 million deaths per year" in the US alone.
   - Refined: By the end of paragraph 2, the idea has grown from one experiment saving one boy to a global system saving millions annually. The central idea is refined from "one discovery" to "a revolution in preventive medicine."

   **Central Idea 2: mRNA vaccine technology represents a fundamental shift that dramatically accelerates vaccine development and opens new possibilities for disease prevention.**
   - Introduced: Paragraph 3 opens with "the arrival of mRNA vaccine technology represented a fundamental shift."
   - Developed: The author explains how mRNA vaccines work differently (deliver genetic instructions instead of weakened pathogens), why this matters (faster design and manufacture), and provides evidence of the speed (authorized within less than a year versus the traditional ten to fifteen years).
   - Refined: The final sentence expands from "faster vaccines" to "a platform for attacking diseases that resisted traditional approaches," including cancers. The idea has developed from a technical description to a vision of expanded medical possibility.

2. Central Idea 1 establishes the enormous value of vaccination and the traditional method's slow development timeline (ten to fifteen years). This sets the stage for Central Idea 2 by showing the reader what was at stake: a proven, life-saving technology that was limited by the speed of its own process. When Central Idea 2 introduces mRNA technology as achieving the same goal in less than a year, the contrast with the established timeline makes the significance of the breakthrough immediately clear.

3. The chronological structure helps the reader understand mRNA vaccines by building a context of comparison. Starting in 1796 with Jenner's cowpox experiment establishes where vaccination began. Tracing two centuries of progress shows how the traditional method worked and its inherent slowness -- "ten to fifteen years from initial research to widespread distribution." When the passage then reveals that mRNA vaccines were "developed and authorized for emergency use within less than a year," the reader has a two-hundred-year timeline against which to measure this acceleration. Without the chronological buildup, the speed of mRNA development would seem impressive but abstract. With it, the reader understands exactly how unprecedented the achievement was.

4. Answers will vary. A strong response might further develop Central Idea 2: "The potential applications of mRNA technology extend well beyond infectious diseases. Researchers at several major universities are testing mRNA-based treatments for melanoma and pancreatic cancer, using the same principle: instructing the patient's own cells to produce proteins that the immune system can target and attack. Early trials have shown promising results, with some patients experiencing significant tumor reduction within weeks. If successful, mRNA cancer vaccines could transform oncology the way mRNA pathogen vaccines transformed infectious disease prevention. The technology also offers advantages in speed of customization -- because mRNA sequences can be adjusted quickly, doctors may one day be able to design personalized cancer vaccines tailored to an individual patient's specific tumor." (Development techniques: examples, expert testimony, cause-and-effect, comparison.)

### Interleaved Review
5. In 1796, Edward Jenner demonstrated that exposure to cowpox could protect against smallpox, launching the development of vaccination as a medical practice. Over the next two centuries, vaccines were developed against dozens of diseases, saving millions of lives annually through a process that typically took ten to fifteen years per vaccine. The recent development of mRNA vaccine technology has fundamentally changed this timeline by enabling vaccine creation in less than a year. Scientists are now exploring mRNA approaches for other diseases, including cancers, that have resisted traditional vaccine methods.
6. The author's purpose is to inform the reader about the history and transformative potential of vaccination technology. This purpose is conveyed through the selection of specific milestones (Jenner's 1796 experiment, the eradication of smallpox, the development of mRNA vaccines), concrete statistics (2.5 million deaths prevented annually, ten to fifteen years for traditional development, less than one year for mRNA), and a forward-looking final sentence that frames current research as an exciting frontier. The tone is celebratory of scientific progress rather than neutral, suggesting the author also aims to inspire appreciation for medical innovation.
