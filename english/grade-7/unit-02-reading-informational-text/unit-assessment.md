## Section 1: Passage A — "The Hidden Cost of Food Waste" (Expository)

Read the passage. Then answer questions 1-5.

> Every year, approximately one-third of all food produced for human consumption worldwide is lost or wasted -- roughly 1.3 billion metric tons. In the United States alone, the Department of Agriculture estimates that 30 to 40 percent of the food supply goes uneaten, amounting to approximately 133 billion pounds of food discarded annually. This waste occurs at every stage of the supply chain: crops rot in fields because they are cosmetically imperfect and retailers will not purchase them; grocery stores discard products that have passed their "sell by" dates, even though these dates indicate peak quality rather than safety; restaurants prepare more food than customers order; and households throw away leftovers, forgotten produce, and impulse purchases that spoil before they are eaten.
>
> The environmental consequences of food waste are staggering and often underappreciated. When food decomposes in landfills, it produces methane, a greenhouse gas approximately 80 times more potent than carbon dioxide over a twenty-year period. The Environmental Protection Agency reports that landfills are the third-largest source of methane emissions in the United States, and food waste is the single largest category of material sent to landfills. But the damage begins long before the landfill. Producing food that is never eaten wastes all the resources that went into growing, harvesting, processing, and transporting it: the water used to irrigate crops, the fuel burned by trucks and refrigeration units, the fertilizers and pesticides applied to fields, and the labor of thousands of workers at every step. The United Nations estimates that if food waste were a country, it would be the third-largest emitter of greenhouse gases in the world, behind only the United States and China.
>
> Solutions exist at every level. France became the first country to ban supermarkets from discarding unsold food, requiring them instead to donate it to food banks or convert it to animal feed or compost. South Korea implemented a pay-by-weight system for food waste disposal, charging households based on how much food they throw away -- a policy that reduced food waste by 30 percent within five years. In the United States, nonprofit organizations like Feeding America redirect billions of pounds of surplus food to people experiencing food insecurity each year. At the individual level, meal planning, proper food storage, understanding the difference between "sell by" and "use by" labels, and simply buying less food more frequently can dramatically reduce household waste.

## Section 2: Passage B — "Rethinking Animal Testing in Science" (Persuasive)

Read the passage. Then answer questions 6-11.

> For more than a century, animals have served as test subjects for medical research, drug development, and product safety testing. Mice, rats, rabbits, and primates have been exposed to experimental drugs, surgical procedures, and chemical compounds so that scientists could predict how these substances would affect human beings. This practice has contributed to genuine medical advances: the development of insulin for diabetes, vaccines for polio and measles, and surgical techniques for organ transplants all involved animal testing at some stage. It would be dishonest to deny that animal research has saved human lives.
>
> However, the scientific case for continuing to rely on animal testing as a primary research method is weakening. Studies published in the British Medical Journal have found that approximately 95 percent of drugs that pass animal trials fail in human clinical trials, meaning the animal tests did not accurately predict how the drugs would behave in people. The biological differences between species are significant enough that a compound safe for a mouse may be toxic to a human, and vice versa. Meanwhile, advances in technology have produced alternatives that may be more accurate. Organ-on-a-chip technology, which uses miniature devices lined with human cells to simulate the functions of human organs, allows researchers to test drug reactions on actual human tissue. Computer modeling can now simulate thousands of molecular interactions in hours, a process that once required years of animal experiments. Three-dimensional bioprinted tissue models enable researchers to study disease progression and treatment responses in structures that closely mimic human organs.
>
> Critics of reducing animal testing argue that these alternatives are unproven and that no computer model or cell culture can replicate the complexity of a living organism. There is truth in this objection: biological systems involve interactions among trillions of cells across multiple organ systems, and current technology cannot fully simulate this complexity. But this argument assumes that animal models do simulate that complexity accurately, which the 95 percent failure rate strongly contradicts. The question is not whether alternatives are perfect but whether they are better predictors of human outcomes than animal models -- and a growing body of evidence suggests that for many applications, they are. The ethical dimension also deserves weight. An estimated 100 million animals are used in laboratory research worldwide each year, often subjected to procedures that cause significant suffering. As the scientific rationale for animal testing weakens, the ethical cost becomes harder to justify.

> On September 12, 1992, Mae Carol Jemison became the first African American woman to travel into space, orbiting the Earth for nearly eight days aboard the Space Shuttle Endeavour. The achievement was remarkable not only for its historic significance but for the path Jemison had taken to reach it. Born in 1956 in Decatur, Alabama, and raised in Chicago, Jemison was fascinated by science from childhood but grew up in an era when neither the scientific establishment nor the space program welcomed women of color. She also established an international science camp for students aged 12 to 16, emphasizing hands-on experimentation and critical thinking. In a 2009 interview, Jemison reflected on her approach to barriers: "Never be limited by other people's limited imaginations. Her career demonstrates that the path to groundbreaking achievement often requires not just talent and training but the willingness to persist in spaces that were not designed with you in mind.

## Section 4: Passage D — "The Science of Forgetting" (Scientific)

Read the passage. Then answer questions 17-20.

> Most people think of forgetting as a failure of memory -- a mental malfunction that should be avoided. Cognitive scientists increasingly argue the opposite: forgetting is not a flaw but a feature of the human brain, essential for efficient thinking and psychological well-being. Research by neuroscientist Blake Richards at the University of Toronto has shown that the brain actively works to forget unnecessary details, not because the memory storage is full, but because retaining every piece of information would make it harder to identify patterns, make decisions, and think flexibly. A brain that remembered every face in every crowd, every word of every conversation, and every meal it had ever eaten would be overwhelmed by irrelevant data and unable to focus on what matters.
>
> The process of selective forgetting appears to follow predictable patterns. German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus, working in the 1880s, discovered what is now known as the "forgetting curve": a steep decline in memory retention that occurs within the first 24 hours after learning new information, followed by a gradual leveling off. Ebbinghaus found that learners typically forget approximately 70 percent of new information within 24 hours if they do not review it. However, each time information is reviewed, the forgetting curve flattens -- the brain retains the material for longer periods before it begins to fade. This finding is the scientific basis for spaced repetition, a study technique in which learners review material at increasing intervals. Students who use spaced repetition consistently outperform those who cram all their studying into a single session, even when total study time is identical.
>
> Some researchers believe that the brain's forgetting mechanisms also serve an emotional purpose. Painful or traumatic memories naturally lose their vividness over time as the brain gradually strips away the emotional intensity attached to them. While the factual content of a difficult experience may be retained, the raw emotional charge fades, allowing people to recall the event without reliving the full force of the original pain. This process, which psychologists call "fading affect bias," may be one of the brain's most important tools for maintaining psychological resilience. Without it, every painful experience would remain as vivid and distressing as the moment it occurred, making it difficult to move forward.

The author's main claim is that scientific reliance on animal testing should be reduced in favor of newer technologies that may more accurately predict human outcomes. This argument is built on two primary reasons. First, the author argues that animal testing is an unreliable predictor, citing the striking statistic from the British Medical Journal that "approximately 95 percent of drugs that pass animal trials fail in human clinical trials." This evidence is both relevant and powerful because it directly challenges the fundamental purpose of animal testing. Second, the author presents emerging alternatives -- including organ-on-a-chip technology that "uses miniature devices lined with human cells to simulate the functions of human organs" and computer modeling that "can now simulate thousands of molecular interactions in hours" -- as potentially more accurate options. A significant strength of the argument is the concede-and-counter technique used with the critic's objection: the author acknowledges that "biological systems involve interactions among trillions of cells across multiple organ systems" but rebuts by noting that this same complexity argument undermines animal testing, since the 95 percent failure rate shows animals do not replicate human complexity either. A weakness, however, is that the passage claims alternatives "may be more accurate" without providing specific comparative data. The reader is told that alternatives exist and seem promising, but no study is cited showing organ-on-a-chip or computer modeling outperforming animal testing in a direct comparison. Providing such data would significantly strengthen the argument.
*Scoring: 1 point for identifying claim and at least 2 reasons, 1 point for evaluating evidence relevance/sufficiency, 1 point for identifying a strength, 1 point for identifying a weakness, 1 point for at least 2 cited pieces of evidence.*

## Remediation Guidance

If a student does not meet mastery on a specific standard, use the following targeted reteaching plan:

**RI.7.1 (Citing Several Pieces of Evidence and Making Inferences):**
- Reteach using Lessons 1-2, focusing on the C-MEI method and the Inference Ladder
- Provide two additional short passages and have the student practice selecting several distinct (not redundant) pieces of evidence for specific claims
- For inference questions, review the Gather-Reason-State process and practice supporting inferences with multiple pieces of evidence
- Common issue: students cite only one piece of evidence or cite multiple pieces that all say the same thing

**RI.7.2 (Central Ideas, Development, and Objective Summary):**
- Reteach using Lessons 3-5, focusing on the Two-Pass Method and the CIOD summary process
- Provide a passage with two clearly distinct central ideas and have the student identify both using color-coded highlighting
- For summary writing, provide a student-written summary containing hidden opinions and have the student identify and remove them
- Common issues: students find only one central idea; students include opinions in summaries; students confuse topic with central idea

**RI.7.6 (Author's Point of View and Position Distinguishing):**
- Reteach using Lessons 6-7, focusing on the three detectors (word choice, detail selection, structure) and the CCRR techniques
- Provide two short passages on the same topic written from different perspectives and have the student identify the point of view in each
- Practice mapping opposing positions and the author's responses using the Position Mapping graphic organizer
- Common issues: students confuse purpose with point of view; students fail to identify how the author distinguishes from opposing views

**RI.7.8 (Argument Tracing and Evaluation):**
- Reteach using Lessons 8-9, focusing on the Argument Map pyramid and the R-S-L evaluation test
- Provide a passage with a clear argument structure and have the student map the claim, reasons, and evidence before evaluating
- Practice evaluating evidence for relevance, sufficiency, and soundness using short, focused examples
- Common issues: students confuse claims with evidence; students accept all evidence as sufficient without evaluating; students cannot identify reasoning flaws
