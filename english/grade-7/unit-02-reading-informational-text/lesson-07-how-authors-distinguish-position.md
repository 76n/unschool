# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 7

## Topic
How Authors Distinguish Their Position

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Analyze how an author distinguishes his or her position from the positions of others mentioned or referenced in the text
- Identify the specific techniques authors use to set their views apart, including concession-and-rebuttal, contrasting evidence, and rhetorical framing
- Explain how an author's handling of opposing positions strengthens or weakens the author's own argument

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine an author's point of view and purpose in informational text (Lesson 6, RI.7.6)
- Ability to identify word choice, detail selection, and structure as clues to purpose and perspective (Lesson 6)
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence (Lessons 1-2, RI.7.1)
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Position Mapping graphic organizer (columns: Author's Position, Others' Position, How Author Distinguishes -- technique used, Effect on Reader)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in three colors (author's position, others' positions, transition/framing language)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept that informational authors often reference other viewpoints in order to position their own.

**Activity: Whose Voice Is Speaking?**

Say: "In Lesson 6, you learned to identify what an author believes and why the author wrote the text. Today we focus on a specific skill: analyzing how authors set their position apart from what others believe. In informational text, authors do not just state their view -- they often bring in other voices and then distinguish their position from those voices."

Write on the board:
- "Some people argue that video games are purely harmful to young people. However, recent research from Oxford University suggests that moderate gaming can actually improve problem-solving skills and social connection."

Say: "How many voices or positions do you hear in these two sentences?"

Allow the student to respond. (Answer: Two -- "some people" who believe games are harmful, and the author who is citing research suggesting moderate gaming has benefits.)

Say: "The author brought in the opposing view ('some people argue...') and then distinguished the author's own position by introducing contrasting evidence ('however, recent research suggests...'). Today you will learn to identify and analyze these moves."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students four techniques authors use to distinguish their position from others.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Concession:** When an author acknowledges that an opposing point has some validity before presenting a counterargument
- **Rebuttal:** A direct response to an opposing claim that argues against it using evidence or reasoning
- **Contrasting evidence:** Evidence the author presents that directly contradicts or challenges an opposing position
- **Rhetorical framing:** The way an author presents or describes an opposing position, which can make it seem stronger or weaker before responding to it

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Authors use four main techniques to distinguish their position from others. I call them the CCRR techniques."

Write on the board:
1. **Concede and Counter:** The author admits the other side has a point, then shows why the author's position is still stronger. Signal phrases: "While it is true that...," "Although some argue...," "This is a valid concern, but..."
2. **Contrast with Evidence:** The author presents evidence that directly contradicts the opposing position. Signal phrases: "However, research shows...," "In fact, data reveals...," "On the contrary..."
3. **Reframe the Issue:** The author redefines the question or problem in a way that makes the opposing position seem less relevant. Signal phrase: "The real question is not whether... but whether..."
4. **Represent and Rebut:** The author describes the opposing view, then dismantles it point by point. Signal phrases: "Critics argue that... However, this overlooks...," "Opponents claim... but this fails to account for..."

Say: "Let me show you all four techniques in one passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Should Cities Invest in Public Transportation?"**

> Many American cities are grappling with traffic congestion, air pollution, and transportation inequality, and a growing body of evidence suggests that expanding public transit systems is one of the most effective solutions. Cities that have invested heavily in bus rapid transit, light rail, and subway systems have seen measurable reductions in commute times, vehicle emissions, and transportation costs for low-income residents. A study by the American Public Transportation Association found that every dollar invested in public transit generates approximately four dollars in economic returns through reduced congestion, lower fuel consumption, and increased access to employment.
>
> Critics of public transit expansion raise several objections. Some argue that Americans are too attached to their cars to use public transit regularly. While personal vehicle preference is deeply ingrained in American culture, cities like Portland, Denver, and Minneapolis have demonstrated that when public transit is reliable, frequent, and well-connected, ridership grows steadily. Others contend that public transit systems are too expensive to build and maintain, pointing to cost overruns in projects like California's high-speed rail. This is a valid concern -- large infrastructure projects often exceed initial budgets. However, the cost of doing nothing is also enormous: the Texas A&M Transportation Institute estimates that traffic congestion costs American commuters more than 87 billion dollars annually in wasted fuel and lost productivity. The real question is not whether public transit is expensive but whether the long-term costs of car-dependent infrastructure are sustainable.
>
> Perhaps the most persistent objection is that public transit only works in dense urban areas like New York City, not in sprawling cities. But this argument confuses cause and effect. Low-density sprawl is not a fixed condition -- it is the result of decades of policy choices that prioritized highway construction over transit investment. Cities that build transit first often see development cluster naturally around stations, creating the density that supports further transit use. The choice is not between transit and cars but between investing in a system that serves all residents and continuing to subsidize a system that primarily benefits those who can afford to own, insure, and maintain a personal vehicle.

Say: "Let me map the positions and techniques."

Write on the board:

| Others' Position | Author's Technique | Author's Response |
|---|---|---|
| Americans love cars too much to use transit | **Contrast with Evidence** | Portland, Denver, Minneapolis show ridership grows when transit is reliable |
| Transit is too expensive | **Concede and Counter** | Admits "valid concern" about cost overruns, then contrasts with $87 billion annual congestion cost |
| Transit is too expensive (continued) | **Reframe the Issue** | "The real question is not whether public transit is expensive but whether car-dependent infrastructure is sustainable" |
| Transit only works in dense cities | **Represent and Rebut** | Argues this confuses cause and effect -- transit creates the density it needs; low-density sprawl is a policy choice, not a fixed condition |

Say: "Notice how the author handles each opposing view differently. For the car-culture argument, the author provides counter-examples. For the cost argument, the author concedes the point and then reframes the issue. For the density argument, the author rebuts the logic itself. Skilled authors choose different techniques for different objections."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying how an author distinguishes their position from others.

**Passage B: "The Value of Arts Education in Schools"**

> When school budgets tighten, arts programs -- music, visual art, theater, and dance -- are often the first to be cut. Administrators and school board members justify these cuts by arguing that academic subjects like math, science, and reading are more essential to students' futures and that arts programs are luxuries schools cannot afford in lean times. This reasoning, while understandable given financial pressures, mischaracterizes what arts education actually provides.
>
> Research consistently demonstrates that students who participate in arts programs perform better academically than those who do not. A ten-year study by the National Endowment for the Arts found that low-income students with high arts involvement were three times more likely to earn a bachelor's degree than low-income students with low arts involvement. Arts education develops skills that transfer directly to academic success: creative problem-solving, collaboration, persistence through difficulty, and the ability to communicate complex ideas. These are not soft extras -- they are the foundational competencies that employers consistently rank as most important in hiring decisions.
>
> Some critics acknowledge the benefits of arts education but insist that schools simply cannot afford it when standardized test scores in math and reading are the primary measure of school success. This position treats test scores as the sole purpose of education, ignoring the reality that schools are also responsible for developing well-rounded individuals capable of creative thought, empathy, and cultural understanding. A school that produces students who score well on math tests but cannot think creatively, collaborate with diverse peers, or express themselves meaningfully has not fully succeeded in its mission.

Say: "Use the Position Mapping graphic organizer. Identify at least two opposing positions and analyze how the author distinguishes from each. Name the technique used."

Allow the student to work for three minutes.

Expected response:

| Others' Position | Author's Technique | Author's Response |
|---|---|---|
| Arts programs are luxuries that can be cut when budgets tighten; math, science, and reading are more essential | **Concede and Counter** | Acknowledges the reasoning is "understandable given financial pressures" but counters that it "mischaracterizes what arts education actually provides," then presents research showing arts students outperform academically |
| Schools cannot afford arts when test scores are the primary measure of success | **Reframe the Issue** | Reframes by arguing that "this position treats test scores as the sole purpose of education" and that schools have a broader mission including "creative thought, empathy, and cultural understanding" |

Say: "You identified two opposing positions and two different techniques. The author concedes financial pressure is real but counters with evidence, then reframes what the purpose of education actually is. These moves distinguish the author's position as more nuanced and complete than the opposing views."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how an author distinguishes their position from others.

**Passage C: "The Debate Over Year-Round Schooling"**

> The traditional American school calendar, with a long summer vacation from June through August, was designed for an agricultural society in which children were needed to help with farm work during growing season. That society no longer exists for the vast majority of American families, yet the calendar persists. Advocates for year-round schooling argue that distributing school days more evenly throughout the year -- with shorter, more frequent breaks replacing the long summer -- would better serve today's students by reducing the learning loss that occurs during extended time away from school.
>
> Opponents of year-round schooling raise several concerns. Parents worry about the disruption to family vacation traditions and summer camp schedules. Teachers express concern about burnout if they do not receive a sustained break from instruction. The tourism and summer camp industries argue that a shift away from summer vacation would devastate businesses that depend on families having extended free time in warm months. These are not trivial objections -- they reflect genuine attachments to a way of life that many families value deeply.
>
> However, the educational costs of the traditional calendar deserve equal weight in this debate. Research by the RAND Corporation shows that students lose an average of one to three months of learning over the summer, with the greatest losses occurring among students from low-income families who have fewer opportunities for educational enrichment during the break. Over the course of elementary school, cumulative summer learning loss can add up to nearly two full years of achievement difference between wealthy and low-income students. Year-round schooling would not eliminate this gap, but studies from schools that have adopted year-round calendars show that it significantly narrows it. The question families must ask is whether the convenience of a three-month summer vacation is worth the educational cost that disproportionately falls on the students who can least afford it.

**Directions:**

1. **(On-level)** Identify the author's position on year-round schooling. Then identify at least two opposing positions mentioned in the passage. For each opposing position, name the technique the author uses to distinguish from it and cite specific evidence.
2. **(On-level)** How does the author's final sentence function as a position-distinguishing technique? What type of technique is it, and how does it affect the reader?
3. **(Stretch)** The author writes: "These are not trivial objections -- they reflect genuine attachments to a way of life that many families value deeply." Why does the author include this concession? How does acknowledging the opposing side's emotional weight affect the persuasiveness of the author's argument?
4. **(Challenge)** Rewrite the second paragraph of this passage from the perspective of someone who opposes year-round schooling. Use the same four techniques (concede and counter, contrast with evidence, reframe the issue, represent and rebut) but in favor of the traditional calendar.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.6) What is the author's purpose in writing this passage? How is it conveyed through detail selection?
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.2) Write an objective summary of this passage in 3-4 sentences. Do not include personal opinions.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> While some parents and educators advocate for banning all screen time for children under age five, the American Academy of Pediatrics takes a more measured position. The Academy recommends avoiding screens for children under 18 months except for video calls with family members, and suggests that children ages two to five be limited to one hour of high-quality programming per day. Critics of this recommendation argue that any screen time for young children is harmful, but a longitudinal study from the University of Texas found that moderate exposure to educational programming was associated with improved vocabulary development in children from low-literacy households.

1. Identify one opposing position and explain how the author of this passage distinguishes from it. Name the specific technique.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Opposing position: Some parents and educators advocate for banning all screen time for children under five. The author distinguishes from this position using the **contrast with evidence** technique. The author introduces the opposing "ban all screens" position, then presents evidence from the University of Texas that "moderate exposure to educational programming was associated with improved vocabulary development in children from low-literacy households." This contrasting evidence directly challenges the absolutist position by showing that some screen time can actually be beneficial. The author also uses the American Academy of Pediatrics' more nuanced recommendation as an authoritative middle position.

**Mastery = correctly identifies an opposing position and names the technique the author uses to distinguish from it, with supporting textual evidence**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the CCRR techniques using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Practice identifying opposing positions first (look for signal phrases like "some argue," "critics claim," "opponents say") before analyzing the author's response
- Give the student a passage with the opposing positions highlighted and have them focus only on identifying the technique used to respond
- Use the Position Mapping graphic organizer with sentence starters

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8 on tracing arguments and claims
- Encourage the student to notice position-distinguishing techniques in opinion articles, editorials, and persuasive essays

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often struggle to see the difference between an author mentioning an opposing view and an author distinguishing from an opposing view. Mentioning means the author acknowledges the other view exists. Distinguishing means the author actively positions their own view as different from, and typically stronger than, the opposing view. The techniques (concede and counter, contrast with evidence, reframe the issue, represent and rebut) are the specific moves that accomplish this distinction.
- The concession technique is counterintuitive for many students. They think acknowledging the other side weakens the argument. In fact, concession often strengthens an argument because it shows the author has considered opposing views fairly. Help students see that "this is a valid concern, but..." is more persuasive than simply ignoring the concern.
- Color-coding is especially helpful in this lesson. Use one color for the author's position, another for others' positions, and a third for the transition language that signals the move between them (however, but, while, although, despite, on the other hand).
- This lesson completes the RI.7.6 pair (Lessons 6-7). Lesson 6 taught students to identify purpose and point of view. This lesson teaches them to analyze how the author positions that view relative to others. Together, these skills prepare students for the argument evaluation work in Lessons 8-9.
- Some passages in this lesson use three or more opposing positions. Do not require students to analyze every opposing position -- two is sufficient for mastery. The goal is understanding the technique, not exhaustive cataloging.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Author's position:** The traditional school calendar is outdated and should be replaced with year-round schooling to reduce summer learning loss, particularly for low-income students.
   **Opposing position 1:** Parents worry about disruption to family vacations and summer traditions. **Technique:** Concede and counter. The author concedes these are "not trivial objections" that "reflect genuine attachments to a way of life that many families value deeply," but then counters with evidence about learning loss (one to three months per summer, nearly two full years of cumulative difference for low-income students).
   **Opposing position 2:** The tourism and summer camp industries argue that year-round schooling would devastate their businesses. **Technique:** Represent and rebut. The author represents the economic concern fairly but then reframes the debate by presenting the educational costs as deserving "equal weight" and by arguing that the convenience of summer should be weighed against the disproportionate harm to low-income students.

2. The final sentence -- "The question families must ask is whether the convenience of a three-month summer vacation is worth the educational cost that disproportionately falls on the students who can least afford it" -- is a **reframing** technique. It redefines the issue from "should we change the calendar?" to "is convenience worth inequality?" By framing the debate in terms of equity, the author makes the opposing position (keeping summer vacation) seem like a choice to prioritize adult comfort over children's educational opportunity. This affects the reader by making it emotionally and morally harder to side with the traditional calendar.

3. The author includes the concession "these are not trivial objections" to build credibility and demonstrate fairness. By acknowledging that the opposing side's concerns are genuine and emotionally meaningful, the author prevents the reader from feeling that the argument dismisses people's real attachments. This concession makes the author seem reasonable and empathetic, which makes the subsequent argument about learning loss more persuasive. If the author had simply dismissed vacation concerns as unimportant, the reader might have resisted the argument. By honoring the emotional weight of the opposing view before presenting the educational evidence, the author creates the impression that the evidence must be very strong indeed to outweigh such valid concerns.

4. Answers will vary. A strong response might include: "Advocates for year-round schooling point to research showing summer learning loss, particularly among low-income students. This concern is legitimate and well-documented. However, restructuring the school calendar assumes that the problem is time away from school, when the real issue is the lack of summer enrichment opportunities in low-income communities. Instead of overhauling a calendar that serves most families well, schools could invest in targeted summer programs for at-risk students at a fraction of the cost. Furthermore, year-round schooling has not been shown to produce dramatic improvements in achievement: studies show it 'significantly narrows' the gap but does not eliminate it, suggesting that the disruption to families, businesses, and communities may not be justified by modest gains."

### Interleaved Review
5. The author's purpose is to argue that the traditional school calendar should be replaced with year-round schooling to reduce summer learning loss and its disproportionate impact on low-income students. This purpose is conveyed through detail selection: the author includes specific research on learning loss (RAND Corporation data, one to three months lost, nearly two years cumulative difference), presents the traditional calendar as a relic of agricultural society ("that society no longer exists"), and frames the summer break in terms of equity rather than convenience.
6. The traditional American school calendar, designed for an agricultural era, features a long summer vacation that advocates for year-round schooling argue causes significant learning loss. Opponents raise concerns about disrupted family traditions, teacher burnout, and impacts on summer-dependent businesses. Research indicates that summer learning loss averages one to three months and disproportionately affects low-income students, with cumulative effects reaching nearly two years over elementary school. Schools that have adopted year-round calendars have reported that the change significantly narrows this achievement gap.
