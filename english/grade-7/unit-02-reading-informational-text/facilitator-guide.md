# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2: Reading Informational Text

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview
Unit 2 advances students' informational reading skills from the foundational work completed in Grade 6 Unit 2 and Grade 7 Unit 1 (Reading Literature). In Grade 6, students learned to cite textual evidence, determine a single central idea, analyze how authors introduce and elaborate on key individuals and ideas, identify author's purpose, and integrate information from different media. Grade 7 Unit 2 raises the rigor significantly: students must now cite several pieces of evidence (not just one), identify and trace two or more central ideas within a single text, analyze how an author distinguishes his or her position from opposing views, and evaluate the soundness and sufficiency of an argument's reasoning and evidence. These skills prepare students to be critical, analytical readers of the nonfiction texts they encounter across disciplines and in everyday life. By the end of this unit, students will approach informational texts not as passive receivers of information but as active evaluators of how authors construct meaning, advance positions, and build arguments.

### Prerequisite Skills (Grade 6)
Students should be able to:
- Cite textual evidence to support analysis of explicit information and inferences (RI.6.1)
- Determine a central idea and how it is conveyed through particular details; provide an objective summary (RI.6.2)
- Analyze how a key individual, event, or idea is introduced, illustrated, and elaborated in a text (RI.6.3)
- Determine an author's point of view or purpose in a text and explain how it is conveyed (RI.6.6)
- Integrate information presented in different media or formats to develop a coherent understanding (RI.6.7)

### Facilitation Tips
- **Push beyond single-evidence citation.** The critical leap from Grade 6 to Grade 7 for RI.7.1 is the word "several." Students must learn to weave together multiple pieces of evidence, not just locate one good quote. Model how two or three pieces of evidence work together to build a stronger case than any single piece alone.
- **Make central idea identification a two-step process.** For RI.7.2, students must now find two or more central ideas in a single text. Teach them to read for the first central idea, then deliberately search for a second thread. Many students will identify one central idea and assume they are finished. Use color-coded highlighting -- one color per central idea -- to make the multiple threads visible.
- **Teach the distinction between purpose and point of view.** Purpose is why the author wrote the text (to inform, persuade, raise awareness). Point of view is what the author believes about the topic. RI.7.6 requires both, and students often conflate them. Use the formula: "The author wrote this to [purpose] because the author believes [point of view]."
- **Treat argument analysis as a structural skill.** For RI.7.8, students need to see argument as a structure with identifiable parts: claim, reasons, evidence. Teach them to map these parts before evaluating them. Students who skip the mapping step often make vague judgments about "good" or "bad" arguments without identifying what specifically is strong or weak.
- **Model the difference between sound and unsound reasoning.** Lesson 9 introduces the critical skill of evaluating whether evidence actually supports a claim. Use clear, exaggerated examples of logical fallacies first (e.g., "My neighbor's cat is orange, so all cats must be orange"), then move to the subtler reasoning errors found in real-world informational texts.
- **Build from the concrete to the integrated.** Lessons 1-9 each target one standard. Lesson 10 integrates all four standards in a single analysis. Remind students during Lesson 10 that they already know each skill individually -- the new challenge is applying them simultaneously.
- **Use diverse, engaging topics.** Passages in this unit cover science discoveries, environmental issues, technology, community matters, and historical events. Leverage this variety to sustain student interest and demonstrate that analytical reading applies across every subject area.
- **Encourage written analysis, not just answers.** At the Grade 7 level, students should be writing analytical responses, not just circling multiple-choice options. Even when an answer seems simple, push students to explain their reasoning in writing. This builds the argumentative and explanatory writing skills they will need in Unit 3.

### Common Misconceptions
| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| Students think citing "several" pieces of evidence means repeating the same idea in different words | Several pieces of evidence means distinct details that each contribute something different to the analysis. Teach students to ask: "Does this new piece of evidence add something the previous pieces did not?" If not, they need to keep searching. |
| Students identify one central idea and assume the task is complete | RI.7.2 explicitly requires two or more central ideas. Teach students to use a "second pass" strategy: after identifying the first central idea, reread specifically looking for a second major thread the author develops. Use two-column graphic organizers to make both ideas visible. |
| Students confuse the topic with a central idea | The topic is what the text is about in a word or phrase ("ocean pollution"). A central idea is the specific claim or insight the author develops about the topic ("Ocean pollution threatens marine ecosystems in ways that also endanger human food supplies"). Practice distinguishing these with multiple examples. |
| Students include personal reactions or judgments in objective summaries | An objective summary restates what the author says without adding what the reader thinks about it. Teach students to scan summaries for opinion markers: "I think," "interesting," "important," "unfortunately," "the best part." Replace these with neutral restatements. |
| Students equate author's purpose with author's point of view | Purpose is the reason for writing (to inform, persuade, argue). Point of view is the author's position or belief about the subject. An author may write to inform (purpose) while clearly believing that climate change is an urgent threat (point of view). Teach these as related but distinct concepts. |
| Students think that if an author presents evidence, the argument must be sound | Evidence alone does not make an argument sound. The evidence must be relevant (connected to the claim), sufficient (enough to prove the claim), and logically connected (the reasoning from evidence to claim must follow). Teach students to evaluate each element separately. |
| Students struggle to identify when an author is distinguishing their position from others | Authors use signal phrases like "some argue," "critics claim," "opponents believe," followed by their own rebuttal. Teach students to look for these transition markers and map whose position is being stated at each point in the text. |

### Assessment Guidance
- **Formative:** Use exit tickets after every lesson to assess mastery of that lesson's target standard. Track whether students can cite multiple evidence pieces (Lessons 1-2), identify two central ideas (Lessons 3-5), analyze author's position relative to others (Lessons 6-7), and evaluate argument structure and reasoning (Lessons 8-9). Review exit ticket results before each new session and reteach as needed.
- **Summative:** The Unit Assessment includes original informational passages with approximately 24 questions covering all four standards. It features a mix of multiple-choice (2 points each), short-answer (3 points each), and extended-response items (5 points each), totaling approximately 60 points. Mastery is 70% overall (42 out of 60 points), with at least 50% on each individual standard.
- **Portfolio:** Consider having students compile an analytical reading portfolio throughout the unit, including annotated passages, multi-evidence responses, dual central idea maps, and argument evaluation worksheets. This portfolio provides a record of growth and gives students tangible evidence of their developing analytical skills.

### Differentiation at a Glance
- **Below grade level:** Provide shorter passages (100-150 words) or pre-annotated versions with key evidence highlighted and central ideas underlined. Pre-teach vocabulary before each lesson. Use sentence frames for analytical responses (e.g., "The first central idea is ___ because the author states, '___.' The second central idea is ___ because ___"). Allow students to discuss answers orally before writing. For argument analysis, provide a partially completed argument map that students fill in.
- **On grade level:** Use the lesson passages and questions as written. Students complete the On-level tier of independent practice. Encourage students to write multi-sentence analytical responses that cite several pieces of evidence, identify multiple central ideas, and evaluate reasoning with specific textual support.
- **Above grade level:** Assign the Challenge tier of independent practice. Encourage students to analyze real-world nonfiction texts (news editorials, science articles, opinion columns) using the skills from this unit. Have students write their own persuasive passages that deliberately include sound reasoning for peers to evaluate. Challenge students to compare how two different authors cover the same topic, analyzing differences in point of view and argument structure.

### Connection to Future Units
The critical reading skills developed in this unit form the analytical foundation for all subsequent English Language Arts work in Grades 7 and 8. The multi-evidence citation skills from RI.7.1 prepare students for Grade 8's requirement to cite the "strongest" textual evidence, requiring even sharper judgment about evidence quality. The dual central idea analysis from RI.7.2 builds toward Grade 8's analysis of how central ideas develop in relation to one another and to the text's overall structure. The author's position analysis from RI.7.6 leads directly into Grade 8's work on analyzing how differences in point of view create effects such as suspense, humor, or dramatic irony in informational contexts. The argument evaluation skills from RI.7.8 progress toward Grade 8's requirement to delineate and evaluate arguments in texts with conflicting information -- a skill essential for navigating the complex information landscape students will encounter in high school and beyond. Within Grade 7 itself, this unit's skills directly support Unit 3 (Writing), where students will construct their own arguments with claims, reasons, and evidence, and Unit 4 (Grammar and Language), where sentence-level precision supports clearer analytical expression.
