# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 8

## Topic
Tracing Arguments and Claims

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the structure of an argument in informational text: main claim, supporting reasons, and evidence
- Trace how an author builds an argument by connecting claims to reasons and reasons to evidence
- Distinguish between claims (arguable positions), reasons (explanations of why the claim is true), and evidence (specific facts, data, or examples that support the reasons)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine author's point of view and purpose (Lesson 6, RI.7.6)
- Ability to analyze how an author distinguishes their position from others (Lesson 7, RI.7.6)
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence (Lessons 1-2, RI.7.1)
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Argument Map graphic organizer (pyramid structure: Claim at top, Reasons in middle row, Evidence at bottom)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in three colors (one for claims, one for reasons, one for evidence)
- Index cards for sorting activity (optional)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept of argument as a structure with identifiable parts and connect it to the author's purpose and point of view work from Lessons 6-7.

**Activity: Building Blocks of an Argument**

Say: "In Lessons 6 and 7, you learned to identify what an author believes and how the author positions that belief against other views. Now we go under the hood: How does an author build an argument? What are the building blocks?"

Write on the board:
- **Claim:** Schools should require students to learn a second language starting in kindergarten.
- **Reason:** Early language learning is more effective than starting in middle or high school.
- **Evidence:** A study by Cornell University found that children who began learning a second language before age seven achieved near-native fluency rates 40 percent higher than those who started after age twelve.

Say: "An argument is a structure. The claim is the author's main position -- the statement they want you to accept. The reason explains why the claim should be accepted. The evidence is the specific data, fact, or example that proves the reason. Today you will learn to trace this structure in informational text."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify and map the claim-reason-evidence structure of arguments in informational text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Claim:** An arguable position or assertion that the author wants the reader to accept; the main point of the argument
- **Reason:** An explanation of why the claim is true or valid; the logical bridge between the claim and the evidence
- **Evidence:** Specific facts, statistics, examples, expert opinions, or research findings used to support a reason
- **Argument structure:** The organized framework of an argument, consisting of a claim supported by reasons, each backed by specific evidence

### Instruction Notes

Say: "To trace an argument, I use what I call Argument Mapping. Think of it as building a pyramid."

Draw on the board:
```
          [CLAIM]
        /         \
   [REASON 1]   [REASON 2]
   /      \       /      \
[Evidence] [Evidence] [Evidence] [Evidence]
```

Say: "The claim sits at the top. Underneath it are the reasons -- usually two or three. Under each reason are the specific pieces of evidence that support it. When you can fill in this pyramid, you have traced the argument. Let me demonstrate."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Why Every Student Should Learn to Code"**

> In an economy increasingly driven by technology, the ability to write computer code is becoming as fundamental as the ability to read and write. Schools that do not teach coding are leaving students unprepared for a workforce in which more than half of all high-paying jobs require some level of programming skill. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, computer science occupations are projected to grow 15 percent over the next decade -- more than twice the average growth rate for all occupations -- and the median annual wage for these positions exceeds 97,000 dollars.
>
> Beyond career preparation, coding develops thinking skills that benefit students in every subject. Programming requires logical reasoning: students must break complex problems into smaller steps, identify patterns, and test solutions systematically. A study published in the Journal of Educational Computing Research found that elementary school students who participated in a semester-long coding program showed significant improvement in mathematical reasoning and problem-solving skills, even on tasks unrelated to computers. These cognitive benefits persist long after the specific programming language is forgotten.
>
> Some argue that not every student needs to become a programmer, and this is true. But the same argument could be applied to algebra, chemistry, or essay writing -- subjects taught not because every student will use them professionally but because they develop essential ways of thinking. Coding teaches students to think precisely, to debug their own mistakes, and to create something functional from nothing but logic. In a world shaped by algorithms, students who understand how those algorithms work will be better equipped to navigate, question, and shape the systems that affect their lives.

Say: "Let me map this argument."

Write on the board:
- **Claim:** Every student should learn to code because it is becoming a fundamental skill for career success and cognitive development.
- **Reason 1:** Coding prepares students for a job market that increasingly requires programming skills.
  - Evidence: More than half of high-paying jobs require some programming skill.
  - Evidence: Computer science jobs projected to grow 15 percent over the next decade.
  - Evidence: Median annual wage exceeds $97,000.
- **Reason 2:** Coding develops transferable thinking skills that benefit students in every subject.
  - Evidence: Programming requires logical reasoning, breaking problems into steps, and testing solutions.
  - Evidence: Journal of Educational Computing Research study found coding improved mathematical reasoning in elementary students.
- **Reason 3:** Even students who do not become programmers benefit from understanding the algorithmic systems that shape their world.
  - Evidence: Comparison to algebra and chemistry -- taught for thinking skills, not direct professional use.
  - Evidence: In a world shaped by algorithms, understanding how they work helps students "navigate, question, and shape" systems.

Say: "Notice how each reason has its own evidence, and all three reasons support the central claim. The argument is layered: career preparation, cognitive benefits, and citizenship in a technological society. That is a well-structured argument."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice tracing an argument's structure with facilitator support.

**Passage B: "The Case for Preserving Urban Green Spaces"**

> Cities around the world are facing intense pressure to develop every available plot of land into housing, offices, or commercial space. In this competitive environment, parks, community gardens, and other green spaces are often viewed as luxuries that cannot compete with the economic returns of development. This perspective, while economically rational in the short term, ignores a substantial body of evidence demonstrating that urban green spaces provide benefits that no building can replicate.
>
> Green spaces serve as critical infrastructure for public health. A meta-analysis published in The Lancet Planetary Health, which combined data from over 140 studies across 20 countries, found that access to green spaces is associated with reduced rates of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, and premature death. The mechanisms are both physical and psychological: green spaces encourage physical activity, reduce air pollution through the filtering capacity of trees, and lower stress hormones in people who spend even twenty minutes in a natural setting. In neighborhoods without parks, residents are significantly less likely to meet recommended physical activity levels.
>
> Urban green spaces also generate measurable economic value that challenges the assumption that development always offers the best financial return. Property values within 500 feet of a well-maintained park are, on average, 8 to 20 percent higher than comparable properties farther away. Parks attract tourism, support local businesses, and reduce municipal spending on stormwater management because permeable ground and tree canopies absorb rainwater that would otherwise overwhelm sewer systems. A study by the Trust for Public Land estimated that urban parks in the United States generate more than 30 billion dollars in health and environmental benefits annually.

Say: "Use the Argument Map. Identify the claim, then trace the reasons and evidence. Work through it step by step."

Allow the student to work for three minutes.

Expected response:
- **Claim:** Cities should preserve urban green spaces because they provide irreplaceable health and economic benefits.
- **Reason 1:** Green spaces serve as critical infrastructure for public health.
  - Evidence: Lancet Planetary Health meta-analysis (140+ studies, 20 countries) found reduced rates of cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and premature death.
  - Evidence: Green spaces encourage physical activity, reduce air pollution, and lower stress hormones.
  - Evidence: Residents in neighborhoods without parks are less likely to meet physical activity recommendations.
- **Reason 2:** Green spaces generate measurable economic value that can exceed the returns from development.
  - Evidence: Property values within 500 feet of a park are 8-20% higher.
  - Evidence: Parks attract tourism and support local businesses.
  - Evidence: Parks reduce stormwater management costs.
  - Evidence: Trust for Public Land estimates more than $30 billion in annual health and environmental benefits.

Say: "You traced the argument clearly. The claim is about preservation, and it is built on two pillars -- health and economics. Each pillar has multiple pieces of evidence. That is a strong argument structure."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently trace argument structure in a new passage.

**Passage C: "Rethinking the School Lunch"**

> The typical American school lunch -- a reheated entree, canned vegetables, flavored milk, and a packaged dessert -- bears little resemblance to the meals nutritional experts recommend for growing adolescents. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, fewer than 10 percent of American students eat the recommended daily servings of fruits and vegetables, and school meals are a major contributor to this shortfall. A 2022 analysis by the School Nutrition Association found that 87 percent of school districts reported that federal reimbursement rates do not cover the true cost of preparing nutritious meals, forcing cafeterias to rely on processed, shelf-stable foods that are cheap to purchase but nutritionally poor.
>
> Schools that have invested in higher-quality meal programs report improvements that extend well beyond the cafeteria. A pilot program in Minneapolis replaced processed entrees with meals prepared from scratch using locally sourced ingredients and saw a 22 percent increase in meal participation, a 35 percent decrease in cafeteria food waste, and measurable improvement in afternoon classroom behavior and focus. Teachers at participating schools reported that students were more attentive and less irritable after lunch. Similar results emerged from a program in Oakland, California, where students who ate improved school meals showed gains in standardized test performance compared to a control group eating the standard menu.
>
> The argument against improving school lunches typically centers on cost. Better food costs more money, and school district budgets are already stretched thin. This is undeniably true. However, the costs of poor nutrition are also real and measurable: childhood obesity, type 2 diabetes, and diet-related health conditions cost the American healthcare system more than 170 billion dollars annually. Investing in school nutrition is not an expense separate from education -- it is a direct investment in students' capacity to learn. A student who is hungry, malnourished, or processing a sugar crash from a low-quality lunch cannot perform at the same level as a student who has eaten a balanced, nourishing meal.

**Directions:** Use the Argument Map to trace the argument in this passage.

1. **(On-level)** Identify the author's main claim. Then identify at least two reasons that support the claim and cite at least two pieces of evidence for each reason.
2. **(On-level)** Draw or describe the argument pyramid for this passage, showing how claim, reasons, and evidence are connected.
3. **(Stretch)** The third paragraph addresses a counterargument about cost. How does the author handle this counterargument? Identify the technique (from Lesson 7) and explain how it serves the overall argument.
4. **(Challenge)** Write a counter-argument to this passage. State a claim that opposes the author's position, provide at least two reasons, and support each reason with evidence (you may invent plausible evidence). Then explain which argument -- the passage's or yours -- has a stronger structure and why.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.3) How does the author introduce the problem of school lunch quality in the first paragraph? What specific details establish the reader's understanding of the issue?
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.2) Identify two central ideas in this passage. State each as a complete sentence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Public libraries should expand their digital lending programs to include more e-books, audiobooks, and streaming content. Many library patrons, particularly working parents and people with disabilities, cannot visit a physical library during regular hours. A 2023 survey by the American Library Association found that libraries offering robust digital collections saw a 45 percent increase in total borrowing and reached 30 percent more unique users than libraries with limited digital options.

1. Identify the claim, one reason, and one piece of evidence in this passage.

**Expected Answers:**
1. **Claim:** Public libraries should expand their digital lending programs to include more e-books, audiobooks, and streaming content. **Reason:** Many patrons cannot visit a physical library during regular hours, so digital access makes the library available to them. **Evidence:** The ALA survey found that libraries with robust digital collections saw a 45 percent increase in borrowing and reached 30 percent more unique users.

**Mastery = correctly identifies the claim as an arguable position, provides a reason that logically supports the claim, and cites a specific piece of evidence**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Argument Map using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Practice distinguishing claims from reasons from evidence using a sorting activity: write each on a separate card and have the student arrange them in the pyramid
- Start with very simple arguments (2-3 sentences) before returning to full passages
- Use sentence starters: "The author's claim is ___. One reason supporting this claim is ___. The evidence for this reason is ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9 on evaluating reasoning and evidence
- Encourage the student to map arguments they encounter in news articles and opinion pieces

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Argument Deconstruction:** Find an opinion article or editorial (with facilitator approval) and create a complete Argument Map showing the claim, all reasons, and all evidence. Assess: Are there any reasons without evidence? Any evidence without a clear connection to a reason?
2. **Build Your Own Argument:** Choose a topic you care about. Construct a complete argument with a claim, at least two reasons, and at least two pieces of evidence per reason. Organize it in pyramid form.
3. **Weak vs. Strong Arguments:** Provide two arguments on the same topic -- one well-structured with strong evidence and one poorly structured with weak evidence. Have the student map both and explain which is stronger and why.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common error is students confusing claims with evidence. A claim is an arguable position ("Schools should teach coding"). Evidence is a specific fact or data point ("Computer science jobs are projected to grow 15 percent"). If a student identifies evidence as the claim, ask: "Is this something that can be argued for or against, or is it a fact that supports an argument?"
- Reasons are the trickiest element for students to identify because they are often implicit rather than explicitly stated. A reason answers the question "Why should I believe the claim?" If the claim is "Schools should improve lunch quality" and the evidence is about improved test scores, the reason (which may not be stated explicitly) is "Better nutrition improves academic performance." Help students articulate the logical bridge.
- Some passages contain multiple claims rather than one central claim with supporting reasons. In this lesson, focus on passages with a single central claim to keep the mapping clear. Multi-claim arguments are more complex and will be addressed as students develop greater analytical skill.
- Connecting this lesson to Lesson 7: the position-distinguishing techniques from Lesson 7 (concede and counter, contrast with evidence, reframe, represent and rebut) often appear within the argument structure as ways of handling counterarguments. Help students see that tracing the argument and analyzing how the author handles opposing views are complementary skills.
- The Argument Map pyramid is a concrete tool that makes abstract argument structure visible. Encourage students to draw the pyramid on scratch paper whenever they analyze an argument, even outside of formal assignments.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Claim:** Schools should invest in higher-quality lunch programs because improved nutrition leads to better academic performance and long-term health outcomes.
   **Reason 1:** Higher-quality school meals produce measurable improvements in student behavior, engagement, and academic performance.
   - Evidence: Minneapolis pilot program saw 22% increase in meal participation, 35% decrease in food waste, and improved afternoon behavior.
   - Evidence: Teachers reported students were "more attentive and less irritable after lunch."
   - Evidence: Oakland program showed gains in standardized test performance for students eating improved meals.
   **Reason 2:** The long-term health costs of poor school nutrition far exceed the cost of improving meals.
   - Evidence: Childhood obesity and diet-related conditions cost the American healthcare system more than $170 billion annually.
   - Evidence: Fewer than 10% of students eat recommended daily servings of fruits and vegetables.
   - Evidence: 87% of school districts report federal reimbursement does not cover the cost of nutritious meals.

2. Argument pyramid:
   - Top (Claim): Schools should invest in higher-quality lunch programs.
   - Middle left (Reason 1): Better meals improve student performance and behavior.
   - Middle right (Reason 2): The health costs of poor nutrition far exceed the cost of better meals.
   - Bottom left (Evidence for Reason 1): Minneapolis pilot results, teacher reports, Oakland test score gains.
   - Bottom right (Evidence for Reason 2): $170 billion healthcare costs, fewer than 10% of students eat enough produce, 87% of districts underfunded.

3. The third paragraph addresses the cost counterargument using the **concede and counter** technique from Lesson 7. The author first concedes: "Better food costs more money, and school district budgets are already stretched thin. This is undeniably true." By acknowledging the financial reality, the author builds credibility. The author then counters by presenting the even larger cost of inaction -- "$170 billion annually" in healthcare costs from diet-related conditions. This serves the overall argument by making the cost of improving lunches seem small compared to the cost of not improving them. The author also **reframes the issue** by stating that investing in nutrition "is not an expense separate from education -- it is a direct investment in students' capacity to learn," changing the frame from "spending money on food" to "investing in learning."

4. Answers will vary. A strong counter-argument might claim: "Schools should focus their limited budgets on academic programs rather than meal quality." Reason 1: Academic programs have a more direct and proven impact on student outcomes than food quality. (Plausible evidence: School districts that invested in smaller class sizes or tutoring programs saw larger test score improvements than those that invested in cafeteria upgrades.) Reason 2: Families, not schools, bear the primary responsibility for children's nutrition. (Plausible evidence: Students eat two of three daily meals at home; improving one meal at school cannot compensate for poor nutrition during the rest of the day.) Analysis of which argument is stronger should consider: the original passage has more specific, real evidence (named studies with quantified results), while the counter-argument relies on plausible but invented evidence. Structure alone does not determine strength -- the quality and specificity of evidence matters.

### Interleaved Review
5. The author introduces the school lunch problem by describing the typical lunch ("a reheated entree, canned vegetables, flavored milk, and a packaged dessert") and then contrasting it with nutritional recommendations. The specific statistic that "fewer than 10 percent of American students eat the recommended daily servings of fruits and vegetables" quantifies the gap between reality and ideal. The detail that "87 percent of school districts reported that federal reimbursement rates do not cover the true cost of preparing nutritious meals" explains the systemic cause of the problem, moving the reader from seeing what is wrong to understanding why.
6. Central Idea 1: American school lunches are nutritionally poor because federal funding does not cover the cost of preparing healthy meals, resulting in reliance on cheap, processed foods. Central Idea 2: Schools that have invested in higher-quality meal programs have seen measurable improvements in student participation, behavior, academic performance, and reduced food waste.
