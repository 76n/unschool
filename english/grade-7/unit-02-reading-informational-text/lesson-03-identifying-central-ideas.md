# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 3

## Topic
Identifying Central Ideas

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify two or more central ideas within a single informational text
- Distinguish between the topic of a text, a supporting detail, and a central idea
- Articulate each central idea as a complete analytical statement rather than a single word or phrase

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine a single central idea and how it is conveyed through details (RI.6.2)
- Ability to distinguish between topic and central idea (Grade 6 Unit 2)
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence (Lessons 1-2, RI.7.1)
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Dual Central Idea Map graphic organizer (two parallel columns: Central Idea 1 with supporting details, Central Idea 2 with supporting details)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (one per central idea)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the distinction between topic and central idea, and introduce the concept of finding multiple central ideas.

**Activity: One Topic, Two Ideas**

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned to find one central idea in a text. Remember: the topic is what the text is about in a word or two. The central idea is the specific point the author makes about that topic. For example, the topic might be 'ocean pollution,' and the central idea might be 'Ocean pollution threatens marine ecosystems in ways that also endanger human food supplies.'"

Write on the board:
- Topic: Sleep
- Central Idea 1: Teenagers need more sleep than adults, but school start times work against their natural sleep patterns.
- Central Idea 2: Chronic sleep deprivation impairs memory, mood, and academic performance in adolescents.

Say: "Both of these are central ideas about the topic of sleep, but they develop different threads. The first is about the structural problem -- school timing versus biology. The second is about the consequences of not sleeping enough. A complex informational text often develops more than one central idea. Today, you will learn to find two or more."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students a systematic method for identifying multiple central ideas in informational text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Central idea:** The most important point the author makes about a topic, developed through details, examples, and evidence throughout the text
- **Multiple central ideas:** Two or more distinct central ideas that an author develops within a single text, each supported by its own set of details
- **Supporting detail:** A specific fact, example, statistic, or explanation that develops or proves a central idea
- **Topic:** The subject of the text stated in a word or brief phrase; broader than a central idea

### Instruction Notes

Say: "To find two or more central ideas, I use what I call the Two-Pass Method."

Write on the board:
1. **First Pass:** Read the entire text. Identify the first central idea -- the most prominent point the author makes.
2. **Second Pass:** Reread with a new question: 'What else is the author trying to tell me?' Look for a second thread of meaning that runs through the text.
3. **Test Each Idea:** For each central idea, check: Can I find at least two supporting details? Is this truly a central idea (developed across the text) or just one supporting detail?

Say: "The Second Pass is the new skill. Many students stop after finding one central idea. You must deliberately search for the second thread. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Science of Wildfires"**

> Wildfires are a natural part of many ecosystems, playing an essential role in maintaining the health of forests and grasslands. Fire clears dead wood and leaf litter from the forest floor, returns nutrients to the soil, and stimulates the germination of seeds that require heat to sprout. Some tree species, such as the lodgepole pine, have cones that remain sealed for decades and only open to release seeds after exposure to the intense heat of a fire. Grassland ecosystems in the American Great Plains evolved with regular fires that prevented forests from encroaching and maintained the open landscape that grazing animals depend on. For thousands of years, Indigenous peoples across North America used controlled burns to manage landscapes, improve hunting grounds, and reduce the buildup of flammable material.
>
> However, more than a century of aggressive fire suppression in the United States has disrupted this natural cycle. Beginning in the early 1900s, government policy aimed to extinguish all wildfires as quickly as possible, believing fire to be purely destructive. This approach allowed decades of dead wood, brush, and leaf litter to accumulate on forest floors, creating enormous reservoirs of fuel. When fires inevitably break through these defenses, they burn hotter and spread faster than they would have in a forest that experienced regular, low-intensity burns. Climate change has intensified the problem by extending drought seasons and raising temperatures, drying out vegetation and creating conditions in which a single spark can ignite a catastrophic blaze. The result is a paradox: by trying to eliminate fire, we have made wildfires far more dangerous.

Say: "First Pass -- what is the most prominent central idea?"

Write on the board:
- **Central Idea 1:** Wildfires are a natural and necessary part of healthy ecosystems, playing vital roles in nutrient cycling, seed germination, and landscape management.
- Supporting details: Fire clears dead wood and returns nutrients to soil; lodgepole pine cones require fire to release seeds; grassland ecosystems evolved with regular fire; Indigenous peoples used controlled burns.

Say: "Now, Second Pass. I reread and ask: What else is the author trying to tell me? The second paragraph shifts. It introduces a different thread."

Write on the board:
- **Central Idea 2:** Decades of fire suppression, combined with climate change, have paradoxically made wildfires more destructive than they would have been naturally.
- Supporting details: Government policy aimed to extinguish all fires; dead wood and fuel accumulated for decades; fires now burn hotter and spread faster; climate change extends drought and raises temperatures.

Say: "Notice how each central idea has its own set of supporting details. Central Idea 1 is about fire's natural role. Central Idea 2 is about how human intervention backfired. Same topic -- wildfires -- but two distinct threads of meaning."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying two central ideas with facilitator support.

**Passage B: "The Psychology of Color"**

> Color influences human behavior in ways most people never consciously notice. Researchers have found that the color of a room can affect everything from how long shoppers stay in a store to how much food diners eat at a restaurant. Warm colors like red and orange tend to stimulate appetite and create a sense of urgency, which is why fast-food chains overwhelmingly favor red, yellow, and orange in their branding. Cool colors like blue and green have the opposite effect, promoting feelings of calm and relaxation. Hospitals and medical offices frequently paint their walls in soft blues and greens to reduce patient anxiety. Studies have shown that students tested in blue rooms perform better on creative tasks, while students in red rooms perform better on detail-oriented tasks requiring focused attention.
>
> The cultural associations people have with colors vary dramatically across the world and throughout history. In many Western cultures, white is associated with purity, weddings, and new beginnings. In many East Asian cultures, white is the traditional color of mourning and funerals. Red, which in Western cultures often signals danger or warning, is the color of luck, prosperity, and celebration in Chinese culture. Even within a single culture, color associations shift over time. In the early twentieth century, pink was considered a strong, bold color suitable for boys, while blue was seen as delicate and feminine. These associations reversed in the mid-twentieth century for reasons historians still debate. The lesson is clear: the meanings people assign to colors are learned, not innate, and they reflect the values and traditions of specific times and places.

Say: "Use the Two-Pass Method. On your first pass, find the first central idea. Then do a second pass for the second."

Allow the student to work for two minutes.

Expected response:
- **Central Idea 1:** Color has measurable psychological effects on human behavior, influencing mood, performance, and decision-making.
- Supporting details: Room color affects shopper behavior and dining habits; warm colors stimulate appetite and urgency; cool colors promote calm; blue rooms improve creative performance while red rooms improve focused attention.
- **Central Idea 2:** The cultural meanings people assign to colors are learned rather than innate, varying across cultures and changing over time.
- Supporting details: White means purity in Western cultures but mourning in East Asian cultures; red signals danger in the West but luck in Chinese culture; pink was for boys and blue for girls in the early 1900s, then reversed.

If the student finds only one central idea, ask: "Your first central idea covers the first paragraph well. Now reread the second paragraph. Is it making the same point, or is the author developing a different thread?"

Say: "Excellent. You identified two distinct central ideas -- one about the psychological effects of color and one about the cultural construction of color meaning. Notice how they relate to the same topic but develop different insights."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify two or more central ideas in a new passage.

**Passage C: "The Ocean's Plastic Problem"**

> Approximately 8 million metric tons of plastic waste enter the world's oceans each year, accumulating in massive floating debris fields and sinking to the deepest ocean trenches. The largest of these accumulations, the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, covers an area roughly twice the size of Texas and contains an estimated 80,000 metric tons of plastic. Much of this waste consists not of large, visible items but of microplastics -- fragments smaller than five millimeters that result from the breakdown of larger plastic products by sunlight and wave action. Microplastics have been found in every marine environment tested, from Arctic sea ice to the sediment at the bottom of the Mariana Trench, and they are now so pervasive that researchers have detected them in the tissue of more than 700 marine species, including fish, sea turtles, seabirds, and whales.
>
> The effects of plastic pollution on marine ecosystems are severe and varied. Sea turtles mistake floating plastic bags for jellyfish and ingest them, leading to intestinal blockage and starvation. Seabirds feed plastic fragments to their chicks, filling their stomachs with indigestible material. At the microscopic level, microplastics absorb toxic chemicals from the surrounding water and concentrate them at levels hundreds or thousands of times higher than the water itself. When marine animals consume microplastics, these concentrated toxins enter the food chain and accumulate in progressively higher concentrations in larger predators -- a process scientists call biomagnification. Researchers have found microplastics and their associated toxins in commercially harvested fish and shellfish, meaning the plastic that humans dump into the ocean is making its way back to human dinner plates.

**Directions:** Use the Two-Pass Method to identify two central ideas. For each central idea, state it as a complete sentence and list at least two supporting details.

1. **(On-level)** Identify two central ideas in this passage. State each as a complete sentence. For each, cite at least two supporting details from the text.
2. **(On-level)** How are the two central ideas related to each other? Write 2-3 sentences explaining the connection.
3. **(Stretch)** A classmate says: "The central idea of this passage is that there is too much plastic in the ocean." Is this a central idea or a topic? Explain why, and then write a more precise central idea statement.
4. **(Challenge)** Write a third possible central idea that the passage develops, even if less prominently than the first two. Cite at least two supporting details.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.1) Cite two pieces of textual evidence that show microplastics are widespread throughout the ocean. Use signal phrases and quotation marks.
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.3) How does the author introduce the scale of the plastic problem in the first paragraph? What specific details does the author use to help the reader grasp the enormity of the issue?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Scientists estimate that the Amazon rainforest produces approximately 20 percent of the world's oxygen and absorbs vast quantities of carbon dioxide, making it a critical buffer against climate change. The forest is also home to an estimated 10 percent of all known species on Earth, including thousands of plant species used in modern medicine. Yet deforestation has claimed roughly 17 percent of the Amazon's tree cover over the past 50 years, driven primarily by cattle ranching, soybean farming, and logging.

1. Identify two central ideas in this passage. State each as a complete sentence.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Central Idea 1: The Amazon rainforest plays an irreplaceable role in regulating global climate and supporting biodiversity. (Supported by: produces 20% of world's oxygen, absorbs carbon dioxide, home to 10% of all species, plant species used in medicine.) Central Idea 2: Despite its critical importance, the Amazon is being steadily destroyed by human economic activities. (Supported by: 17% of tree cover lost in 50 years, driven by cattle ranching, soybean farming, and logging.)

**Mastery = correctly identifies two distinct central ideas as complete sentences, each supported by at least one detail from the text**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Two-Pass Method using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Practice distinguishing topic, central idea, and supporting detail with a sorting activity: give the student a list of statements and have them categorize each
- Use the Dual Central Idea Map graphic organizer to scaffold the process
- Start with a passage where the two central ideas are signaled by clear paragraph breaks

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 4 on analyzing how central ideas develop
- Encourage the student to look for multiple central ideas when reading informational texts independently

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Central Idea Comparison:** Provide two short passages on similar topics. Have the student identify the central ideas in each and compare: Do the passages share any central ideas? Where do they differ?
2. **Detail Sorting:** Provide a passage with two central ideas and a list of ten supporting details. Have the student sort details into two columns based on which central idea each supports.
3. **Write Your Own:** Have the student write a short informational paragraph (5-7 sentences) that develops two central ideas about a topic of their choice. Then have them highlight the details that support each idea.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common error is students who identify the topic rather than the central idea. "Ocean plastic" is a topic. "Plastic pollution has become so pervasive that microplastics are now found in every marine environment tested" is a central idea. Consistently push students to state central ideas as complete, analytical sentences.
- Some students will identify one central idea and one supporting detail, thinking they have found two central ideas. A supporting detail is specific and local (one fact, one example). A central idea is broad and developed across multiple sentences or paragraphs. If a "central idea" can only be supported by one detail, it is probably a detail itself.
- The Two-Pass Method is deliberately structured to overcome the natural tendency to stop after one finding. Encourage students to trust the process: even if they feel they have found the main point, a second pass almost always reveals additional threads.
- When students struggle to articulate a central idea, use this formula: "The author is saying that [topic] is/does/causes [what about it]." This forces them beyond a one-word topic and into an analytical statement.
- Color-coded highlighting is especially effective for this lesson. Assign one color per central idea and have students highlight supporting details in the matching color. The visual result makes the two threads immediately apparent.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Central Idea 1:** Plastic pollution in the ocean has reached a staggering scale, with microplastics now found in virtually every marine environment on Earth. Supporting details: 8 million metric tons enter oceans annually; the Great Pacific Garbage Patch covers an area twice the size of Texas; microplastics have been found in Arctic sea ice, the Mariana Trench, and in more than 700 marine species.
**Central Idea 2:** Ocean plastic pollution causes severe harm to marine life and ultimately returns to threaten human health through the food chain. Supporting details: Sea turtles ingest plastic bags and starve; microplastics absorb and concentrate toxic chemicals; biomagnification carries toxins up the food chain; microplastics and toxins have been found in commercially harvested fish and shellfish.

2. The two central ideas are connected in a cause-and-consequence relationship. The first central idea establishes the scale of the problem -- plastic is everywhere in the ocean. The second central idea shows what happens because of that pervasiveness -- marine life is harmed, and the contamination cycles back to humans. The second central idea would not exist without the conditions described in the first.

3. "There is too much plastic in the ocean" is closer to a topic statement than a central idea because it is vague and does not capture the specific insights the author develops. A more precise central idea would be: "Plastic pollution has become so pervasive that microplastics are now present in every marine environment tested, from surface waters to the deepest ocean trenches, and in hundreds of marine species." This version specifies the nature of the pollution (microplastics), its extent (every environment tested), and its reach (hundreds of species).

4. A third central idea the passage develops, though less prominently: Human consumption of plastic-contaminated seafood means ocean plastic pollution is a direct human health threat, not just an environmental one. Supporting details: "Researchers have found microplastics and their associated toxins in commercially harvested fish and shellfish"; "the plastic that humans dump into the ocean is making its way back to human dinner plates." This thread suggests the pollution problem is circular -- it returns to its source.

### Interleaved Review
5. Evidence 1: According to the passage, "microplastics have been found in every marine environment tested, from Arctic sea ice to the sediment at the bottom of the Mariana Trench." Evidence 2: The author notes that "researchers have detected them in the tissue of more than 700 marine species, including fish, sea turtles, seabirds, and whales."
6. The author introduces the scale of the problem using large numbers and vivid comparisons: "approximately 8 million metric tons of plastic waste enter the world's oceans each year" gives a numerical scale; the Great Pacific Garbage Patch "covers an area roughly twice the size of Texas" uses a familiar geographic comparison; and the patch "contains an estimated 80,000 metric tons of plastic" adds further weight. These specific, large-scale details help the reader grasp an otherwise abstract problem.
