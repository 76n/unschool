# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic
Evaluating Reasoning and Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Assess whether the reasoning in an argument is sound (logical and free of fallacies) or unsound (flawed in logic)
- Evaluate whether the evidence in an argument is relevant (connected to the claim) and sufficient (enough to support the claim)
- Identify common reasoning flaws, including overgeneralization, false cause, and appeals to emotion over evidence

## Prerequisites
- Ability to trace the structure of an argument: claim, reasons, evidence (Lesson 8, RI.7.8)
- Ability to determine author's point of view and purpose (Lessons 6-7, RI.7.6)
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence (Lessons 1-2, RI.7.1)
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Reasoning Evaluation Checklist graphic organizer (sections: Is the reasoning logical?, Is the evidence relevant?, Is the evidence sufficient?, Are there reasoning flaws?)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (one for strong reasoning, one for weak reasoning)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the distinction between an argument that looks persuasive and one that is actually sound.

**Activity: Sounds Good, But...**

Say: "In Lesson 8, you learned to trace the structure of an argument -- claim, reasons, evidence. You can now see the building blocks. Today we ask a harder question: Are those building blocks actually strong? Just because an argument has a claim, reasons, and evidence does not mean the argument is sound."

Write on the board three arguments:
- A: "Students who eat breakfast score higher on tests. Therefore, eating breakfast causes higher test scores."
- B: "My friend tried the new study method and got an A. Therefore, the study method works for everyone."
- C: "A survey of 5,000 students across 30 schools found that students who ate breakfast scored an average of 12 percentage points higher on standardized tests, even when controlling for household income and prior academic performance."

Say: "All three relate to the same topic. Which one has the strongest reasoning and evidence? Which ones have flaws?"

Allow the student to respond. Guide them to see: A confuses correlation with causation; B overgeneralizes from one example; C provides specific, controlled research that addresses an alternative explanation.

Say: "Today you will learn to spot these kinds of flaws and evaluate whether an argument's reasoning and evidence are truly strong enough to support its claims."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students three evaluation criteria and three common reasoning flaws.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sound reasoning:** Logical connections between claims, reasons, and evidence that hold up under scrutiny; the conclusions follow logically from the premises
- **Relevant evidence:** Evidence that is directly connected to and actually supports the specific claim being made
- **Sufficient evidence:** Enough evidence to convincingly support the claim; a single example or anecdote is usually not sufficient for a broad claim
- **Reasoning flaw:** An error in logic that weakens an argument, such as overgeneralization, false cause, or appeal to emotion

### Instruction Notes

Say: "To evaluate an argument, I use three questions. I call them the R-S-L test."

Write on the board:
1. **R -- Relevant?** Does the evidence actually connect to the claim? Evidence about apples does not support a claim about oranges, no matter how strong it is.
2. **S -- Sufficient?** Is there enough evidence? One example does not prove a general rule. Look for multiple studies, large data sets, or expert consensus.
3. **L -- Logical?** Does the reasoning make sense? Does the conclusion actually follow from the evidence, or is there a logical gap?

Say: "Three common reasoning flaws to watch for."

Write on the board:
- **Overgeneralization:** Drawing a broad conclusion from too little evidence. ("One school tried later start times and saw improvement. Therefore, all schools should change their schedules.")
- **False cause:** Assuming that because two things happen together, one caused the other. ("Crime rates dropped the same year a new park was built. Therefore, the park reduced crime.")
- **Appeal to emotion over evidence:** Using fear, sympathy, or outrage to convince instead of providing factual support. ("Think of the children!" without any data about actual effects on children.)

Say: "Let me evaluate an argument that contains both strong and weak reasoning."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Should Cities Ban Single-Use Plastic Bags?"**

> Every year, Americans use approximately 100 billion plastic bags, the vast majority of which are used once and then discarded. These bags take up to 1,000 years to decompose in landfills and frequently end up in oceans and waterways, where they pose lethal threats to marine life. Sea turtles, which mistake floating bags for jellyfish, are among the most visible victims, but microplastics from degraded bags enter the food chain at every level. Cities that have banned single-use plastic bags have seen dramatic results: San Francisco reported a 72 percent reduction in plastic bag litter in storm drains within three years of implementing its ban.
>
> The benefits extend beyond environmental protection. When shoppers are required to bring reusable bags, they develop habits of sustainability that carry over into other areas of their lives. A survey conducted by a reusable bag manufacturer found that 85 percent of customers who switched to reusable bags reported making other environmentally conscious changes, such as recycling more often and reducing food waste. Cities also save money on cleanup costs: Los Angeles estimated that its bag ban saved the city 4 million dollars annually in litter removal and storm drain maintenance.
>
> Critics argue that banning plastic bags is an inconvenience that unfairly burdens low-income shoppers who cannot afford to purchase reusable bags. While this is a legitimate concern, most cities with bag bans offer free reusable bags at food banks and community centers. The small cost of a reusable bag -- typically one to two dollars -- is offset many times over by the long-term savings and environmental benefits. When we consider the devastating toll that plastic pollution takes on our oceans, our wildlife, and our planet, the choice becomes clear: the minor inconvenience of carrying a reusable bag is a small price to pay for a cleaner world.

Say: "Let me evaluate this argument using the R-S-L test."

Write on the board:

**Evaluating the evidence:**
- "100 billion plastic bags" used annually -- **Relevant and specific**. Establishes the scale of the problem.
- "Take up to 1,000 years to decompose" -- **Relevant** but worth noting that "up to" is an upper-bound estimate.
- San Francisco 72% reduction -- **Relevant and specific**. Shows bans can work. But is one city **sufficient** evidence for a nationwide claim? Somewhat. It is one strong example, but more cities' data would strengthen the argument.
- "85 percent of customers made other environmental changes" -- **Relevant** but **potentially flawed**. This survey was conducted by a reusable bag manufacturer, which has a financial interest in the results. This is a potential bias issue.
- Los Angeles $4 million savings -- **Relevant and specific**. A second city's data strengthens the sufficiency.

**Evaluating the reasoning:**
- The argument that bag bans reduce pollution is **logically sound**: fewer bags used leads to fewer bags in the environment.
- The argument about "habits of sustainability" is **weaker**: the survey from a bag manufacturer may not be objective (potential bias), and correlation between bag use and other behaviors does not prove the bags caused the change.
- The final paragraph uses an **appeal to emotion**: "the devastating toll on our oceans, our wildlife, and our planet" and "the choice becomes clear" are emotional phrases designed to make opposition feel immoral rather than reasonable.

Say: "This argument has strong evidence in some places and weaker reasoning in others. The data about bag reduction and cost savings is solid. The survey from the manufacturer is potentially biased. And the closing relies more on emotion than evidence. Evaluating an argument means recognizing both strengths and weaknesses."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice evaluating reasoning and evidence with facilitator support.

**Passage B: "Should Middle Schools Require School Uniforms?"**

> School uniforms eliminate one of the most persistent sources of social pressure in middle school: the daily competition over who wears the trendiest, most expensive clothing. When every student wears the same outfit, economic differences become invisible, and the focus shifts from appearance to academics. A study conducted at a large urban school district in the southern United States found that schools that adopted uniform policies saw a 15 percent reduction in disciplinary incidents related to dress-code violations and bullying about clothing.
>
> Parents also benefit from uniform requirements. The average American family spends approximately 700 dollars per child on back-to-school clothing each year. School uniforms typically cost between 100 and 200 dollars for a full year's wardrobe, saving families hundreds of dollars. In an era when many families are struggling to make ends meet, the financial relief is significant.
>
> Students at uniform schools consistently report feeling a stronger sense of belonging and school pride. A principal at a school that recently adopted uniforms told a local newspaper, "The transformation was immediate. Our halls feel calmer, our students feel united, and our test scores have risen for three straight years since we implemented the policy." When students are freed from the distraction of fashion competition, they can devote their full attention to what really matters: learning.

Say: "Use the R-S-L test. Evaluate the reasoning and evidence in this argument. Is the evidence relevant? Sufficient? Is the reasoning logical? Are there any flaws?"

Allow the student to work for three minutes.

Expected response:
- **15% reduction in disciplinary incidents (paragraph 1):** Relevant and specific. However, is one school district sufficient evidence for a general claim? It is a start but would be stronger with multiple districts. Also, disciplinary reduction related to "dress-code violations and bullying about clothing" -- this is a narrow measure; it does not prove uniforms improve overall school climate.
- **$700 vs. $100-200 cost comparison (paragraph 2):** Relevant and appears logically sound. But the $700 figure is for "back-to-school clothing" -- families with uniforms may still need to buy casual clothing for evenings and weekends, so actual savings may be less than implied. The reasoning has a **logical gap**: it assumes uniform costs replace rather than add to clothing expenses.
- **Principal's quote (paragraph 3):** This is an **anecdote from one person** with a vested interest in the policy they implemented. It is not objective evidence. The claim that "test scores have risen for three straight years" is presented without any data connecting uniforms to the rise -- this is a **false cause** flaw (many other factors could explain improving test scores). The principal also has a bias: they implemented the policy and have a professional interest in it appearing successful.
- **"What really matters: learning" (final sentence):** This is an **appeal to emotion** -- it frames opposition to uniforms as opposition to learning, which is not a logical connection.

Say: "Excellent evaluation. You identified both strengths and weaknesses. The cost comparison and disciplinary data are relevant starting points, but the principal's quote and the test score claim both have significant reasoning flaws."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently evaluate reasoning and evidence in a new passage.

**Passage C: "The Benefits of Reducing Meat Consumption"**

> Global meat production is one of the leading contributors to greenhouse gas emissions, accounting for approximately 14.5 percent of all human-generated emissions worldwide -- more than the entire transportation sector. Cattle farming alone produces massive quantities of methane, a greenhouse gas 80 times more potent than carbon dioxide over a twenty-year period. Reducing meat consumption, even modestly, could have a significant impact on climate change. A study published in the journal Science found that if every person in the United States reduced meat consumption by 25 percent, the resulting decrease in agricultural emissions would be equivalent to taking 20 million cars off the road.
>
> The health benefits of reducing meat consumption are equally compelling. The World Health Organization has classified processed meats such as bacon, sausages, and hot dogs as Group 1 carcinogens, meaning there is sufficient evidence that they cause cancer. Diets high in red meat have been linked to increased rates of heart disease, stroke, and type 2 diabetes. A major study by Harvard University tracked the dietary habits of more than 120,000 adults over 30 years and found that those who ate the most red and processed meat had significantly higher rates of premature death than those who ate the least. Switching even one meal per day from meat to plant-based protein can lower cholesterol, reduce blood pressure, and decrease the risk of developing chronic diseases.
>
> My neighbor stopped eating meat two years ago and says she has never felt healthier. She lost fifteen pounds, her energy levels improved, and her doctor told her that her cholesterol dropped by 40 points. If one person can experience such dramatic results, imagine what would happen if an entire community made the same change. The evidence is overwhelming: eating less meat is better for the planet, better for our health, and better for our future.

**Directions:** Use the R-S-L test (Relevant? Sufficient? Logical?) to evaluate this argument.

1. **(On-level)** Evaluate the evidence in paragraphs 1 and 2 using the R-S-L test. For each piece of evidence, state whether it is relevant, sufficient, and logically connected to the claim. Explain your reasoning.
2. **(On-level)** Evaluate the reasoning in paragraph 3. Identify at least two reasoning flaws. Name the type of flaw and explain why it weakens the argument.
3. **(Stretch)** Compare the quality of evidence in paragraphs 1-2 versus paragraph 3. What makes the evidence in the first two paragraphs stronger than the evidence in the third? Be specific.
4. **(Challenge)** Rewrite paragraph 3 so that it supports the same claim but with sound reasoning and strong evidence instead of anecdotal evidence and logical flaws. You may invent plausible studies or statistics.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.6) What is the author's purpose in writing this passage? How does the author's detail selection reveal this purpose?
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.1) Cite two pieces of evidence from paragraphs 1-2 that support the claim that reducing meat consumption has health benefits. Use signal phrases and quotation marks.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> A recent article in a local newspaper argued: "Our town should build a new community swimming pool. Last summer, temperatures reached 100 degrees on fifteen days. Every child deserves a safe place to cool off during dangerous heat. Additionally, towns in our state with public pools report 25 percent fewer heat-related emergency room visits during summer months. My daughter loves swimming and would use the pool every day."

1. Identify one piece of strong evidence and one reasoning flaw in this argument. Explain each.

**Expected Answers:**
1. **Strong evidence:** The statistic that "towns in our state with public pools report 25 percent fewer heat-related emergency room visits during summer months" is relevant (directly connects a pool to a measurable health outcome), specific (25 percent, from comparable towns), and logically connected to the claim that a pool benefits the community. **Reasoning flaw:** "My daughter loves swimming and would use the pool every day" is an **anecdotal/personal** piece of evidence that does not support a community-wide argument. One child's preference does not demonstrate community need. This is an example of **insufficient evidence** -- a single personal example used to support a broad claim. (Also accept: "Every child deserves a safe place to cool off" as an **appeal to emotion** that assumes the pool is the only or best solution without evidence.)

**Mastery = correctly identifies one strong piece of evidence with reasoning AND identifies one reasoning flaw with the correct type**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the R-S-L test using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Practice with very simple two-sentence arguments that contain obvious flaws: "My cat likes tuna. Therefore, all cats like tuna." (overgeneralization); "I wore my lucky socks and aced the test. The socks caused the good grade." (false cause)
- Use the Reasoning Evaluation Checklist with each piece of evidence before moving to the next
- Focus on just one evaluation question at a time: first relevance, then sufficiency, then logic

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10, the integrated analysis lesson
- Encourage the student to evaluate reasoning and evidence when reading news articles, advertisements, and opinion pieces

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Flaw Finder:** Collect three short opinion paragraphs from news websites or editorial pages (with facilitator approval). Have the student identify and classify any reasoning flaws using the vocabulary from this lesson.
2. **Build a Flawed Argument, Then Fix It:** Write a short argument (4-5 sentences) that deliberately contains one overgeneralization, one false cause, and one appeal to emotion. Then rewrite it with sound reasoning and strong evidence.
3. **Evidence Quality Ranking:** Provide five pieces of evidence about the same topic, ranging from a personal anecdote to a large-scale scientific study. Have the student rank them from weakest to strongest and explain the ranking criteria.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students at this age are developing critical thinking skills but may struggle to critique arguments they personally agree with. Passage C is deliberately designed to present a claim most students would support (eating less meat is healthier) but includes an obviously flawed third paragraph. This teaches the crucial lesson that an argument can reach a correct conclusion through bad reasoning -- and that bad reasoning weakens the argument regardless of whether the conclusion is correct.
- The three common reasoning flaws (overgeneralization, false cause, appeal to emotion) are not exhaustive but cover the most frequent errors students will encounter in Grade 7 level texts. Avoid introducing too many fallacy types at once. Three is sufficient for this lesson.
- When evaluating evidence sufficiency, teach students to ask: "How many examples or studies support this claim? Do they come from different sources? Do they cover different populations or contexts?" A single study from one school is a starting point. Multiple studies from different settings provide stronger support.
- The concept of biased sources (like the bag manufacturer survey in Passage A) is important but should be introduced carefully. Not all sources with an interest are wrong -- but their potential bias should make readers seek corroborating evidence from independent sources. Teach students to notice who conducted or funded a study.
- This lesson completes the RI.7.8 pair (Lessons 8-9). Lesson 8 taught argument structure (claim, reasons, evidence). This lesson teaches evaluation (sound, relevant, sufficient). Together, these skills prepare students for the integrated analysis in Lesson 10 and for the argument evaluation questions on the unit assessment.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Paragraph 1 evidence evaluation:**
   - "14.5 percent of all human-generated emissions" (meat production): Relevant -- directly connects meat production to climate impact. Sufficient -- this is a global statistic, not a single example. Logically connected to the claim that reducing meat consumption helps the climate.
   - "Methane is 80 times more potent than carbon dioxide over a twenty-year period": Relevant -- explains why cattle farming is particularly harmful. Logically connected because it shows the disproportionate impact of meat-related emissions.
   - Science journal study (25% reduction = 20 million fewer cars equivalent): Relevant, specific, and from a reputable source. Provides a concrete, quantified consequence of the proposed action. Logically sound -- it translates dietary change into a measurable environmental outcome.

   **Paragraph 2 evidence evaluation:**
   - WHO classification of processed meats as Group 1 carcinogens: Relevant and authoritative -- the WHO is a globally recognized health organization. The evidence directly supports the health benefit claim.
   - Harvard University study (120,000+ adults, 30 years): Relevant, sufficient (large sample, long duration), and logically connected. The size and duration make it strong evidence. The finding that higher meat consumption correlated with higher premature death rates directly supports the claim.
   - "Switching even one meal per day" benefits: Relevant but less specific -- which study shows this? The claim is presented as fact without a specific source. Still logically connected but would be stronger with attribution.

2. **Paragraph 3 reasoning flaws:**
   - **Overgeneralization:** The author cites one person's experience (the neighbor) and then extrapolates to an entire community: "If one person can experience such dramatic results, imagine what would happen if an entire community made the same change." One person's results cannot reliably predict community-wide outcomes. Many factors specific to the neighbor (her previous diet, activity level, genetics) may not apply to others.
   - **Anecdotal evidence used in place of data:** The neighbor's story is a personal anecdote, not scientific evidence. Her weight loss, energy improvement, and cholesterol drop are not controlled or verified and could have many causes beyond eliminating meat. This is **insufficient evidence** for the broad claims being made.
   - (Also accept: **Appeal to emotion** in the final sentence -- "better for our future" is designed to make disagreement feel irresponsible, without adding factual support.)

3. The evidence in paragraphs 1-2 is stronger because it comes from large-scale, specific, authoritative sources: a Science journal study analyzing national-level data, a World Health Organization classification based on extensive research review, and a Harvard study tracking 120,000 adults over 30 years. This evidence is relevant to the claims, sufficient in scope (large samples, long timeframes, multiple populations), and logically connected to the argument. The evidence in paragraph 3 is a single personal anecdote from one unnamed neighbor. It lacks sample size (one person), lacks controls (no way to know what caused her improvements), and lacks objectivity (the neighbor is self-reporting, and the author has a personal connection). The first two paragraphs build the argument with data; the third undermines it with storytelling.

4. Answers will vary. A strong revision: "Community-level studies confirm what individual experiences suggest. A 2021 study published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition followed 12,000 participants in three cities over five years and found that those who reduced meat consumption by at least 25 percent experienced an average decrease of 18 points in LDL cholesterol, a 12 percent reduction in risk of developing type 2 diabetes, and a measurable reduction in their individual carbon footprint. When the city of Ghent, Belgium, promoted a weekly meat-free day among its residents, tracking showed a collective reduction of 18,000 metric tons of carbon emissions in the first year -- equivalent to taking 3,500 cars off the road. The evidence from controlled studies and community-scale initiatives is clear: reducing meat consumption produces measurable benefits for both individual health and environmental sustainability."

### Interleaved Review
5. The author's purpose is to persuade the reader that reducing meat consumption benefits both the environment and human health. This purpose is conveyed through detail selection: the author includes dramatic statistics about environmental impact (14.5% of emissions, methane 80 times more potent than CO2), authoritative health evidence (WHO cancer classification, Harvard study), and accessible action steps (switching one meal per day). The author selects only evidence that supports the argument and does not include any evidence about potential downsides of reducing meat consumption (nutritional challenges, cultural significance of meat, economic impacts on farming communities).
6. Evidence 1: According to the World Health Organization, "processed meats such as bacon, sausages, and hot dogs" have been classified "as Group 1 carcinogens, meaning there is sufficient evidence that they cause cancer." Evidence 2: The passage cites a Harvard study that tracked "more than 120,000 adults over 30 years" and "found that those who ate the most red and processed meat had significantly higher rates of premature death than those who ate the least."
