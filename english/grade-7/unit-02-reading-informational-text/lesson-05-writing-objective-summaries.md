# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 5

## Topic
Writing Objective Summaries of Informational Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write an objective summary of an informational text that captures two or more central ideas and their key supporting details
- Eliminate personal opinions, judgments, and reactions from a summary
- Condense a multi-paragraph text into a concise summary that preserves the author's meaning without distortion

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify two or more central ideas in an informational text (Lesson 3, RI.7.2)
- Ability to trace how central ideas develop over the course of a text (Lesson 4, RI.7.2)
- Understanding of the difference between objective restatement and personal opinion (RI.6.2)
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Summary Checklist graphic organizer (checkboxes: Includes Central Idea 1?, Includes Central Idea 2?, Includes key supporting details?, Free of personal opinion?, Preserves author's meaning?, Concise?)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Red pen or pencil for editing (optional)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the difference between objective and subjective language and introduce the challenge of summarizing texts with multiple central ideas.

**Activity: Opinion Detector**

Say: "In Grade 6, you learned to write summaries that are distinct from personal opinions. Today we build on that skill with a Grade 7 challenge: you must now summarize texts that contain two or more central ideas -- and you must capture both ideas without inserting your own reactions."

Write on the board two summary attempts for a hypothetical passage about space exploration:

- **Summary A:** "The passage explains that space agencies are developing reusable rockets that dramatically reduce launch costs. It also describes how private companies have entered the space industry, creating competition that has accelerated innovation."
- **Summary B:** "The passage is about the exciting new era of space exploration. It is amazing how reusable rockets work, and it is great that private companies are making space travel possible for everyone."

Say: "Which is objective? Which includes personal opinions?"

Allow the student to respond.

Say: "Summary B contains opinion markers: 'exciting,' 'amazing,' and 'great.' It also distorts the passage by claiming space travel is 'possible for everyone,' which may not be what the author actually said. Summary A sticks to what the author wrote. Today, we will practice writing summaries like Summary A -- accurate, objective, and complete."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students a structured process for writing objective summaries of texts with multiple central ideas.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Objective summary:** A concise restatement of a text's central ideas and key supporting details that does not include the reader's personal opinions, reactions, or judgments
- **Subjective language:** Words or phrases that express personal feelings, evaluations, or opinions (e.g., "interesting," "unfortunately," "the best part")
- **Condense:** To reduce a text to its essential ideas, removing unnecessary details while preserving the core meaning
- **Distortion:** Changing the author's meaning by overstating, understating, or misrepresenting what the text actually says

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Writing an objective summary of a Grade 7 informational text requires four steps. I call this the CIOD method."

Write on the board:
1. **C -- Central Ideas:** Identify the two (or more) central ideas.
2. **I -- Important Details:** Select 2-3 key details per central idea that are essential for understanding.
3. **O -- Objectivity Check:** Remove any words that express your feelings, opinions, or judgments.
4. **D -- Draft and Refine:** Write the summary in 4-6 sentences, connecting the central ideas smoothly.

Say: "Watch me use CIOD with this passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Water Beneath Our Feet"**

> Beneath the surface of every continent lies an invisible reservoir that holds far more freshwater than all the world's rivers and lakes combined: groundwater. Stored in the tiny spaces between grains of sand, gravel, and rock in underground formations called aquifers, groundwater supplies drinking water to more than two billion people worldwide and provides roughly 40 percent of all water used for irrigation in agriculture. The Ogallala Aquifer, which stretches beneath eight states in the American Great Plains, irrigates nearly one-third of all cropland in the United States and is the primary water source for millions of people.
>
> However, many of the world's most important aquifers are being drained far faster than nature can replenish them. The Ogallala Aquifer, which took millions of years to accumulate, has seen water levels drop by more than 150 feet in some areas of western Kansas and the Texas Panhandle since the 1950s. Worldwide, satellite data from NASA reveal that 21 of the planet's 37 largest aquifers are being depleted, with 13 classified as significantly stressed. Replenishment rates vary, but in many arid regions, an aquifer that took thousands of years to fill may need centuries to recover even if pumping stopped entirely. The consequences of depletion are already visible: in parts of central California's San Joaquin Valley, the land surface has sunk by nearly 30 feet over the past century as underground water was pumped out and the rock layers compacted. Scientists warn that without changes in water management practices, some of the world's most productive agricultural regions may face permanent water shortages within the next few decades.

Say: "Step 1 -- Central Ideas."

Write on the board:
- Central Idea 1: Groundwater stored in aquifers is a critical freshwater resource that supports billions of people and massive agricultural systems.
- Central Idea 2: The world's most important aquifers are being depleted far faster than they can naturally replenish, threatening long-term water security.

Say: "Step 2 -- Important Details. I select 2-3 essential details per idea."

Write on the board:
- For CI 1: Supplies drinking water to 2 billion people; provides 40% of irrigation water; Ogallala Aquifer irrigates one-third of US cropland.
- For CI 2: Ogallala water levels dropped 150+ feet since the 1950s; 21 of 37 largest aquifers being depleted; recovery could take centuries; land subsidence in California.

Say: "Step 3 -- Objectivity Check. Let me make sure I am not inserting opinions."

Write on the board:
- "This is a scary problem" -- REMOVE (opinion)
- "The statistics are alarming" -- REMOVE (judgment)
- "Scientists should do more research" -- REMOVE (recommendation not in text)

Say: "Step 4 -- Draft and Refine."

Write on the board:
**Summary:** Groundwater stored in underground aquifers holds more freshwater than all rivers and lakes combined and supplies drinking water to more than two billion people while providing roughly 40 percent of water used for agricultural irrigation. The Ogallala Aquifer alone irrigates nearly one-third of US cropland. However, many of the world's largest aquifers are being drained far faster than they can naturally replenish. The Ogallala Aquifer has seen water levels drop by more than 150 feet in some areas since the 1950s, and NASA satellite data show that 21 of 37 major aquifers worldwide are being depleted. The consequences include land subsidence and the possibility of permanent water shortages in productive agricultural regions within coming decades.

Say: "Notice: five sentences, two central ideas, key details, zero opinions. That is an objective summary."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice writing an objective summary with facilitator support.

**Passage B: "The Science of Sleep in Adolescents"**

> The human body regulates sleep through a biological mechanism called the circadian rhythm -- an internal clock that signals when it is time to feel alert and when it is time to feel drowsy. In most adults, the circadian rhythm triggers sleepiness around 10:00 or 11:00 at night and alertness around 6:00 or 7:00 in the morning. However, during adolescence, the circadian rhythm shifts significantly. Research published in the journal Sleep found that the adolescent brain begins releasing melatonin, the hormone that triggers sleepiness, roughly two hours later than in adults -- around midnight rather than 10:00 PM. This means that most teenagers are biologically incapable of falling asleep before 11:00 PM, regardless of how early they go to bed.
>
> This biological shift collides directly with the demands of the modern school schedule. The average public high school in the United States starts classes at 8:00 AM, requiring many students to wake by 6:30 AM or earlier. When adolescents who cannot fall asleep until midnight must wake at 6:30, they accumulate a chronic sleep deficit. The consequences are well-documented: sleep-deprived teens show reduced performance in memory, concentration, and problem-solving; they are more likely to experience anxiety and depression; and they face higher rates of drowsy-driving accidents. The American Academy of Pediatrics has recommended that middle and high schools start no earlier than 8:30 AM, and districts that have adopted later start times report improvements in attendance, test scores, and student well-being.

Say: "Use the CIOD method. First, identify the two central ideas. Then select key details. Check for objectivity. Finally, draft your summary in 4-6 sentences."

Allow the student to work for four minutes, then review together.

Expected response:
**Summary:** During adolescence, the brain's circadian rhythm shifts to release melatonin approximately two hours later than in adults, making it biologically difficult for most teenagers to fall asleep before 11:00 PM. This natural shift conflicts with early school start times, which in the United States average 8:00 AM. The resulting chronic sleep deficit has been linked to reduced academic performance, higher rates of anxiety and depression, and increased drowsy-driving accidents among teens. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that schools start no earlier than 8:30 AM, and districts that have adopted later start times have reported improvements in attendance, test scores, and student well-being.

If the student includes opinion language, point it out: "You wrote 'it is unfair that schools start so early.' That is your opinion. What does the passage actually say about school start times?"

Say: "Your summary captured both central ideas -- the biological shift and the school schedule conflict -- in just four sentences without adding your own opinions. That is exactly what an objective summary should do."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write objective summaries of a new passage.

**Passage C: "Fast Fashion and Its Hidden Costs"**

> The average American buys roughly 68 garments per year, more than one new piece of clothing per week. This pace of consumption is made possible by the fast fashion industry, which produces trendy clothing rapidly and inexpensively by manufacturing in countries where labor is cheap and environmental regulations are minimal. Major fast fashion retailers can move a design from concept to store shelf in as little as two weeks, a process that once took months. The business model depends on convincing consumers to view clothing as disposable -- worn a few times and then replaced by the next trend. The result is that the average garment is now worn only seven to ten times before being discarded.
>
> The environmental consequences of this system are staggering. The fashion industry is responsible for approximately 10 percent of global carbon emissions, more than all international flights and maritime shipping combined. Textile production consumes roughly 93 billion cubic meters of water annually -- enough to meet the needs of five million people. When cheap synthetic fabrics like polyester are washed, they release microplastic fibers into waterways; a single load of laundry can release more than 700,000 microplastic particles. The human costs are equally troubling. Garment workers in many manufacturing countries earn wages far below a living standard, and factory conditions have repeatedly been linked to building collapses, fires, and chronic health problems caused by exposure to dyes and chemicals. Consumer choices that seem small -- buying a five-dollar shirt instead of a twenty-dollar one -- carry hidden costs that are paid by the environment and by workers half a world away.

**Directions:**

1. **(On-level)** Write an objective summary of this passage in 4-6 sentences using the CIOD method. Capture both central ideas and key supporting details without including personal opinions.
2. **(On-level)** Review your summary using the Summary Checklist. Does it include both central ideas? Is it free of opinion language? Does it preserve the author's meaning without distortion?
3. **(Stretch)** A classmate writes: "This passage is about how fast fashion is terrible for the environment and we should all stop buying cheap clothes." Identify two problems with this as an objective summary and write a corrected version.
4. **(Challenge)** Write two different objective summaries of the passage: one in exactly 3 sentences and one in exactly 6 sentences. How does the level of detail change? Which summary better represents the passage? Explain your reasoning.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.1) Cite two pieces of textual evidence that support the claim that fast fashion has a significant environmental impact. Use signal phrases and quotation marks.
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.3) How does the author introduce the concept of fast fashion in the first paragraph? What specific details establish the reader's understanding of how the industry works?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Electric vehicles produce zero direct emissions while driving, but the electricity used to charge them often comes from power plants that burn fossil fuels. A study by the Union of Concerned Scientists found that even when accounting for electricity generation, electric vehicles produce roughly 50 percent less carbon dioxide over their lifetime than comparable gasoline-powered cars. However, the manufacturing of electric vehicle batteries requires mining lithium, cobalt, and nickel -- processes that generate significant pollution and raise concerns about labor conditions in mining regions.

Write an objective summary of this passage in 2-3 sentences.

**Expected Answers:**
Electric vehicles produce no direct emissions while driving, but the electricity that charges them and the mining required for battery production have environmental costs. Despite these costs, a study found that electric vehicles produce roughly 50 percent less carbon dioxide over their lifetime than gasoline cars. The environmental benefits of electric vehicles must therefore be weighed against the impacts of electricity generation and battery mineral extraction.

(Note: Deduct points if student writes opinions such as "electric cars are still better" or "everyone should buy electric cars." The summary must restate the passage's balanced presentation without taking a side.)

**Mastery = summary captures at least two key points from the passage, is factually accurate, and is free of personal opinions or judgments**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the CIOD method using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Give the student a summary containing three hidden opinions and have them identify and remove each one
- Practice with a shorter passage (4-5 sentences) where the student can focus on the objectivity skill without the challenge of condensing
- Provide sentence starters: "The passage explains that ___. It also describes ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 6 on determining author's point of view and purpose
- Encourage the student to practice writing objective summaries of news articles or textbook sections

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Summary Revision Workshop:** Provide three student-written summaries of varying quality. Have the student rank them from best to worst, identify the specific problems in each, and revise the weakest one.
2. **Summary Length Challenge:** Summarize Passage C in exactly 50 words. This forces extreme condensation while maintaining accuracy.
3. **Objective vs. Persuasive:** Write both an objective summary and a persuasive summary of the same passage. Then explain how they differ in word choice, detail selection, and tone.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common error in summary writing is including opinions disguised as facts. Students may write "The passage shows that fast fashion is a serious problem" and believe they are being objective because they attribute it to the passage. But "serious problem" is still a judgment. Teach students to use neutral language: "The passage describes the environmental and human costs of fast fashion."
- Students may also err in the other direction, producing summaries so stripped of content that they say almost nothing. A summary is not just "The passage is about fast fashion." It must include the central ideas and enough detail to convey the author's meaning. The CIOD method guards against both over-opinionating and under-summarizing.
- Encourage students to write summaries in their own words rather than stringing together quotes. A good summary paraphrases; a poor summary is a quote collage. If students rely too heavily on the author's exact language, have them close the passage and write from memory, then check for accuracy.
- This lesson completes the RI.7.2 sequence (Lessons 3-5). Lesson 3 taught identification, Lesson 4 taught development analysis, and this lesson teaches summary writing. Reinforce that all three skills work together: you cannot write a good summary without first identifying the central ideas and understanding how they develop.
- The Summary Checklist is a powerful self-monitoring tool. Encourage students to use it every time they write a summary, even outside of this class.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Sample objective summary: The fast fashion industry produces trendy clothing rapidly and cheaply, enabling Americans to buy an average of 68 garments per year, most of which are worn only seven to ten times before being discarded. This disposable approach to clothing has severe environmental consequences: the fashion industry accounts for approximately 10 percent of global carbon emissions, consumes roughly 93 billion cubic meters of water annually, and releases hundreds of thousands of microplastic particles per laundry load. The human costs are also significant, as garment workers in many countries earn below-living-standard wages and face dangerous factory conditions. The passage presents consumer clothing purchases as carrying hidden environmental and human costs that extend far beyond the price tag.

2. Checklist application: The summary should include Central Idea 1 (fast fashion's business model creates disposable clothing culture), Central Idea 2 (the environmental and human costs are significant), key supporting details (statistics on carbon, water, microplastics, worker conditions), no opinion language (no "terrible," "shocking," "we should"), and accurate representation of the author's meaning.

3. Problems with the classmate's summary: (a) "Terrible" is a personal judgment, not an objective restatement -- the passage presents evidence and lets the reader draw conclusions. (b) "We should all stop buying cheap clothes" is a recommendation that the passage never makes. The passage describes hidden costs but does not tell readers what to do. Corrected version: "This passage describes how the fast fashion industry produces large quantities of inexpensive clothing designed to be quickly discarded. The environmental costs include significant carbon emissions, water consumption, and microplastic pollution, while the human costs include low wages and unsafe factory conditions for garment workers."

4. **3-sentence version:** The fast fashion industry enables Americans to buy over one new garment per week by manufacturing clothing cheaply and rapidly, but most garments are discarded after only seven to ten wearings. This disposable model generates approximately 10 percent of global carbon emissions, consumes billions of cubic meters of water, and releases microplastics into waterways. Garment workers in manufacturing countries also pay a cost through low wages and dangerous working conditions. **6-sentence version:** [Would expand to include details about the two-week design-to-shelf timeline, the 93 billion cubic meters of water, the 700,000 microplastic particles per laundry load, specific worker conditions, and the final point about hidden costs behind low-price clothing.] The 6-sentence version better represents the passage because it preserves the specific evidence that makes the author's points compelling, while the 3-sentence version must sacrifice detail for brevity.

### Interleaved Review
5. Evidence 1: According to the passage, "the fashion industry is responsible for approximately 10 percent of global carbon emissions, more than all international flights and maritime shipping combined." Evidence 2: The text notes that "textile production consumes roughly 93 billion cubic meters of water annually -- enough to meet the needs of five million people."
6. The author introduces fast fashion with a striking statistic -- "the average American buys roughly 68 garments per year" -- that immediately establishes the scale of consumption. The author then explains how this pace is possible: manufacturing "in countries where labor is cheap and environmental regulations are minimal" and a design-to-shelf timeline of "as little as two weeks." The detail about garments being "worn only seven to ten times before being discarded" establishes that the industry depends on a disposable mindset. This introduction moves from the consumer behavior to the business model to the waste it creates, building the reader's understanding step by step.
