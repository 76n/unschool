# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 1

## Topic
Citing Multiple Evidence in Nonfiction

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Select and cite several distinct pieces of textual evidence to support an analytical claim about an informational text
- Integrate multiple pieces of evidence into a cohesive written response that demonstrates how each piece contributes to the analysis
- Distinguish between redundant evidence (repeating the same point) and complementary evidence (adding new support)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis of explicit information (RI.6.1)
- Ability to use the C-E-C method (Claim, Evidence, Connection) from Grade 6
- Familiarity with signal phrases for introducing quoted evidence
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Multi-Evidence Citation Chart graphic organizer (columns: Claim, Evidence 1, Evidence 2, Evidence 3, How They Work Together)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in three colors (recommended)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge from Grade 6 about citing evidence and introduce the idea that multiple pieces of evidence create a stronger analysis.

**Activity: One vs. Many**

Say: "In sixth grade, you mastered citing evidence using the C-E-C method -- Claim, Evidence, Connection. You got very good at finding one strong piece of evidence and explaining how it supports your point. In seventh grade, we are raising the standard. Instead of one piece of evidence, you will learn to cite several pieces of evidence and show how they work together."

Write on the board:
- One piece of evidence: "The author says the coral reef is declining."
- Several pieces of evidence: "The author reports that 'coral coverage has dropped 50 percent since 1990,' notes that 'rising ocean temperatures cause coral bleaching,' and adds that 'pollution from agricultural runoff introduces chemicals that weaken coral structures.'"

Say: "Which version gives you a more complete picture of what is happening to coral reefs? Why?"

Allow the student to respond. Guide them to notice that each piece of evidence adds a different dimension -- one gives a statistic, another explains a cause, and a third introduces an additional factor.

Say: "Today we are going to learn how to select several pieces of evidence that each contribute something different, and then weave them together into a strong analytical response."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students a systematic process for selecting, organizing, and integrating multiple pieces of evidence from informational text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Several pieces of evidence:** Three or more distinct textual details that each contribute something different to support an analytical claim
- **Complementary evidence:** Pieces of evidence that add new dimensions or perspectives to the analysis, rather than repeating the same point
- **Integrate:** To combine multiple pieces of evidence into a unified response that shows how they work together
- **Synthesis:** The act of combining evidence and reasoning to build a larger understanding than any single piece provides

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In Grade 6, you used C-E-C: Claim, Evidence, Connection. In Grade 7, we expand this to what I call the C-MEI method: Claim, Multiple Evidence, Integration."

Write on the board:
1. **Claim** -- State your analytical point clearly.
2. **Multiple Evidence** -- Select at least three distinct pieces of evidence from the text.
3. **Integration** -- Explain how the pieces of evidence work together to support your claim.

Say: "The key word is 'distinct.' Each piece of evidence should add something new. Watch how I model this with the following passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Urban Vertical Farming"**

> As city populations swell and available farmland shrinks, a growing number of agricultural scientists are turning to vertical farming -- the practice of growing crops in stacked layers inside controlled indoor environments. Vertical farms use hydroponic or aeroponic systems that deliver nutrients directly to plant roots without soil, reducing water consumption by as much as 95 percent compared to traditional agriculture. Because the growing environment is fully controlled, vertical farms can produce crops year-round regardless of season or weather, and they eliminate the need for chemical pesticides since insects and weeds cannot enter sealed growing facilities. A single vertical farm occupying one acre of building space can produce the equivalent of ten to twenty acres of conventional farmland. However, the technology faces significant obstacles. The electricity required to power grow lights, climate control systems, and water pumps is enormous -- a mid-sized vertical farm may consume as much energy as a small factory. The startup costs are also daunting: constructing a commercial vertical farm can require an investment of tens of millions of dollars. Critics point out that only high-value crops like leafy greens, herbs, and strawberries are currently profitable in vertical farms, meaning staple foods such as wheat, rice, and corn remain far cheaper to grow outdoors. Despite these challenges, proponents argue that as energy costs decline with renewable power sources and as urban populations continue to grow, vertical farming will become an essential part of the global food system.

Say: "Here is my question: What evidence supports the claim that vertical farming offers significant advantages over traditional agriculture?"

Say: "Watch my process. First, I scan the passage for evidence related to advantages. I see several candidates. Let me pick three that each add something different."

Write on the board:
- **Claim:** Vertical farming offers several significant advantages over traditional agriculture.
- **Evidence 1 (resource efficiency):** The passage states that vertical farms "reduc[e] water consumption by as much as 95 percent compared to traditional agriculture."
- **Evidence 2 (productivity):** The author notes that "a single vertical farm occupying one acre of building space can produce the equivalent of ten to twenty acres of conventional farmland."
- **Evidence 3 (environmental benefit):** The text adds that vertical farms "eliminate the need for chemical pesticides since insects and weeds cannot enter sealed growing facilities."
- **Integration:** Together, these three pieces of evidence reveal that vertical farming addresses multiple agricultural challenges simultaneously: it conserves water, multiplies land productivity, and reduces chemical use. Each advantage operates in a different domain -- resource use, output, and environmental impact -- which suggests that the benefits of vertical farming are broad and interconnected rather than limited to a single improvement.

Say: "Notice how each piece of evidence adds a new dimension. If I had cited three facts about water use, that would be redundant -- the same point stated three different ways. Instead, I chose evidence about water, about land productivity, and about pesticides. That is what makes evidence complementary."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice selecting and integrating multiple pieces of evidence with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us work together with the same passage but a different question. Use the C-MEI method."

**Question: What evidence suggests that vertical farming faces significant challenges?**

Say: "Scan the passage for evidence about challenges. Find at least three distinct pieces of evidence. Use your highlighter to mark them. I will give you two minutes."

Allow the student to scan and highlight.

Say: "What did you find? Let us build our response together."

Expected response:
- **Claim:** Vertical farming faces significant challenges that limit its current viability.
- **Evidence 1 (energy costs):** The passage states that "the electricity required to power grow lights, climate control systems, and water pumps is enormous" and that "a mid-sized vertical farm may consume as much energy as a small factory."
- **Evidence 2 (financial barriers):** The author notes that "constructing a commercial vertical farm can require an investment of tens of millions of dollars."
- **Evidence 3 (crop limitations):** The text points out that "only high-value crops like leafy greens, herbs, and strawberries are currently profitable," meaning staple foods remain cheaper to grow outdoors.
- **Integration:** These three challenges work on different levels -- operational costs, initial investment, and product range -- suggesting that vertical farming's obstacles are not a single solvable problem but a set of interconnected barriers that must all be addressed for the technology to become widely viable.

If the student selects redundant evidence, ask: "Does your second piece of evidence add something new that the first did not? If they both make the same point about energy costs, can you find a piece that adds a different dimension?"

Say: "You did excellent work building a multi-evidence response. Notice how your integration sentence showed how the pieces fit together rather than just listing them."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently select and integrate multiple pieces of evidence from a new passage.

**Passage B: "The Hidden World of Soil"**

> Beneath every forest, field, and garden lies one of the most complex ecosystems on Earth: soil. A single teaspoon of healthy soil contains more microorganisms than there are people on the planet -- as many as one billion bacteria, several yards of fungal threads, and thousands of protozoa. These microscopic communities are not idle passengers. Bacteria break down dead plant material and convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms that plants can absorb through their roots. Fungi form vast underground networks, sometimes stretching for miles, that connect the roots of different trees and allow them to share nutrients and chemical warning signals about insect attacks. Scientists have begun calling these fungal networks the "wood wide web" because they function as a communication and resource-sharing system for entire forests. Yet despite its extraordinary importance, soil is being degraded at alarming rates worldwide. Intensive farming practices that rely on heavy tilling, chemical fertilizers, and single-crop planting strip soil of its organic matter and destroy the microbial communities that keep it fertile. The United Nations estimates that one-third of the world's topsoil has already been degraded and that at current rates, the remaining topsoil could be significantly diminished within sixty years. Soil erosion is also accelerated by deforestation, which removes the root systems that anchor soil in place, and by climate change, which intensifies the rainfall events that wash topsoil away. Restoring degraded soil is possible but slow: building just one inch of new topsoil through natural processes can take five hundred years or more.

**Directions:** Answer each question using the C-MEI method. Cite at least three distinct pieces of evidence with quotation marks and signal phrases. Write an integration statement explaining how the evidence works together.

1. **(On-level)** What evidence supports the idea that soil is a complex and active ecosystem? Cite at least three pieces of evidence.
2. **(On-level)** What evidence demonstrates that soil is being threatened? Cite at least three pieces of evidence.
3. **(Stretch)** The passage mentions both the value of soil and the threats it faces. Cite evidence from both halves of the passage and explain how understanding soil's complexity makes the threats more alarming.
4. **(Challenge)** A reader claims: "Since building one inch of topsoil takes five hundred years, soil restoration is essentially impossible." Do you agree or disagree? Cite several pieces of evidence from the passage to build your argument.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.2) Reread the passage. What is one central idea of this passage? State it as a complete sentence and identify two details that convey it.
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.6) What is the author's purpose in writing this passage? Cite one textual clue that reveals this purpose.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> The Atlantic puffin, a seabird recognizable by its brightly colored beak, has experienced a sharp population decline in recent decades. Rising ocean temperatures have shifted the distribution of sand eels, the puffin's primary food source, northward and away from traditional breeding colonies. A 2021 survey of puffin colonies along the coast of Iceland found that breeding success had dropped by 40 percent over the previous fifteen years. Conservationists have also noted that increased storm intensity during the nesting season destroys burrows and drowns chicks that cannot yet fly.

1. Cite at least three pieces of evidence that support the claim that Atlantic puffins are in danger. Use signal phrases and quotation marks.
2. In one sentence, explain how these pieces of evidence work together.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Evidence 1: The passage states that the puffin "has experienced a sharp population decline in recent decades." Evidence 2: The author notes that "rising ocean temperatures have shifted the distribution of sand eels, the puffin's primary food source, northward." Evidence 3: The text reports that "breeding success had dropped by 40 percent over the previous fifteen years." (Accept also: "increased storm intensity during the nesting season destroys burrows and drowns chicks.")
2. Together, these pieces of evidence show that puffins face threats on multiple fronts -- declining food availability, reduced breeding success, and worsening weather -- which suggests their survival is endangered by a combination of interconnected factors rather than a single cause.

**Mastery = cites at least 3 distinct pieces of evidence with proper formatting AND writes an integration sentence that explains how they work together**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the C-MEI method using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Have the student highlight all potential pieces of evidence in a passage, then sort them into groups: "same point" versus "new point"
- Practice with a shorter passage (3-4 sentences) where the student must find three distinct details
- Use the Multi-Evidence Citation Chart to organize evidence before writing

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 2 on making and supporting inferences
- Encourage the student to practice multi-evidence citation when reading informational texts independently

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Evidence Audit:** Provide a sample multi-evidence response that contains one redundant piece of evidence. Have the student identify which evidence is redundant and replace it with a complementary piece from the passage.
2. **Real-World Application:** Have the student read a short science or news article and write a C-MEI response citing at least three pieces of evidence to support a claim about the article's content.
3. **Evidence Strength Ranking:** Give the student a claim and five possible pieces of evidence from the passage. Have them rank the evidence from strongest to weakest and explain their ranking criteria.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common error at this level is students who cite three pieces of evidence that all say essentially the same thing. This is redundancy, not multi-evidence citation. Before students write, have them check: "Does each piece of evidence add something the others do not?"
- Some students may struggle with the integration statement. Model it as answering: "What do these pieces of evidence show when you consider them as a group?" The integration should reveal a pattern, a broader conclusion, or a deeper understanding than any single piece provides.
- Signal phrase variety becomes more important with multiple evidence pieces. Encourage students to use different signal phrases for each citation: "The author states," "The text reveals," "According to the passage," "The author further notes." This prevents the response from sounding repetitive.
- If a student finds only two pieces of evidence, prompt them to reread with a specific question: "We know about X and Y. What else does the passage tell us?" Often the third piece of evidence is in a part of the text the student skipped over on first reading.
- The shift from C-E-C (Grade 6) to C-MEI (Grade 7) is significant. Be patient with students who are still in the habit of finding one piece of evidence and stopping. The goal is to build the instinct to keep searching.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Claim:** Soil is a remarkably complex and active ecosystem. **Evidence 1:** The passage states that "a single teaspoon of healthy soil contains more microorganisms than there are people on the planet -- as many as one billion bacteria, several yards of fungal threads, and thousands of protozoa." **Evidence 2:** The author explains that "bacteria break down dead plant material and convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms that plants can absorb through their roots." **Evidence 3:** The text reveals that "fungi form vast underground networks, sometimes stretching for miles, that connect the roots of different trees and allow them to share nutrients and chemical warning signals." **Integration:** These three pieces of evidence show that soil is not inert matter but a thriving community of organisms that are actively performing vital functions -- decomposing organic matter, cycling nutrients, and facilitating communication among plants. The sheer number of organisms, the chemical processes they perform, and the sophisticated networks they build together demonstrate a level of complexity rivaling any above-ground ecosystem.

2. **Claim:** Soil faces multiple serious threats that are degrading it worldwide. **Evidence 1:** The passage states that "intensive farming practices that rely on heavy tilling, chemical fertilizers, and single-crop planting strip soil of its organic matter and destroy the microbial communities that keep it fertile." **Evidence 2:** The author notes that "the United Nations estimates that one-third of the world's topsoil has already been degraded and that at current rates, the remaining topsoil could be significantly diminished within sixty years." **Evidence 3:** The text adds that "deforestation removes the root systems that anchor soil in place" and "climate change intensifies the rainfall events that wash topsoil away." **Integration:** These pieces of evidence reveal that soil degradation is driven by a combination of agricultural practices, environmental destruction, and climate change, and that the scale of the damage is already enormous. The fact that threats operate through different mechanisms -- chemical depletion, physical erosion, and climate intensification -- means no single solution will be sufficient.

3. **Claim:** Understanding soil's biological complexity makes the threats to it especially alarming. **Evidence (value):** The passage describes soil as containing "one billion bacteria, several yards of fungal threads, and thousands of protozoa" per teaspoon, with fungi forming networks that allow trees "to share nutrients and chemical warning signals." **Evidence (threats):** The author reports that farming practices "destroy the microbial communities that keep it fertile" and that "building just one inch of new topsoil through natural processes can take five hundred years or more." **Integration:** The evidence from both halves of the passage creates a disturbing picture: the soil ecosystem that took centuries or millennia to develop -- with its billions of interconnected organisms -- can be destroyed in a few years by intensive farming or erosion. The passage's detailed description of soil's living complexity makes the threats feel more urgent because the reader understands that what is being lost is not just dirt but an intricate web of biological relationships.

4. Answers will vary. A strong response might argue: The claim that restoration is "essentially impossible" overstates the passage's evidence. The passage states that "restoring degraded soil is possible but slow" -- it says possible, not impossible. However, the five-hundred-year timeline for building one inch of topsoil suggests that once soil is severely degraded, recovery within a human lifespan is unlikely through natural processes alone. The passage also notes that "one-third of the world's topsoil has already been degraded," meaning the scale of damage is enormous. A nuanced response would acknowledge that while soil can technically be restored, the timescale is so long and the current degradation so extensive that for practical purposes, prevention is far more realistic than repair.

### Interleaved Review
5. Central idea: Soil is a vital, complex ecosystem that is being degraded at alarming rates due to human activity. Detail 1: A single teaspoon contains one billion bacteria, fungal threads, and thousands of protozoa performing essential functions. Detail 2: One-third of the world's topsoil has already been degraded, and the remaining could be significantly diminished within sixty years.
6. The author's purpose is to raise awareness about soil's importance and the urgency of protecting it. Textual clue: The author uses alarming language and statistics -- "degraded at alarming rates," "one-third of the world's topsoil has already been degraded," "five hundred years or more" to rebuild one inch -- to convey urgency and motivate the reader to see soil conservation as a pressing concern.
