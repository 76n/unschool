# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 10

## Topic
Integrated Informational Text Analysis

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply all four unit standards (RI.7.1, RI.7.2, RI.7.6, RI.7.8) simultaneously to analyze a new informational text
- Cite several pieces of evidence to support analysis of explicit information and inferences (RI.7.1)
- Identify two or more central ideas, trace their development, and write an objective summary (RI.7.2)
- Determine the author's point of view and analyze how the author distinguishes their position from others (RI.7.6)
- Trace and evaluate the argument's claims, reasoning, and evidence (RI.7.8)

## Prerequisites
- All skills from Lessons 1-9
- Ability to work independently through multi-step analytical tasks
- Ability to write extended analytical responses citing textual evidence

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passage
- Integrated Analysis Worksheet (sections for each standard: Evidence Citation, Central Ideas, Author's Position, Argument Evaluation)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in four colors (one per standard, recommended)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all four standards and prepare students for integrated analysis.

**Activity: Standards Recap**

Say: "Over the past nine lessons, you have developed four powerful analytical skills. Today you bring them all together to analyze a single text. Let us review what each standard asks you to do."

Write on the board:
- **RI.7.1:** Cite SEVERAL pieces of evidence for explicit claims and inferences.
- **RI.7.2:** Find TWO OR MORE central ideas, trace their development, write an objective summary.
- **RI.7.6:** Determine the author's POINT OF VIEW and PURPOSE. Analyze how the author DISTINGUISHES their position from others.
- **RI.7.8:** TRACE the argument (claim, reasons, evidence). EVALUATE the reasoning and evidence (relevant? sufficient? sound?).

Say: "In real-world reading, you never use just one of these skills at a time. When you read a newspaper article, a science report, or an opinion piece, you need all four simultaneously. That is what we practice today."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model integrated analysis on the first portion of the lesson passage, demonstrating how all four standards work together.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Integrated analysis:** Applying multiple analytical skills simultaneously to develop a comprehensive understanding of a text
- **Analytical lens:** A specific focus or framework through which to examine a text (e.g., evidence lens, central idea lens, author's position lens, argument evaluation lens)
- **Multi-standard response:** A written analysis that addresses more than one standard in a single, cohesive response

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When I do an integrated analysis, I read the text once for understanding, then I apply each analytical lens. I do not try to do everything at once -- I layer the analysis. Let me show you with the first part of a passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Digital Divide in American Education"**

> The shift to online learning during the health crisis of 2020 exposed a gap that had been growing quietly for years: millions of American students lacked the internet access and devices necessary to participate in a digital education system. While students in well-funded suburban districts logged on to virtual classrooms from home computers with high-speed broadband connections, students in rural areas and low-income urban neighborhoods struggled with unreliable internet, shared devices, or no connectivity at all. The Federal Communications Commission estimated that approximately 17 million children -- disproportionately Black, Latino, and Native American -- lacked adequate home internet access. This gap, known as the digital divide, is not merely a technology problem. It is an equity crisis that threatens to deepen the educational disparities that already separate wealthy communities from poor ones.
>
> The consequences of the digital divide are measurable and growing. Students without reliable internet access missed an average of three to five additional hours of instruction per week during the period of remote learning, according to data from the National Center for Education Statistics. Over the course of a full school year, this lost time accumulates into hundreds of hours of missed learning -- the equivalent of losing several months of school. Even as in-person instruction has resumed, the digital divide continues to affect students. Modern homework increasingly requires internet access for research, collaboration, and submission. Students without broadband at home must travel to libraries, fast-food restaurants with free wireless access, or school parking lots to complete assignments, spending time and energy on logistics that their connected peers never have to consider.
>
> Some policymakers have proposed that the federal government should treat broadband internet as a public utility -- like electricity or running water -- and invest in infrastructure to guarantee high-speed access for every household. Proponents of this approach point to the success of the Rural Electrification Act of the 1930s, which brought electricity to 90 percent of American farms within two decades and transformed rural economic life. They argue that broadband internet is the twenty-first-century equivalent: a resource so essential that leaving its distribution to market forces alone guarantees that underserved communities will remain underserved. Critics counter that government-funded broadband would be prohibitively expensive, that private telecommunications companies are already expanding coverage, and that government management of internet infrastructure could lead to inefficiency and waste. However, the track record of private expansion has been uneven: telecommunications companies have invested heavily in profitable urban and suburban markets while leaving rural and low-income areas with slow, expensive, or nonexistent service. A 2022 report by the Brookings Institution found that the average cost of broadband internet in low-income neighborhoods is 30 percent higher than in affluent areas, despite lower incomes to pay for it.

Say: "Let me demonstrate layered analysis on this passage."

**Lens 1 -- RI.7.1 (Evidence):**

Say: "First, I identify several pieces of evidence that support the claim that the digital divide is a serious problem."

Write on the board:
- Evidence 1: "Approximately 17 million children lacked adequate home internet access" (FCC data)
- Evidence 2: Students without internet "missed an average of three to five additional hours of instruction per week" (NCES data)
- Evidence 3: "The average cost of broadband internet in low-income neighborhoods is 30 percent higher than in affluent areas" (Brookings Institution)
- Integration: These three pieces of evidence, drawn from different authoritative sources, demonstrate that the digital divide is large in scale (17 million children), costly in educational terms (hundreds of lost hours), and economically inequitable (higher costs for those least able to pay).

**Lens 2 -- RI.7.2 (Central Ideas):**

Write on the board:
- Central Idea 1: The digital divide is an equity crisis that disproportionately harms low-income, rural, and minority students.
- Central Idea 2: Broadband internet should be treated as essential public infrastructure, similar to electricity, to close the digital divide.

**Lens 3 -- RI.7.6 (Author's Position):**

Write on the board:
- Purpose: To argue that the federal government should ensure universal broadband access.
- Point of view: The author believes the digital divide is an equity crisis caused by market failure and that government investment is necessary.
- Distinguishing position: The author acknowledges critics who say government broadband is expensive and that private companies are already expanding. The author rebuts this with evidence that private expansion has been "uneven" and that broadband costs more in low-income areas -- a contrast with evidence technique.

**Lens 4 -- RI.7.8 (Argument Evaluation):**

Write on the board:
- Claim: Broadband should be treated as a public utility with guaranteed access.
- Reasoning: Sound -- the comparison to the Rural Electrification Act provides historical precedent. The argument that market forces alone leave underserved communities behind is supported by Brookings Institution data.
- Evidence: Relevant and from authoritative sources (FCC, NCES, Brookings). Multiple sources strengthen sufficiency.
- Potential weakness: The Rural Electrification Act comparison assumes that what worked for electricity in the 1930s would work for broadband today. Different technology, different costs, different political context. The analogy is relevant but not conclusive proof.

Say: "See how each lens reveals something different? Evidence citation shows the data. Central idea analysis shows the themes. Author's position analysis reveals the argument's perspective. Evaluation reveals both strengths and weaknesses. Together, they give you a complete understanding."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply integrated analysis to a new passage with facilitator support.

Say: "Now we will work through an integrated analysis together on a different passage."

**Passage: "The Future of Electric School Buses"**

> More than 26 million American students ride diesel-powered school buses every day, making the school bus fleet the largest mass transit system in the country. These buses, while reliable, produce significant air pollution. Diesel exhaust contains fine particulate matter and nitrogen oxides that are linked to asthma, respiratory infections, and reduced lung development in children. A study published in the American Journal of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine found that children who regularly ride diesel school buses have 30 percent higher exposure to these pollutants than children who walk or are driven to school in private vehicles. The irony is difficult to ignore: the very vehicles designed to transport children safely may be harming their health along the way.
>
> Electric school buses offer a solution to this problem while also providing unexpected economic benefits. Electric buses produce zero tailpipe emissions, eliminating children's exposure to diesel exhaust. Although the upfront cost of an electric bus is approximately 300,000 to 400,000 dollars -- roughly three times the cost of a new diesel bus -- the operating costs are dramatically lower. Electric buses require no diesel fuel, which costs school districts an average of 17,000 dollars per bus per year, and they need far less maintenance because electric motors have fewer moving parts than diesel engines. Over a twelve-year lifespan, the total cost of owning an electric bus is roughly comparable to a diesel bus. Additionally, electric bus batteries can store energy and feed it back to the electrical grid during peak demand periods, potentially generating revenue for school districts.

Say: "Let us work through all four lenses. Start with RI.7.1 -- cite several pieces of evidence that the passage makes a case for electric school buses."

Guide the student through each lens. Expected responses:

**RI.7.1:** Evidence 1: Children who ride diesel buses have "30 percent higher exposure" to pollutants (AJRCCM study). Evidence 2: Diesel exhaust is "linked to asthma, respiratory infections, and reduced lung development." Evidence 3: Electric buses eliminate children's exposure to diesel exhaust. Evidence 4: Over a twelve-year lifespan, total cost is "roughly comparable" to diesel.

**RI.7.2:** Central Idea 1: Diesel school buses pose a health risk to the millions of children who ride them daily. Central Idea 2: Electric school buses can eliminate this health risk while remaining economically viable over their lifespan.

**RI.7.6:** Purpose: To argue that school districts should transition from diesel to electric buses. Point of view: The author believes diesel buses are a health hazard for children and that the higher upfront cost of electric buses is justified by health benefits and long-term cost savings.

**RI.7.8:** Claim: Electric buses should replace diesel buses. Reason 1: Diesel buses harm children's health (supported by AJRCCM study, specific pollutants). Reason 2: Electric buses are economically viable over their lifespan (supported by fuel savings, maintenance savings, potential revenue from grid feed-back). Evaluation: Evidence is relevant and from credible sources. The health evidence is strong. The economic argument is somewhat strong but depends on the twelve-year lifespan assumption and does not address battery replacement costs or charging infrastructure investments.

Say: "You applied all four lenses and found both strengths and potential gaps. That is a complete integrated analysis."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently perform an integrated analysis of a new passage.

**Passage: "Should Communities Ban Leaf Blowers?"**

> On any autumn morning in suburban America, the roar of gas-powered leaf blowers shatters the quiet of residential neighborhoods. These machines, which homeowners and landscaping companies use to clear leaves, grass clippings, and debris, are among the most polluting consumer tools in existence. A single commercial gas-powered leaf blower running for one hour emits the same amount of smog-forming pollution as driving a car from Los Angeles to Denver -- a distance of more than 1,000 miles. The two-stroke engines used in most commercial blowers are staggeringly inefficient: up to 30 percent of the fuel they consume is expelled unburned as aerosol pollutants. The noise is equally problematic, with commercial blowers generating 80 to 100 decibels at the operator's ear -- levels that cause hearing damage with prolonged exposure.
>
> The environmental damage extends beyond emissions and noise. Leaf blowers strip away the layer of decomposing leaves that provides habitat for insects, earthworms, and ground-nesting pollinators essential to healthy ecosystems. This leaf litter layer also protects soil from erosion, retains moisture, and naturally decomposes into nutrients that feed lawns and gardens without the need for synthetic fertilizers. When blowers clear this layer entirely, homeowners often replace its functions with chemical treatments that introduce additional pollutants into waterways.
>
> Over 100 cities in the United States have enacted some form of leaf blower restriction, ranging from seasonal bans to complete prohibitions on gas-powered models. Supporters of these bans argue that electric alternatives are now powerful enough for residential and light commercial use at a fraction of the noise and pollution levels. Critics, however, maintain that battery-powered blowers lack the power and runtime needed for professional landscaping work and that bans would devastate small landscaping businesses that cannot afford to replace their entire fleet of equipment. Both concerns have some validity: while electric blower technology has improved dramatically, the most powerful commercial models still fall short of gas-powered equivalents in sustained heavy-duty use, and the cost of transitioning a landscaping fleet can reach tens of thousands of dollars.
>
> Yet the health and environmental costs of inaction are difficult to dismiss. A study by the California Air Resources Board found that operating a commercial gas leaf blower for one hour exposes the operator and nearby residents to fine particulate matter at levels exceeding air quality standards for healthy breathing. Landscaping workers, many of whom use blowers for six to eight hours per day without adequate hearing protection, face elevated rates of hearing loss, respiratory illness, and cardiovascular stress. When communities weigh the convenience of gas-powered leaf removal against the documented harm to air quality, ecosystems, and human health, the case for transitioning to cleaner alternatives becomes increasingly compelling.

**Directions:** Perform a complete integrated analysis of this passage.

1. **(On-level — RI.7.1)** Cite at least three pieces of evidence from the passage that support the claim that gas-powered leaf blowers are harmful. Explain how the evidence pieces work together.
2. **(On-level — RI.7.2)** Identify two central ideas in the passage. For each, state it as a complete sentence and cite at least one supporting detail.
3. **(On-level — RI.7.6)** What is the author's point of view about leaf blowers? How does the author distinguish their position from the position of critics? Identify the technique used.
4. **(Stretch — RI.7.8)** Trace the argument in this passage: identify the claim, at least two reasons, and the evidence for each. Then evaluate: Is the reasoning sound? Is the evidence relevant and sufficient?
5. **(Challenge — all standards)** Write an extended response (6-8 sentences) that integrates all four standards. Your response should: state the author's central argument and purpose; cite several pieces of evidence; analyze how the author handles opposing views; and evaluate whether the overall argument is convincing. Identify at least one strength and one potential weakness.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.3) How does the author introduce the problem of gas-powered leaf blowers in the first paragraph? What sensory details and comparisons does the author use to establish the severity of the issue?
7. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.2) Write an objective summary of this passage in 4-5 sentences. Do not include personal opinions.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Researchers at Stanford University have found that students who take handwritten notes retain information significantly better than those who type notes on laptops. Handwriting forces students to process and summarize information in real time because they cannot write fast enough to transcribe everything verbatim. Laptop users, by contrast, tend to type lectures word-for-word, engaging in shallow processing that does not promote long-term memory. Some educators now argue that laptops should be banned from classrooms during lectures, though critics counter that laptops provide essential accessibility tools for students with certain learning differences.

1. Apply two different analytical lenses from this unit to this passage. For each, write 2-3 sentences of analysis.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Any two of the following:
   - **RI.7.1:** The passage provides several pieces of evidence supporting the benefits of handwritten notes. It cites Stanford research showing "students who take handwritten notes retain information significantly better," explains the mechanism that "handwriting forces students to process and summarize information in real time," and contrasts this with laptop users who "tend to type lectures word-for-word, engaging in shallow processing." Together, these pieces explain both the finding and the reason behind it.
   - **RI.7.6:** The author's point of view favors handwritten notes, as indicated by the emphasis on evidence supporting handwriting and the description of laptop use as "shallow processing." The author distinguishes from critics' position by noting their concern about accessibility but does not provide evidence to support the counter-position, giving it less weight than the pro-handwriting evidence.
   - **RI.7.8:** The argument's claim is that handwritten notes are superior for learning. The reasoning is sound: the mechanism (forced processing versus verbatim transcription) logically explains why handwriting aids retention. However, the evidence may not be sufficient -- only one university's research is cited. Additional studies would strengthen the claim.

**Mastery = applies at least two different analytical lenses correctly with specific textual evidence**

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is the capstone of the unit, bringing together all nine previous lessons. If a student struggles with a particular standard, note it carefully: this information guides reteaching before the unit assessment and helps identify which skills need reinforcement.
- The integrated analysis approach mirrors how students will encounter informational text on standardized assessments and in real life. Emphasize that these skills are not just for English class -- they apply whenever students read a science article, a news report, or a social studies source.
- The four-color highlighting system (one color per standard) is particularly effective in this lesson because it makes the integration visible. Students can see at a glance which parts of the text they analyzed through which lens.
- For the Challenge question (question 5), do not expect perfection. An integrated response is a complex task. Look for evidence that the student can apply multiple lenses and move between them. Fluency will develop with practice.
- Use the exit ticket results from this lesson to plan review activities for the day before the unit assessment. If most students struggle with RI.7.8, spend the review session on argument evaluation. If most struggle with RI.7.2, focus on central idea identification. Target the weakest area.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **RI.7.1:** Evidence 1: The passage states that "a single commercial gas-powered leaf blower running for one hour emits the same amount of smog-forming pollution as driving a car from Los Angeles to Denver." Evidence 2: The author notes that "up to 30 percent of the fuel they consume is expelled unburned as aerosol pollutants." Evidence 3: The text reports that leaf blowers "strip away the layer of decomposing leaves that provides habitat for insects, earthworms, and ground-nesting pollinators essential to healthy ecosystems." (Also accept: decibel levels, California Air Resources Board study on fine particulate matter, landscaping worker health impacts.) Integration: These three pieces of evidence demonstrate that gas-powered leaf blowers cause harm across multiple domains -- air pollution, ecosystem disruption, and noise damage -- showing that the problem is broad and multifaceted rather than limited to a single type of harm.

2. **RI.7.2:** Central Idea 1: Gas-powered leaf blowers cause significant harm to air quality, ecosystems, and human health through pollution, noise, and habitat destruction. Supporting detail: One hour of operation equals the smog emissions of driving a car more than 1,000 miles. Central Idea 2: Transitioning to electric alternatives is feasible but faces legitimate practical and economic challenges for professional landscapers. Supporting detail: Electric technology has "improved dramatically" but the most powerful commercial models still fall short of gas equivalents, and fleet replacement costs can reach tens of thousands of dollars.

3. **RI.7.6:** The author's point of view is that gas-powered leaf blowers should be replaced by cleaner alternatives because their health and environmental costs outweigh the convenience they provide. The author distinguishes their position from critics by using the **concede and counter** technique: the author acknowledges that critics have "some validity" in their concerns about electric blower power and landscaping business costs, but then counters with evidence from the California Air Resources Board about health harms to operators and residents, framing the health costs as the more compelling consideration. The author's final sentence -- "the case for transitioning to cleaner alternatives becomes increasingly compelling" -- reveals the author's position while framing it as a conclusion drawn from evidence rather than a personal opinion.

4. **RI.7.8:** Claim: Communities should transition from gas-powered leaf blowers to cleaner alternatives. Reason 1: Gas-powered blowers produce extreme levels of air pollution and noise. Evidence: LA-to-Denver emissions comparison; 30% unburned fuel as pollutants; 80-100 decibels at the operator's ear. Reason 2: Gas blowers cause ecological harm by destroying leaf litter habitat. Evidence: Leaf litter provides habitat for pollinators; removal leads to chemical fertilizer use. Reason 3: The health costs to operators and residents are documented. Evidence: California Air Resources Board study on fine particulate matter; elevated rates of hearing loss and respiratory illness in landscaping workers. Evaluation: The reasoning is generally sound -- the evidence logically supports each reason, and the reasons logically support the claim. The evidence is relevant (all directly about leaf blowers) and comes from credible sources. Potential weakness: The passage does not quantify the economic impact on landscaping businesses beyond saying it "can reach tens of thousands of dollars." A stronger argument would address how communities plan to support the transition for small businesses. Additionally, the one-hour-equals-LA-to-Denver comparison, while striking, deserves scrutiny -- the conditions under which this equivalence holds (type of blower, measurement methodology) are not specified.

5. Answers will vary. A strong integrated response: "The author argues that communities should transition from gas-powered leaf blowers to electric alternatives because the health and environmental costs of gas blowers are severe and well-documented. The passage develops two central ideas: that gas blowers cause multidimensional harm (air pollution, ecosystem destruction, noise damage, worker health risks) and that electric alternatives, while imperfect, represent a feasible path forward. The author's evidence is drawn from credible sources, including the California Air Resources Board and specific emissions data, and several pieces work together to build a compelling case -- the LA-to-Denver pollution comparison, the 30 percent unburned fuel statistic, and the health impacts on landscaping workers each address a different dimension of harm. The author acknowledges critics' concerns about electric blower performance and economic impact on small businesses, conceding that 'both concerns have some validity,' but counters by emphasizing the documented health harms as a more urgent consideration. This concede-and-counter technique strengthens the argument by showing fairness before reasserting the author's position. A strength of the argument is the diversity and specificity of its evidence. A potential weakness is that the passage does not propose concrete solutions for helping landscaping businesses transition, which leaves the economic counterargument partially unresolved."

### Interleaved Review

The opening -- "the roar of gas-powered leaf blowers shatters the quiet of residential neighborhoods" -- uses auditory imagery to make the reader feel the noise disruption. The comparison of one hour of blower use to "driving a car from Los Angeles to Denver" translates an abstract pollution concept into a concrete, geographic image. The detail about 30 percent of fuel being expelled unburned as pollutants adds technical specificity. Together, these details establish the severity of the issue by making it visceral and measurable rather than vague.
