# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 2, Lesson 2

## Topic
Making and Supporting Inferences in Nonfiction

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Draw logical inferences from informational text by combining explicit details with background knowledge and reasoning
- Support each inference with several pieces of textual evidence that work together
- Distinguish between reasonable inferences (grounded in the text) and unsupported assumptions (based on opinion or guesswork)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence to support explicit claims (Lesson 1, RI.7.1)
- Understanding of the difference between explicit information and inference (RI.6.1)
- Ability to use signal phrases and quotation marks when citing evidence
- Ability to read Grade 7 level informational text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Inference Ladder graphic organizer (columns: Explicit Detail 1, Explicit Detail 2, Explicit Detail 3, My Reasoning, My Inference)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors (one for explicit details, one for inference clues)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about inference and connect it to the multi-evidence citation skill learned in Lesson 1.

**Activity: Stated vs. Unstated**

Say: "In Lesson 1, you learned to cite several pieces of evidence to support claims about what a text says explicitly -- information stated directly. Today we focus on the other half of RI.7.1: inferences. An inference is a conclusion you draw from evidence in the text combined with your own reasoning. The text suggests it but does not state it outright."

Write on the board:
- Explicit: "The school installed solar panels on its roof last year." (This is stated directly.)
- Inference: The school values environmental sustainability. (This is not stated, but the detail about solar panels supports it.)

Say: "But here is the Grade 7 challenge -- one piece of evidence is not enough to make a strong inference. You need several pieces of evidence pointing in the same direction. Think of it like a detective gathering clues. One clue is interesting. Three clues that all point to the same suspect are convincing."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to construct well-supported inferences by combining multiple explicit details with logical reasoning.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Inference:** A logical conclusion drawn by combining evidence from the text with the reader's own reasoning; what the text suggests but does not state directly
- **Explicit information:** Details, facts, or statements directly expressed in the text
- **Reasoning:** The logical thinking process that connects evidence to a conclusion
- **Unsupported assumption:** A conclusion that lacks sufficient evidence from the text and relies instead on guesswork, personal opinion, or stereotypes

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Making a strong inference in seventh grade follows what I call the Inference Ladder. You climb three rungs."

Write on the board:
1. **Gather** -- Find several explicit details in the text that relate to your question.
2. **Reason** -- Ask: What do these details suggest when I consider them together? What logical conclusion do they point toward?
3. **State and Support** -- Write your inference and cite the several pieces of evidence that support it.

Say: "The critical step is Rung 2 -- the reasoning. This is where you connect the dots. Without reasoning, you just have a list of quotes. With reasoning, you have an analytical inference. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Disappearance of Honeybees"**

> Since 2006, beekeepers around the world have reported a troubling phenomenon: entire colonies of honeybees vanishing almost overnight, leaving behind empty hives with no dead bees in sight. Scientists named this mystery Colony Collapse Disorder, or CCD. At its peak, some beekeepers were losing 30 to 40 percent of their colonies each winter, far above the normal loss rate of 10 to 15 percent. Researchers have investigated dozens of possible causes, including pesticides called neonicotinoids that attack the nervous systems of insects, a parasitic mite known as the Varroa destructor that feeds on bee larvae and spreads deadly viruses, and the stress of commercial beekeeping practices that truck colonies thousands of miles across the country to pollinate different crops in succession. Most scientists now believe that CCD results not from a single cause but from a combination of stressors that weaken bee immune systems. Honeybees pollinate approximately one-third of the food crops consumed in the United States, including almonds, apples, blueberries, and cherries. The economic value of honeybee pollination in the United States alone is estimated at more than 15 billion dollars annually.

Say: "Here is an inference question: What can you infer about the potential consequences if Colony Collapse Disorder is not resolved?"

Say: "The passage does not directly state what will happen. I need to gather evidence, reason through it, and state my inference. Watch my process."

Write on the board:
- **Gather:**
  - Detail 1: "Honeybees pollinate approximately one-third of the food crops consumed in the United States."
  - Detail 2: "The economic value of honeybee pollination in the United States alone is estimated at more than 15 billion dollars annually."
  - Detail 3: "Some beekeepers were losing 30 to 40 percent of their colonies each winter."
- **Reason:** If bees pollinate one-third of food crops and their populations are declining dramatically, then a significant portion of the food supply depends on an organism that is disappearing. The economic figure suggests the damage would be measured in billions of dollars.
- **State and Support:** If Colony Collapse Disorder is not resolved, both the American food supply and agricultural economy could face severe disruption. The passage reveals that "honeybees pollinate approximately one-third of the food crops consumed in the United States," that the "economic value of honeybee pollination" exceeds "15 billion dollars annually," and that beekeepers were "losing 30 to 40 percent of their colonies each winter." Together, these details indicate that continued bee decline would shrink the supply of fruits, nuts, and vegetables that depend on pollination, driving up food prices and causing billions in economic losses.

Say: "Notice that the passage never says 'food prices will rise' or 'the economy will suffer.' Those are my inferences. But they are not guesses -- they are logical conclusions drawn from several converging pieces of evidence."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice constructing inferences with facilitator support using the Inference Ladder.

Say: "Let us work through another inference together using the same passage."

**Question: What can you infer about why scientists have had difficulty finding a single cause for Colony Collapse Disorder?**

Say: "Start at Rung 1: Gather. What explicit details help us understand why this mystery is hard to solve?"

Allow the student to scan the passage. Guide them toward relevant details.

Expected response:
- **Gather:**
  - Detail 1: "Researchers have investigated dozens of possible causes."
  - Detail 2: The causes listed include different types of threats: "pesticides called neonicotinoids that attack the nervous systems of insects," "a parasitic mite known as the Varroa destructor," and "the stress of commercial beekeeping practices."
  - Detail 3: "Most scientists now believe that CCD results not from a single cause but from a combination of stressors."
- **Reason:** The passage shows that the potential causes operate through completely different mechanisms -- chemical poisoning, parasitic infection, and physical stress. If the problem came from a single source, research would likely have identified it. The fact that scientists now believe it is a combination suggests that looking for one cause was the wrong approach.
- **State and Support:** Scientists have struggled to find a single cause for CCD because the disorder appears to result from multiple interacting factors rather than one identifiable source. The passage notes that "researchers have investigated dozens of possible causes" spanning different categories: chemical (neonicotinoid pesticides), biological (Varroa mites), and environmental (commercial beekeeping stress). The fact that "most scientists now believe that CCD results not from a single cause but from a combination of stressors" confirms that the search for a single villain was unsuccessful because the real answer involves many contributing factors working together.

Say: "Your reasoning step was the key. You did not just list the evidence -- you explained why the variety of causes made the mystery harder to solve."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently construct inferences supported by several pieces of evidence.

**Passage B: "The Rise of Telemedicine"**

> During the spring of 2020, when a global health crisis forced millions of people to stay home, visits to doctors' offices dropped by nearly 60 percent in the United States. At the same time, the use of telemedicine -- medical consultations conducted by video call, phone, or online messaging -- increased by more than 150 percent in a matter of weeks. What had been a niche service used mainly by patients in remote rural areas suddenly became a mainstream method of healthcare delivery. Patients discovered that many routine visits, including prescription renewals, mental health counseling, follow-up appointments, and initial symptom assessments, could be handled effectively without traveling to a doctor's office. Doctors reported that telemedicine reduced no-show rates by 50 percent and allowed them to see more patients per day. Surveys conducted after the initial surge showed that 76 percent of patients were interested in continuing to use telemedicine even after in-person visits became safe again. However, the expansion of telemedicine also exposed significant gaps. Patients without reliable internet access -- disproportionately those in low-income communities and rural areas with limited broadband infrastructure -- were unable to participate. A study by the Federal Communications Commission found that approximately 21 million Americans lacked broadband internet in their homes. Physical examinations, diagnostic imaging, blood tests, and surgical procedures remained impossible to conduct remotely, limiting telemedicine to a subset of medical interactions.

**Directions:** Use the Inference Ladder (Gather, Reason, State and Support) for each question. Cite at least three pieces of evidence per inference.

1. **(On-level)** What can you infer about how the health crisis of 2020 changed public attitudes toward technology in healthcare? Support your inference with several pieces of evidence.
2. **(On-level)** What can you infer about which communities are most likely to be left behind by the expansion of telemedicine? Cite several pieces of evidence.
3. **(Stretch)** The passage describes both advantages and limitations of telemedicine. What can you infer about the future role of telemedicine in healthcare -- will it replace in-person visits or exist alongside them? Cite evidence from both sides of the passage.
4. **(Challenge)** A reader infers: "Telemedicine proves that people never really needed to visit doctors' offices." Is this a reasonable inference based on the passage, or an unsupported assumption? Explain your reasoning using evidence.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.3) How does the author introduce the concept of telemedicine in this passage? What technique does the author use in the opening sentences, and what effect does it create?
6. (Grade 6 review -- RI.6.1) Cite one piece of explicit textual evidence that shows doctors benefited from telemedicine. Use a signal phrase and quotation marks.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> The invasive spotted lanternfly, first detected in Pennsylvania in 2014, has since spread to more than a dozen states along the eastern United States. The insect feeds on the sap of over 70 plant species, including grapevines, apple trees, and hardwood forests, leaving behind a sticky residue called honeydew that promotes the growth of black sooty mold. Agricultural officials estimate that the lanternfly could cause more than 300 million dollars in annual damage to Pennsylvania's grape and wine industry alone if left unchecked.

1. What can you infer about the potential economic impact of the spotted lanternfly beyond Pennsylvania? Cite at least two pieces of evidence from the passage.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Inference: The spotted lanternfly could cause massive economic damage across the entire eastern United States, not just in Pennsylvania. The passage states that the insect "has since spread to more than a dozen states," indicating its range is growing rapidly. It also notes that the lanternfly "feeds on the sap of over 70 plant species, including grapevines, apple trees, and hardwood forests," meaning many different agricultural and forestry industries are at risk. If damage to Pennsylvania's grape industry alone could exceed "300 million dollars annually," then the combined damage across a dozen states and multiple crop types could be many times larger.

**Mastery = draws a logical inference supported by at least 2 distinct pieces of evidence with reasoning that connects the evidence to the conclusion**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Inference Ladder using Passage A from Direct Instruction
- Practice distinguishing between explicit information and inference with a series of paired statements
- Give the student a short passage and pre-selected evidence, then have them write only the reasoning and inference steps
- Use the Inference Ladder graphic organizer to scaffold the process

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3 on identifying central ideas
- Encourage the student to practice making inferences when reading informational texts independently

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Inference vs. Assumption Sort:** Provide five statements about the telemedicine passage. Have the student sort them into "reasonable inference" or "unsupported assumption" and explain each judgment.
2. **Write the Question:** Give the student a passage and have them write an inference question that requires combining at least three pieces of evidence to answer, along with a model answer.
3. **Cross-Text Inference:** Have the student use evidence from both Passage A (honeybees) and Passage B (telemedicine) to infer a general pattern about how modern problems often have multiple interacting causes.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The biggest challenge for students at this level is confusing inference with personal opinion. An inference is grounded in textual evidence and logical reasoning. "I think telemedicine is a good idea" is an opinion. "The evidence suggests telemedicine will continue to grow" is an inference that can be supported with textual evidence. Teach students to check: "Can I point to specific text that supports this conclusion?"
- Some students leap to extreme inferences. If a student writes "Telemedicine will completely replace hospitals," redirect them to the evidence: the passage specifically mentions limitations. Strong inferences account for all the evidence, not just the parts that support a dramatic conclusion.
- The Inference Ladder deliberately separates the "Gather" step from the "Reason" step because many students try to do both simultaneously. Slowing down to gather evidence first, then think about what it means, produces better inferences.
- Encourage students to use qualifying language in their inferences: "This suggests," "It is likely that," "The evidence indicates." This hedging is not weakness -- it is precision. It shows the student understands that an inference is a strong probability, not a proven fact.
- Connect this lesson to Lesson 1: both lessons address RI.7.1. Lesson 1 focused on explicit evidence; this lesson focuses on inference. Together, they cover the full scope of the standard.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Inference:** The 2020 health crisis dramatically shifted public attitudes toward technology in healthcare, moving telemedicine from skepticism to widespread acceptance. **Evidence 1:** Before the crisis, telemedicine was "a niche service used mainly by patients in remote rural areas," suggesting most people did not consider it a viable option. **Evidence 2:** During the crisis, telemedicine use "increased by more than 150 percent in a matter of weeks," showing that necessity drove rapid adoption. **Evidence 3:** After the initial surge, "76 percent of patients were interested in continuing to use telemedicine even after in-person visits became safe again," indicating that direct experience converted skeptics into supporters. **Reasoning:** The shift from niche to mainstream and the 76 percent continued interest rate suggest that the crisis functioned as a forced trial that changed long-held attitudes about the viability of remote medical care.

2. **Inference:** Low-income communities and rural areas without reliable broadband internet are most likely to be excluded from telemedicine's benefits. **Evidence 1:** The passage notes that "patients without reliable internet access -- disproportionately those in low-income communities and rural areas with limited broadband infrastructure -- were unable to participate." **Evidence 2:** The Federal Communications Commission found that "approximately 21 million Americans lacked broadband internet in their homes." **Evidence 3:** Ironically, telemedicine had originally been "used mainly by patients in remote rural areas," yet rural areas are also those with "limited broadband infrastructure." **Reasoning:** The people who might benefit most from remote healthcare -- those living far from medical facilities -- are the same people least likely to have the technology needed to access it, creating a healthcare inequality that mirrors existing economic and geographic divides.

3. **Inference:** Telemedicine will most likely exist alongside in-person visits rather than replacing them. **Evidence (advantages):** The passage shows that many routine visits "could be handled effectively without traveling to a doctor's office" and that "76 percent of patients were interested in continuing to use telemedicine." **Evidence (limitations):** However, "physical examinations, diagnostic imaging, blood tests, and surgical procedures remained impossible to conduct remotely," and 21 million Americans lack broadband access. **Reasoning:** The evidence suggests a hybrid future: telemedicine is effective for routine, low-complexity interactions, but physical medicine cannot be eliminated. The most likely outcome is a healthcare system that uses telemedicine for what it does well and in-person visits for what requires physical presence.

4. The claim "people never really needed to visit doctors' offices" is an unsupported assumption. While the passage does show that many routine visits can be handled remotely, it explicitly states that "physical examinations, diagnostic imaging, blood tests, and surgical procedures remained impossible to conduct remotely." The passage also notes that telemedicine was effective for only "a subset of medical interactions." A reasonable inference would be: "Many routine medical interactions do not require an in-person visit." The original claim overgeneralizes by ignoring the evidence about telemedicine's limitations.

### Interleaved Review
5. The author introduces telemedicine by first describing the health crisis that caused doctor visits to "drop by nearly 60 percent," then showing the simultaneous rise of telemedicine by "more than 150 percent." This before-and-after technique creates a dramatic contrast that emphasizes how quickly and completely the situation changed, making the reader understand telemedicine's rise as a direct response to a crisis rather than a gradual trend.
6. According to the passage, "doctors reported that telemedicine reduced no-show rates by 50 percent and allowed them to see more patients per day."
