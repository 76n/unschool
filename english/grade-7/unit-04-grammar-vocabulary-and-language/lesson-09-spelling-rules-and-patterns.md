# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4, Lesson 9

## Topic
Spelling Rules and Patterns

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply five key spelling rules: doubling the final consonant, dropping silent e, changing y to i, the i-before-e pattern, and adding prefixes
- Use spelling strategies (mnemonics, word families, syllable breakdown) to spell frequently misspelled words
- Identify and correct spelling errors in their own and others' writing

## Prerequisites
- Familiarity with basic syllable patterns and vowel sounds (Grade 4-5)
- Experience with common word roots, prefixes, and suffixes (Grade 5-6)
- Ability to proofread and edit for surface-level errors (Grade 6)
- Understanding of how correct spelling supports clear communication (Grade 5-6)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected word lists and example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Personal spelling notebook or vocabulary section
- Spelling Rules Reference Card (to be created during the lesson)
- Dictionaries (print or digital)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate awareness of spelling as a pattern-based skill, not pure memorization.

**Activity: Spot the Pattern**

Say: "Look at each group of correctly spelled words below. What pattern do you notice in each group? Write the pattern in your notebook."

Write on the board:
1. running, sitting, hopping, swimming, planning
2. hoping, writing, making, coming, dancing
3. happier, easier, funnier, busier, lazier
4. receive, ceiling, perceive, deceive, receipt

After two minutes, discuss.

Say: "Group 1: all doubled the final consonant before adding -ing. Group 2: all dropped the silent e before adding -ing. Group 3: all changed y to i before adding -er. Group 4: all have 'ei' after the letter c. These are not random — English spelling has rules, and once you know them, you can spell hundreds of words you have never even seen before. That is far more powerful than memorizing one word at a time."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach five major spelling rules with clear explanations, examples, and common exceptions.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Doubling rule:** When adding a suffix that begins with a vowel to a word that ends in a single consonant preceded by a single vowel, double the final consonant (if the final syllable is stressed)
- **Silent-e rule:** When adding a suffix that begins with a vowel to a word that ends in silent e, drop the e
- **Y-to-i rule:** When adding a suffix to a word that ends in a consonant + y, change the y to i (unless the suffix begins with i)
- **Mnemonic:** A memory aid that helps you remember how to spell a difficult word (e.g., "there is a rat in separate")

### Instruction Notes

Say: "We are going to learn five spelling rules today. For each one, I will give you the rule, show examples, and point out the most important exceptions. You will build a Spelling Rules Reference Card as we go."

**Rule 1: Doubling the Final Consonant**

Say: "When you add a suffix that starts with a vowel (-ing, -ed, -er, -est) to a one-syllable word that ends in one consonant after one vowel, double the final consonant."

Write on the board:
- run → run**n**ing (one vowel 'u' + one consonant 'n' → double)
- sit → sit**t**ing
- hop → hop**p**ed
- swim → swim**m**er

Say: "For words with more than one syllable, double only if the stress falls on the last syllable."
- begin → begin**n**ing (stress on 'gin' → double)
- occur → occur**r**ed (stress on 'cur' → double)
- open → opening (stress on 'o-' → do NOT double)
- travel → traveling (stress on 'trav-' → do NOT double — though British English doubles it)

Say: "Do NOT double if the word ends in two consonants (jump → jumping) or if there are two vowels before the final consonant (rain → raining)."

**Rule 2: Dropping Silent E**

Say: "When you add a suffix that begins with a vowel (-ing, -ed, -able, -ous) to a word that ends in silent e, drop the e."

Write on the board:
- hope → hoping (not "hopeing")
- write → writing
- make → making
- love → lovable
- fame → famous

Say: "Keep the e when the suffix begins with a consonant."
- hope → hope**ful** (keep the e)
- care → care**less** (keep the e)
- state → state**ment** (keep the e)

Say: "Major exception: keep the e when dropping it would cause confusion or change pronunciation."
- notice → noticeable (keep the e so the c stays soft — /s/ not /k/)
- courage → courageous (keep the e so the g stays soft — /j/ not /g/)

**Rule 3: Changing Y to I**

Say: "When a word ends in a consonant + y, change the y to i before adding any suffix — unless the suffix starts with i."

Write on the board:
- happy → happi**er**, happi**est**, happi**ness**, happi**ly**
- study → studi**ed**, studi**es** (BUT: study + -ing = study**ing**, not "studiing")
- easy → easi**er**, easi**ly**
- carry → carri**ed**, carri**es** (BUT: carry + -ing = carry**ing**)

Say: "If the word ends in a vowel + y, just add the suffix — no change."
- play → played, playing, player
- enjoy → enjoyed, enjoying, enjoyable

**Rule 4: I Before E**

Say: "The classic rule: i before e, except after c, or when sounding like 'ay' as in neighbor and weigh."

Write on the board:
- **I before E:** believe, achieve, field, chief, piece, thief, brief
- **Except after C:** receive, ceiling, perceive, deceive, receipt, conceive
- **Sounding like 'ay':** neighbor, weigh, freight, vein, reign, sleigh

Say: "This rule has real exceptions — words like 'weird,' 'seize,' 'protein,' 'either,' and 'neither' do not follow it. For these, you need to memorize them individually. But the rule works for the majority of ie/ei words."

**Rule 5: Adding Prefixes**

Say: "This is the simplest rule: when you add a prefix to a word, do not change the spelling of the original word. Just attach the prefix."

Write on the board:
- un + necessary = unnecessary (double n)
- mis + spell = misspell (double s)
- dis + satisfied = dissatisfied (double s)
- il + legal = illegal (double l)
- re + evaluate = reevaluate (double e)
- un + natural = unnatural (double n)

Say: "Students often misspell these words by dropping one of the doubled letters. Remember: the prefix brings its own letters, and the base word keeps all of its letters."

**Commonly Misspelled Words — Strategies, Not Just Memorization:**

Write on the board:

| Word | Strategy |
|------|----------|
| separate | There is A RAT in sep**a**r**a**te. |
| definitely | It contains the word FINITE — defi**nite**ly. |
| occurrence | Double c, double r — think of "occur" (double r) + "rence." |
| embarrass | Double r, double s — "I turned Really Red and felt So Silly." |
| accommodate | Double c, double m — "it can aCCoMModate a lot of letters." |
| necessary | One c, two s's — "one Collar, two Sleeves." |
| calendar | Three a's — c**a**lend**a**r, not "calender." |
| rhythm | No ordinary vowels — rh**y**thm. |

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Applying the Doubling Rule:**
Say: "How do I spell 'occurred'? The base word is 'occur.' It ends in one consonant (r) after one vowel (u), and the stress is on the final syllable (oc-CUR). I am adding -ed, which starts with a vowel. Double the r: o-c-c-u-r-r-e-d."

**Example 2 — Applying Multiple Rules:**
Say: "How do I spell 'unbelievable'? Start with 'believe.' I before e — b-e-l-i-e-v-e. Add the suffix -able (starts with a vowel) — drop the silent e: believable. Add the prefix un- — do not change the base word: unbelievable. Three rules applied in one word."

**Example 3 — Proofreading:**
Write: "The atheletes recieved there trophys at the cermony."

Say: "I see four errors. 'Atheletes' — the correct spelling is 'athletes' (ath-letes, not ath-e-letes). 'Recieved' — after c, it is ei: 'received.' 'There' — this should be 'their' (possessive, not location). 'Trophys' — consonant + y, so change y to i and add -es: 'trophies.' 'Cermony' — the correct spelling is 'ceremony' (cer-e-mo-ny). Corrected: 'The athletes received their trophies at the ceremony.'"

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply spelling rules with facilitator support and practice proofreading.

**Part A — Apply the Rules:** Spell each word correctly by applying the appropriate rule. Name the rule you used.

1. hope + -ing = ?
2. run + -er = ?
3. happy + -ness = ?
4. un + noticed = ?
5. begin + -ing = ?
6. courage + -ous = ?
7. beauty + -ful = ?
8. forget + -able = ?

**Part B — Proofread:** Find and correct the spelling errors in each sentence.

9. "The commitee definately needs to reveiw the scedule before the assembly."
10. "She was truely embarassed when she mispelled the word in front of the class."

Work through items 1-3 and item 9 together on the board. For the rest, have students work independently, then compare with a partner.

**Expected Answers — Part A:**
1. **hoping** — Drop silent e before -ing (Rule 2)
2. **runner** — Double the final consonant: one vowel (u) + one consonant (n), one syllable (Rule 1)
3. **happiness** — Change y to i before -ness (consonant + y) (Rule 3)
4. **unnoticed** — Add prefix un- without changing the base word (Rule 5). Note: double n.
5. **beginning** — Double the final consonant: stress on last syllable (be-GIN), one vowel (i) + one consonant (n) (Rule 1)
6. **courageous** — Keep the e so the g stays soft (Exception to Rule 2)
7. **beautiful** — Change y to i before -ful (consonant + y) (Rule 3)
8. **forgettable** — Double the final consonant: stress on last syllable (for-GET), one vowel (e) + one consonant (t) (Rule 1)

**Expected Answers — Part B:**
9. "The **committee** **definitely** needs to **review** the **schedule** before the assembly." (committee: double m, double t; definitely: definite + ly; review: i before e — actually, "review" has "ie" after 'v', which is standard; schedule: sch-e-dule)
10. "She was **truly** **embarrassed** when she **misspelled** the word in front of the class." (truly: drop the e from "true" before -ly — exception because suffix starts with consonant but the e is still dropped; embarrassed: double r, double s; misspelled: mis + spelled = misspelled, double s from prefix rule)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Spell correctly: stop + -ed = ?
2. Spell correctly: believe + -able = ?
3. Find and correct the two spelling errors: "The athelete achived her personal best in the competion."

**Expected Answers:**
1. **stopped** — Double the final consonant: one syllable, one vowel (o) + one consonant (p), suffix starts with a vowel. (Rule 1)
2. **believable** — Drop silent e before -able (suffix starts with a vowel). I before e in "believe." (Rules 2 and 4)
3. Corrected: "The **athlete** **achieved** her personal best in the **competition**." Three errors: "athelete" → "athlete"; "achived" → "achieved" (i before e); "competion" → "competition" (compet-i-tion).

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct, with correct spelling and accurate rule identification.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on one rule at a time — practice the doubling rule with ten words, then the silent-e rule with ten words, before mixing rules
- Use multisensory spelling practice: say the word, spell it aloud, write it, check it
- Create flashcards for the most commonly misspelled words with the mnemonic or rule on the back
- Practice proofreading with only one type of error at a time before mixing error types

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 10, which integrates all grammar and language skills from the unit
- Encourage the student to keep a personal spelling log and add new words throughout the year
- Challenge the student to proofread all future writing assignments for spelling before submitting

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Word Family Exploration:** Choose one of the five spelling rules. Find ten additional words that follow that rule and five that are exceptions. Create a poster or chart showing the pattern and the exceptions.
2. **Spelling Bee Preparation:** Create a study list of 25 challenging words, organized by the spelling rule they follow. Quiz a partner using the list.
3. **Etymology Investigation:** Choose three commonly misspelled words. Research their origins (Latin, Greek, French, etc.) and explain how knowing the word's history can help with spelling. For example, "necessary" comes from Latin "necessarius" — knowing the Latin root explains why there is one c and two s's.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Spelling instruction at the seventh-grade level should focus on patterns and strategies, not isolated memorization. Students who understand the rules can spell unfamiliar words by analogy; students who only memorize word lists are limited to the words they have practiced.
- The five rules in this lesson cover the most productive patterns — they apply to hundreds of common words. However, English spelling has genuine irregularities, and students should know that exceptions exist without feeling defeated by them.
- The "commonly misspelled words" section is important because many of the most frequently misspelled words do not follow simple rules — they require individual attention. The mnemonics and strategies provided give students tools that go beyond rote memorization.
- Be sensitive to students who may have dyslexia or other language-based learning differences that affect spelling. For these students, the rule-based approach is actually more helpful than memorization, but they may need additional time and repetition.
- Connect spelling to credibility in writing: misspelled words in an argumentative essay or a formal letter undermine the writer's authority. This is not about "perfection for its own sake" — it is about the real-world impact of spelling on how readers perceive a writer's competence.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **controlled** — Double the final consonant: stress on last syllable (con-TROL), one vowel (o) + one consonant (l). (Rule 1)
2. **writing** — Drop silent e before -ing. (Rule 2)
3. **funniest** — Change y to i before -est (consonant + y). (Rule 3)
4. **disappear** — Add prefix dis- without changing the base word. (Rule 5)
5. **traveling** — Do NOT double: stress is on the first syllable (TRAV-el), not the last. (Rule 1 — condition not met, so do not double.) Note: "travelling" is accepted in British English.
6. **noticeable** — Keep the e so the c stays soft (/s/ sound). (Exception to Rule 2)
7. **reliable** — Change y to i before -able (consonant + y). (Rule 3)
8. **illogical** — Add prefix il- without changing the base word. Double l from prefix attachment. (Rule 5)
9. Corrected: "The **government** **officials** **believe** the new **environment** **policy** will **benefit** the **community**." Errors: goverment → government (n before m); officals → officials (double f); beleive → believe (i before e); enviroment → environment (n before m); polisy → policy (c, not s); benifit → benefit (e, not i); commmunity → community (double m only, not triple).
10. Corrected: "**Apparently**, the **restaurant** is **permanently** closed **because** of the **unusually** harsh **weather**." Errors: Apparantly → Apparently (ent, not ant); resteraunt → restaurant (au, not au reversed); permanantly → permanently (ent, not ant); becuase → because (a-u-s-e); unusualy → unusually (double l — usual + ly); wether → weather (ea).

### Interleaved Review
11. Two nonstandard features: "done" should be "did" (past tense, not past participle without helping verb), and "real good" should be "really well" ("really" is the adverb form, and "well" is the adverb modifying "did"). Standard English: "The team **did really well** at the tournament last weekend." (L.6.1e)
12. "The new gymnasium**,** which was completed over the summer**,** has a regulation-size basketball court." The clause "which was completed over the summer" is nonrestrictive — it adds extra information about the gymnasium but does not identify which gymnasium. (L.6.2a)
