# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4, Lesson 2

## Topic
Understanding Clauses

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define what a clause is and explain how it differs from a phrase
- Distinguish between independent clauses and dependent clauses
- Identify subordinating conjunctions and relative pronouns that signal dependent clauses

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify subjects and verbs in sentences (Grade 6)
- Understanding of the four phrase types from Lesson 1 (prepositional, appositive, participial, infinitive)
- Familiarity with coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) from Grade 6 sentence variety work (L.6.3a)
- Experience recognizing complete sentences versus fragments (Grade 5-6)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils or highlighters (blue for independent clauses, green for dependent clauses)
- Index cards for clause-sorting activity
- Subordinating Conjunction Reference List

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the phrase-versus-clause distinction and activate prior knowledge of complete thoughts.

**Activity: Complete or Incomplete?**

Say: "Yesterday we learned about phrases — groups of words that do not contain a subject-verb pair working together. Today we are looking at a different kind of word group. For each item below, write 'complete thought' or 'incomplete thought' in your notebook."

Write on the board:
1. The thunderstorm knocked out the power.
2. Because the thunderstorm knocked out the power.
3. When the bus finally arrived.
4. The bus finally arrived.
5. Although she studied for hours.
6. She studied for hours.

After two minutes, review as a class.

Say: "Items 1, 4, and 6 are complete thoughts — they could stand alone as sentences. Items 2, 3, and 5 feel unfinished, like you are waiting for more. Notice something interesting: items 2 and 1 have the exact same subject and verb, but item 2 has an extra word at the beginning — 'because.' That single word changes everything. Today we are going to learn why."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the two clause types, the role of subordinating conjunctions and relative pronouns, and strategies for identification.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Clause:** A group of words that contains a subject and a verb
- **Independent clause:** A clause that expresses a complete thought and can stand alone as a sentence
- **Dependent clause (subordinate clause):** A clause that contains a subject and a verb but does not express a complete thought; it depends on an independent clause to make sense
- **Subordinating conjunction:** A word that introduces a dependent clause and shows the relationship between the dependent clause and the independent clause (because, although, when, while, if, since, unless, after, before, until)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Let me start with the big picture. Both phrases and clauses are groups of words, but a clause always has a subject and a verb working together. A phrase does not."

Write on the board:
- **Phrase:** *Running toward the bus stop* (no subject performing the action)
- **Clause:** *She was running toward the bus stop* (subject "she" + verb "was running")

Say: "Now, clauses come in two types: independent and dependent. An independent clause is a complete thought — it can be a sentence all by itself. A dependent clause has a subject and a verb, but it starts with a special word that makes it incomplete. It depends on an independent clause to finish the thought."

**Identifying Independent Clauses:**

Say: "An independent clause needs only two things: a subject, a verb, and a complete thought. If you can put a period at the end and it sounds like a real sentence, it is independent."

Write on the board:
- The science fair starts next Friday. (Subject: "science fair." Verb: "starts." Complete thought? Yes.)
- My lab partner designed an incredible experiment. (Subject: "lab partner." Verb: "designed." Complete thought? Yes.)

**Identifying Dependent Clauses:**

Say: "A dependent clause has a subject and a verb, but it begins with a subordinating conjunction or a relative pronoun that makes it incomplete. Here are the most common subordinating conjunctions."

Write on the board:

**Common Subordinating Conjunctions:**
| Time | Cause/Effect | Contrast | Condition |
|------|-------------|----------|-----------|
| when, while, after, before, until, since, as | because, since, so that, in order that | although, even though, though, whereas, while | if, unless, provided that, as long as |

Say: "Relative pronouns can also introduce dependent clauses: who, whom, whose, which, that. These create clauses that describe nouns — we call them relative clauses."

Write on the board:
- *Because the gym was flooded*, practice was moved outdoors. ("Because" makes this dependent.)
- The student *who won the spelling bee* is in my homeroom. ("Who" introduces a relative clause describing "student.")
- *Although the test was difficult*, most students passed. ("Although" signals contrast.)

**The Dependency Test:**

Say: "Here is a simple test. Read the clause by itself. If it sounds complete — like a sentence — it is independent. If it sounds like something is missing — like you are waiting for more — it is dependent."

Write on the board:
- *After we finished the experiment* — sounds incomplete. Dependent.
- *We finished the experiment* — sounds complete. Independent.
- *That she recommended* — sounds incomplete. Dependent.
- *She recommended the book* — sounds complete. Independent.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:**
Write: "When the final bell rang, students rushed to their lockers."

Say: "I see two groups of words with subject-verb pairs. 'When the final bell rang' — subject 'bell,' verb 'rang.' But it starts with 'when,' a subordinating conjunction. Read it alone: 'When the final bell rang.' Does that sound complete? No — I am waiting for more. This is a dependent clause. 'Students rushed to their lockers' — subject 'students,' verb 'rushed.' Read it alone: 'Students rushed to their lockers.' Complete thought? Yes. This is an independent clause."

**Example 2:**
Write: "The coach praised the player who scored the tying goal in the final minute."

Say: "The main clause is 'The coach praised the player' — independent. Then I see 'who scored the tying goal in the final minute.' 'Who' is a relative pronoun, 'scored' is the verb. This is a dependent clause that describes 'the player.' Notice it is embedded inside the sentence, not at the beginning."

**Example 3:**
Write: "Although Marcus studied until midnight, he still felt nervous before the exam."

Say: "'Although Marcus studied until midnight' — subject 'Marcus,' verb 'studied,' but it starts with 'although.' Dependent. 'He still felt nervous before the exam' — subject 'he,' verb 'felt.' Independent. The dependent clause comes first, followed by a comma, then the independent clause."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students identify and classify clauses with facilitator support and immediate feedback.

Say: "For each sentence below, do three things: (1) find and underline the independent clause in blue, (2) find and underline the dependent clause in green, and (3) identify the subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun that begins the dependent clause."

1. Because the library was closed, we studied at a coffee shop instead.
2. The experiment failed even though we followed every step carefully.
3. If you finish your homework early, you may read your independent reading book.
4. The teacher who sponsors the robotics club announced a new competition.
5. While the rest of the team warmed up, the captain reviewed the game plan.
6. The documentary that we watched in science class explained how coral reefs form.
7. Unless the weather improves by tomorrow, the field trip will be postponed.
8. She earned the highest score because she practiced every single day.

Work through items 1-3 together on the board, marking each clause type and circling the subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun. For items 4-8, have students work independently and compare with a partner before reviewing.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Dependent: "Because the library was closed" (because). Independent: "we studied at a coffee shop instead."
2. Independent: "The experiment failed." Dependent: "even though we followed every step carefully" (even though).
3. Dependent: "If you finish your homework early" (if). Independent: "you may read your independent reading book."
4. Independent: "The teacher announced a new competition." Dependent: "who sponsors the robotics club" (who) — embedded relative clause describing "teacher."
5. Dependent: "While the rest of the team warmed up" (while). Independent: "the captain reviewed the game plan."
6. Independent: "The documentary explained how coral reefs form." Dependent: "that we watched in science class" (that) — embedded relative clause describing "documentary." Note: "how coral reefs form" is also a dependent clause acting as a noun (what the documentary explained).
7. Dependent: "Unless the weather improves by tomorrow" (unless). Independent: "the field trip will be postponed."
8. Independent: "She earned the highest score." Dependent: "because she practiced every single day" (because).

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to identify independent and dependent clauses and the words that signal them.

**Directions:** For items 1-8, identify each clause as independent (IND) or dependent (DEP), and circle the subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun in each dependent clause. For items 9-10, write original sentences as directed.

1. Before the concert started
2. The audience cheered loudly
3. Although the hike was challenging
4. Everyone reached the summit by noon
5. The poem that Maya shared with the class moved several students to tears. (Find both clauses.)
6. Since we had a substitute teacher, the class worked on independent projects.
7. The students organized a food drive after they learned about local hunger statistics.
8. Unless you have a hall pass, you cannot leave the classroom during the period.
9. Write a sentence that begins with a dependent clause starting with "while." Follow it with an independent clause.
10. Write a sentence that contains a relative clause beginning with "who."

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-8.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-10.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-10, then write a short paragraph (3-4 sentences) about a time you faced a challenge. Include at least two dependent clauses. Underline them in green and circle the subordinating conjunctions.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 6 review — L.6.2a) Add commas, parentheses, or dashes to set off the nonrestrictive element: "The annual talent show always a highlight of the spring semester will be held in the new auditorium this year."
12. (Grade 6 review — L.6.1c) Choose the correct pronoun: "Each of the students must submit (his or her / their) project by Friday." Note: Both options are acceptable in modern usage — explain your choice.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Identify whether each clause is independent or dependent: (a) "After she finished the lab report" (b) "The results surprised everyone"
2. What word makes clause (a) dependent? What would happen if you removed it?
3. Write one sentence that contains both an independent clause and a dependent clause. Label each.

**Expected Answers:**
1. (a) **Dependent** — it starts with "after" and does not express a complete thought. (b) **Independent** — it expresses a complete thought and can stand alone.
2. "After" is the subordinating conjunction. If you remove it, the clause becomes "She finished the lab report," which is an independent clause — a complete thought.
3. Answers vary. Example: "[When the bell rang] (DEP), [the students gathered their materials] (IND)."

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct, with accurate clause identification and understanding of the subordinating conjunction's role.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the "dependency test": have the student read each clause aloud in isolation and decide whether it sounds complete or like something is missing
- Provide the Subordinating Conjunction Reference List and practice circling these signal words in sentences
- Start with sentences where the dependent clause comes first (comma makes the boundary visible) before moving to sentences where it comes second or is embedded
- Use physical manipulation: write clauses on index cards and have the student physically sort them into "can stand alone" and "needs more"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 3, which explores how phrases and clauses function within sentences to modify, restrict, or expand meaning
- Encourage the student to notice dependent and independent clauses in their own reading

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Clause Detective:** Find five sentences in a book or article that contain dependent clauses. Copy them, underline the dependent clause, and identify the subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun.
2. **Clause Conversion:** Take five independent clauses and turn each one into a dependent clause by adding a subordinating conjunction. Then add an independent clause to complete each sentence.
3. **Subordinating Conjunction Collection:** Create a personal reference card with all the subordinating conjunctions you can find, organized by the relationship they signal (time, cause, contrast, condition). Add to it throughout the unit.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common student error is confusing phrases and clauses. Reinforce the key test: does the word group have a subject and a verb working together? If yes, it is a clause. If no, it is a phrase.
- Students sometimes struggle with relative clauses (who, which, that) because these are embedded inside the independent clause rather than appearing at the beginning or end. Sentence diagramming or bracket notation can help make the structure visible.
- Be aware that "since" and "while" can function as either subordinating conjunctions (introducing dependent clauses) or as other parts of speech. In this lesson, focus on their subordinating function.
- The term "dependent clause" and "subordinate clause" mean the same thing. Use both terms so students encounter both, but be consistent about which you use more frequently.
- When reviewing item 12 in interleaved review, handle the singular "they" discussion with care. Both "his or her" and "their" are widely accepted. The key point is that the pronoun must refer back to a singular indefinite antecedent, and students should know both options exist.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **DEP** — "Before" is a subordinating conjunction; the clause does not express a complete thought.
2. **IND** — Complete thought; can stand alone as a sentence.
3. **DEP** — "Although" is a subordinating conjunction; the clause does not express a complete thought.
4. **IND** — Complete thought; can stand alone as a sentence.
5. Independent clause: "The poem moved several students to tears." Dependent clause: "that Maya shared with the class" — relative clause introduced by "that," describes "the poem."
6. Dependent: "Since we had a substitute teacher" (since). Independent: "the class worked on independent projects."
7. Independent: "The students organized a food drive." Dependent: "after they learned about local hunger statistics" (after).
8. Dependent: "Unless you have a hall pass" (unless). Independent: "you cannot leave the classroom during the period."
9. Answers vary. Example: "While the team practiced free throws, the coach reviewed game footage on the sideline." Dependent clause: "While the team practiced free throws." Independent clause: "the coach reviewed game footage on the sideline."
10. Answers vary. Example: "The student who volunteers at the animal shelter every weekend won the community service award." Relative clause: "who volunteers at the animal shelter every weekend."

### Interleaved Review
11. Acceptable punctuation: "The annual talent show**,** always a highlight of the spring semester**,** will be held in the new auditorium this year." OR "The annual talent show**—**always a highlight of the spring semester**—**will be held in the new auditorium this year." The phrase "always a highlight of the spring semester" is a nonrestrictive appositive and must be set off from the rest of the sentence. (L.6.2a)
12. Both are correct. "His or her" is the traditional formal choice for agreement with the singular indefinite "each." "Their" is the accepted modern choice for singular indefinite reference (singular "they"). The important point is recognizing that the antecedent is "each" (singular), so the pronoun must account for that. (L.6.1c)
