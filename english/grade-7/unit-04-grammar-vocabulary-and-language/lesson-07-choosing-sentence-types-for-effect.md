# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4, Lesson 7

## Topic
Choosing Sentence Types for Effect

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Analyze how different sentence types create different effects on the reader (emphasis, pacing, clarity, sophistication)
- Choose among simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex sentences to match purpose and audience
- Revise a passage by deliberately varying sentence types to improve its impact

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify all four sentence types: simple, compound, complex, compound-complex (Lessons 4-6)
- Understanding of how subordination shifts emphasis (Lesson 5)
- Experience varying sentence patterns for meaning and style (L.6.3a)
- Familiarity with audience and purpose from argumentative writing (Unit 3)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example passages (before-and-after revisions)
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (one color per sentence type)
- Copies of a short student writing sample for revision practice
- Sentence Type Flowchart (completed in Lessons 4-6)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate that sentence type choice affects the reader's experience, even when the content stays the same.

**Activity: Same Story, Different Feel**

Say: "I am going to show you two versions of the same paragraph. Read both silently. Then write one sentence in your notebook describing how the two versions feel different."

Write on the board:

**Version A:**
"The gymnasium was silent. The scoreboard showed a tie. The clock showed three seconds left. The point guard dribbled. She looked at the basket. She shot the ball. It went in. The crowd exploded."

**Version B:**
"Although the scoreboard showed a tie with only three seconds remaining, the gymnasium was eerily silent. The point guard dribbled once, and she looked at the basket. She shot. The ball arced through the air, and when it dropped through the net, the crowd exploded."

After two minutes, discuss.

Say: "Version A uses only simple sentences. It creates a choppy, rapid-fire effect — almost like a heartbeat. Version B mixes all four types. It builds tension with a compound-complex opener, keeps some quick pacing with a short simple sentence ('She shot.'), and resolves with a compound-complex sentence that captures the moment of release. Neither version is wrong — each creates a different reading experience. Today we learn how to make these choices deliberately."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to match sentence type to purpose, audience, and desired effect.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Pacing:** The speed at which a reader moves through a text; short simple sentences speed up pacing; longer compound-complex sentences slow it down
- **Rhetorical effect:** The impact a writing choice has on the reader's understanding, emotions, or response
- **Sentence variety:** Using a deliberate mix of sentence types to create rhythm, avoid monotony, and guide the reader's attention
- **Purpose-driven structure:** Choosing a sentence type not randomly but because it serves the writer's goal for that particular moment in the text

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Every sentence type has strengths. A skilled writer chooses the type that does the best job in each moment. Let me show you when each type shines."

**Simple Sentences — For Emphasis, Clarity, and Impact:**

Say: "A simple sentence is short and direct. When you place one after a series of longer sentences, it hits like an exclamation point. It is also ideal when you want the reader to focus on one idea with no distractions."

Write on the board:
- After months of grueling practice, after setbacks and injuries and moments of doubt, after everything that tried to stop her — she made the team. (The short final clause delivers the payoff.)
- The evidence is clear. (In an argument, this short declaration carries authority.)

**Compound Sentences — For Showing Equal Ideas and Balance:**

Say: "Use compound sentences when two ideas deserve equal weight — comparisons, parallel events, or balanced alternatives."

Write on the board:
- The debate team argued passionately, and the judges listened intently. (Both actions matter equally.)
- You can volunteer at the shelter on Saturday, or you can help with the food drive on Sunday. (Balanced alternatives.)

**Complex Sentences — For Showing Cause, Condition, Time, and Emphasis:**

Say: "Use complex sentences when one idea provides context for another. The independent clause gets the emphasis; the dependent clause provides the background."

Write on the board:
- Because she understood the material deeply, she could explain it to others in her own words. (Emphasis on the ability to explain; the understanding is the reason.)
- Although the project required weeks of research, the final product was worth every hour. (Emphasis on the payoff; the effort is acknowledged but subordinated.)

**Compound-Complex Sentences — For Layered, Nuanced Ideas:**

Say: "Use compound-complex sentences when you need to express multiple connected ideas with different relationships — for example, a condition that leads to two related outcomes."

Write on the board:
- When the school board approved the new budget, the music program received additional funding, and the drama department was able to hire a professional set designer. (One condition, two related results.)

**The Danger of Overusing Any Type:**

Say: "Any sentence type becomes monotonous if overused. A paragraph of all simple sentences sounds choppy and childish. A paragraph of all compound-complex sentences sounds dense and exhausting. The goal is variety — mixing types to create rhythm and keep the reader engaged."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Revising for Effect:**
Write the original passage:
"The art show opened on Friday evening. Many students displayed their work. Parents came to see the exhibits. The principal gave a short speech. Everyone enjoyed the refreshments."

Say: "This is all simple sentences — informative but flat. Let me revise for variety and effect."

Write the revision:
"When the art show opened on Friday evening, the hallways of Franklin Middle School were transformed into a gallery. Many students displayed their work, and parents moved from exhibit to exhibit, admiring the creativity on display. Although the principal's speech was brief, she made a point that resonated with everyone: art matters. The refreshments disappeared quickly."

Say: "I used a complex sentence to set the scene, a compound sentence to show the parallel actions of students and parents, a compound-complex sentence to deliver the principal's message with emphasis, and a short simple sentence for a light, humorous ending. Each type serves a purpose."

**Example 2 — Matching Structure to Argument:**
Say: "In argumentative writing, sentence structure helps persuade."

Write:
- "Some people argue that recess is a waste of instructional time. They are wrong." (Complex thought followed by a blunt simple sentence for impact.)
- "Although opponents claim that extended recess reduces academic time, research consistently shows that physical activity improves focus, and students who take regular breaks perform better on standardized tests." (Compound-complex: acknowledges the opposition, then delivers two pieces of evidence.)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students analyze sentence type choices in a passage and practice revising for effect.

**Part A — Analyze:** Read the following paragraph. For each numbered sentence, identify its type and explain why the author might have chosen that structure.

"(1) When the judges announced the results of the regional science fair, the auditorium fell completely silent. (2) Maya held her breath. (3) Her project on water purification had taken three months of research, and she had rebuilt her filtration model twice after the first version failed. (4) The head judge stepped to the microphone. (5) Although Maya's hands were trembling, she kept her eyes fixed on the stage, and she forced herself to smile. (6) First place went to Maya Hernandez."

**Part B — Revise:** The following paragraph uses only simple sentences. Rewrite it using a mix of at least three different sentence types. Keep all the original information.

"The debate team traveled to the state capitol. They competed against twelve other schools. The first round was easy. The second round was much harder. Our team won in the final round. Everyone was exhausted but proud."

Work through Part A items 1-2 and discuss Part B strategies together. For the rest, have students work independently.

**Expected Answers — Part A:**
1. **Complex** — Sets the scene with a time clause ("When the judges announced..."). The dependent clause builds anticipation; the independent clause delivers the moment of silence.
2. **Simple** — Short and tense. Slows the reader down to a single focused moment. Creates suspense.
3. **Compound** — Shows two equally important efforts (research and rebuilding) connected by "and." Gives both contributions equal weight.
4. **Simple** — Quick action sentence. Keeps the pace moving and focuses on a single visual moment.
5. **Compound-complex** — Layers Maya's physical reaction (trembling hands — dependent clause), her determination (eyes fixed — first independent), and her composure (forced smile — second independent). One sentence captures three simultaneous experiences.
6. **Simple** — The climactic moment delivered in the shortest sentence of the paragraph. Maximum impact through contrast with the longer sentences before it.

**Expected Answers — Part B:**
Answers vary. Example: "After the debate team traveled to the state capitol, they competed against twelve other schools in a grueling tournament. The first round was easy, but the second round was much harder. Although the competition was fierce, our team won in the final round. Everyone was exhausted but proud."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to choose sentence types deliberately and revise writing for effect.

**Directions:** For items 1-4, read each scenario and write 1-2 sentences using the sentence type that would be most effective. Explain your choice. For items 5-8, revise each passage by varying the sentence types.

1. **Scenario:** You are writing an argument essay about the importance of school libraries. You want to deliver your thesis statement with maximum impact. (Write the thesis.)
2. **Scenario:** You are describing two equally important discoveries made during a science experiment. (Write the sentence about both discoveries.)
3. **Scenario:** You are writing a narrative about a soccer game. The winning goal has just been scored, and you want the reader to feel the sudden, dramatic moment. (Write the moment of the goal.)
4. **Scenario:** You are writing an informational paragraph about how volcanoes form. You need to explain that magma rises because of pressure, and when it reaches the surface, it erupts as lava and the volcano grows. (Write 1-2 sentences.)
5. **Revise this passage (all simple sentences):** "The hallway was crowded. Students rushed to their lockers. The bell was about to ring. Nobody wanted to be late. The new attendance policy was strict."
6. **Revise this passage (all compound sentences):** "The team practiced every day after school, and they studied the opponent's strategies. The coach designed new plays, and the players memorized every formation. The game was on Friday, and everyone felt ready."
7. Write a four-sentence paragraph about your favorite book, movie, or show. Use each sentence type exactly once (simple, compound, complex, compound-complex). Label each sentence.
8. Look at the paragraph you wrote for item 7. Write 2-3 sentences explaining why you placed each sentence type where you did — what effect were you trying to create?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-6.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-8.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-8, then choose a paragraph from a recent writing assignment. Analyze the sentence types you used. Revise the paragraph to improve variety and effect. Write a brief reflection comparing the original and revised versions.

### Interleaved Review
9. (Grade 6 review — L.6.1c) Correct the agreement error: "The group of volunteers are meeting at the community center at nine o'clock."
10. (Grade 6 review — L.6.2a) Add dashes to set off the parenthetical element for dramatic emphasis: "The final score a stunning 98 to 97 was displayed on the scoreboard as the crowd went wild."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Which sentence type would be most effective for delivering a surprising or dramatic moment? Why?
2. Revise this pair of sentences into a single compound-complex sentence: "The rain started during the outdoor assembly. The students ran inside. The principal continued the announcements over the intercom."

**Expected Answers:**
1. A **simple sentence** is most effective for a surprising or dramatic moment because its brevity creates emphasis and impact. After longer, more complex sentences, a short simple sentence stops the reader and forces them to focus on one powerful idea.
2. "**When** the rain started during the outdoor assembly**,** the students ran inside**,** **and** the principal continued the announcements over the intercom." (Compound-complex: the rain is the condition/cause, and the two responses — students running in and principal switching to intercom — are the coordinated results.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 items correct, with clear reasoning about effect and correct sentence construction.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the "strengths" of each sentence type with additional examples
- Provide a "Sentence Type Purpose Chart" that lists each type with its best use cases
- Practice revising one paragraph at a time, changing only one or two sentences per revision to make the process less overwhelming
- Read revised passages aloud — hearing the rhythm helps students understand why variety matters

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 8, which focuses on commas with coordinate adjectives
- Encourage the student to apply sentence type variety in all future writing assignments, not just grammar exercises

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Author Study:** Choose a passage from a novel or nonfiction book you admire. Identify the sentence type of every sentence. Create a chart showing the pattern. Write a brief analysis: how does the author use sentence variety to create pacing, emphasis, or mood?
2. **Revision Workshop:** Exchange a piece of writing with a partner. Each person identifies the sentence types used, suggests one place where a different type would be more effective, and explains why.
3. **Sentence Type Soundscape:** Read two versions of the same paragraph aloud — one with no variety (all simple) and one with deliberate variety. Record yourself reading both. Listen to the recordings and describe the difference in rhythm, pacing, and engagement.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is the culmination of the sentence type sequence (Lessons 4-7). It shifts from identification and construction to rhetorical choice — the highest level of understanding. Not all students will master rhetorical analysis immediately; the goal is to plant the seed that sentence structure is a writer's tool, not just a grammar rule.
- The warm-up comparison (Version A vs. Version B) is the anchor for this entire lesson. Return to it if students struggle with the concept of sentence type as a rhetorical choice.
- When students revise passages, resist the urge to prescribe specific sentence types. There is no single "correct" revision — the goal is deliberate choice, not a formula. Accept any revision where the student can explain why they chose the structures they did.
- Connect explicitly to Unit 3 (argumentative writing): skilled argument writing uses sentence variety to guide the reader, give emphasis to key claims, and make transitions feel natural. If students revised argumentative essays in Unit 3, reference that experience here.
- Some students may default to compound-complex sentences because they believe longer = better. Gently redirect: the goal is variety and purpose, not length. Sometimes the most powerful sentence in a paragraph is the shortest one.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Answers vary. A strong thesis using a **simple sentence** for impact: "Every school in the district needs a fully funded library." Using a **complex sentence** for a qualified claim: "Because literacy is the foundation of all academic success, every school in the district needs a fully funded library." Both choices are valid with explanation.
2. Answers vary. A **compound sentence** showing equal importance: "The experiment revealed that salt water conducts electricity more efficiently than fresh water, and it also demonstrated that temperature significantly affects conductivity."
3. Answers vary. A **simple sentence** for dramatic impact: "The ball hit the back of the net." A well-placed short sentence after description of the buildup creates the most dramatic effect.
4. Answers vary. A **compound-complex sentence** for layered explanation: "Because intense pressure forces magma upward through the earth's crust, the molten rock eventually reaches the surface, and it erupts as lava that builds the volcanic cone over time."
5. Answers vary. Example: "Because the new attendance policy was strict, nobody wanted to be late. The hallway was crowded, and students rushed to their lockers as the bell was about to ring." (Complex + compound-complex, reducing five choppy sentences to two flowing ones.)
6. Answers vary. Example: "Because the game was on Friday, the team practiced every day after school, studying the opponent's strategies with relentless focus. The coach designed new plays, and the players memorized every formation. Everyone felt ready." (Complex opener, compound middle, simple closer.)
7-8. Answers vary. Evaluate based on correct sentence type identification and thoughtful explanation of rhetorical choices.

### Interleaved Review
9. Corrected: "The group of volunteers **is** meeting at the community center at nine o'clock." The subject is "group" (singular collective noun acting as a unit), not "volunteers" (object of the preposition "of"). (L.6.1c)
10. "The final score**—**a stunning 98 to 97**—**was displayed on the scoreboard as the crowd went wild." Dashes set off the parenthetical element "a stunning 98 to 97" with dramatic emphasis. (L.6.2a)
