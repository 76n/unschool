# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4, Lesson 5

## Topic
Complex Sentences

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define and identify complex sentences (one independent clause + one or more dependent clauses)
- Explain how subordinate clauses affect the meaning and emphasis of a sentence
- Correctly punctuate complex sentences, including the comma rule when a dependent clause begins the sentence

## Prerequisites
- Ability to distinguish independent and dependent clauses (Lesson 2)
- Understanding of subordinating conjunctions and relative pronouns (Lesson 2)
- Ability to identify and build compound sentences (Lesson 4)
- Familiarity with how clause placement affects emphasis (Lesson 3)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (blue for independent clauses, green for dependent clauses)
- Subordinating Conjunction Reference List (from Lesson 2)
- Sentence Type Flowchart (ongoing from Lesson 4)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Contrast compound and complex structures and set up the concept of subordination.

**Activity: Equal or Unequal?**

Say: "In a compound sentence, two ideas are joined as equals — they have equal grammatical weight. But sometimes one idea is the main point and the other is background, a condition, a reason, or a time frame. Read each pair of sentences below and decide: are the two ideas equal partners, or is one the main idea and the other supporting?"

Write on the board:
1. (a) The fire alarm went off, and the students evacuated the building.
   (b) When the fire alarm went off, the students evacuated the building.
2. (a) I studied for hours, but I still struggled with the last section.
   (b) Although I studied for hours, I still struggled with the last section.

After two minutes, discuss.

Say: "In the (a) versions, both ideas get equal billing — compound sentences. In the (b) versions, one idea is promoted to the main event and the other is made into a supporting detail. Version (b) uses subordination — it makes one clause dependent on the other. That is a complex sentence, and it gives you much more control over emphasis and meaning. Today we learn how to build and use them."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define complex sentences, teach punctuation rules, and demonstrate how subordination affects meaning and emphasis.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Complex sentence:** A sentence that contains one independent clause and at least one dependent clause
- **Subordination:** The grammatical technique of making one idea less prominent by placing it in a dependent clause, while the main idea stays in the independent clause
- **Emphasis:** The stress or importance given to an idea based on its position and grammatical role in a sentence
- **Adverb clause:** A dependent clause that modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb; begins with a subordinating conjunction (when, because, although, if, etc.)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "A complex sentence has exactly one independent clause and one or more dependent clauses. The independent clause carries the main idea. The dependent clause provides context — time, reason, contrast, condition."

Write on the board:
- **Complex:** *Because the river flooded the trail*, the park rangers closed the hiking paths.
  - Dependent clause: "Because the river flooded the trail" (reason)
  - Independent clause: "the park rangers closed the hiking paths" (main idea)

Say: "Compare that to a compound version: 'The river flooded the trail, and the park rangers closed the hiking paths.' In the compound version, both ideas feel equally important. In the complex version, the flooding is the background reason and the closure is the main event."

**Punctuation Rule:**

Say: "Here is the punctuation rule for complex sentences, and it is beautifully simple."

Write on the board:
- **Dependent clause FIRST → use a comma:** *When the bell rings*, gather your materials and line up.
- **Independent clause FIRST → usually no comma:** Gather your materials and line up *when the bell rings*.

Say: "When the dependent clause comes first, it is an introductory element, and a comma separates it from the main clause. When the independent clause comes first, no comma is usually needed — the subordinating conjunction serves as the transition."

**Exception — Nonrestrictive Relative Clauses:**

Say: "Remember from Lesson 3: nonrestrictive relative clauses always get commas, regardless of position."

Write on the board:
- The science museum, *which opened last spring*, has a new exhibit on climate change. (Commas — nonrestrictive, extra information)
- The museum *that opened last spring* is on Oak Street. (No commas — restrictive, identifies which museum)

**How Subordination Affects Meaning:**

Say: "The same two ideas can create different effects depending on which one you subordinate. Watch."

Write on the board:
- (a) *Although the test was extremely difficult*, every student in the class passed. (Emphasis on passing despite difficulty — an achievement story)
- (b) *Although every student in the class passed*, the test was extremely difficult. (Emphasis on difficulty despite passing — a warning story)
- (c) *Because she practiced her speech twenty times*, Leah delivered it without a single stumble. (Emphasis on the result — confident delivery)
- (d) *Because Leah delivered her speech without a single stumble*, the audience assumed she was a natural public speaker. (The delivery is now background; the audience's assumption is the main idea)

Say: "The idea in the independent clause gets the spotlight. The idea in the dependent clause becomes the supporting player. As a writer, you choose which idea to emphasize by choosing which clause is independent."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Identifying a Complex Sentence:**
Write: "The hiking group turned back before the storm reached the mountain pass."

Say: "I see 'the hiking group turned back' — subject 'group,' verb 'turned back,' complete thought. Independent clause. Then 'before the storm reached the mountain pass' — starts with 'before' (subordinating conjunction), has a subject 'storm' and verb 'reached,' but is not a complete thought on its own. Dependent clause. One independent + one dependent = complex sentence. The independent clause comes first, so no comma is needed."

**Example 2 — Building a Complex Sentence:**
Write two ideas: "The power went out." "We were in the middle of a group presentation."

Say: "I want to show that the power outage happened during the presentation. I will use 'while' to subordinate the timing: 'While we were in the middle of a group presentation, the power went out.' The outage is the main event; the presentation is the context. Dependent clause first, so I use a comma."

**Example 3 — Shifting Emphasis:**
Write: "Marcus made the winning basket. His teammates rushed to congratulate him."

Say: "Which idea do I want to emphasize? If the main point is the congratulation: 'After Marcus made the winning basket, his teammates rushed to congratulate him.' If the main point is the basket: 'Marcus made the winning basket before his teammates could even react.' Same events, different emphasis — because I chose different independent clauses."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students identify complex sentences, apply punctuation rules, and practice controlling emphasis through subordination.

**Part A — Identify and Punctuate:** Label each sentence as simple (S), compound (CD), or complex (CX). Add any missing commas.

1. Although the cafeteria was crowded we found a table near the window.
2. The science team won the regional competition and advanced to the state finals.
3. If you need extra time on the assignment please speak with the teacher before class.
4. The basketball game went into overtime, but our team pulled ahead in the final seconds.
5. The poem that we analyzed in class yesterday was written during the Harlem Renaissance.
6. Because the forecast predicted heavy snow the school district canceled all after-school activities.

**Part B — Build Complex Sentences:** Combine each pair into a complex sentence. Choose a subordinating conjunction that shows the correct relationship, and punctuate correctly.

7. The lab partners worked efficiently. They finished the experiment ahead of schedule. (Relationship: cause-effect)
8. The school play opens this Friday. The cast has been rehearsing for two months. (Relationship: time/background)

Work through items 1-2 and item 7 together. For the rest, have students work independently, then compare with a partner.

**Expected Answers:**
1. **CX** — "**Although** the cafeteria was crowded**,** we found a table near the window." (Dependent clause first → comma needed.)
2. **S** — One subject ("science team"), compound verb ("won and advanced"). Simple sentence.
3. **CX** — "**If** you need extra time on the assignment**,** please speak with the teacher before class." (Dependent clause first → comma needed.)
4. **CD** — Two independent clauses joined by ", but." Compound sentence.
5. **CX** — "that we analyzed in class yesterday" is a dependent clause (relative, restrictive). No comma needed because it is restrictive and the independent clause comes first.
6. **CX** — "**Because** the forecast predicted heavy snow**,** the school district canceled all after-school activities." (Dependent clause first → comma needed.)
7. "**Because** the lab partners worked efficiently**,** they finished the experiment ahead of schedule." OR "The lab partners finished the experiment ahead of schedule **because** they worked efficiently." (No comma when independent clause comes first.)
8. "**After** the cast had been rehearsing for two months**,** the school play opens this Friday." OR "The school play opens this Friday **after** the cast has been rehearsing for two months." Other subordinating conjunctions (since, now that) also work.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to identify, build, punctuate, and analyze complex sentences independently.

**Directions:** For items 1-4, label each sentence as simple, compound, or complex. Add any missing punctuation. For items 5-8, combine each pair into a complex sentence using the relationship indicated. For items 9-10, follow the specific directions.

1. When the final whistle blew the crowd erupted in celebration.
2. The student council organized a spirit week, and every grade participated.
3. The novel that our teacher assigned for summer reading was over four hundred pages long.
4. Unless the school board approves the budget the new gym equipment will not be ordered.
5. The track meet was postponed. It rained all morning. (cause-effect)
6. She volunteers at the animal shelter every weekend. She wants to become a veterinarian. (reason/motivation)
7. The test results were posted. Several students checked their scores immediately. (time)
8. The documentary was over two hours long. It held the audience's attention throughout. (contrast)
9. Write a complex sentence about a school event where the dependent clause comes first. Punctuate correctly and underline the dependent clause.
10. Take this compound sentence and rewrite it as a complex sentence. Decide which idea to subordinate and explain why: "The gym was being repainted, so the basketball team practiced in the cafeteria."

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-8.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-10.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-10, then write a short paragraph (4-5 sentences) about a time when something did not go as planned. Use at least three complex sentences with different subordinating conjunctions. Underline each dependent clause and explain the relationship it signals (time, cause, contrast, condition).

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 6 review — L.6.1c) Choose the correct pronoun: "Every member of the choir needs to bring (his or her / their) own sheet music to the performance." (Note: both options are acceptable — explain your reasoning.)
12. (Grade 6 review — L.6.1e) This sentence mixes registers: "The experiment's results were totally mind-blowing and exceeded the hypothesized parameters." Rewrite it in a consistent academic register.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Is this sentence compound or complex? "The school delayed its opening because freezing rain coated the roads."
2. Add correct punctuation: "Before the assembly started the principal asked everyone to silence their phones."
3. Rewrite this compound sentence as a complex sentence and explain how the emphasis changes: "The hike was exhausting, but the view from the summit was incredible."

**Expected Answers:**
1. **Complex** — One independent clause ("The school delayed its opening") and one dependent clause ("because freezing rain coated the roads"). The subordinating conjunction "because" creates a complex sentence, not a compound one.
2. "**Before** the assembly started**,** the principal asked everyone to silence their phones." Comma after the introductory dependent clause.
3. "**Although** the hike was exhausting**,** the view from the summit was incredible." By subordinating "the hike was exhausting," the emphasis shifts to the incredible view — the payoff. In the compound version, both ideas had equal weight.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct, with accurate sentence type identification, correct punctuation, and clear explanation of emphasis shift.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the distinction between compound (two equal independent clauses + coordinating conjunction) and complex (one independent + one dependent + subordinating conjunction)
- Practice identifying the subordinating conjunction as the "signal word" for complex sentences
- Return to the comma rule: dependent clause first = comma; independent clause first = usually no comma
- Provide sentence frames: "Although _____________, _____________." and "_____________ because _____________."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 6, which introduces compound-complex sentences
- Encourage the student to look for complex sentences in their own writing and in published texts, paying attention to how authors use subordination to control emphasis

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Emphasis Experiment:** Choose three important events from a story you are reading. For each event, write two complex sentences — one that emphasizes the cause and one that emphasizes the effect. Explain which version is more effective and why.
2. **Sentence Type Collection:** Find examples of simple, compound, and complex sentences in a news article or opinion piece. Copy one of each type, label them, and explain why the author chose that structure for that particular idea.
3. **Subordination Challenge:** Write a paragraph using only complex sentences (no simple or compound sentences). Then write the same paragraph using only simple sentences. Compare the two: how does the reading experience differ?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common error students make is confusing compound and complex sentences. Reinforce the key distinction: compound sentences use coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) to join two equal independent clauses; complex sentences use subordinating conjunctions or relative pronouns to make one clause dependent on the other.
- Some students may resist the "no comma when the independent clause comes first" rule because they hear a pause when they read the sentence aloud. Explain that not every oral pause corresponds to a written comma. The comma rule for complex sentences is structural, not based on pauses.
- When teaching emphasis shifts, use side-by-side comparisons extensively. Students grasp the concept much more readily when they can see two versions of the same idea and discuss which one foregrounds which information.
- This lesson connects directly to the writing students have done in Unit 3 (argumentative essays). Argumentative writing often benefits from complex sentences: "Although some people believe X, the evidence clearly shows Y." Encourage students to find opportunities to use complex sentences in their arguments.
- Update the Sentence Type Flowchart: "One independent clause + one or more dependent clauses = complex."

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Complex** — "**When** the final whistle blew**,** the crowd erupted in celebration." Dependent clause first → comma needed.
2. **Compound** — Two independent clauses joined by ", and."
3. **Complex** — "that our teacher assigned for summer reading" is a restrictive relative clause (dependent). No additional punctuation needed.
4. **Complex** — "**Unless** the school board approves the budget**,** the new gym equipment will not be ordered." Dependent clause first → comma needed.
5. "The track meet was postponed **because** it rained all morning." OR "**Because** it rained all morning**,** the track meet was postponed."
6. "She volunteers at the animal shelter every weekend **because** she wants to become a veterinarian." OR "**Since** she wants to become a veterinarian**,** she volunteers at the animal shelter every weekend."
7. "**When** the test results were posted**,** several students checked their scores immediately." OR "Several students checked their scores immediately **after** the test results were posted."
8. "**Although** the documentary was over two hours long**,** it held the audience's attention throughout." OR "**Even though** the documentary was over two hours long**,** it held the audience's attention throughout."
9. Answers vary. Example: "**Before the talent show began,** the performers gathered backstage to warm up." Dependent clause: "Before the talent show began" — comma used because dependent clause comes first.
10. Complex version: "**Because** the gym was being repainted**,** the basketball team practiced in the cafeteria." Explanation: Subordinating the gym's condition ("because the gym was being repainted") emphasizes the team's adaptation (practicing in the cafeteria) as the main point, rather than giving both ideas equal weight.

### Interleaved Review
11. Both are correct. "His or her" is the traditional formal option that agrees with the singular indefinite "every member." "Their" is the widely accepted modern singular pronoun for indefinite antecedents. The important point is that the pronoun refers back to "every member" (singular). (L.6.1c)
12. Revised: "The experiment's results were remarkable and exceeded the hypothesized parameters." OR "The experiment's results were significant and exceeded the hypothesized parameters." The original mixes informal ("totally mind-blowing") with formal/academic language ("exceeded the hypothesized parameters"). In an academic register, "mind-blowing" should be replaced with a formal equivalent. (L.6.1e)
