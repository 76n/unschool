# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4, Lesson 1

## Topic
Understanding Phrases

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define what a phrase is and explain how it differs from a clause
- Identify and classify four types of phrases: prepositional, appositive, participial, and infinitive
- Explain the function of each phrase type within a sentence (what it modifies or renames)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify subjects, verbs, and objects in sentences (Grade 6)
- Understanding of prepositions and prepositional phrases from earlier grades
- Familiarity with varying sentence patterns for meaning and style (L.6.3a)
- Experience with punctuation for nonrestrictive elements (L.6.2a)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils or highlighters (orange for phrases)
- Index cards for phrase-sorting activity
- Phrase Types Reference Chart (prepositional, appositive, participial, infinitive)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of sentence parts and introduce the concept of phrases as meaningful groups of words.

**Activity: Word Groups**

Say: "I am going to show you six groups of words. For each one, decide: is this a complete sentence, or is it only a piece of a sentence? Write 'complete' or 'piece' in your notebook."

Write on the board:
1. Under the bleachers after the game
2. The relay team won the championship.
3. My best friend, an incredible artist
4. Racing toward the finish line
5. She wanted to join the science club.
6. To improve her free-throw percentage

After two minutes, review as a class.

Say: "Items 2 and 5 are complete sentences — they have a subject and a verb and express a complete thought. The others are pieces — groups of related words that add meaning but cannot stand alone. Those pieces are called phrases. Today we are going to learn four specific types of phrases and discover what each one does inside a sentence."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the four phrase types with clear definitions, examples, and identification strategies.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Phrase:** A group of related words that functions as a single part of speech but does not contain both a subject and a verb working together
- **Prepositional phrase:** A phrase beginning with a preposition and ending with a noun or pronoun (its object); functions as an adjective or adverb
- **Appositive phrase:** A noun phrase placed next to another noun to rename or explain it; usually set off by commas
- **Participial phrase:** A phrase beginning with a present participle (-ing) or past participle (-ed, -en, or irregular form) that functions as an adjective

### Instruction Notes

Say: "A phrase is a group of related words that works together as a unit, but it is missing something that a clause has — we will learn about clauses tomorrow. For now, the key idea is that a phrase does not have a subject doing an action. Let me show you four types."

**Type 1: Prepositional Phrases**

Say: "You already know these from earlier grades. A prepositional phrase starts with a preposition — words like in, on, under, between, during, after, with — and ends with a noun or pronoun called the object of the preposition. These phrases work as adjectives (describing nouns) or adverbs (describing verbs)."

Write on the board:
- *During the thunderstorm*, the soccer game was canceled. (adverb phrase — tells when)
- The book *on the top shelf* belongs to my older brother. (adjective phrase — tells which book)
- We practiced *in the gymnasium* *after school*. (two adverb phrases — tell where and when)

**Type 2: Appositive Phrases**

Say: "An appositive phrase renames or explains a noun right next to it. Think of it as a mini-definition inserted into the sentence. Appositives are usually set off by commas — you practiced this in Grade 6 with nonrestrictive elements."

Write on the board:
- Ms. Rivera, *our seventh-grade science teacher*, assigned a project on ecosystems.
- My hometown, *a small city near the coast*, has an annual kite festival.
- The goalkeeper, *the tallest player on the team*, blocked every shot.

Say: "Notice that if I remove the appositive phrase, the sentence still makes sense: 'Ms. Rivera assigned a project on ecosystems.' The appositive adds useful information, but the sentence does not depend on it."

**Type 3: Participial Phrases**

Say: "A participial phrase begins with a participle — a verb form that acts as an adjective. Present participles end in -ing (running, laughing, studying), and past participles usually end in -ed or -en (excited, broken, amazed). The whole phrase describes a noun."

Write on the board:
- *Sprinting across the field*, Marcus scored the winning goal.
- The homework *assigned on Monday* is due Friday.
- *Exhausted from the relay race*, the runners collapsed on the grass.

Say: "Notice that 'sprinting across the field' describes Marcus — it tells us what he was doing. 'Assigned on Monday' describes the homework. These phrases add vivid detail to sentences."

**Type 4: Infinitive Phrases**

Say: "An infinitive phrase begins with 'to' plus a verb: to run, to study, to become. These phrases can function as nouns, adjectives, or adverbs."

Write on the board:
- *To earn a spot on the varsity team* was her biggest goal this year. (noun — acts as subject)
- She developed a plan *to improve her essay grade*. (adjective — describes "plan")
- The students stayed after school *to rehearse for the play*. (adverb — tells why)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Identifying Phrase Type:**
Write: "Balancing carefully on the beam, the gymnast completed her routine without a single wobble."

Say: "I see the group of words 'Balancing carefully on the beam.' Does it start with a participle? Yes — 'balancing' is a present participle (-ing form). Does it describe a noun? Yes — it describes 'the gymnast.' This is a participial phrase. I also see 'on the beam' inside it — that is a prepositional phrase nested within the participial phrase. And 'without a single wobble' is another prepositional phrase."

**Example 2 — Multiple Phrases in One Sentence:**
Write: "My neighbor, a retired Marine Corps pilot, volunteers at the local animal shelter to walk the dogs every Saturday morning."

Say: "Let me find all the phrases. 'A retired Marine Corps pilot' renames 'my neighbor' — that is an appositive phrase. 'At the local animal shelter' starts with a preposition — prepositional phrase telling where. 'To walk the dogs' begins with 'to' plus a verb — infinitive phrase telling why he volunteers. 'Every Saturday morning' functions as an adverb telling when. One sentence, four phrases — each one adding specific detail."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students identify and classify phrases with facilitator support and immediate feedback.

Say: "For each sentence below, find the underlined phrase, identify its type (prepositional, appositive, participial, or infinitive), and explain what it does in the sentence — what does it modify or rename?"

1. *Hoping to make the honor roll*, Jayden studied every night for two weeks.
2. The mural *on the cafeteria wall* was painted by last year's art class.
3. Our principal, *a former college basketball player*, organized a faculty-versus-students game.
4. The team practiced hard *to prepare for the regional tournament*.
5. *Covered in mud from the obstacle course*, the campers lined up for the hose.
6. The novel *on my nightstand* is the one I chose for my book report.
7. Elena, *the fastest sprinter in our grade*, anchored the relay team.
8. *To finish the group project on time*, we met at the library after school.

Work through items 1-3 together on the board, modeling the identification process explicitly. For items 4-8, have students write answers independently and compare with a partner before reviewing as a class.

**Expected Answers:**
1. "Hoping to make the honor roll" — participial phrase, describes Jayden
2. "on the cafeteria wall" — prepositional phrase (adjective), describes which mural
3. "a former college basketball player" — appositive phrase, renames "our principal"
4. "to prepare for the regional tournament" — infinitive phrase (adverb), tells why they practiced
5. "Covered in mud from the obstacle course" — participial phrase, describes the campers
6. "on my nightstand" — prepositional phrase (adjective), describes which novel
7. "the fastest sprinter in our grade" — appositive phrase, renames Elena
8. "To finish the group project on time" — infinitive phrase (adverb), tells why we met

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to identify, classify, and explain the function of phrases independently.

**Directions:** For items 1-8, identify each underlined phrase by type and explain its function. For items 9-10, write original sentences using the phrase type indicated.

1. The library book *with the torn cover* needs to be replaced.
2. *Grinning from ear to ear*, Sofia accepted the science fair trophy.
3. Mr. Okafor, *our new basketball coach*, held tryouts on Wednesday.
4. *To learn a new language* takes patience and daily practice.
5. The student council met *in the auditorium* *before first period*.
6. *Frustrated by the confusing directions*, the hikers consulted their map.
7. Her dream *to become a marine biologist* inspired her choice of electives.
8. The documentary, *a ninety-minute film about coral reefs*, will be shown during science class.
9. Write a sentence that includes a participial phrase describing a person.
10. Write a sentence that includes an infinitive phrase explaining a purpose.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-8.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-10.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-10, then write a short paragraph (3-4 sentences) about a school event that includes at least one of each phrase type. Underline and label each phrase.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 6 review — L.6.1c) Choose the correct verb: "Neither the captain nor the other players (was / were) ready for the surprise drill."
12. (Grade 6 review — L.6.3a) Rewrite this pair of short, choppy sentences as one varied sentence: "The rain started during lunch. The outdoor pep rally was moved to the gym."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Identify the phrase type: "*Determined to break her personal record*, Amara trained every morning before school."
2. Identify the phrase type and its function: "The trail *through the old-growth forest* is closed for repairs."
3. What is the key difference between a phrase and a clause?

**Expected Answers:**
1. "Determined to break her personal record" is a **participial phrase** (past participle "determined" + modifiers). It describes Amara.
2. "Through the old-growth forest" is a **prepositional phrase** functioning as an adjective — it tells which trail.
3. A phrase is a group of related words that does not contain a subject-verb pair working together; a clause does contain a subject and a verb.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct, with correct identification of phrase type and function.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review each phrase type one at a time with additional examples, focusing on the identifying feature of each (preposition at the start, renaming a noun, -ing/-ed verb form as adjective, "to" + verb)
- Provide a Phrase Types Reference Chart for the student to use during identification
- Practice with simpler sentences that contain only one phrase each before moving to sentences with multiple phrases
- Have the student highlight the first word of each phrase to identify the type (preposition? participle? "to"? noun renaming another noun?)

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 2, which introduces independent and dependent clauses
- Encourage the student to identify phrases in their own writing and in texts they read for other classes

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Phrase Hunt:** Choose a page from a novel or textbook you are currently reading. Find and label at least two examples of each phrase type. Record them in your notebook with the phrase type and function.
2. **Sentence Expansion:** Start with a simple sentence (e.g., "The dog barked.") and expand it by adding one of each phrase type, one at a time. Observe how each phrase adds a new layer of detail.
3. **Phrase-Type Sorting Game:** Write ten phrases on index cards (mixing all four types). Shuffle and sort them into four piles by type. Time yourself and try to beat your own record.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often confuse participial phrases with gerund phrases. At this level, the distinction is straightforward: if the -ing phrase acts as an adjective (describes a noun), it is participial; if it acts as a noun (subject, object), it is a gerund phrase. You do not need to introduce the term "gerund" in this lesson, but be prepared if a student asks.
- Appositives were introduced in Grade 6 in the context of nonrestrictive elements and comma usage (L.6.2a). This lesson reinforces that knowledge while adding the formal term "appositive phrase."
- Some students may struggle to see infinitive phrases because "to" also functions as a preposition. The key distinction: in an infinitive, "to" is followed by a verb (to run, to study); in a prepositional phrase, "to" is followed by a noun (to the store, to my friend).
- Encourage students to think of phrases as "detail-adders." Every phrase in a sentence answers a question: Which one? What kind? When? Where? Why? How? This functional approach helps students understand not just what phrases are but why writers use them.
- When students encounter sentences with multiple phrases, teach them to work from the outside in — identify the main subject and verb first, then find the phrases that add detail around them.
