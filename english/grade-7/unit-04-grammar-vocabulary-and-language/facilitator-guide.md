# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4: Grammar, Vocabulary & Language

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview

Unit 4 deepens students' command of English grammar and conventions by building on the agreement, punctuation, and sentence-variety skills developed in Grade 6. Students begin by learning to identify and analyze phrases (prepositional, appositive, participial, and infinitive) and clauses (independent and dependent), understanding how these building blocks function within sentences to modify, restrict, or expand meaning. With this foundation, students explore the four sentence types — simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex — and learn to choose deliberately among them to signal relationships between ideas and create specific effects for their audience. The unit also addresses two targeted conventions: using commas correctly with coordinate adjectives and applying spelling rules and patterns strategically. Throughout the unit, grammar instruction is rooted in authentic, context-rich sentences drawn from the kinds of texts students encounter in Units 1-3 (literary analysis, informational reading, and argumentative writing). The goal is not grammar as an abstract exercise but grammar as a tool for more powerful reading, writing, and communication.

### Facilitation Tips

- **Build from parts to wholes.** The unit is sequenced intentionally: phrases (smallest units) lead to clauses, which lead to sentence types. Resist the temptation to skip ahead. Students who understand phrases and clauses deeply will master sentence types much more easily.
- **Use color-coding consistently.** Assign specific colors throughout the unit: blue for independent clauses, green for dependent clauses, orange for phrases, red for commas and punctuation marks. This visual system helps students see sentence structure at a glance.
- **Connect grammar to writing purpose.** Every grammar concept should answer the question "Why does this matter for my writing?" When teaching compound-complex sentences, for instance, show students how these structures allow them to express nuanced relationships between ideas in their argumentative essays (Unit 3) or literary analyses (Unit 1).
- **Model thinking aloud.** When analyzing a sentence, narrate every step of your decision-making: "I see the word 'although' — that is a subordinating conjunction, so the clause that follows it must be a dependent clause. Let me find where the dependent clause ends and the independent clause begins."
- **Use mentor sentences from real texts.** Draw example sentences from the novels, articles, and speeches students have encountered in Units 1-3. This reinforces the connection between grammar and authentic reading and writing.
- **Embrace the coordinate adjective decision tree.** For Lesson 8, the two-question test (Can you reverse the adjectives? Can you put "and" between them?) is the single most reliable tool. Practice it until it becomes automatic.
- **Teach spelling through patterns, not memorization.** Lesson 9 focuses on rules and strategies — doubling consonants, dropping silent e, changing y to i — because understanding patterns gives students tools they can apply to unfamiliar words.
- **Leverage interleaved review.** Each lesson includes two review items from Grade 6 language standards (L.6.1c, L.6.1e, L.6.2a, L.6.3a). Use these to reinforce prior learning and help students see how earlier grammar skills connect to current ones.

### Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| Students confuse phrases and clauses, believing any group of words is a clause. | A clause must contain both a subject and a verb. A phrase does not. The phrase "running down the street" has a verb form but no subject performing the action; the clause "who runs down the street" has both a subject ("who") and a verb ("runs"). |
| Students believe that all dependent clauses begin with subordinating conjunctions. | Dependent clauses can also begin with relative pronouns (who, whom, whose, which, that). These are called relative clauses and function as adjectives or nouns within sentences. |
| Students assume compound sentences are always joined by "and." | Compound sentences can use any coordinating conjunction (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so — FANBOYS) or a semicolon. Each conjunction signals a different relationship: "but" signals contrast, "so" signals cause-effect, "or" signals alternatives. |
| Students add commas between all adjectives that appear before a noun. | Only coordinate adjectives (adjectives of the same type/level that independently modify the noun) take commas. Cumulative adjectives — where one adjective modifies the combination of the next adjective plus the noun — do not. Use the reversal and "and" tests to determine which type you have. |
| Students think longer sentences are always more sophisticated. | Sentence sophistication comes from choosing the right type for the right purpose. A short, simple sentence can be more powerful than a compound-complex one when the goal is emphasis or clarity. |
| Students treat spelling as purely arbitrary and believe there are no learnable patterns. | While English spelling has genuine irregularities, many words follow predictable patterns (doubling rules, silent-e dropping, i-before-e). Teaching these patterns gives students strategies for spelling unfamiliar words, rather than requiring rote memorization of every word. |

### Assessment Guidance

The Unit Assessment contains five sections covering all four standards, with a total of 60 points. Students should be assessed after completing all 10 lessons. Administer the assessment in a single sitting of approximately 40-50 minutes.

**Formative Assessment:** Each lesson includes a Check for Understanding (Exit Ticket) with 2-3 quick items. Use these to monitor progress daily and identify students who need additional support before the summative assessment.

**Summative Assessment:** The Unit Assessment uses a mix of multiple-choice, identification, sentence combining, short writing, passage editing, and analysis tasks to assess all four standards.

**Portfolio Option:** Have students collect their best writing samples from the unit — sentences and paragraphs that demonstrate effective use of phrases and clauses, deliberate sentence type choices, correct comma usage with coordinate adjectives, and correct spelling. Students write a brief reflection identifying one grammar skill they improved most and how it affects their writing.

**Mastery Thresholds:**
- **Overall mastery:** 70% (42 out of 60 points)
- **Per-standard minimum:** 50% on each standard section
- Students who meet overall mastery but fall below 50% on any standard should receive targeted re-teaching on that standard before progressing.

### Differentiation at a Glance

**Below Level:**
- Provide a grammar reference card with phrase types, clause types, sentence type definitions, and the coordinate adjective decision tree
- Reduce the number of Independent Practice items (complete odd-numbered items only)
- Offer sentence frames for all production tasks
- Allow oral responses before written ones
- Pair with an on-level peer for guided practice activities
- Use highlighting or underlining to pre-mark key structural elements before asking the student to analyze them

**On Level:**
- Complete all Independent Practice items as written
- Use the standard lesson pacing
- Encourage self-checking with the answer key after independent work
- Use the "Stretch" tier for additional challenge when ready

**Above Level:**
- Assign the Challenge tier in Independent Practice consistently
- Ask students to write paragraphs that deliberately use all four sentence types and explain their choices
- Have students analyze sentence structure choices in published texts (mentor sentences from novels, articles, speeches)
- Encourage students to "teach" a concept to a peer, reinforcing their own understanding
- Provide more complex editing tasks with multiple error types embedded in a single passage

### Connection to Other Units and Future Learning

Unit 4 is designed to work in concert with Units 1-3. The literary analysis skills from Unit 1 and the informational text skills from Unit 2 provide rich source material for grammar analysis — students can examine how professional authors use phrases, clauses, and sentence types to create meaning. The argumentative writing skills from Unit 3 give students an immediate application for grammar choices — selecting sentence structures that strengthen their arguments and guide their readers.

Looking ahead, the phrase and clause knowledge developed in Lessons 1-3 prepares students for Grade 8 work with verbals (gerunds, participles, infinitives) and their functions. The sentence type fluency built in Lessons 4-7 supports the increasingly complex writing expected across all content areas in Grade 8 and beyond. The coordinate adjective and spelling skills from Lessons 8-9 contribute to the overall precision and polish expected in academic writing at the high school level.
