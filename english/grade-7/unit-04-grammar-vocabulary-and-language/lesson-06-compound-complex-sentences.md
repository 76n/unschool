# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4, Lesson 6

## Topic
Compound-Complex Sentences

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define and identify compound-complex sentences (two or more independent clauses + at least one dependent clause)
- Construct compound-complex sentences that express layered relationships among ideas
- Correctly punctuate compound-complex sentences using the rules learned for compound and complex structures

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify independent and dependent clauses (Lesson 2)
- Understanding of compound sentences and their punctuation (Lesson 4)
- Understanding of complex sentences and their punctuation (Lesson 5)
- Familiarity with subordinating conjunctions, coordinating conjunctions, and relative pronouns (Lessons 2, 4, 5)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (blue for independent clauses, green for dependent clauses, red for conjunctions and punctuation)
- Sentence Type Flowchart (ongoing from Lessons 4-5)
- FANBOYS reference card and Subordinating Conjunction Reference List

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate knowledge of all three sentence types learned so far and motivate the need for a fourth type.

**Activity: The Sentence That Needs Both**

Say: "Read the following three ideas. They are all related. Try to express all three in a single sentence."

Write on the board:
- Idea 1: The power went out during the storm.
- Idea 2: The students could not finish the online quiz.
- Idea 3: The teacher gave everyone extra time the next day.

Give students two minutes to attempt this. Then ask volunteers to share their sentences.

Say: "If you wrote something like 'When the power went out during the storm, the students could not finish the online quiz, so the teacher gave everyone extra time the next day,' you created a compound-complex sentence. It has a dependent clause (when the power went out during the storm), and two independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (the students could not finish...so the teacher gave...). This is the most sophisticated sentence type, and today we learn how it works."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define compound-complex sentences, teach identification strategies, and demonstrate construction and punctuation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Compound-complex sentence:** A sentence that contains two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause
- **Layered relationship:** When a sentence expresses multiple connections among ideas — such as time AND cause-effect, or contrast AND addition — requiring both coordination and subordination
- **Coordination:** Joining elements of equal grammatical rank (independent clause + independent clause)
- **Subordination:** Making one idea dependent on another (independent clause + dependent clause)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Let me build the complete picture. We now know four sentence types."

Write on the board:

| Type | Structure | Example |
|------|-----------|---------|
| Simple | 1 independent clause | The team won the game. |
| Compound | 2+ independent clauses | The team won the game, and the fans celebrated. |
| Complex | 1 independent clause + 1+ dependent clause | When the final buzzer sounded, the team won the game. |
| Compound-Complex | 2+ independent clauses + 1+ dependent clause | When the final buzzer sounded, the team won the game, and the fans rushed onto the court. |

Say: "A compound-complex sentence combines both techniques. It uses subordination to provide context or conditions (the dependent clause) and coordination to connect multiple main ideas (the independent clauses). Think of it as a compound sentence with a dependent clause attached — or a complex sentence with an extra independent clause added."

**How to Identify a Compound-Complex Sentence:**

Say: "Use this three-step checklist."

Write on the board:
1. **Count the independent clauses.** Are there two or more? (If only one, it is simple or complex, not compound-complex.)
2. **Find at least one dependent clause.** Is there a clause introduced by a subordinating conjunction or relative pronoun? (If no dependent clause, it is just compound.)
3. **Confirm both are present.** Two or more independent clauses AND at least one dependent clause = compound-complex.

**Punctuation Rules — No New Rules Needed:**

Say: "Here is the good news: you already know all the punctuation rules. A compound-complex sentence simply combines the rules from compound and complex sentences."

Write on the board:
- Dependent clause first → comma after the dependent clause
- Two independent clauses → comma + FANBOYS between them (or semicolon)
- Nonrestrictive elements → commas around them
- Restrictive elements → no commas

**Example with Punctuation Breakdown:**
Write: "Although the rehearsal started late, the choir performed every song on the set list, and the director was pleased with their progress."

Say: "'Although the rehearsal started late' — dependent clause first, so I use a comma. 'The choir performed every song on the set list' — first independent clause. 'And the director was pleased with their progress' — second independent clause, joined by comma + 'and.' All the rules we already know, applied together."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Identifying:**
Write: "Because the science fair deadline was moved up by a week, several students rushed to finish their projects, and the teachers extended library hours to give them extra workspace."

Say: "Step 1 — count independent clauses. 'Several students rushed to finish their projects' — independent. 'The teachers extended library hours to give them extra workspace' — independent. That is two. Step 2 — find a dependent clause. 'Because the science fair deadline was moved up by a week' — starts with 'because,' has a subject and verb, but does not express a complete thought alone. Dependent. Step 3 — two independent clauses and one dependent clause. This is a compound-complex sentence."

**Example 2 — Building from Scratch:**
Write three separate ideas:
- The forecast predicted clear skies.
- The outdoor concert went ahead as planned.
- The organizers had prepared an indoor backup just in case.

Say: "I want to show the relationship: the forecast was the condition, the concert going ahead was the main result, and the backup plan adds a contrasting detail. I will subordinate the forecast: 'Since the forecast predicted clear skies, the outdoor concert went ahead as planned, but the organizers had prepared an indoor backup just in case.' 'Since' creates the dependent clause; 'but' joins the two independent clauses with a contrast."

**Example 3 — Embedded Dependent Clause:**
Write: "The player who scored the winning goal celebrated with her teammates, and the crowd gave the entire team a standing ovation."

Say: "This one is trickier because the dependent clause is embedded inside the first independent clause. 'The player celebrated with her teammates' — first independent clause. 'Who scored the winning goal' — dependent relative clause (adjective, restrictive, no commas) describing 'the player.' 'The crowd gave the entire team a standing ovation' — second independent clause, joined by ', and.' Two independent + one dependent = compound-complex."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students identify compound-complex sentences and practice building them with correct punctuation.

**Part A — Identify:** Label each sentence as simple (S), compound (CD), complex (CX), or compound-complex (CC). Add any missing punctuation.

1. After the assembly ended the students returned to their classrooms and the teachers began the afternoon lessons.
2. The principal announced the new dress code, but several students had questions about the policy.
3. When the championship trophy arrived, the athletes gathered in the gym, and the coach gave a brief speech.
4. The student who designed the school's new logo presented her work to the art department and explained her creative process.
5. Although the reading assignment was long the students finished it on time and they were ready for the class discussion the next morning.

**Part B — Build:** Combine the three ideas into a single compound-complex sentence with correct punctuation.

6. Ideas: The museum was closed for renovations. We visited the botanical garden instead. We enjoyed it more than we expected.
7. Ideas: The essay contest deadline is next Friday. I plan to submit my entry early. My teacher can review it before the deadline.

Work through items 1 and 6 together on the board. For the rest, have students work independently and compare with a partner.

**Expected Answers:**
1. **CC** — "**After** the assembly ended**,** the students returned to their classrooms**,** **and** the teachers began the afternoon lessons." One dependent clause + two independent clauses.
2. **CD** — Two independent clauses joined by ", but." No dependent clause present.
3. **CC** — "**When** the championship trophy arrived**,** the athletes gathered in the gym**,** **and** the coach gave a brief speech." One dependent + two independent.
4. **S** — One subject ("student") with compound verb ("presented and explained") plus a relative clause ("who designed the school's new logo"). Despite the complexity, this has only one independent clause core. The relative clause is dependent, but there is only one independent clause, so this is complex — not compound-complex. (Accept CX.)
5. **CC** — "**Although** the reading assignment was long**,** the students finished it on time**,** **and** they were ready for the class discussion the next morning."
6. "**Because** the museum was closed for renovations**,** we visited the botanical garden instead**,** **and** we enjoyed it more than we expected." (Other subordinating conjunctions like "since" also work.)
7. "**Since** the essay contest deadline is next Friday**,** I plan to submit my entry early**,** **so** my teacher can review it before the deadline." OR "I plan to submit my essay contest entry early **so that** my teacher can review it**,** **and** I will still meet the Friday deadline."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to identify, build, and punctuate compound-complex sentences independently.

**Directions:** For items 1-4, label each sentence by type (S, CD, CX, or CC) and add any missing punctuation. For items 5-8, combine the given ideas into compound-complex sentences. For items 9-10, follow the specific directions.

1. While the band played the school fight song the football players ran onto the field and the cheerleaders performed their halftime routine.
2. The librarian recommended several novels, and I chose the one about a girl who survives alone in the wilderness.
3. If the weather cooperates we will hold the ceremony outdoors.
4. The volunteers painted the community mural on Saturday they cleaned the park on Sunday and the mayor thanked them at a special assembly on Monday.
5. The math test was postponed. The teacher was absent. The substitute assigned a review worksheet instead. (time/cause)
6. The school play received excellent reviews. The lead actress forgot one of her lines. Nobody in the audience noticed. (contrast)
7. You finish your reading log by Thursday. You may choose a free-reading day on Friday. You must also complete the vocabulary worksheet. (condition + addition)
8. The debate team won the regional championship. They had practiced every day after school. The coach rewarded them with a pizza party. (reason + result)
9. Write an original compound-complex sentence about something that happened at school. Underline each independent clause in blue and each dependent clause in green.
10. Explain in 2-3 sentences: why would a writer choose a compound-complex sentence instead of breaking the ideas into separate simple or compound sentences?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-8.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-10.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-10, then find a compound-complex sentence in a book or article. Copy it, identify all the clauses (independent and dependent), and explain why the author chose this structure instead of simpler alternatives.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 6 review — L.6.2a) Punctuate the nonrestrictive element: "The seventh-grade field trip which takes place every October will visit the natural history museum this year."
12. (Grade 6 review — L.6.3a) The following passage uses only simple sentences. Rewrite it using a mix of sentence types for better flow: "The bell rang. Students packed their bags. They walked to the buses. The parking lot was noisy and crowded."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. How many independent clauses and dependent clauses does a compound-complex sentence require?
2. Label this sentence by type and add correct punctuation: "After the final exam ended the students celebrated in the hallway and the teachers began grading the papers."
3. Why might a writer choose a compound-complex sentence over two separate sentences?

**Expected Answers:**
1. A compound-complex sentence requires **at least two independent clauses** and **at least one dependent clause**.
2. **Compound-complex (CC).** "**After** the final exam ended**,** the students celebrated in the hallway**,** **and** the teachers began grading the papers." Dependent clause first → comma. Two independent clauses joined by ", and."
3. A writer might choose a compound-complex sentence to show how multiple ideas are connected — for example, a condition or time frame that affects two related outcomes. Keeping them in one sentence makes the relationships clearer and creates a smoother reading experience.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct, with accurate clause counting, correct punctuation, and thoughtful explanation.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review compound and complex sentences separately before combining them
- Use a color-coding approach: have the student highlight independent clauses in blue and dependent clauses in green, then count each color
- Practice building compound-complex sentences in stages: (1) Write two independent clauses as a compound sentence, (2) add a dependent clause to one end, (3) check punctuation
- Provide sentence frames: "Because _____________, _____________, and _____________." "Although _____________, _____________, but _____________."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 7, which focuses on choosing among all four sentence types for rhetorical effect
- Challenge the student to use at least one compound-complex sentence in their next writing assignment and explain why they chose that structure

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Sentence Type Upgrade:** Take a piece of your own writing from a previous assignment. Find two places where you could combine simple or compound sentences into a compound-complex sentence. Rewrite those sections and explain how the revision improves the writing.
2. **Published Sentence Analysis:** Find a compound-complex sentence in a published novel, speech, or article. Break it into its individual clauses. Then write it as separate simple sentences. Compare the two versions: what is gained and lost?
3. **Four-Type Challenge:** Write a four-sentence paragraph where each sentence is a different type (simple, compound, complex, compound-complex). The paragraph should be about a single topic and read smoothly.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Compound-complex sentences are the most challenging structure for students at this level. Do not expect immediate mastery — this lesson introduces the concept, and Lesson 7 provides further practice through application.
- The most common error is mislabeling complex sentences as compound-complex. Students sometimes count a phrase as a second independent clause. Reinforce: an independent clause must have its own subject and verb AND express a complete thought. A compound verb with one subject is still just one independent clause.
- Item 4 in Guided Practice is deliberately tricky — it looks compound-complex but is actually complex (or simple with a relative clause and compound verb). Use this as a teaching moment: the presence of a dependent clause does not automatically make a sentence compound-complex. There must also be two or more independent clauses.
- When students build compound-complex sentences, encourage them to draft first and punctuate second. Trying to get the punctuation right while composing the sentence can be overwhelming. Write the ideas, identify the clauses, then apply the punctuation rules.
- Complete the Sentence Type Flowchart: "Two or more independent clauses + at least one dependent clause = compound-complex." Students should now have a complete reference for all four types.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **CC** — "**While** the band played the school fight song**,** the football players ran onto the field**,** **and** the cheerleaders performed their halftime routine." One dependent + two independent.
2. **CC** — "The librarian recommended several novels" (independent) + "I chose the one about a girl" (independent, joined by ", and") + "who survives alone in the wilderness" (dependent relative clause). Two independent + one dependent.
3. **CX** — "**If** the weather cooperates**,** we will hold the ceremony outdoors." One independent + one dependent. Complex, not compound-complex.
4. **CC** — "The volunteers painted the community mural on Saturday**;** they cleaned the park on Sunday**,** **and** the mayor thanked them at a special assembly on Monday." Three independent clauses (the first two joined by semicolon, the last joined by ", and"). Note: as written, there is no dependent clause — this is technically compound. If a student adds a dependent clause in the rewrite (e.g., "After they finished their weekend projects, the volunteers..."), accept CC.
5. "**When** the math test was postponed **because** the teacher was absent**,** the substitute assigned a review worksheet instead." OR "**Because** the teacher was absent**,** the math test was postponed**,** **and** the substitute assigned a review worksheet instead."
6. "**Although** the lead actress forgot one of her lines**,** the school play received excellent reviews**,** **and** nobody in the audience noticed the mistake."
7. "**If** you finish your reading log by Thursday**,** you may choose a free-reading day on Friday**,** **but** you must also complete the vocabulary worksheet."
8. "**Because** the debate team had practiced every day after school**,** they won the regional championship**,** **and** the coach rewarded them with a pizza party."
9. Answers vary. Example: "**When the fire drill interrupted third period** (dependent — green), **the students filed out of the building quickly** (independent — blue), **and the teachers took attendance in the parking lot** (independent — blue)."
10. Answers vary. A strong response should include the idea that compound-complex sentences show how multiple ideas are connected through relationships like cause-effect, time, and contrast. Breaking them into separate sentences would lose those connections and make the writing feel choppy or disconnected. Compound-complex sentences allow a writer to express nuanced, layered thinking in a single cohesive unit.

### Interleaved Review
11. "The seventh-grade field trip**,** which takes place every October**,** will visit the natural history museum this year." The clause "which takes place every October" is nonrestrictive — it provides extra information but does not change which field trip is being discussed. Commas set it off. (L.6.2a)
12. Answers vary. Example: "When the bell rang, students packed their bags and walked to the buses. The parking lot was noisy and crowded." This version uses a complex sentence to combine the first three ideas (time relationship) and keeps the final simple sentence for descriptive effect. Other variations are acceptable as long as sentence types are varied. (L.6.3a)
