# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4, Lesson 3

## Topic
Functions of Phrases and Clauses in Sentences

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain how phrases and clauses function as modifiers, restrictors, or expanders of meaning within sentences
- Analyze the effect of removing, moving, or changing a phrase or clause on a sentence's meaning
- Identify whether a phrase or clause is functioning as an adjective, adverb, or noun within a sentence

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify and classify four phrase types: prepositional, appositive, participial, infinitive (Lesson 1)
- Ability to distinguish independent and dependent clauses and identify subordinating conjunctions (Lesson 2)
- Understanding of nonrestrictive versus restrictive elements from Grade 6 (L.6.2a)
- Familiarity with parts of speech: noun, adjective, adverb (Grade 5-6)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences and short passages
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (orange for phrases, green for dependent clauses)
- "Function Cards" — index cards labeled Adjective, Adverb, and Noun for sorting activities

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate that phrases and clauses do meaningful work in sentences by showing what happens when they are removed.

**Activity: What's Missing?**

Say: "I am going to show you two versions of the same sentence. In each pair, the second version has had a phrase or clause removed. Read both and tell me: what information is lost?"

Write on the board:
1. (a) Exhausted from the cross-country race, the runners collapsed on the grass near the finish line.
   (b) The runners collapsed.
2. (a) The student who sits next to me in history class lent me a pencil.
   (b) The student lent me a pencil.
3. (a) To prepare for the debate tournament, our team met every afternoon for two weeks.
   (b) Our team met.

After two minutes, discuss as a class.

Say: "In each case, the stripped-down version is grammatically correct — it is still a sentence. But look how much meaning is lost. We lose who the runners are, which student, why the team met, when, and where. Phrases and clauses are the parts of a sentence that carry the detail, the specificity, the meaning. Today we are going to analyze exactly how they do this."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to analyze the grammatical function of phrases and clauses within sentences.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Modifier:** A word, phrase, or clause that describes or limits another element in the sentence; adjective modifiers describe nouns; adverb modifiers describe verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs
- **Restrictive element:** A phrase or clause that is essential to the meaning of the sentence — removing it changes which person, place, or thing is being discussed
- **Nonrestrictive element:** A phrase or clause that adds extra information but is not essential — removing it does not change which person, place, or thing is being discussed
- **Noun function:** When a phrase or clause takes the place of a noun (acting as a subject, object, or complement)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Every phrase and clause in a sentence has a job. That job falls into one of three categories: it functions as an adjective, an adverb, or a noun. Let me show you each one."

**Adjective Function — Describes or Identifies a Noun:**

Say: "Phrases and clauses functioning as adjectives answer the questions: Which one? What kind? They always appear near the noun they describe."

Write on the board:
- The trail *through the national park* attracts thousands of visitors. (Prepositional phrase — which trail?)
- Ms. Chen, *our seventh-grade English teacher*, assigned a poetry project. (Appositive phrase — which Ms. Chen? / what additional info?)
- The essay *that earned the highest score* was written by Darius. (Relative clause — which essay?)
- The dog *barking at the mail carrier* belongs to my neighbor. (Participial phrase — which dog?)

**Adverb Function — Describes a Verb, Adjective, or Another Adverb:**

Say: "Phrases and clauses functioning as adverbs answer: When? Where? Why? How? Under what condition?"

Write on the board:
- The team celebrated *after winning the championship game*. (Prepositional phrase — when?)
- *Because the forecast predicted freezing rain*, the school delayed its start time. (Dependent clause — why?)
- She spoke *with quiet confidence* during her presentation. (Prepositional phrase — how?)
- *To earn extra credit*, several students attended the evening lecture. (Infinitive phrase — why?)

**Noun Function — Takes the Place of a Noun:**

Say: "Some clauses and phrases do not modify anything — instead, they act as a subject, a direct object, or another noun role."

Write on the board:
- *What the principal announced at the assembly* surprised the entire student body. (Noun clause — subject of the sentence)
- *To qualify for the advanced track* requires a score of 85 or above. (Infinitive phrase — subject)
- The coach explained *how the new play works*. (Noun clause — direct object; what did the coach explain?)

**Restrictive vs. Nonrestrictive — The Meaning Test:**

Say: "In Grade 6, you learned about nonrestrictive elements and how to punctuate them. Now let us connect that to function. A restrictive phrase or clause narrows down which noun you mean — without it, the sentence is about a different thing or is unclear. A nonrestrictive phrase or clause adds bonus information — the sentence still refers to the same thing without it."

Write on the board:
- **Restrictive:** The students *who completed the extra-credit project* earned higher grades. (Which students? Only those who completed it. Removing the clause changes the meaning — now ALL students earned higher grades.)
- **Nonrestrictive:** The seventh graders, *who had been studying all week*, performed well on the exam. (We already know which group — all the seventh graders. The clause adds information but does not restrict which ones.)

Say: "Restrictive elements get no commas. Nonrestrictive elements get commas. The punctuation signals the function."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Analyzing a Complex Sentence:**
Write: "After the science fair judges announced the winners, Maya, the girl who built a water filtration system, jumped out of her seat."

Say: "Let me map every phrase and clause. 'After the science fair judges announced the winners' — dependent clause (adverb function, tells when). 'Maya' — subject. 'The girl who built a water filtration system' — appositive phrase containing a relative clause. The appositive renames Maya (adjective function), and 'who built a water filtration system' is a relative clause inside the appositive (adjective function, describes 'the girl'). 'Jumped out of her seat' — verb plus prepositional phrase (adverb function, tells where she jumped)."

**Example 2 — Effect of Moving a Clause:**
Write two versions:
- (a) Although she was nervous, Keisha delivered her speech with confidence.
- (b) Keisha delivered her speech with confidence although she was nervous.

Say: "Same words, different emphasis. In version (a), the dependent clause comes first, so the reader processes the nervousness before learning about the confident delivery — the emphasis falls on overcoming the nervousness. In version (b), the confident delivery comes first, and the nervousness is almost an afterthought. Moving a clause changes the emphasis and the reader's experience."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students analyze the function of phrases and clauses with facilitator support.

Say: "For each sentence, find the underlined phrase or clause, identify its function (adjective, adverb, or noun), and explain what question it answers or what role it plays."

1. The mural *painted by the art club* brightened the entire hallway.
2. *Before the basketball game started*, the band performed the national anthem.
3. *To make the varsity team* was the goal that motivated her all summer.
4. The teacher recognized the students *who volunteered at the community garden*.
5. *Disappointed by the cancellation*, the fans returned their tickets at the box office.
6. My older brother explained *why the experiment produced unexpected results*.
7. The trail, *which winds through a dense pine forest*, is the most scenic route in the park.
8. She practiced her violin *in the music room* *after school every day*.

Work through items 1-3 together, modeling the analysis. For items 4-8, have students work independently, then compare with a partner.

**Expected Answers:**
1. "painted by the art club" — participial phrase, **adjective function**, describes which mural
2. "Before the basketball game started" — dependent clause, **adverb function**, tells when the band performed
3. "To make the varsity team" — infinitive phrase, **noun function**, serves as the subject of the sentence
4. "who volunteered at the community garden" — relative clause, **adjective function**, identifies which students (restrictive — no commas)
5. "Disappointed by the cancellation" — participial phrase, **adjective function**, describes the fans
6. "why the experiment produced unexpected results" — noun clause, **noun function**, serves as the direct object (what did the brother explain?)
7. "which winds through a dense pine forest" — relative clause, **adjective function**, describes the trail (nonrestrictive — commas signal extra information)
8. "in the music room" — prepositional phrase, **adverb function**, tells where; "after school every day" — prepositional phrase, **adverb function**, tells when

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze phrase and clause functions and demonstrate understanding of how they contribute to meaning.

**Directions:** For items 1-8, identify the underlined element as a phrase or clause, state its function (adjective, adverb, or noun), and explain what it contributes to the sentence. For items 9-10, follow the specific writing directions.

1. The backpack *sitting on the bench outside the gym* belongs to a student in my homeroom.
2. *Since the school renovated the science wing*, enrollment in STEM electives has doubled.
3. *Whoever finishes the reading assignment first* may choose a book from the classroom library.
4. The students lined up *to board the buses for the field trip*.
5. My neighbor, *a retired firefighter with thirty years of service*, coaches our Little League team.
6. *Wrapped in layers of old newspaper*, the fragile pottery survived the move without a crack.
7. The counselor reminded us *that applications are due by the end of the month*.
8. The championship game, *which drew the largest crowd of the season*, went into double overtime.
9. Write a sentence in which a dependent clause functions as an adjective. Underline the clause and label its function.
10. Write a sentence about a school event that contains at least two phrases with different functions. Underline each phrase and label its function.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-8.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-10.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-10, then choose a paragraph from a book or article you are currently reading. Copy it and identify at least four phrases or clauses, labeling each with its type and function. Write a brief sentence explaining how these elements contribute to the paragraph's meaning.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 6 review — L.6.1e) Read this sentence: "Me and my friends was going to the mall after school." Identify two features that differ from standard English and rewrite the sentence in standard English.
12. (Grade 6 review — L.6.1c) Choose the correct verb: "The team of researchers (has / have) published its findings in a national journal."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. What is the function of the underlined clause? "The book *that my English teacher recommended* changed the way I think about history."
2. What would happen to the meaning of this sentence if you removed the underlined phrase? "The students *in the advanced math class* competed in the regional math olympiad."
3. Is the underlined element functioning as an adjective, adverb, or noun? "*To understand the causes of the American Revolution* is the main goal of this week's lessons."

**Expected Answers:**
1. "That my English teacher recommended" is a relative clause functioning as an **adjective** — it identifies which book (restrictive; no commas).
2. Removing "in the advanced math class" would change the meaning significantly. Without it, the sentence says ALL students competed, not just the ones in the advanced class. This is a restrictive prepositional phrase — it narrows down which students.
3. "To understand the causes of the American Revolution" is an infinitive phrase functioning as a **noun** — it is the subject of the sentence (what is the main goal?).

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct, with accurate identification of function and clear explanation.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three function categories with a simple anchor chart: Adjective = describes a noun (which? what kind?), Adverb = describes a verb (when? where? why? how?), Noun = acts as a subject or object
- Practice with one function at a time — give the student five sentences that all contain adjective-function elements, then five with adverb-function elements, then five with noun-function elements
- Use the "question test" as a scaffold: What question does this phrase or clause answer? If it answers "which?" or "what kind?" it is an adjective. If it answers "when?" "where?" "why?" or "how?" it is an adverb. If it IS the subject or object, it is a noun.
- Revisit the restrictive/nonrestrictive distinction with additional examples and the comma-or-no-comma test

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 4, which applies phrase and clause knowledge to understanding simple and compound sentences
- Challenge the student to find examples of noun clauses in their reading — these are the most difficult to identify and reward close attention

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Sentence Surgery:** Take a bare-bones sentence like "The dog ran" and perform "surgery" — add phrases and clauses one at a time, each with a different function. Write out the sentence after each addition. Observe how meaning builds.
2. **Function Flip:** Write a sentence with a restrictive clause (no commas). Then rewrite it with the clause as nonrestrictive (with commas). Explain how the meaning shifts.
3. **Passage Analysis:** Choose a paragraph from a favorite novel or article. Identify every phrase and clause that is not part of the main subject-verb core. For each one, label its function (adjective, adverb, or noun). Write a brief paragraph explaining how these elements contribute to the author's meaning and style.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is the synthesis of Lessons 1 and 2 — it requires students to draw on their knowledge of both phrases and clauses and add a new layer (function). Some students may need a quick review of phrase types and clause types before diving into function analysis.
- The distinction between restrictive and nonrestrictive elements is one of the most important practical applications here. It connects directly to Grade 6 work on comma usage with nonrestrictive elements (L.6.2a) and prepares students for the comma decisions they will make in Lesson 8 (coordinate adjectives).
- Noun clauses and noun-function infinitive phrases are the most challenging concept in this lesson. Students are more accustomed to modifiers (adjective and adverb function) than to phrases and clauses that replace nouns. Give extra practice with noun-function elements if students struggle.
- When analyzing sentences, encourage students to find the main subject and verb first (the "skeleton" of the sentence) and then identify everything else as a modifier or expander. This top-down approach prevents students from getting lost in complex sentences.
- Connect to writing: ask students to look at a paragraph from their own recent writing (from Unit 3 argumentative essays, for example). Can they identify the phrases and clauses they used? Are there places where adding a phrase or clause would improve clarity or detail?

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. "sitting on the bench outside the gym" — **participial phrase**, **adjective function**, describes which backpack
2. "Since the school renovated the science wing" — **dependent clause**, **adverb function**, explains why enrollment has doubled (cause-effect)
3. "Whoever finishes the reading assignment first" — **noun clause**, **noun function**, serves as the subject of the sentence (who may choose?)
4. "to board the buses for the field trip" — **infinitive phrase**, **adverb function**, tells why the students lined up (purpose)
5. "a retired firefighter with thirty years of service" — **appositive phrase**, **adjective function**, renames "my neighbor" (nonrestrictive — commas used)
6. "Wrapped in layers of old newspaper" — **participial phrase**, **adjective function**, describes the pottery
7. "that applications are due by the end of the month" — **noun clause**, **noun function**, serves as the direct object (what did the counselor remind us?)
8. "which drew the largest crowd of the season" — **relative clause**, **adjective function**, describes the championship game (nonrestrictive — commas used)
9. Answers vary. Example: "The video *that she posted about the science experiment* has already been viewed two hundred times." The relative clause "that she posted about the science experiment" functions as an adjective, identifying which video (restrictive).
10. Answers vary. Example: "*During the homecoming assembly* (prepositional phrase, adverb — tells when), the principal introduced the guest speaker, *a former Olympic gymnast* (appositive phrase, adjective — renames 'the guest speaker')."

### Interleaved Review
11. Two features differing from standard English: (1) "Me and my friends" — the subject pronoun should be "I" and typically comes last in a compound subject: "My friends and I." (2) "was going" — the subject "my friends and I" is plural, so the verb should be "were going." Standard English version: "My friends and I were going to the mall after school." (L.6.1e)
12. **has** — The subject is "team" (singular collective noun acting as a unit), not "researchers" (object of the preposition "of"). A singular subject takes a singular verb. (L.6.1c)
