# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 4, Lesson 4

## Topic
Simple and Compound Sentences

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define and identify simple sentences, including those with compound subjects and compound verbs
- Define and identify compound sentences and explain how they join two independent clauses
- Correctly punctuate compound sentences using coordinating conjunctions (with a comma) and semicolons

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify independent and dependent clauses (Lesson 2)
- Understanding of coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) from Grade 6 (L.6.3a)
- Familiarity with commas and semicolons for separating items in a series (Grade 5-6)
- Experience varying sentence patterns for meaning and style (L.6.3a)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector)
- Printed or projected example sentences
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Colored pencils (blue for independent clauses, red for conjunctions/punctuation)
- FANBOYS reference card (For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So)
- Sentence Type Flowchart (to be built throughout Lessons 4-6)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review independent clauses and set up the concept of joining them.

**Activity: One or Two?**

Say: "For each sentence below, count the independent clauses. Remember, an independent clause has a subject, a verb, and expresses a complete thought. Write '1' or '2' in your notebook."

Write on the board:
1. The soccer season starts next week.
2. The soccer season starts next week, and the team has been practicing every afternoon.
3. Maria and James presented their science project to the class.
4. Maria presented her science project, but James was absent with the flu.
5. The rain pounded the windows and flooded the parking lot.
6. The rain pounded the windows, so the outdoor assembly was moved inside.

After two minutes, review as a class.

Say: "Items 1, 3, and 5 each have one independent clause — even though item 3 has two subjects and item 5 has two verbs. Items 2, 4, and 6 each have two independent clauses joined together. Today we are going to name these two sentence types and learn the rules for building them correctly."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define simple and compound sentences, teach joining strategies, and model correct punctuation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Simple sentence:** A sentence that contains exactly one independent clause; it may have compound subjects, compound verbs, and multiple phrases, but it has only one subject-verb core
- **Compound sentence:** A sentence that contains two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction (with a comma) or a semicolon
- **Coordinating conjunction:** One of seven words that join grammatically equal elements: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so (FANBOYS)
- **Semicolon:** A punctuation mark (;) that joins two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction

### Instruction Notes

**Simple Sentences:**

Say: "A simple sentence has one independent clause. But do not let the word 'simple' fool you — a simple sentence can be long and detailed. What makes it simple is that it has only one subject-verb core, even if that core is compound."

Write on the board:
- The cat slept. (Simple — one subject, one verb)
- The cat and the dog slept on the couch all afternoon. (Simple — compound subject, one verb, plus phrases)
- The relay team sprinted down the track, crossed the finish line, and collapsed in exhaustion. (Simple — one subject, compound verb with three parts)

Say: "That last sentence is long, but it is still simple because there is only one subject — 'the relay team' — doing all three actions. Every verb shares the same subject."

**Compound Sentences — Method 1: Comma + Coordinating Conjunction:**

Say: "A compound sentence joins two independent clauses — two complete thoughts. The most common method uses a comma followed by one of the FANBOYS conjunctions."

Write on the board:

**The FANBOYS and Their Relationships:**
| Conjunction | Relationship | Example |
|-------------|-------------|---------|
| **F**or | reason/cause | I studied extra hard, **for** the exam covers three chapters. |
| **A**nd | addition | The choir rehearsed the finale, **and** the stage crew set up the lighting. |
| **N**or | negative alternative | The library does not allow food, **nor** does it permit loud conversations. |
| **B**ut | contrast | I wanted to join the debate team, **but** the meetings conflict with track practice. |
| **O**r | alternative/choice | You can present your project on Monday, **or** you can wait until Wednesday. |
| **Y**et | contrast (surprise) | The test was the hardest of the year, **yet** most students passed. |
| **S**o | result/effect | The power went out during the storm, **so** we finished our homework by flashlight. |

Say: "Notice the pattern: independent clause + comma + FANBOYS + independent clause. The comma goes BEFORE the conjunction, not after it."

**Compound Sentences — Method 2: Semicolon:**

Say: "When two independent clauses are closely related, you can join them with a semicolon instead of a comma and conjunction. This creates a tighter connection."

Write on the board:
- The auditorium was packed; every seat was taken before the doors officially opened.
- Our team won the first game; we lost the second by a single point.

Say: "Use a semicolon only when the two ideas are closely related and you want the reader to see that connection immediately. If the ideas are not closely related, use a period and start a new sentence."

**Common Error — The Comma Splice:**

Say: "One of the most common errors in student writing is the comma splice — using only a comma to join two independent clauses, without a coordinating conjunction. This is incorrect."

Write on the board:
- INCORRECT: The bell rang, the students rushed into the hallway.
- CORRECT: The bell rang, **and** the students rushed into the hallway.
- CORRECT: The bell rang**;** the students rushed into the hallway.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Identifying Sentence Type:**
Write: "The debate team practiced their arguments and reviewed the opposition's evidence before the tournament."

Say: "How many independent clauses? I see one subject — 'the debate team' — and two verbs — 'practiced' and 'reviewed.' Both verbs share the same subject. This is a simple sentence with a compound verb, not a compound sentence."

**Example 2 — Building a Compound Sentence:**
Write two simple sentences: "The experiment produced unexpected results." "The students decided to run it again with a larger sample size."

Say: "I want to join these. What is the relationship? The unexpected results caused the students to try again — that is a cause-and-effect relationship. I will use 'so': 'The experiment produced unexpected results, so the students decided to run it again with a larger sample size.' Comma before 'so,' and now I have a compound sentence."

**Example 3 — Fixing a Comma Splice:**
Write: "Maya finished her essay early, she helped her partner with the bibliography."

Say: "This has two independent clauses separated by only a comma — that is a comma splice. I have three options: (1) Add a conjunction: 'Maya finished her essay early, and she helped her partner with the bibliography.' (2) Use a semicolon: 'Maya finished her essay early; she helped her partner with the bibliography.' (3) Make them separate sentences: 'Maya finished her essay early. She helped her partner with the bibliography.'"

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students identify sentence types and practice building and punctuating compound sentences.

**Part A — Identify:** Label each sentence as simple (S) or compound (C).

1. The goalkeeper dove to the left and blocked the penalty kick.
2. The science project is due on Friday, but I still need to finish the data analysis.
3. My grandmother and grandfather met in college and married three years later.
4. The movie received terrible reviews; however, it broke box-office records.
5. You can write your report by hand, or you can type it on the computer.
6. Lightning flashed across the sky, thunder rumbled in the distance, and the wind howled through the trees.

**Part B — Combine:** Join each pair of simple sentences into one compound sentence. Choose the FANBOYS conjunction that best shows the relationship, and punctuate correctly.

7. The bus was late. We walked to school instead.
8. The orchestra played beautifully. The audience gave a standing ovation.

Work through items 1-2 and item 7 together on the board. For the remaining items, have students work independently and compare with a partner.

**Expected Answers:**
1. **S** — One subject ("goalkeeper"), compound verb ("dove and blocked").
2. **C** — Two independent clauses joined by ", but."
3. **S** — Compound subject ("grandmother and grandfather"), compound verb ("met and married").
4. **C** — Two independent clauses joined by a semicolon (with the conjunctive adverb "however").
5. **C** — Two independent clauses joined by ", or."
6. **C** — Three independent clauses joined by commas and "and."
7. "The bus was late, so we walked to school instead." (cause-effect)
8. "The orchestra played beautifully, and the audience gave a standing ovation." (addition) OR "The orchestra played beautifully; the audience gave a standing ovation." (semicolon for closely related ideas)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students demonstrate ability to identify, build, and punctuate simple and compound sentences independently.

**Directions:** For items 1-4, label each sentence as simple or compound. For items 5-8, combine each pair into a compound sentence with correct punctuation. For items 9-10, follow the specific directions.

1. The art teacher displayed the best paintings and sculptures from every class in the front hallway.
2. I finished my homework before dinner, yet I still felt unprepared for the quiz.
3. The principal and the vice principal greeted the students at the door every morning.
4. The gym was reserved for the basketball game; the volleyball team had to practice outside.
5. The cafeteria ran out of pizza. Most students chose the chicken sandwich instead.
6. I wanted to try out for the school play. Rehearsals overlap with my guitar lessons.
7. The field trip to the science museum was educational. It was also incredibly fun.
8. We could study at the library. We could meet at my house after school.
9. Write an original simple sentence that has a compound subject and a compound verb. Underline the subjects once and the verbs twice.
10. Write an original compound sentence that uses a semicolon. Make sure both independent clauses are closely related.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete items 1-8.
**Stretch:** Complete items 1-10.
**Challenge:** Complete items 1-10, then write a paragraph (4-5 sentences) about your favorite class or extracurricular activity. Include at least two simple sentences and two compound sentences (one using a comma + FANBOYS, one using a semicolon). Label each sentence type.

### Interleaved Review
11. (Grade 6 review — L.6.2a) Add commas to set off the nonrestrictive element: "The history textbook which was published just last year includes a chapter on recent space exploration."
12. (Grade 6 review — L.6.3a) Rewrite this passage to vary the sentence patterns. The original has four short, choppy sentences: "The gym was quiet. The lights were off. A single spotlight shone on the stage. The talent show was about to begin."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Is this sentence simple or compound? "The student council president and the secretary organized the fundraiser and promoted it on social media."
2. Fix the comma splice: "The rehearsal ran late, everyone was tired by the end."
3. Combine these into a compound sentence showing contrast: "The math test was challenging." "Most of the class earned passing grades."

**Expected Answers:**
1. **Simple** — One compound subject ("president and secretary") and one compound verb ("organized and promoted"). There is only one independent clause.
2. "The rehearsal ran late, **and** everyone was tired by the end." OR "The rehearsal ran late**;** everyone was tired by the end." (Adding a conjunction or replacing the comma with a semicolon fixes the splice.)
3. "The math test was challenging, **but** most of the class earned passing grades." OR "The math test was challenging, **yet** most of the class earned passing grades." ("But" or "yet" shows contrast.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 items correct, with proper punctuation and correct sentence type identification.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the core distinction: simple = one independent clause (even with compound parts); compound = two or more independent clauses
- Practice the "period test": try putting a period where the comma is. If both parts can stand alone as sentences, it is compound. If one part cannot stand alone, it is not compound.
- Provide additional comma splice examples and have the student practice the three repair strategies (add conjunction, use semicolon, or separate into two sentences)
- Use physical manipulation: write each clause on a separate index card, then practice "joining" cards with conjunction cards and punctuation cards

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready to proceed to Lesson 5, which introduces complex sentences using subordinate clauses
- Challenge the student to notice compound sentences in their reading and observe which coordinating conjunctions authors use most frequently

## Extension (Optional)
1. **FANBOYS Analysis:** Choose a page from a novel or article. Find every compound sentence and identify which FANBOYS conjunction is used. Tally the results. Which conjunction appears most often? Why might that be?
2. **Comma Splice Patrol:** Exchange a piece of your own recent writing with a partner. Hunt for comma splices and fix each one using a different method (conjunction, semicolon, or period).
3. **Sentence-Type Transformation:** Take a passage of 5-6 sentences and rewrite it using only simple sentences. Then rewrite it using only compound sentences. Compare the two versions: how does the sentence type affect the reading experience?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common confusion at this point is between simple sentences with compound verbs and compound sentences. The key test: do the verbs share the same subject? If yes, it is simple (compound verb). If each verb has its own subject, it is compound (two independent clauses).
- Students often put a comma before "and" in a compound verb, creating a false compound sentence: "The dog chased the ball, and brought it back." This is incorrect — no comma is needed when "and" joins two verbs with the same subject. Help students distinguish between "and" joining two clauses (comma needed) and "and" joining two verbs (no comma needed).
- Item 4 in Guided Practice introduces the semicolon with a conjunctive adverb ("however"). Mention that conjunctive adverbs (however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless) are NOT coordinating conjunctions and cannot be used with just a comma to join clauses. This is a frequent source of comma splices.
- This lesson connects directly to Grade 6 work on varying sentence patterns (L.6.3a). In Grade 6, students practiced combining sentences for variety; now they are learning the formal terminology and rules behind those combinations.
- Begin building the Sentence Type Flowchart: "How many independent clauses? One = simple. Two or more joined by comma+FANBOYS or semicolon = compound." Students will add to this flowchart in Lessons 5 and 6.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Simple** — One subject ("art teacher") with one verb ("displayed"), plus multiple phrases. Despite the length, there is only one independent clause.
2. **Compound** — Two independent clauses: "I finished my homework before dinner" + "I still felt unprepared for the quiz," joined by ", yet."
3. **Simple** — Compound subject ("principal and vice principal"), one verb ("greeted"). One independent clause.
4. **Compound** — Two independent clauses joined by a semicolon: "The gym was reserved for the basketball game" + "the volleyball team had to practice outside."
5. "The cafeteria ran out of pizza, **so** most students chose the chicken sandwich instead." (Cause-effect: "so" shows the result.)
6. "I wanted to try out for the school play, **but** rehearsals overlap with my guitar lessons." (Contrast: "but" shows the conflict.)
7. "The field trip to the science museum was educational, **and** it was also incredibly fun." (Addition: "and" adds a second quality.) OR with semicolon for closely related ideas.
8. "We could study at the library, **or** we could meet at my house after school." (Choice: "or" presents alternatives.)
9. Answers vary. Example: "**The coach and the assistant** (compound subject) **organized** the drills **and timed** each lap." (compound verb)
10. Answers vary. Example: "The first half of the game was slow and uneventful**;** the second half was packed with scoring and dramatic plays."

### Interleaved Review
11. "The history textbook**,** which was published just last year**,** includes a chapter on recent space exploration." The relative clause "which was published just last year" is nonrestrictive (there is only one history textbook being discussed), so it must be set off by commas. (L.6.2a)
12. Answers vary. Example: "The gym was quiet, and the lights were off. A single spotlight shone on the stage — the talent show was about to begin." This version combines two of the short sentences with a coordinating conjunction and uses a dash to create dramatic effect. Other variations are acceptable as long as the sentence patterns are varied. (L.6.3a)
