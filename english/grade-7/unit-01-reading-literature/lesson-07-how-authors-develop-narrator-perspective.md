# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 7

## Topic
How Authors Develop Narrator Perspective

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify specific techniques authors use to establish and develop a narrator's perspective (diction, sensory focus, selective detail, and tone)
- Analyze how shifting between narrator perspectives within a text creates meaning
- Explain how the narrator's perspective shapes the reader's access to information and emotional experience

## Prerequisites
- Ability to analyze how authors develop and contrast character points of view (Lesson 6)
- Understanding of first-person, third-person limited, and third-person omniscient narration (Grade 6)
- Experience explaining how an author develops the point of view of a narrator or speaker (RL.6.6)
- Familiarity with citing several pieces of evidence to support analysis (Lessons 1-2)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Highlighters in three colors
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate that the way a narrator tells a story shapes everything the reader can know.

**Activity: Same Room, Two Voices**

Say: "In Lesson 6, we analyzed how different characters see the same events differently. Today, we are going to focus specifically on narrators — the voice telling the story. The narrator is like a camera. Where the camera points determines what the audience sees. And the author chooses where to point the camera."

Write on the board — two descriptions of the same room:

**Narrator A:** "The kitchen smelled like cinnamon and burnt toast. A half-eaten bowl of cereal sat on the counter, the milk turning gray. Somewhere in the house, a radio was playing a song I did not recognize."

**Narrator B:** "The kitchen was twelve feet by fourteen feet with laminate countertops and a single window facing east. A cereal bowl with approximately six ounces of remaining milk occupied the counter. The ambient temperature was sixty-eight degrees."

Say: "Same room. Completely different narrators. What do you notice about each voice? Talk to a partner for thirty seconds."

Expected observations: Narrator A is personal, sensory, and emotional — they notice smells and sounds, and they use "I." Narrator B is detached, precise, and clinical — they measure and quantify. The voice shapes what feels important in the room.

Say: "Authors choose narrator voices deliberately. Today, you are going to analyze how they build those voices and why they make the choices they do."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach four techniques authors use to develop narrator perspective.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Diction:** The specific word choices a narrator uses, which reveal personality, education, emotion, and attitude.
- **Sensory focus:** The senses a narrator pays attention to (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste), which reveal what matters to them.
- **Selective detail:** The information a narrator includes or omits, which shapes what the reader knows and does not know.
- **Tone:** The narrator's attitude toward the events, characters, or setting, conveyed through word choice and sentence structure.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When an author develops a narrator's perspective, they are making four key decisions."

Write on the board — **Four Decisions That Build a Narrator:**
1. **Diction** — What words does the narrator use? Formal? Casual? Technical? Poetic?
2. **Sensory focus** — What does the narrator notice? Sound? Color? Texture? Temperature?
3. **Selective detail** — What does the narrator choose to describe and what do they leave out?
4. **Tone** — How does the narrator feel about what they are describing? Affectionate? Bitter? Detached? Anxious?

Say: "These four decisions add up to a perspective — a unique lens that shapes everything the reader experiences. Let me show you with a passage that uses two different narrator perspectives on the same event."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Move" (alternating perspectives)**

> **Sana's Perspective:**
>
> The moving truck arrived at seven in the morning, which was too early for anything good to happen. I sat on the porch steps and watched two men carry our couch down the walkway, the cushions wrapped in plastic that crinkled in the quiet air. The couch looked strange outside — small and exposed, like a fish pulled out of water. Everything looks wrong when you take it out of the place it belongs.
>
> My mother came out with a box labeled KITCHEN and set it on the truck's ramp. She was smiling. I did not understand how she could smile on a day we were dismantling our house. The hydrangea bush my father had planted the year I was born was blooming — the flowers were blue this year, which meant the soil was acidic, which meant nothing to anyone but me.
>
> **Her Mother's Perspective (as told by Sana later):**
>
> My mother told me, years later, what she had been thinking that morning. She had been thinking about space. The new apartment had two bedrooms instead of one. It had a dishwasher. It was four blocks from the community college where she had enrolled for night classes. She had been counting the blocks between the new apartment and the bus stop, the new apartment and the grocery store, the new apartment and the school. Every distance was shorter. Every distance meant she would have more time.
>
> She said she saw me sitting on the porch steps, watching the movers, and she knew I was grieving. But she had been grieving for years — for the cramped apartment, for the bus ride that took forty-five minutes each way, for the education she had put on hold. The move was not a loss to her. It was the end of a loss.

Say: "Let me analyze the narrator techniques for each perspective."

Write on the board:

**Sana's perspective — developed through:**
- **Diction:** Personal, sensory, metaphorical — "like a fish pulled out of water," "dismantling our house." Her word choices are emotionally charged.
- **Sensory focus:** She notices visual details (plastic crinkling, blue hydrangeas, soil acidity). She is focused on the physical objects and the space being taken apart.
- **Selective detail:** She includes the hydrangea bush her father planted — a detail her mother would not have mentioned. She omits any mention of the new apartment's benefits.
- **Tone:** Mournful and resistant. She does not understand her mother's smile. The morning is "too early for anything good to happen."

**Mother's perspective — developed through:**
- **Diction:** Practical, spatial, forward-looking — "two bedrooms instead of one," "every distance was shorter," "more time." Her words are about measurement and improvement.
- **Sensory focus:** She notices logistics — blocks, bus stops, distances. Her focus is navigational rather than emotional.
- **Selective detail:** She includes the community college, the shorter distances, the dishwasher. She omits the hydrangea bush entirely.
- **Tone:** Relieved and purposeful. The move is "the end of a loss" — a phrase that reframes the entire event.

**What the contrast reveals:** By developing two perspectives on the same morning, the author shows that loss and liberation can exist simultaneously. Sana grieves because she is losing the only home she knows. Her mother is relieved because she is finally escaping conditions she has endured for years. Neither perspective is wrong — but the reader could not understand the full picture without both.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying narrator development techniques with facilitator support.

Say: "Read this passage and identify the four techniques the author uses to develop the narrator's perspective. Use your highlighters: one color for diction choices, another for sensory details, and a third for tone markers."

**Passage: "The New Kid's First Day" (first-person narrator)**

> I have been the new kid four times. New school in third grade because my mother got transferred. New school in fourth because the apartment lease ended. New school in fifth because — I stopped asking why after a while. Now seventh grade, new school number four, and I have a system.
>
> Step one: find the cafeteria before anyone else. An empty cafeteria is the safest room in a school. No one watches you when there is no one there. Step two: pick a seat near the wall but not in the corner. The corner says "I am hiding." The wall says "I am here, but I am not asking for attention." Step three: eat quickly. Not because I am hungry but because eating gives my hands something to do.
>
> The cafeteria at this school smelled like floor wax and reheated pasta. The tables were long and bolted to the floor, the kind with attached benches that flip up. I chose the third table from the vending machines, wall side, and opened my lunch bag. Turkey sandwich, apple, water bottle. I ate without tasting.
>
> A girl sat down across from me. She did not ask if the seat was taken. She just sat, unzipped her own lunch bag, and started eating. After a minute, she said, "You are new."
>
> It was not a question. I nodded.
>
> "I have been here since kindergarten," she said. "If you want, I can tell you which teachers give extra credit and which ones do not."
>
> I did not want to talk. But I also did not want to be the kind of person who refuses a hand when it is offered. "Okay," I said.
>
> She smiled and stuck out her hand. "I am Bea."
>
> I shook it. Her grip was firm, like she meant it. "I am Joaquin," I said.

Say: "With a partner, identify how the author develops Joaquin's narrator perspective using the four techniques. Write your findings in your notebook."

Allow 2 minutes for partner work.

Expected responses:
- **Diction:** Joaquin's language is strategic and analytical — "I have a system," "the corner says," "the wall says." His words frame social interaction as a problem to be solved rather than an experience to enjoy.
- **Sensory focus:** He notices institutional details — floor wax, reheated pasta, tables bolted to the floor, benches that flip up. He catalogs the environment the way someone cases a building.
- **Selective detail:** He includes the rules of his system but omits personal feelings about being new. He mentions eating "without tasting" — selecting a detail that reveals emotional numbness through physical experience.
- **Tone:** Guarded, practiced, slightly weary. Phrases like "I stopped asking why after a while" and "new school number four" convey resignation. But the ending — "Her grip was firm, like she meant it" — shows a crack in the guard; Joaquin notices sincerity and responds to it.

Say: "How does the ending shift the narrator's perspective? What does 'like she meant it' reveal about what Joaquin values?"

Guide discussion: It reveals that Joaquin has encountered insincerity before — people who go through social motions without meaning them. Bea's firm handshake stands out because it is genuine. This single detail develops Joaquin's perspective: he is not antisocial; he is cautious because experience has taught him to be.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how authors develop narrator perspective.

**Passage: "Two Mornings" (two narrators, same household)**

> **Narrator 1 — Ava:**
>
> I woke up before the alarm, the way I always do on test days. The house was dark and quiet. I made my bed, set out my clothes, and reviewed my biology flashcards at the kitchen table while the coffee maker my mother had programmed the night before dripped into the pot. I like the sound of the coffee maker — the burbling, the hiss of steam. It means the day has started and I am ahead of it.
>
> I left the house at seven fifteen, backpack zipped, study guide in the front pocket. The walk to school takes eleven minutes. I have timed it.
>
> **Narrator 2 — her brother, Leo:**
>
> The alarm went off and I turned it off. Then it went off again because I had set a backup alarm, which past-Leo was smart enough to do even though present-Leo hated him for it. I rolled out of bed, put on whatever was on the floor, and walked to the kitchen. Ava's flashcards were spread across the table like a crime scene of highlighters and index cards. The coffee was already made. I poured a cup even though I do not really like coffee. It just seemed like the thing to do on a day I was supposed to be awake for.
>
> Ava had already left. I could tell because the house was too still — she takes the energy with her when she goes, the way a radio takes the music when you unplug it. I left at seven forty-two, which gave me three minutes to get to school if I jogged. I did not jog.

**Directions:** Read the passage carefully and analyze the narrator development techniques.

1. For each narrator (Ava and Leo), identify the author's choices in diction, sensory focus, selective detail, and tone. Cite at least one piece of evidence for each technique per narrator.
2. How does the author's development of Ava's narrator perspective shape the reader's understanding of who she is? Explain using at least two techniques with cited evidence.
3. Leo describes Ava's flashcards as "a crime scene of highlighters and index cards." What does this description reveal about Leo's perspective on Ava's study habits? How does it contrast with Ava's own presentation of the same activity?
4. Both narrators describe the coffee maker. How does each narrator's treatment of this same detail reveal their different perspectives?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 with cited evidence.
**Stretch:** In your response to question 1, arrange your analysis so that you explicitly compare each technique across both narrators — show how the same technique (e.g., diction) operates differently in each voice.
**Challenge:** Write a third narrator perspective for the same morning — the mother who programmed the coffee maker. Use all four techniques deliberately to create a distinct voice. Then write a brief analysis of your own choices.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.5) How does the structural choice of placing Ava's section first and Leo's second contribute to the reader's experience? What would change if the order were reversed?
6. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.1) Cite two pieces of evidence that suggest Leo is aware of — and possibly bothered by — the contrast between himself and Ava.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> The park looked different depending on when you visited. In the morning, the joggers owned it — their sneakers tapping the concrete path, their earbuds in, their eyes forward. They saw the park as a track. At noon, the lunch crowd arrived with sandwiches and laptops. They saw the park as an office with better lighting. In the evening, the children came, and the park became what it was always meant to be: a place to run without a destination.

1. Identify two narrator techniques the author uses to develop the different perspectives on the park. Cite evidence for each.

**Expected Answers:**
1. First, the author uses **selective detail** to develop each group's perspective: the joggers' "sneakers tapping the concrete path" and "earbuds in, eyes forward" reduce the park to a running surface; the lunch crowd's "sandwiches and laptops" transform it into a workspace. Each group notices only the details that serve their purpose. Second, the author uses **diction** to redefine the park: "a track," "an office with better lighting," and "a place to run without a destination." Each metaphor reflects the perspective of the group using the park, and the final phrase — "what it was always meant to be" — introduces the narrator's own perspective, breaking the neutrality to suggest the children's use is the most authentic.

**Mastery = Student identifies two narrator development techniques, cites evidence for each, and explains how the technique shapes the reader's understanding of the perspectives.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the four techniques using the warm-up example (same room, two voices) and have the student label each technique.
- Provide a simplified passage with one narrator and ask the student to identify the diction, sensory focus, and tone before moving to comparison.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8, which examines unreliable and limited narrators.
- Encourage the student to analyze narrator techniques in their independent reading.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Voice Imitation:** The student selects a narrator from a book they are reading and writes a paragraph in that narrator's voice describing their own school. They then annotate the paragraph, identifying the diction, sensory focus, selective detail, and tone choices they made.
2. **Technique Shift:** The student rewrites the first paragraph of Ava's section using Leo's narrator techniques (casual diction, humorous tone, selective attention to non-academic details). They then explain how the shift changes the reader's perception of the same morning.
3. **Narrator Analysis Essay:** The student writes a short essay (1 page) analyzing how the author of "Two Mornings" uses narrator development techniques to create two distinct voices. They must address all four techniques.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Key concept for this lesson:** The narrator is not a transparent window — the narrator is a constructed lens. Every narrator technique is a choice the author makes to shape the reader's experience. Push students to see narrator perspective as something the author builds rather than something that just exists.
- **Humor in the Leo passage:** Leo's narration is intentionally humorous ("past-Leo was smart enough to do even though present-Leo hated him for it"). Use this to show that humor is a narrator technique — it reveals a character who processes the world through self-deprecation and wit.
- **Common error:** Students may focus only on what happens in the passage rather than how it is told. Redirect: "You described the events. Now describe the voice — what does the narrator sound like, and what does that sound tell us about who they are?"
- **Scaffolding tip:** If students struggle with four techniques at once, have them focus on diction and tone first — these are the most accessible entry points for seventh graders.
- **Pacing note:** The worked example in Direct Instruction covers two perspectives in detail. If time is tight, model only Sana's perspective fully and have students analyze the mother's perspective during Guided Practice.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Ava — Diction:** Precise and structured — "seven fifteen," "eleven minutes," "I have timed it." Her language is numerical and controlled. **Sensory focus:** She notices the coffee maker's sounds — "the burbling, the hiss of steam" — and interprets them positively as signals of readiness. **Selective detail:** She includes her preparation steps (making bed, setting out clothes, reviewing flashcards) and omits any mention of anxiety or uncertainty. **Tone:** Confident and controlled — "I am ahead of it" suggests she measures her worth by preparedness.

   **Leo — Diction:** Casual and self-deprecating — "past-Leo was smart enough to do even though present-Leo hated him for it," "whatever was on the floor." His language is loose, humorous, and informal. **Sensory focus:** He notices the stillness of the house after Ava leaves and compares it to an unplugged radio — an auditory absence rather than a visual detail. **Selective detail:** He includes that he poured coffee even though he does not like it, selecting a detail that reveals aimlessness. He omits any mention of studying. **Tone:** Self-aware, ironic, slightly admiring of Ava — the radio simile shows he notices her absence and the energy she carries.

2. Ava's narrator perspective shapes the reader's understanding through her diction and selective detail. Her diction is consistently precise — she counts minutes, names times, and describes sequences of preparation. This creates a portrait of someone who manages anxiety through control and routine. Her selective detail reinforces this: she describes what she has done (made bed, set out clothes, reviewed flashcards) but never mentions how she feels about the test. The absence of emotional language is itself a technique — it suggests Ava channels her feelings into action rather than reflection. Together, these choices build a character who is competent but possibly rigid, prepared but potentially unable to improvise.

3. Leo's description of the flashcards as "a crime scene of highlighters and index cards" reveals that he sees Ava's organized study process as excessive or chaotic from the outside — the word "crime scene" implies disorder, even though Ava's own narration suggests careful planning. This contrast shows that the same physical scene (flashcards on a table) looks entirely different depending on the narrator's values. Ava would describe the flashcards as tools for preparation; Leo sees them as evidence of intensity he does not share. The contrast reveals Leo's perspective as someone who stands outside academic ambition and observes it with bemusement.

4. Ava describes the coffee maker's sound — "the burbling, the hiss of steam" — and interprets it positively: "It means the day has started and I am ahead of it." The coffee maker is a signal of her preparedness. Leo pours coffee "even though I do not really like coffee" because "it just seemed like the thing to do on a day I was supposed to be awake for." The coffee maker is a prop Leo uses to perform adulthood and readiness he does not actually feel. The same object serves a completely different narrative function for each character: it confirms Ava's sense of control and exposes Leo's sense of drift.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.6.5) Placing Ava's section first establishes a baseline of order and preparedness — the reader enters the morning through a controlled, efficient lens. When Leo's section follows, the contrast is immediate and striking. The reader experiences the same morning from a disorganized, self-deprecating perspective, which reframes Ava's precision as one way of being rather than the only way. If the order were reversed, the reader would first experience the morning as chaotic and then encounter Ava's version as corrective or judgmental. The current order is more generous to both perspectives.

6. (RL.6.1) First, Leo describes Ava's flashcards as "a crime scene of highlighters and index cards," using language that mixes humor with a sense of being overwhelmed by her level of preparation. This suggests he is aware that her approach to the test day is radically different from his own, and the exaggerated metaphor implies discomfort. Second, Leo says Ava "takes the energy with her when she goes, the way a radio takes the music when you unplug it." This simile reveals that Leo defines the house's energy by Ava's presence — when she leaves, what remains is stillness and absence. This suggests he is not only aware of the contrast but feels diminished by it.
