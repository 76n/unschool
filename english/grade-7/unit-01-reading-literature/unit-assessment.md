# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1: Reading Literature

## Unit Assessment

### Overview
This assessment evaluates student mastery of the four standards addressed in Unit 1:
- **RL.7.1** — Cite several pieces of textual evidence to support analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the text
- **RL.7.2** — Determine a theme or central idea of a text and analyze its development over the course of the text; provide an objective summary of the text
- **RL.7.6** — Analyze how an author develops and contrasts the points of view of different characters or narrators in a text
- **RL.7.9** — Compare and contrast a fictional portrayal of a time, place, or character and a historical account of the same period as a means of understanding how authors of fiction use or alter history

### Administration Notes
- **Estimated time:** 50-60 minutes
- **Format:** 5 sections with original passages, totaling 24 questions
- **Question types:** Multiple choice (2 pts each), short answer (3 pts each), and extended response (5 pts each)
- **Scoring:** Total = 60 points (see scoring guide)
- **Mastery threshold:** 70% overall (42/60) AND at least 50% in each standard
- Students may reread passages as needed
- Read directions aloud if needed for students with accommodations

## Section 1: "The Cartographer's Daughter" (Prose — Realistic Fiction)
*Standards assessed: RL.7.1 (Evidence/Inference), RL.7.2 (Theme)*

> Navya's father drew maps for the city transportation department — bus routes, subway lines, the colored veins and arteries that carried people from one place to another. His maps were taped to the walls of his office, layered over each other like the rings of a tree, each revision adding a route, removing a stop, adjusting for a new neighborhood that had not existed the year before.
>
> Navya loved the maps. Not because she cared about bus routes, but because her father drew them by hand first — pencil on vellum, careful and slow — before transferring them to the computer. The hand-drawn versions had a quality the digital ones did not: small imperfections. A line that curved slightly where it should have been straight. A label written at a slight angle. Her father called these "human signatures" and said they were the proof that a person had made something, not a machine.
>
> When Navya was twelve, the department switched to a software program that generated maps automatically. No pencil, no vellum, no human signatures. Her father's hand-drawn maps were archived in a closet. He continued going to work, but he came home quieter, the way a room is quieter after someone turns off the music.
>
> One evening, Navya found him at the kitchen table with a blank sheet of vellum. He was drawing a map of their neighborhood — not a transit map, but a personal one. He marked the bakery where they bought bread on Saturday mornings, the bench where Navya had learned to tie her shoes, the storm drain where a family of ducks appeared every spring. He drew a dotted line connecting all the places and labeled it "The Route That Matters."
>
> "This one is not for the department," he said.
>
> "I know," Navya said. She sat down across from him. "Can I add the library? You always forget the library."
>
> Her father smiled — the first real smile she had seen from him in weeks — and handed her the pencil.

B. Navya's father finds personal meaning and identity in the craft of drawing by hand, and the loss of that craft has diminished something essential in him.
C. Navya wants to become a cartographer like her father when she grows up.
D. The transportation department made a mistake by switching to software because the maps are lower quality.

**2.** (RL.7.1 — Short Answer — 3 points) Cite at least two pieces of evidence that show the impact of the software switch on Navya's father. Explain how the pieces of evidence work together to reveal the depth of that impact.

**3.** (RL.7.2 — Short Answer — 3 points) What is the theme of this passage? State it as a complete sentence and identify two specific moments where the theme is developed.

**4.** (RL.7.2 — Short Answer — 3 points) Write an objective summary of this passage in 3-4 sentences. Your summary must include key events and the central idea without personal opinions or evaluative language.

**5.** (RL.7.1 — Short Answer — 3 points) Navya says, "Can I add the library? You always forget the library." What does this moment reveal about the relationship between Navya and her father? Cite this evidence and at least one other detail to support your inference.

## Section 2: "The River and the Road" (Two Narrators — Contrasting Perspectives)
*Standards assessed: RL.7.6 (Point of View), RL.7.2 (Theme Development)*

> **Narrator 1 — Amira:**
>
> The river is the first thing I see every morning. My bedroom window faces east, and the Willow Creek runs along the bottom of the hill behind our house. In winter, it freezes at the edges, and the ice catches the sunrise like shattered glass. In spring, it floods and turns brown, carrying branches and leaves from somewhere upstream. I know the river the way I know my own handwriting — every bend, every shallow, every rock where the water splits and rejoins.
>
> When I heard the town council planned to reroute the creek to make room for a new access road, I felt something break inside me — not dramatically, not like a bone snapping, but like a thread pulling loose from fabric. The river was not just water. It was my clock, my calendar, my proof that something in the world stayed constant while everything else changed.
>
> **Narrator 2 — Mr. Sadiq (Amira's father):**
>
> When the town council announced the road project, I read the full proposal: two lanes, a sidewalk, a traffic light at the intersection where three accidents had happened in the past year. The creek would be redirected into a concrete channel that ran underground for two hundred meters before resurfacing east of the highway.
>
> I understand my daughter's attachment to the creek. She has watched it from her window since she was small. But I also remember carrying groceries four blocks in February because the nearest road did not connect to our street. I remember Fatima — my wife, Amira's mother — driving twenty minutes to reach a hospital that should have been five minutes away because the road infrastructure in this part of town was built decades ago for half the current population.
>
> Amira sees the creek as a constant. I see it as a barrier — beautiful, yes, but a barrier that has cost this neighborhood time, money, and in some cases, safety. The road will not replace the creek. It will redirect it. And the neighborhood will finally have what the east side of town has had for years: access.
>
> I have not told Amira about the accidents or the hospital. She is thirteen. She will understand the trade-offs when she is older. For now, I let her grieve the creek. She is not wrong to love it.

**Questions:**

**6.** (RL.7.6 — Short Answer — 3 points) How does the author develop and contrast Amira's and Mr. Sadiq's points of view? For each character, identify one technique the author uses (diction, sensory focus, selective detail, or tone) and cite evidence.

**7.** (RL.7.6 — Multiple Choice — 2 points) What is the primary difference between Amira's and Mr. Sadiq's perspectives on the creek?

A. Amira sees the creek as beautiful and her father sees it as ugly.
B. Amira values the creek for its emotional and personal significance, while her father values practical access and safety for the neighborhood.
C. Amira wants to keep the creek and her father wants to destroy it completely.
D. Amira understands the trade-offs and her father does not.

**8.** (RL.7.6 — Short Answer — 3 points) Mr. Sadiq says, "I have not told Amira about the accidents or the hospital. She is thirteen. She will understand the trade-offs when she is older." What does this decision reveal about his point of view as a parent? How does it shape what Amira can and cannot see about the situation?

**9.** (RL.7.2 — Short Answer — 3 points) How does the theme develop across the two narrators' accounts? Identify the theme and explain how each narrator's perspective contributes a different dimension to it.

## Section 3: "The Clockmaker's Apprentice" (Prose — Limited Narrator)
*Standards assessed: RL.7.6 (Unreliable/Limited Narrator), RL.7.1 (Evidence)*

> I am the best apprentice Mr. Voss has ever had. I know this because he gives me the most difficult repairs — the antique mantel clocks with worn escapements, the cuckoo clocks with stripped gears, the pocket watches with hairspring fractures so fine you need a magnifying loupe to see them. He would not give me the hard ones if he did not trust my skill.
>
> The other apprentice, Tomoko, gets the simple jobs: battery replacements, band adjustments, crystal buffing. She has been here six months longer than I have, but she still works on the easy repairs. I think Mr. Voss is being kind to her — letting her work at her own pace — but the truth is, she is not as precise as I am. My hands are steadier. My adjustments are tighter. These are not opinions. They are observations.
>
> Yesterday, a woman brought in a wristwatch that had been her grandmother's. She said it was worth more than her car. Mr. Voss examined it, made some notes, and placed it in the repair queue. This morning, I saw it on Tomoko's bench.
>
> I asked Mr. Voss about it. He said, "Tomoko has the right touch for this one."
>
> I do not understand what that means. Touch is touch. Precision is precision. Either the repair is executed correctly or it is not.
>
> At lunch, I watched Tomoko working on the watch through the glass partition. She held it the way you hold a baby bird — barely touching, as if the weight of her fingers alone might damage it. She spent ten minutes just looking at it before she picked up a tool. When she finally made her first adjustment, her movement was so small I almost missed it.
>
> I went back to my bench and picked up the mantel clock I had been repairing. The escapement clicked into place on the first try. A perfect repair. I looked at the clock and then at Tomoko through the glass. For the first time, I wondered if there was a difference between precision and care — and if Mr. Voss had been trying to teach me that all along.

**Questions:**

**10.** (RL.7.6 — Short Answer — 3 points) Identify the source of the narrator's limitation at the beginning of the passage. What does the narrator believe, and what does the text suggest is actually happening? Cite at least two pieces of evidence for each level.

**11.** (RL.7.6 — Multiple Choice — 2 points) When the narrator says "These are not opinions. They are observations," what does this statement most likely reveal?

A. The narrator is objectively correct about their superiority.
B. The narrator is unaware that their assessment is shaped by pride and a narrow definition of skill.
C. The narrator is lying about their abilities.
D. Mr. Voss agrees with the narrator's self-assessment.

**12.** (RL.7.1 — Short Answer — 3 points) Cite the evidence describing how Tomoko handles the grandmother's watch. What inference can you draw about why Mr. Voss chose Tomoko for this particular repair? Use at least two pieces of evidence.

**13.** (RL.7.1 — Short Answer — 3 points) The narrator says, "For the first time, I wondered if there was a difference between precision and care." Cite this evidence and at least one earlier detail to explain how the narrator's understanding has begun to shift by the end of the passage.

## Section 4: "The Crossing" (Paired Passages — Fiction and Historical Account)
*Standards assessed: RL.7.9 (Fiction vs. History), RL.7.1 (Evidence)*

**Passage A: Historical Account — "Angel Island Immigration Station, 1910-1940"**

> Angel Island Immigration Station, located in San Francisco Bay, served as the processing center for hundreds of thousands of immigrants entering the United States through the West Coast. While immigrants from many nations passed through Angel Island, Chinese immigrants faced the longest detentions. Under the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and its extensions, Chinese workers were largely barred from entering the country, and those who claimed exemptions — as merchants, students, or family members of citizens — were subjected to intensive interrogation.
>
> Detainees were held in crowded barracks, sometimes for weeks or months, while their claims were investigated. They were separated from family members upon arrival and had limited contact with the outside world. The conditions were difficult: the barracks were cold, the food was unfamiliar to many detainees, and the uncertainty of the process caused significant emotional distress.
>
> Many detainees carved or wrote poems on the walls of the barracks. These poems, rediscovered in 1970, expressed feelings of frustration, longing, and defiance. They remain a powerful record of the immigrant experience at Angel Island.

**Passage B: Fictional Portrayal — "The Wall Poem"**

> On the fourteenth day, Wei Lin found the poems. She had been staring at the walls of the women's barracks for two weeks — wooden walls, unpainted, rough to the touch — and she had thought the scratches were damage. Claw marks from rats, maybe, or gouges from furniture being dragged. But on the fourteenth day, a woman from Guangdong named Mrs. Fong pointed to a section of wall near the window and said, "Read."
>
> Wei Lin leaned close. The scratches were characters — Chinese characters, carved into the wood with something sharp. A hairpin, maybe, or a nail. The poem was four lines long:
>
> *I left my village with the spring planting.*
> *Now the autumn harvest has passed and I am still here.*
> *The walls do not answer when I speak.*
> *But they hold the words of those who came before me.*
>
> Wei Lin ran her finger along the grooves of the characters. Someone had stood in this exact spot — maybe a year ago, maybe ten — and carved these words so that the next person would know they were not alone.
>
> She found more poems on other walls. Some were angry. Some were sad. One was only four characters: *I will not break.*
>
> That night, Wei Lin could not sleep. The cot was thin, the room was cold, and the sound of the bay outside was the loneliest sound she had ever heard. She lay awake and thought about the woman who had carved *I will not break.* She wondered if the woman had entered the country or been sent back. She wondered if it mattered. The words were here. The woman had left something permanent in a place designed to be temporary.
>
> In the morning, Wei Lin borrowed a hairpin from Mrs. Fong. She knelt by the wall near the door and carved her own line: *The walls remember what the country forgets.*

**Questions:**

**14.** (RL.7.9 — Short Answer — 3 points) Identify two historical facts that the fictional passage preserves from the historical account. For each, cite evidence from both passages showing the connection.

**15.** (RL.7.9 — Short Answer — 3 points) What does the fictional passage add to the reader's understanding of the wall poems that the historical account cannot provide? Cite at least one detail from the fiction and explain what it reveals about the human experience of detention.

**16.** (RL.7.9 — Multiple Choice — 2 points) Wei Lin carves the line: "The walls remember what the country forgets." What does this fictional addition most likely represent?

A. Wei Lin's belief that the building will eventually be torn down.
B. The author's attempt to show that the poems were an act of resistance and collective memory against a system designed to erase the detainees' presence.
C. Wei Lin's anger at the other detainees for not speaking up.
D. The historical fact that the poems were rediscovered in 1970.

**17.** (RL.7.1 — Short Answer — 3 points) In the fictional passage, Wei Lin thinks: "She wondered if the woman had entered the country or been sent back. She wondered if it mattered." Cite this evidence and explain the inference Wei Lin is making about the significance of the poems. Use at least one additional piece of evidence to support your analysis.

## Section 5: "The Inheritance" (Prose — Extended Response)
*Standards assessed: All four standards (RL.7.1, RL.7.2, RL.7.6, RL.7.9)*

> **Historical context:** In many cultures, oral storytelling has been the primary method of preserving history and transmitting knowledge across generations. Before written records, stories carried genealogies, moral teachings, agricultural knowledge, and community identity from elders to young people. The introduction of written language and, later, digital technology has gradually displaced oral tradition in many communities.

> **Fictional passage: "The Voice and the Page"**
>
> Grandmother Kofi did not read or write. She had never learned — not because she lacked intelligence, but because the village where she grew up in Ghana had no school, and by the time one was built, she was already raising children and had no time for classrooms. She carried everything she knew in her voice: the history of the family going back five generations, the names of every cousin and second cousin, the story of how her grandfather walked two hundred miles to find work in the cocoa fields, the recipe for groundnut soup that used no measurements, only the feel of the ingredients in her hands.
>
> Her granddaughter, Abena, lived in Columbus, Ohio, and carried a laptop everywhere. She was fourteen and kept a digital journal, a family tree app, and a recipe folder organized by cuisine. When Grandmother Kofi visited for three weeks in July, Abena opened the family tree app and said, "Tell me everyone. I will type it all in."
>
> Grandmother Kofi began speaking. She started with her own parents and moved backward — names, marriages, births, migrations, feuds, reconciliations. Abena typed as fast as she could, but the information came in a form the app did not accommodate. "Your great-great-uncle Kwame married a woman from the river village — I do not remember the year, but it was the year the bridge washed out." The app wanted a year. Grandmother Kofi gave a story.
>
> When they reached the groundnut soup recipe, the problem deepened. Grandmother Kofi described the process in gestures and sounds — "You heat the oil until it sings, then you add the onions and wait until they whisper" — but Abena's recipe folder needed tablespoons and temperatures. She tried to translate: "heat oil on medium-high, add one diced onion, cook until translucent." She read it back to Grandmother Kofi.
>
> "That is not my recipe," Grandmother Kofi said. "That is someone else's recipe using my ingredients."
>
> Abena closed the laptop. She sat at the kitchen table across from her grandmother and listened — not to record, not to type, but to hear. Grandmother Kofi talked for two hours. She described the smell of the cocoa fields, the weight of a water jug on her head, the sound of her mother's voice calling the children in from the road at dusk. None of it fit in an app.
>
> That evening, Abena opened a blank document and tried to write what she remembered. She got some of it — the names, the major events, the migration story. But the things she remembered most clearly were the things she could not type: the way Grandmother Kofi's hands moved when she described the soup, the way her voice dropped when she mentioned her grandfather's walk, the pause before she said the name of a relative who had died young.
>
> Abena saved the document. She also saved the memory of the things the document could not hold.

## Answer Key

### Section 1: "The Cartographer's Daughter"

**1.** (RL.7.1 — 2 points) **B.** Navya's father finds personal meaning and identity in the craft of drawing by hand, and the loss of that craft has diminished something essential in him. Evidence includes his concept of "human signatures," his quieter demeanor after the software switch, and his creation of a personal map at the kitchen table — all of which point to a man whose identity is rooted in handcraft. Option A is not supported — there is no evidence of anger. Options C and D are not supported by the text.

**2.** (RL.7.1 — 3 points) The first piece of evidence is that after the switch, "he came home quieter, the way a room is quieter after someone turns off the music." This simile reveals that the software change did not just affect his work — it silenced something in him, as if his creative expression has been turned off. The second piece of evidence is that he begins drawing a personal map at the kitchen table — not for the department but for himself. This shows that the need to draw by hand was not just professional but personal; when the job no longer required it, he sought another outlet. Together, these details move from internal impact (quietness, diminished energy) to active response (creating a new map on his own terms), showing that the loss was deep enough to change his daily demeanor but not deep enough to extinguish the impulse to create.

**3.** (RL.7.2 — 3 points) Theme: The most meaningful things we create are not the ones that serve a system but the ones that carry the marks of who we are — and when our personal expression is removed from our work, we must find new ways to preserve it. The theme is first developed when the father explains "human signatures" — the imperfections in hand-drawn maps that prove a person made them. This introduces the idea that personal marks have value. The theme deepens when the software replaces hand-drawn maps and the father becomes quieter — the removal of personal craft has a tangible emotional cost. The theme crystallizes when the father draws the personal neighborhood map labeled "The Route That Matters" — he redirects his craft from institutional use to personal meaning, and Navya joins him, suggesting the theme extends to legacy and connection.

**4.** (RL.7.2 — 3 points) Navya's father draws transit maps by hand for the city transportation department, valuing the small imperfections he calls "human signatures" as proof of personal craftsmanship. When the department replaces hand-drawn maps with automated software, his hand-drawn maps are archived and he becomes noticeably quieter at home. He begins drawing a personal map of the family's neighborhood, marking meaningful locations and labeling the route "The Route That Matters." Navya joins him, asking to add the library, and the act of drawing together restores something the software transition had diminished.

**5.** (RL.7.1 — 3 points) Navya's request to add the library — and her gentle teasing that "You always forget the library" — reveals a relationship of familiarity, warmth, and shared creative language. The fact that Navya knows which places her father would include and which he would forget suggests she has been paying close attention to his way of seeing the world. This connects to the earlier detail that "Navya loved the maps" not for their transit function but because her father drew them by hand. Together, these pieces of evidence show that Navya's bond with her father runs through creative collaboration — she understands his craft, shares his values, and knows him well enough to complete his work. Her addition of the library also suggests she is now a co-creator of the family's personal map, continuing the tradition of "human signatures."

### Section 2: "The River and the Road"

**6.** (RL.7.6 — 3 points) Amira's perspective is developed through **sensory focus** — she describes the creek in intimate, seasonal detail: "the ice catches the sunrise like shattered glass," "it floods and turns brown." Her language is poetic and personal, reflecting someone who experiences the creek as a living companion. Mr. Sadiq's perspective is developed through **selective detail** — he includes specific practical information: "two lanes, a sidewalk, a traffic light at the intersection where three accidents had happened," distances, and the hospital commute time. His language is logistical and community-oriented. The contrast reveals that Amira and Mr. Sadiq are operating in different registers — one emotional and personal, the other practical and communal — and both are valid responses to the same situation.

**7.** (RL.7.6 — 2 points) **B.** Amira values the creek for its emotional and personal significance, while her father values practical access and safety for the neighborhood.

**8.** (RL.7.6 — 3 points) Mr. Sadiq's decision not to tell Amira about the accidents or the hospital reveals a parental perspective that combines protectiveness with patience. He withholds information not to deceive but because he believes she is too young to weigh safety data against emotional attachment — "She will understand the trade-offs when she is older." This decision shapes what Amira can see: without knowing about the accidents, she perceives the road project only as a threat to something she loves, not as a solution to a safety problem. Mr. Sadiq's withholding creates a form of limited narration in Amira's account — her perspective is genuine but incomplete because her father has filtered the information she receives. The final line — "She is not wrong to love it" — shows that Mr. Sadiq respects Amira's perspective even while knowing it is based on incomplete information.

**9.** (RL.7.2 — 3 points) The theme is that progress and loss often coexist, and the same change can be liberation for one person and grief for another — and both responses reflect real, legitimate values. Amira's narration develops the theme by presenting the creek as a symbol of constancy and identity — "my clock, my calendar, my proof that something in the world stayed constant." Through her perspective, the theme's dimension of loss is established: progress costs something valuable. Mr. Sadiq's narration develops the theme's other dimension: the creek has also been a barrier that has cost the neighborhood time, money, and safety. His phrase "the road will not replace the creek. It will redirect it" adds the complication that progress does not have to be total destruction, but Amira cannot see this nuance because she does not have his information. Together, the two perspectives develop a theme that is richer than either one alone — it is not about whether the road or the creek is more important, but about the complexity of valuing both.

### Section 3: "The Clockmaker's Apprentice"

**10.** (RL.7.6 — 3 points) The narrator's limitation at the beginning is pride-based self-deception. The narrator believes they are the best apprentice because they receive the most difficult repairs: "He would not give me the hard ones if he did not trust my skill." They also claim their hands are "steadier" and adjustments "tighter" and insist "These are not opinions. They are observations." However, the text suggests a different reality. Mr. Voss gives the most valuable watch to Tomoko, not the narrator — and when asked, he says Tomoko has "the right touch for this one." The narrator dismisses this ("Touch is touch. Precision is precision") but the text then shows Tomoko holding the watch "the way you hold a baby bird" and spending ten minutes looking before touching it. These details suggest that Mr. Voss has been giving the narrator the difficult-but-not-delicate repairs because the narrator has precision but may lack the gentleness and patience required for truly irreplaceable objects. The gap between the narrator's self-assessment and Mr. Voss's actions reveals the limitation.

**11.** (RL.7.6 — 2 points) **B.** The narrator is unaware that their assessment is shaped by pride and a narrow definition of skill.

**12.** (RL.7.1 — 3 points) The text says Tomoko "held it the way you hold a baby bird — barely touching, as if the weight of her fingers alone might damage it" and "spent ten minutes just looking at it before she picked up a tool." These details suggest Tomoko approaches the watch with reverence and patience — she understands that a watch worth more than a car requires a different kind of attention than a standard repair. The inference is that Mr. Voss chose Tomoko because the grandmother's watch needed care, not just precision. The narrator's four-second bobbin-exchange speed (metaphorically speaking) is the wrong skill for an object that requires slowness and tenderness. Tomoko's first movement was "so small I almost missed it," indicating a level of delicacy that the narrator — who values speed and tightness — does not practice.

**13.** (RL.7.1 — 3 points) The narrator's wondering "if there was a difference between precision and care" represents a fundamental shift from the beginning of the passage, where the narrator defined skill entirely through precision: "My hands are steadier. My adjustments are tighter." The earlier statement shows someone who measures quality in mechanical terms — accuracy, speed, tightness. The final reflection introduces a new category — care — that includes qualities the narrator had not valued: patience, gentleness, observation before action. The shift does not mean the narrator abandons precision but rather begins to understand that skill is broader than they thought. The phrase "if Mr. Voss had been trying to teach me that all along" suggests the narrator is reconsidering the entire apprenticeship through a new lens: perhaps the "difficult repairs" were not rewards for excellence but lessons in one dimension of skill, while Tomoko's "simple jobs" were developing a different and equally important dimension.

### Section 4: "The Crossing"

**14.** (RL.7.9 — 3 points) First, both passages describe long detentions — the historical account states "detainees were held in crowded barracks, sometimes for weeks or months," and the fiction opens with "On the fourteenth day," establishing that Wei Lin has been detained for two weeks. Second, both passages address the wall poems. The historical account states "Many detainees carved or wrote poems on the walls of the barracks" and the fiction shows Wei Lin discovering carved Chinese characters and reading a four-line poem. The fiction preserves the historical fact that the poems were physically carved into the wood, and the historical account's description of poems expressing "frustration, longing, and defiance" is reflected in the fictional poems' range of emotions — sadness, anger, and determination ("I will not break").

**15.** (RL.7.9 — 3 points) The fictional passage adds the personal experience of discovering the poems — the moment-by-moment emotional journey of reading words left by someone in the same situation. When Wei Lin runs "her finger along the grooves of the characters" and realizes "someone had stood in this exact spot" and carved these words "so that the next person would know they were not alone," the fiction reveals the poems as acts of human connection across time, not just historical artifacts. The historical account describes the poems as "a powerful record of the immigrant experience," but the fiction shows what receiving that record felt like to an individual. The detail about Wei Lin carving her own line adds something the historical account cannot: the active decision to contribute to the wall, showing the poems as a living conversation between detainees across years, not just relics to be studied later.

**16.** (RL.7.9 — 2 points) **B.** The author's attempt to show that the poems were an act of resistance and collective memory against a system designed to erase the detainees' presence.

**17.** (RL.7.1 — 3 points) Wei Lin's reflection — "She wondered if the woman had entered the country or been sent back. She wondered if it mattered" — reveals an inference about the poems' significance that goes beyond individual outcomes. Wei Lin is reasoning that the value of the poem does not depend on whether the poet succeeded in entering the country. The words themselves are the achievement: they are "permanent in a place designed to be temporary." This detail, combined with Wei Lin's earlier observation that someone carved the poem "so that the next person would know they were not alone," builds the inference that the poems served a dual purpose: they were both personal expression (processing grief, anger, and defiance) and communal resistance (ensuring that the experience of detention could not be erased). Wei Lin's decision to add her own poem the next morning confirms this inference — she chooses to participate in the tradition, valuing the act of speaking through the wall over the question of whether anyone will answer.

### Section 5: "The Inheritance"

**18.** (RL.7.1 — 3 points) First, Grandmother Kofi describes family history through stories rather than dates: "Your great-great-uncle Kwame married a woman from the river village — I do not remember the year, but it was the year the bridge washed out." The app wants a year; Grandmother Kofi gives a narrative marker. This shows that her knowledge system is organized by event and meaning, not by chronology. Second, the recipe gap is even deeper: Grandmother Kofi describes cooking through sensory language — "You heat the oil until it sings, then you add the onions and wait until they whisper" — while Abena's folder needs "tablespoons and temperatures." The translation from sensory knowledge to measurement strips the recipe of its human dimension. Third, after listening for two hours, Abena finds that "the things she remembered most clearly were the things she could not type: the way Grandmother Kofi's hands moved... the way her voice dropped... the pause before she said the name of a relative who had died young." This third piece of evidence deepens the gap from information to embodiment: Grandmother Kofi's knowledge lives in gesture, tone, and silence — modalities that no digital tool can capture. Together, these three details build from factual gap (dates vs. stories) through sensory gap (measurements vs. intuition) to existential gap (data vs. embodied memory), showing that the divide between oral and digital preservation is not just technical but fundamental.

**19.** (RL.7.2 — 5 points) Theme: Some forms of knowledge — particularly those carried in the body, voice, and lived experience — cannot be fully translated into written or digital records, and the attempt to preserve them requires accepting that something will always be lost while still valuing the effort.

The theme is introduced when Grandmother Kofi's oral knowledge meets Abena's digital tools. Abena opens the family tree app and says "Tell me everyone. I will type it all in." This moment establishes the assumption that oral knowledge can be transferred to digital format — an assumption the rest of the passage will challenge. The theme is planted as an unexamined confidence in technology's ability to capture everything.

The theme is complicated through two moments of failure. First, the app cannot accommodate Grandmother Kofi's way of organizing history — "it was the year the bridge washed out" instead of a calendar year. The system's categories do not match the knowledge's form. Second, and more powerfully, the recipe translation fails: Grandmother Kofi's response — "That is not my recipe. That is someone else's recipe using my ingredients" — makes the complication explicit. The problem is not that the information is lost but that it is transformed into something different. The translation preserves the ingredients but loses the person.

The theme crystallizes in the final paragraphs. Abena "saved the document" but "also saved the memory of the things the document could not hold." This dual saving — digital and personal — represents the passage's resolution: preservation is not a choice between oral and written but an acceptance that both are needed and neither is complete. The things the document cannot hold (gestures, vocal tone, pauses) are preserved only in Abena's living memory, which means they will be lost when she is gone unless she, too, finds a way to pass them on. The theme reaches its fullest expression: the inheritance of knowledge is always partial, and each generation must decide what to preserve and how to grieve what is lost.

**20.** (RL.7.6 — 3 points) Grandmother Kofi's perspective is developed through **diction and sensory language** — she describes knowledge through physical experience: the oil "sings," the onions "whisper," the water jug has "weight." Her words are embodied — they live in the senses and the body, not in abstractions. Abena's perspective is developed through **actions and tools** — she opens apps, types, organizes folders, and saves documents. Her engagement with knowledge is mediated by technology. The contrast reveals two fundamentally different ways of understanding what "preserving knowledge" means. For Grandmother Kofi, knowledge is preserved by being lived and spoken. For Abena, knowledge is preserved by being recorded and stored. The moment of contrast is sharpest when Grandmother Kofi rejects the translated recipe: Abena's version has the same ingredients and steps, but Grandmother Kofi recognizes that the human dimension — the intuition, the sensory relationship with the food — has been replaced by measurement. The contrast reveals that preservation is not neutral — the medium changes the message.

**21.** (RL.7.9 — 3 points) The historical context describes the displacement of oral tradition in general terms: "The introduction of written language and, later, digital technology has gradually displaced oral tradition in many communities." This is an accurate but abstract statement. The fiction shows what that displacement looks and feels like in a specific family: a grandmother whose knowledge "lives in her voice" and a granddaughter whose tools cannot accommodate that knowledge. The historical account tells us oral tradition has been displaced; the fiction shows us the moment of displacement happening — the failed recipe translation, the app that cannot hold narrative markers instead of dates, and Grandmother Kofi's recognition that translation changes the knowledge itself. The fiction also reveals something the history cannot: the emotional and relational dimensions of the loss. When Abena closes the laptop and "listened — not to record, not to type, but to hear," she temporarily abandons the digital mediation that the historical account describes as progress. This moment of pure listening is the fiction's most powerful addition — it shows that preserving oral tradition may require setting aside the very tools that were supposed to help.

**22.** (RL.7.2 — 3 points) When Grandmother Kofi visits her granddaughter Abena in Ohio, Abena attempts to record the family history and recipes using digital tools. The effort encounters repeated difficulties because Grandmother Kofi's knowledge is organized through stories, sensory descriptions, and embodied experience rather than the dates, measurements, and categories the apps require. After Grandmother Kofi rejects the digitally translated recipe as no longer her own, Abena closes the laptop and listens without recording. She later writes what she can remember while acknowledging that the most vivid elements — gestures, vocal tone, and meaningful pauses — cannot be captured in text.

**23.** (RL.7.6 — 5 points) Grandmother Kofi's statement — "That is not my recipe. That is someone else's recipe using my ingredients" — draws a sharp distinction between information and identity. From Grandmother Kofi's perspective, a recipe is not a list of steps and measurements but a relationship between the cook and the food. Her recipe lives in sensory knowledge: heating oil "until it sings" and adding onions "until they whisper." These descriptions are not imprecise — they are precise in a different language, one that measures through bodily experience rather than instruments. When Abena translates this into "heat oil on medium-high, add one diced onion, cook until translucent," the ingredients are the same but the knowledge system is different. Grandmother Kofi recognizes this immediately: the recipe has been stripped of the person who made it.

Before this moment, Abena likely understood the recipe as a sequence of ingredients and steps that could be captured in any format — text, app, handwritten card. She approached preservation as a transfer problem: move the information from one container (voice) to another (document). The recipe rejection forces Abena to reconsider what "preservation" means — it is not just about capturing the information but about maintaining the relationship between the knowledge and the knower.

This moment contrasts both characters' perspectives on knowledge itself. Grandmother Kofi's perspective, developed through her sensory language and embodied demonstrations, treats knowledge as inseparable from the person carrying it. Abena's perspective, developed through her reliance on digital tools and categorical organization, initially treats knowledge as portable and transferable. The statement "someone else's recipe using my ingredients" crystallizes the contrast: the same ingredients can produce a different recipe when the human element is removed.

The passage provides additional support for this analysis. Earlier, Grandmother Kofi organizes family history by events rather than years — "the year the bridge washed out" — showing that her knowledge system is fundamentally narrative rather than data-driven. Later, Abena discovers that "the things she remembered most clearly were the things she could not type," confirming that the most essential elements of Grandmother Kofi's knowledge resist digital translation. Together, these details and the recipe rejection develop a contrast between two legitimate but incompatible ways of knowing: one that lives in the body and voice, and one that lives in text and technology.

### Scoring Notes

**Multiple-choice questions (1, 7, 11, 16):** 2 points each. Full credit for correct answer only.

**Short-answer questions (2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13, 14, 15, 17, 18, 20, 21, 22):** 3 points each, scored as follows:
- 3 points: Complete, accurate response with cited evidence and clear explanation
- 2 points: Mostly accurate response with evidence but explanation is partial or vague
- 1 point: Response shows basic understanding but lacks evidence or explanation
- 0 points: Response is inaccurate, off-topic, or missing

**Summary question (4, 22):** 3 points each, scored as follows:
- 3 points: Objective summary with key events and central idea, no personal opinions
- 2 points: Summary includes key events but either misses the central idea or includes minor opinion language
- 1 point: Incomplete summary or contains significant opinion language
- 0 points: Not a summary (pure opinion or unrelated content)

**Extended response (19, 23):** 5 points each, scored as follows:
- 5 points: Theme/analysis stated clearly; three or more specific moments identified and explained; clear connection between moments and development; written in student's own words with proper citation
- 4 points: Analysis stated accurately; two moments identified and well-explained; mostly clear connection to development
- 3 points: Analysis stated but may be vague or incomplete; at least one moment identified; some connection to development
- 2 points: Analysis partially identified; limited evidence; weak or unclear development
- 1 point: Attempt made but analysis is a topic (single word) or evidence is absent
- 0 points: No attempt or completely off-topic

**General scoring principles:**
- For evidence citation questions, the student must use quotation marks correctly around exact text. Minor punctuation errors within the citation should not cost full credit if the quote itself is accurate.
- Accept reasonable variations in theme statements as long as the student expresses a complete thought (not just a topic word) that is supported by the text.
- For inference questions, the student must provide a logical conclusion supported by text evidence. Unsupported guesses do not earn credit.
- For point of view questions, the student must identify the perspective or technique, cite evidence, and explain its effect on the reader's understanding.
- For fiction vs. history questions, the student must reference both texts and explain what the comparison reveals.
- For multi-evidence questions (RL.7.1), the student must cite the specified number of evidence pieces and explain how they work together — listing quotes without connecting them does not earn full credit.
