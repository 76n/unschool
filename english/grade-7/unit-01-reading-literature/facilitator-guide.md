# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1: Reading Literature

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview
Unit 1 builds on the literary analysis foundation established in Grade 6 by deepening and extending four essential close-reading skills. Where sixth graders learned to cite textual evidence, seventh graders must now weave together several pieces of evidence to support both explicit and inferential analysis (RL.7.1). Where sixth graders identified theme and summarized its development, seventh graders must trace how themes develop over the course of a text and distinguish objective summary from personal response (RL.7.2). The unit introduces a significant new challenge in point of view analysis: rather than simply explaining how an author develops a narrator's perspective (RL.6.6), seventh graders must now analyze how authors develop and contrast the points of view of different characters or narrators within the same text (RL.7.6), including recognizing unreliable and limited narration. Finally, the unit adds a comparative dimension through RL.7.9, asking students to compare fictional portrayals with historical accounts to understand how authors use or alter history. Across ten lessons, students progress from strengthening evidence skills through thematic and perspective analysis to integrated literary analysis, working with original fiction passages featuring diverse characters and settings appropriate for 12-13 year olds.

### Prerequisite Skills (Grade 6)
Students entering this unit should be able to:
- Cite textual evidence to support analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the text (RL.6.1)
- Determine a theme or central idea of a text and how it is conveyed through particular details; provide a summary distinct from personal opinions (RL.6.2)
- Analyze how a particular sentence, chapter, scene, or stanza fits into the overall structure of a text and contributes to the development of theme, setting, or plot (RL.6.5)
- Explain how an author develops the point of view of the narrator or speaker in a text (RL.6.6)
- Write evidence-based responses using the claim-evidence-explanation structure

If students struggle with these prerequisite skills, use the interleaved review sections in each lesson to reinforce them before moving forward.

### Facilitation Tips
- **Multi-evidence anchor chart:** From Lesson 1, maintain a visible chart showing how to weave multiple pieces of evidence into a single analytical response. Unlike Grade 6 where students learned the Evidence Sandwich (claim-evidence-explanation), Grade 7 builds toward multi-evidence paragraphs where students connect two or three quotes with transitional reasoning.
- **Think-alouds remain essential:** Model your analytical thinking frequently, especially when demonstrating how to choose which pieces of evidence work together rather than in isolation. Seventh graders benefit from seeing how an experienced reader decides that three pieces of evidence are stronger together than any one alone.
- **Point of view tracking charts:** For Lessons 6-8, provide students with two-column or three-column charts where they can track how different characters perceive the same events. This visual tool makes the abstract concept of contrasting perspectives concrete and manageable.
- **Historical context scaffolding:** Lesson 9 requires students to compare fiction with historical accounts. Pre-teach essential historical context so that students can focus on analysis rather than struggling with unfamiliar background knowledge.
- **Turn and discuss with accountability:** Before whole-group sharing, give students 60-90 seconds to discuss with a partner. At this grade level, add accountability by asking pairs to identify their strongest piece of evidence and be ready to share it with the class.
- **Flexible grouping:** Use exit ticket data to form small re-teaching groups. Students who do not meet mastery on a given lesson should receive targeted support before moving on, particularly for the conceptually demanding Lessons 7-9.
- **Passage preview:** Read all passages before the lesson. Some contain themes of personal conflict, cultural identity, and historical injustice that may benefit from a brief content introduction.
- **Cross-lesson connections:** Explicitly link concepts across lessons. For example, when analyzing contrasting perspectives in Lesson 6, remind students to cite several pieces of evidence (Lesson 1) and connect those perspectives to theme development (Lessons 3-4).

### Common Misconceptions
| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| Citing several pieces of evidence means listing multiple quotes without connecting them | Citing several pieces of evidence means selecting multiple quotes that work together to build a layered argument. Each piece of evidence should add something new to the analysis, and the student must explain how the pieces connect to one another and to the overall claim. |
| Theme is stated directly somewhere in the text | In complex texts, theme is rarely stated explicitly. Students must infer theme by examining patterns in character development, conflict resolution, imagery, and the author's choices. Theme emerges from the text as a whole, not from a single sentence. |
| An objective summary should include every detail from the text | An objective summary captures the key events, ideas, and outcomes without including every detail. More importantly, it must be free of personal opinion, judgment, or evaluative language. Brevity and neutrality are both essential. |
| Point of view means the character's opinion about something | Point of view in literary analysis refers to the lens through which a character perceives and interprets events. Different characters may witness the same event but understand it differently based on their experiences, knowledge, biases, and emotional states. Contrasting points of view is about analyzing these different interpretive lenses, not just listing different opinions. |
| An unreliable narrator is lying to the reader | An unreliable narrator does not necessarily lie. Unreliability can come from limited knowledge, emotional bias, youth, memory gaps, or self-deception. Recognizing unreliability means noticing gaps between what the narrator claims and what the text's details suggest. |
| Comparing fiction and history means finding factual errors in the fiction | Comparing fiction with historical accounts under RL.7.9 means analyzing how and why the author chose to use, alter, or reimagine historical facts for literary purposes. The goal is not to catch mistakes but to understand the author's creative and thematic choices. |

### Assessment Guidance
- **Formative:** Use the exit ticket in each lesson to gauge daily mastery. Students scoring below the stated mastery threshold should receive re-teaching before the next lesson. The exit tickets progress in complexity across the unit, so early intervention prevents compounding gaps.
- **Summative:** The unit assessment includes five sections covering all four standards with original passages, multiple-choice items, short-answer questions, and extended-response prompts. Administer it after Lesson 10. Students should score at least 70% overall (42/60 points) and demonstrate competency in each standard (at least 50% per standard).
- **Portfolio option:** If your program uses portfolios, include the student's Lesson 10 integrated analysis response and the unit assessment as evidence of growth. Students' multi-evidence annotations from Lessons 1-2 and their fiction/history comparison from Lesson 9 also make strong portfolio artifacts.

### Differentiation at a Glance
- **Below level:** Provide pre-reading vocabulary support; allow paired reading; use the "On-level" tier in independent practice; offer sentence frames for multi-evidence integration (e.g., "The text first states, '___,' and later adds, '___,' which together suggest that ___ because ___"); for point of view lessons, provide a graphic organizer that maps each character's perspective before asking students to analyze contrasts.
- **On level:** Follow lesson as written; use the "On-level" and "Stretch" tiers; encourage independent annotation and multi-evidence analysis.
- **Above level:** Assign "Challenge" tier activities; encourage analysis that addresses how evidence pieces interact rather than simply accumulate; ask students to compare texts across lessons for recurring themes or contrasting narrative techniques; invite extended written responses that synthesize multiple standards.

### Connection to Future Units
The skills developed in this unit — multi-evidence citation, thematic analysis, perspective analysis, and fiction-history comparison — will be applied and extended in Unit 2 (Reading Informational Text), where students will cite multiple pieces of evidence from nonfiction, analyze central ideas, and evaluate how authors distinguish their positions from those of others. In Unit 3 (Writing), students will draw evidence from literary texts to support their own arguments and analyses, building on the multi-evidence skills from Lessons 1-2. The point of view and perspective skills from Lessons 6-8 prepare students to craft their own narratives with intentional and contrasting character perspectives, and the fiction-history comparison from Lesson 9 supports research-based writing tasks that require integrating narrative and informational sources.
