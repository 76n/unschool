# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Comparing Fiction with Historical Accounts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare a fictional portrayal of a historical period with an informational account of the same period
- Analyze how and why an author of fiction uses, alters, or reimagines historical facts for literary purposes
- Explain what a fictional account reveals about a historical period that a factual account alone cannot

## Prerequisites
- Ability to analyze how authors develop and contrast points of view (Lessons 6-8)
- Experience citing several pieces of evidence to support analysis (Lessons 1-2)
- Understanding of the difference between objective summary and personal response (Lesson 5)
- Familiarity with distinguishing between fiction and nonfiction genres

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Highlighters in two colors (one for fictional elements, one for historical elements)
- Sticky notes (3-4 per student)
- Venn diagram template
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept that fiction based on history is neither pure fact nor pure invention — it occupies a deliberate space between the two.

**Activity: Fact, Fiction, or Both?**

Say: "So far in this unit, we have analyzed evidence, theme, summary, and point of view — all within fiction. Today, we are going to look at what happens when fiction meets history. When an author writes a fictional story set in a real historical period, they make choices: which facts to keep, which to change, and which to invent. Your job is to analyze those choices."

Write on the board:
- **Historical account:** Tells what happened, based on evidence and records.
- **Fictional portrayal:** Tells a story set in a real time and place, using imagined characters and events to explore what the experience might have felt like.
- **The question:** What does the fiction add to, change about, or reveal beyond what the history tells us?

Say: "Fiction does not replace history. But it can show us what history feels like from the inside — the emotions, the daily experiences, the human cost — in ways that a factual account cannot."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to compare fictional and historical accounts using a structured analytical framework.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fictional portrayal:** A narrative set in a real historical time or place that uses imagined characters, dialogue, and events to explore the human experience of that period.
- **Historical account:** A factual description of events, people, and conditions based on evidence, records, and documentation.
- **Historical alteration:** A change an author makes to historical facts — adding characters, compressing time, inventing dialogue, or shifting events — for narrative or thematic purposes.
- **Humanization:** The technique of using fictional characters to give a personal, emotional dimension to historical events that factual accounts describe in general terms.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When you compare a fictional portrayal with a historical account, you are analyzing three things."

Write on the board — **Three Comparison Questions:**
1. **What does the fiction keep?** Which historical facts does the author preserve accurately?
2. **What does the fiction change?** What has the author altered, compressed, invented, or left out?
3. **Why?** What literary or thematic purpose does each change serve?

Say: "The third question is the most important. Authors do not change history by accident. Every alteration serves a purpose — usually to make the reader feel something, to explore a theme, or to show a perspective that a factual account cannot provide."

Say: "Let me show you with a paired example."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: Historical Account — "The Great Migration: Factory Workers in Chicago, 1918-1920"**

> Between 1916 and 1970, approximately six million African Americans moved from the rural Southern United States to cities in the North, Midwest, and West in a demographic shift known as the Great Migration. Many migrants were drawn by the promise of industrial jobs and the hope of escaping the racial violence and economic limitations of the Jim Crow South.
>
> In Chicago, migrants found work in meatpacking plants, steel mills, and factories. The work was physically demanding and often dangerous. Workers faced long hours, low wages, and limited opportunities for advancement. Housing was restricted by discriminatory practices — many landlords refused to rent to Black families, and neighborhoods were divided along racial lines. Migrants frequently settled in overcrowded apartments on the South Side, paying higher rents for less space than white tenants in comparable buildings elsewhere in the city.
>
> Despite these challenges, the Great Migration created vibrant communities. Churches, social clubs, newspapers, and cultural organizations provided support and connection. The South Side of Chicago became a center of African American cultural life, music, and entrepreneurship.

**Passage B: Fictional Portrayal — "The Sound of Steel"**

> The first thing Josephine noticed about Chicago was the noise. In Greenville, the mornings had been quiet — the creak of the porch, the hum of insects, the distant clang of the church bell at seven. Here, the city started before dawn: the rattle of the elevated train, the grinding of truck gears, the factory whistles that split the air like a blade through cloth.
>
> She shared a two-room apartment with her cousin Della and Della's three children. The apartment was on the third floor of a building that leaned slightly to the east, as if it were tired of standing. The radiator clanked but produced no heat. The walls were thin enough that Josephine could hear the family next door praying every evening in a language she did not recognize.
>
> Her job at the box factory started at six in the morning and ended at four in the afternoon. She stood at a table and folded cardboard along scored lines, stacking the finished boxes into columns taller than she was. The work was monotonous, but Josephine did not mind. Each box she folded was a box she had not been allowed to fold in Greenville — not because she lacked the skill, but because the factory in Greenville did not hire Black women.
>
> On Saturday evenings, Della took Josephine to a church social on Wabash Avenue. A woman played piano and a man sang in a deep, rolling voice that reminded Josephine of the river back home. The room was crowded and warm, and for a few hours, Chicago felt less like a city of strangers and more like a neighborhood of people who had all come from somewhere else and were all learning to be from here.

Say: "Let me compare using the three questions."

Write on the board:

**What does the fiction keep?**
- The historical migration from the South to Chicago (Josephine moves from Greenville)
- Factory work as physically demanding and monotonous (box factory)
- Overcrowded, substandard housing (two-room apartment with six people, broken radiator)
- Vibrant community life (church social on Wabash Avenue)
- Discriminatory practices (Greenville factory did not hire Black women)

**What does the fiction change or add?**
- Josephine is an invented character — no single historical figure is represented
- Specific sensory details are invented: the leaning building, the thin walls, the prayer next door
- The contrast between Greenville's quiet and Chicago's noise is an invented narrative device
- The Saturday church social is a composite scene, not a documented event
- Josephine's internal thoughts and emotions are imagined

**Why?**
- The author invents Josephine to humanize the historical statistics — instead of "six million people," we experience one woman's specific journey. The reader feels her displacement, her labor, and her moments of belonging.
- The sensory contrast (quiet Greenville vs. noisy Chicago) creates an emotional experience of dislocation that a factual account cannot produce.
- The church social scene shows community-building as a lived experience — warm, musical, and healing — rather than a demographic statistic.
- Josephine's thought — "Each box she folded was a box she had not been allowed to fold in Greenville" — reframes factory work not as hardship alone but as a paradox: the work is exhausting, but it represents an opportunity that racism had previously denied her. This thematic complexity is something fiction can explore more deeply than a factual account.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice comparing fictional and historical passages with facilitator support.

Say: "Now I want you to try. Reread both passages and use your Venn diagram. On one side, write details unique to the historical account. On the other side, write details unique to the fiction. In the middle, write what they share."

Allow 3 minutes for Venn diagram work.

Say: "Now, let us discuss: what can the reader learn from the fiction that they cannot learn from the historical account alone?"

Expected responses:
- The fiction shows what the migration felt like on a daily, personal level — the sounds, the cold apartment, the monotony of work.
- The fiction reveals the emotional complexity: Josephine does not mind the monotonous work because it represents freedom she did not have before. The historical account describes the work as demanding but does not explore this paradox.
- The fiction shows community-building as a sensory, emotional experience (music, warmth, belonging) rather than a list of institutions.

Say: "What can the reader learn from the historical account that the fiction does not provide?"

Expected responses:
- The historical account gives scope and scale — six million people, not just one.
- It provides specific data: dates, locations, economic conditions, discriminatory housing practices.
- It contextualizes the movement as a national phenomenon, not just one person's story.

Guide students to write a paragraph: The fictional portrayal adds emotional depth and personal perspective to the historical account. While the history explains what happened (the migration, the factory jobs, the housing discrimination), the fiction shows how those conditions felt to one specific person. Josephine's internal thought about the box factory — that each box represents an opportunity denied to her in the South — reveals a complexity the factual account does not address: that even difficult, monotonous work can represent liberation when the alternative was exclusion. The fiction humanizes the statistics, and the historical account contextualizes the personal story.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently compare a paired set of fictional and historical passages.

**Passage A: Historical Account — "The Dust Bowl: Oklahoma, 1934-1936"**

> During the mid-1930s, severe drought combined with decades of poor farming practices created massive dust storms across the Great Plains of the United States. Oklahoma was among the hardest-hit states. Topsoil that had been exposed by over-plowing was lifted by high winds and carried across hundreds of miles, creating "black blizzards" that buried fences, roads, and buildings.
>
> Thousands of farm families lost their livelihoods. Crops failed. Livestock died from inhaling dust. Homes became unlivable as fine dirt seeped through every crack, covering floors, beds, and tables. The federal government organized relief efforts, but many families chose to leave, joining a westward migration to California in search of agricultural work. These migrants, often called "Okies," faced discrimination and exploitation in the regions where they sought employment, frequently earning wages below subsistence level.

**Passage B: Fictional Portrayal — "The Color of Dust"**

> Mae woke to the sound of dirt tapping against the window like a thousand tiny fingers asking to come in. She pulled the damp cloth from over her face — her mother made everyone sleep with a cloth over their nose and mouth — and shook the dust from her blanket onto the floor, where it joined the layer already covering everything. The floor had not been its real color in weeks.
>
> Outside, the sky was brown. Not the warm brown of soil in spring, but a dead brown, the color of something that used to be alive. The fence posts along the road were buried to their middle rails, and the truck in the yard wore a coat of dust so thick that Mae could not tell what color it was underneath.
>
> Her father stood on the porch, looking west. He had been looking west every morning for a month. "Rain," he said, though it sounded more like a prayer than a prediction.
>
> Mae's mother packed plates into a crate. "We cannot eat dust," she said.
>
> Her father turned from the porch. "This land is ours."
>
> "This land is gone," her mother said. She was not angry. She was tired — the kind of tired that is deeper than sleep can fix.
>
> Mae looked at the crate of plates and then at her father's face. She understood, even at twelve, that her parents were not arguing about the land. They were arguing about who they would be if they left it.

**Directions:** Read both passages and compare them using the three analysis questions.

1. What historical facts does the fiction preserve from the historical account? Cite at least three specific connections between the two passages.
2. What does the fiction add that the historical account does not include? Identify at least two invented elements and explain what each adds to the reader's understanding.
3. Analyze Mae's final observation: "They were not arguing about the land. They were arguing about who they would be if they left it." How does this fictional insight deepen the reader's understanding of the historical migration in ways the factual account cannot?
4. Compare how each passage describes the dust. What does the fictional passage's description achieve that the historical account's description does not?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4, citing evidence from both passages.
**Stretch:** In your response to question 3, connect Mae's observation to the historical account's mention of the term "Okies" and the discrimination migrants faced. How does Mae's insight about identity connect to the historical reality of being labeled and marginalized?
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph arguing which passage — the historical account or the fictional portrayal — gives the reader a more complete understanding of the Dust Bowl experience. Use evidence from both passages to support your argument, and explain what "complete understanding" means in this context.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.2) What is the theme of the fictional passage "The Color of Dust"? State it as a complete sentence and cite two pieces of evidence.
6. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.6) How does the author develop Mae's point of view? Identify one technique and explain its effect using evidence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> **Historical fact:** During the 1918 influenza pandemic, schools in many American cities were closed for weeks. Children were kept home to prevent the spread of illness.
>
> **Fictional sentence:** "Clara pressed her face to the window every morning and watched the empty street, wondering if the world outside was still there or if the silence had swallowed it whole."

1. What does the fictional sentence add to the historical fact? Explain what the fiction reveals that the factual statement cannot.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The historical fact tells us what happened — schools closed and children stayed home. The fictional sentence shows what that experience felt like to a child: isolation, uncertainty, and the eerie quality of an empty world. Clara's thought that "the silence had swallowed it whole" transforms a public health measure into an emotional experience of a child wondering whether normalcy still exists. The fiction reveals the psychological impact of quarantine — the loneliness, the visual strangeness of empty streets, the confusion of a child who cannot fully understand why the world has changed. The factual statement provides the context; the fiction provides the human cost.

**Mastery = Student explains at least one specific element the fiction adds to the historical fact and articulates why fiction can convey emotional or experiential dimensions that factual accounts alone cannot.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three comparison questions (What does the fiction keep? What does it change? Why?) using the warm-up examples.
- Provide a simplified paired example and ask the student to fill in a Venn diagram before attempting analysis.
- Use the worked example from Direct Instruction as a model and have the student trace each analytical step.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10, which integrates all four standards in a single analytical task.
- Encourage the student to notice when fiction they read is set in a real historical period and to consider what the author has altered or invented.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Historical Research:** The student selects either the Great Migration or the Dust Bowl and finds one additional factual detail not included in the passages. They then write a fictional sentence or paragraph that brings that detail to life through a character's experience.
2. **Reverse Comparison:** The student writes a brief "historical account" (4-5 sentences of factual summary) based on the fictional passage "The Sound of Steel." They then analyze what was lost in the translation from fiction to fact.
3. **Modern Application:** The student identifies a recent historical event and writes both a brief factual account (3-4 sentences) and a fictional portrayal (one paragraph) of the same event. They then analyze their own choices: what did they keep, change, and why?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Historical sensitivity:** Both paired passages involve difficult historical periods — the Great Migration and the Dust Bowl. Provide brief historical context before reading so students can focus on analysis rather than struggling with unfamiliar background. Be mindful that the Great Migration passage involves racial discrimination, which requires sensitive facilitation.
- **Fiction is not "less true":** Some students may think fiction about history is unreliable or inferior to factual accounts. Emphasize that fiction and history serve different purposes — fiction explores the human experience of events, while history documents the events themselves. Both are valuable, and comparing them enriches understanding.
- **Common error:** Students may focus only on finding differences (what the fiction changes) without analyzing why the changes were made. Push for the "why" — the purpose behind the author's choices.
- **Connection to Lesson 5:** Remind students that the objective summary skills from Lesson 5 are useful here — they can apply neutral, factual language when describing the historical account and analytical language when discussing the fictional portrayal.
- **Pacing note:** The paired passages in both Direct Instruction and Independent Practice are longer than usual. If time is tight, have students read one passage at home and the other in class, then compare during Independent Practice.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The fiction preserves several historical facts: (a) Severe dust storms — the historical account describes "black blizzards" and the fiction shows dust "tapping against the window" and burying fence posts to their middle rails. (b) Homes becoming unlivable — the history states "fine dirt seeped through every crack," and the fiction shows the family sleeping with cloths over their faces and dust covering everything. (c) The westward migration — the historical account describes families leaving for California, and the fiction shows the mother packing plates while the father resists leaving. The fictional family is at the point of deciding whether to join the migration the historical account describes.

2. First, the fiction adds the family's internal conflict about leaving. The historical account describes the migration as an economic decision, but the fiction shows it as an identity crisis — the father says "This land is ours" while the mother says "This land is gone." This adds emotional and psychological complexity to the historical decision. Second, the fiction adds Mae's twelve-year-old perspective — her observation that her parents "were not arguing about the land" but "about who they would be if they left it." This child's understanding adds a dimension the historical account cannot provide: the awareness that migration is not just about geography but about selfhood.

3. Mae's observation reframes the Dust Bowl migration from an economic event to an existential one. The historical account describes families leaving because "crops failed" and "livestock died" — practical reasons. Mae's insight reveals that leaving the land meant losing a part of their identity. The question was not whether they could survive staying (they could not) but who they would become if they left. This fictional insight helps the reader understand why some families, like Mae's father, resisted leaving even when staying was clearly untenable — the land was not just a livelihood but a definition of self. The historical account explains the push factors; the fiction explains the resistance to the pull.

4. The historical account describes dust factually: "Topsoil... was lifted by high winds," "fine dirt seeped through every crack." This conveys the scope and mechanics of the problem. The fictional passage describes dust through sensory, metaphorical language: dust "tapping against the window like a thousand tiny fingers asking to come in," the sky "a dead brown, the color of something that used to be alive," the truck under "a coat of dust so thick that Mae could not tell what color it was underneath." The fiction achieves two things the factual account cannot: first, it makes the dust feel personal and invasive (the personification of fingers suggests an unwanted presence); second, it conveys the psychological weight of living inside a landscape of loss. The metaphor of "something that used to be alive" connects the dead land to the family's dying hope.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.6.2) Theme: Leaving home during crisis is not just a practical decision but an identity crisis — it forces people to decide who they are when the land that defined them is no longer viable. This is supported by the father's statement "This land is ours" (showing identity tied to place) and Mae's observation that "they were not arguing about the land. They were arguing about who they would be if they left it" (making the identity theme explicit). Together, these show that the passage is about the relationship between place and selfhood, not just about dust and migration.

6. (RL.6.6) The author develops Mae's point of view through the technique of mature observation beyond her years. Although Mae is twelve, her final thought — "They were not arguing about the land. They were arguing about who they would be if they left it" — shows an understanding of her parents' conflict that goes deeper than the surface-level argument about staying or leaving. This positions Mae as a perceptive child narrator who can articulate what the adults feel but do not say directly. The effect is that the reader trusts Mae's interpretation and sees the family's dilemma through a lens that is both youthful (she is watching her parents and trying to understand) and wise (she grasps the emotional core of their disagreement).
