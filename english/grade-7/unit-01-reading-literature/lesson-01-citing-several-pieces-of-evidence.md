# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 1

## Topic
Citing Several Pieces of Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Select multiple pieces of textual evidence that collectively support an analytical claim about a passage
- Explain how different pieces of evidence work together to strengthen an analysis rather than simply repeating the same point
- Integrate several cited quotations into a cohesive written response with transitional reasoning between evidence pieces

## Prerequisites
- Ability to cite textual evidence to support analysis of what the text says explicitly (RL.6.1)
- Familiarity with the claim-evidence-explanation structure (the "Evidence Sandwich")
- Experience selecting relevant evidence and distinguishing strong evidence from weak evidence
- Ability to use quotation marks correctly when citing text

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Sticky notes (3-4 per student)
- Highlighters in two colors
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about citing evidence and introduce the shift from single to multiple evidence.

**Activity: One Quote or Three?**

Say: "Last year in sixth grade, you learned how to build an Evidence Sandwich — a claim, a piece of evidence, and an explanation. That was strong work, and it is still the foundation. But this year, we are raising the bar. Instead of one piece of evidence, you will learn to weave together several pieces of evidence that build on each other to make your analysis deeper and more convincing."

Write on the board:
- **Grade 6:** Claim + 1 piece of evidence + explanation
- **Grade 7:** Claim + several pieces of evidence + explanations showing how they connect

Say: "Here is a quick example. Suppose I claim that a character feels torn about a decision. In sixth grade, I might cite one sentence showing her hesitation. In seventh grade, I need to find multiple moments — maybe a sentence showing hesitation, another showing her reasoning, and a third showing what she ultimately does — and explain how those moments work together to reveal her inner conflict."

Say: "Think of it like building a case in court. One piece of evidence might be interesting, but several pieces of evidence that point in the same direction are convincing."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to select multiple pieces of evidence that work together and connect them with transitional reasoning.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Several pieces of evidence:** Multiple quotations or details from a text that collectively support an analytical claim, each adding a new dimension rather than restating the same idea.
- **Transitional reasoning:** The explanatory sentences between cited evidence that show how one piece of evidence connects to or builds upon another.
- **Layered analysis:** An analytical response that deepens with each piece of evidence, moving from surface observation to deeper understanding.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When I say 'cite several pieces of evidence,' I do not mean find three quotes that all say the same thing. I mean find evidence that builds — each new piece adds something the previous one did not. Think of stacking layers, not stacking copies."

Write on the board — **The Multi-Evidence Model:**
1. **Claim** — State what you believe about the text.
2. **Evidence 1** — Cite a piece of evidence that introduces the idea.
3. **Reasoning** — Explain what this first piece shows.
4. **Evidence 2** — Cite a second piece that adds depth or a new angle.
5. **Connection** — Explain how Evidence 2 builds on or complicates Evidence 1.
6. **Evidence 3 (optional but powerful)** — Cite a third piece that strengthens or completes the picture.
7. **Synthesis** — Pull all the evidence together into a closing statement.

Say: "Watch how I use this model with a passage. Pay attention to how each piece of evidence adds something new."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Bridge"**

> Nadia stood at the edge of the footbridge and looked down at the creek below. The water was shallow — barely up to her knees if she waded in — and the bridge itself was only ten feet long, a simple plank of treated wood bolted to two concrete posts. There was nothing dangerous about it. She had crossed it a hundred times before the accident.
>
> But that was the problem. Before the accident, she had crossed without thinking. She had run across it, skipped across it, balanced on the railing while her cousin Javier shouted at her to be careful. Now she stood at the edge and studied the wood grain, the small cracks where rain had seeped in, the slight bow in the center where the plank flexed underfoot. She noticed everything. The bridge had not changed. She had.
>
> Behind her, Javier waited on the path. He did not say anything. He did not tell her to hurry or offer to hold her hand. He just stood there, hands in his pockets, looking at the trees as if they were the most interesting things in the world. Nadia knew what he was doing. He was giving her space to decide on her own terms.
>
> She put one foot on the bridge. The wood creaked. She put the other foot forward. The creek below was still shallow, still harmless. But her heart was pounding as if she were crossing a canyon.
>
> She made it to the other side and kept walking without looking back. Javier followed a few steps behind, still quiet. After a minute, Nadia said, "It is just a bridge."
>
> "Yeah," Javier said. "It is just a bridge."
>
> Neither of them believed it.

Say: "Here is my claim: Nadia's experience of crossing the bridge reveals that fear is not about the physical danger of a situation but about the emotional weight attached to it. Now I need several pieces of evidence that build on each other."

Write on the board:

**Claim:** Nadia's experience of crossing the bridge reveals that fear is not about the physical danger of a situation but about the emotional weight attached to it.

**Evidence 1:** The text establishes that the bridge is objectively safe: "The water was shallow — barely up to her knees if she waded in — and the bridge itself was only ten feet long."

**Reasoning:** This detail makes clear that nothing about the bridge is physically threatening. The danger Nadia feels must come from somewhere other than the bridge itself.

**Evidence 2:** However, the text shows that Nadia now perceives the bridge with intense scrutiny: "She noticed everything. The bridge had not changed. She had."

**Connection:** This second piece of evidence shifts the source of fear from the external to the internal. The bridge is the same object it always was, but Nadia's relationship to it has been transformed by whatever happened in the accident. Her fear is rooted in memory, not in present danger.

**Evidence 3:** This internal source of fear is confirmed by the physical reaction: "Her heart was pounding as if she were crossing a canyon."

**Synthesis:** Together, these three pieces of evidence trace a progression: the bridge is safe (Evidence 1), but Nadia has changed (Evidence 2), and her body responds as if the danger is real (Evidence 3). Each piece adds a layer — external reality, internal transformation, and physical consequence — that collectively prove fear lives in a person's experience, not in the object itself.

Say: "Notice how each piece of evidence added something new. The first established facts. The second introduced the internal shift. The third showed the physical impact. If I had only cited one of these, my analysis would be thinner. Together, they build a complete picture."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice selecting and connecting multiple pieces of evidence with facilitator support.

Say: "Now we are going to read a new passage together. As you read, use your sticky notes to mark at least three moments that reveal something about the main character's emotional state."

**Passage: "The Photograph"**

> Marcus found the photograph in a shoebox at the back of his mother's closet. He was looking for his old soccer cleats, not memories, but there it was: a picture of his father standing in front of a yellow house with a red door. His father was young in the photograph — younger than Marcus had ever known him — wearing a denim jacket and holding a guitar case. He was smiling.
>
> Marcus had seen his father smile, of course. But this smile was different. It was wide and unguarded, the kind of smile that happens before a person learns to be careful with their happiness. Marcus stared at the photograph for a long time, trying to connect this young man with the father who came home tired every evening and fell asleep in the recliner before dinner was ready.
>
> He brought the photograph downstairs and set it on the kitchen table without saying anything. His mother glanced at it while stirring soup. Her hand slowed on the spoon, just for a moment, and then she stirred again at the normal pace.
>
> "That was before we moved here," she said. "Before a lot of things."
>
> Marcus waited for her to say more, but she did not. He picked up the photograph and looked at it again. The yellow house. The red door. The guitar case. He had never heard his father play guitar.
>
> "Did he ever play for you?" Marcus asked.
>
> His mother turned off the stove and wiped her hands on a towel. "He played every night," she said quietly. "Before a lot of things."

Say: "Here is the claim: The photograph reveals a gap between who Marcus's father once was and who he has become, and Marcus is only beginning to understand the significance of that gap. Find three pieces of evidence that support this claim. Write each one on a sticky note and number them in the order you think they should appear in an analysis."

Allow 2 minutes for students to search and select.

Say: "Share your three pieces with a partner. Discuss: do your evidence pieces build on each other, or do some of them repeat the same point?"

Expected strong evidence selections:
- "This smile was different. It was wide and unguarded, the kind of smile that happens before a person learns to be careful with their happiness." (Shows the father was once a different person.)
- "Marcus stared at the photograph for a long time, trying to connect this young man with the father who came home tired every evening." (Shows Marcus perceiving the disconnect between past and present.)
- "He had never heard his father play guitar" combined with the mother's "He played every night. Before a lot of things." (Shows that something fundamental about the father was lost.)

Say: "Now let us build a multi-evidence response together."

Guide students to write: The photograph reveals a gap between who Marcus's father once was and who he has become, and Marcus is only beginning to understand the significance of that gap. The text describes the father's smile in the photo as "wide and unguarded, the kind of smile that happens before a person learns to be careful with their happiness." This detail introduces the idea that the father once had an openness he no longer possesses. The gap deepens when Marcus tries to reconcile the image with reality: he spends "a long time, trying to connect this young man with the father who came home tired every evening and fell asleep in the recliner before dinner was ready." The word "trying" suggests the connection feels impossible — the two versions of his father do not match. The most striking evidence comes at the end, when Marcus learns his father "played every night" but Marcus has "never heard his father play guitar." This detail transforms the gap from something Marcus senses into something concrete and measurable: an entire part of his father's identity has disappeared from their shared life. Together, these three moments move from observation to understanding to loss, tracing Marcus's dawning awareness that his father's life contained chapters he never witnessed.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently select several pieces of evidence and build layered analytical responses.

**Passage: "The Garden"**

> Every Saturday morning, Amara's grandmother woke at five to tend the garden behind their apartment building. It was not much of a garden — a narrow strip of soil between the building's back wall and the chain-link fence that separated their lot from the parking garage next door. The soil was thin and rocky, and the sunlight only reached it for a few hours in the afternoon. But Amara's grandmother treated that strip of earth as if it were the most valuable land in the city.
>
> She grew tomatoes, peppers, herbs, and a row of marigolds along the fence. She composted eggshells and coffee grounds. She talked to the plants in Amharic, the language she had spoken as a girl in Addis Ababa, and the plants seemed to listen — they grew taller and greener than anything else on the block.
>
> Amara helped on Saturdays, though she did not share her grandmother's patience. She wanted the tomatoes to ripen faster. She pulled weeds aggressively, sometimes yanking out seedlings by mistake. Her grandmother never scolded her. She simply replanted the seedling and said, "The garden does not hurry. You should not either."
>
> One Saturday, Amara arrived to find her grandmother sitting on an overturned bucket, not working. The marigolds along the fence had been trampled — tire tracks ran through the soil where a delivery truck had backed in from the parking garage. The tomato cages were bent. Half the pepper plants were crushed.
>
> Amara expected her grandmother to be angry or sad. Instead, she was studying the damage the way she studied the soil each spring — carefully, without rushing to judgment.
>
> "We will fix it," her grandmother said. "The roots are still here."

**Directions:** Read the passage carefully. For each question, cite several pieces of evidence and explain how they work together.

1. What does the passage reveal about the grandmother's character? Cite at least three pieces of evidence that each reveal a different aspect of who she is, and explain how they build a complete portrait.
2. How does the passage develop the contrast between Amara and her grandmother? Cite evidence from at least two different moments and explain how the contrast deepens.
3. What is the significance of the grandmother's final statement, "The roots are still here"? Cite this evidence along with at least one earlier detail that gives the statement deeper meaning.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3, citing at least two pieces of evidence per response with explanation.
**Stretch:** In your response to question 1, ensure your three pieces of evidence move from surface-level traits to deeper character qualities — show a progression from what the grandmother does to who she is.
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph arguing that the garden is a metaphor for something larger in the grandmother's life. Cite at least three pieces of evidence and explain the metaphorical connection for each.

### Interleaved Review
4. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.2) What is the theme of this passage? State it as a complete sentence, not just a topic word.
5. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.6) From whose perspective do we experience the events of this passage? How does this perspective shape what the reader understands about the grandmother?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Kai sat in the waiting room of the veterinary clinic, his dog Biscuit curled on his lap. Biscuit was thirteen — ancient for a beagle — and her breathing had become shallow and ragged over the past week. The vet had said to bring her in today. She had not said what she expected to find. Kai ran his hand along Biscuit's ear, the way he had done every night since he was four years old. Her fur was thinner now, but the ear was the same shape, the same softness. She pressed her head into his palm the way she always had.

1. What can you infer about Kai's emotional state? Cite at least two pieces of evidence and explain how they work together to support your inference.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Kai is bracing himself for the possibility of losing Biscuit while holding onto the comfort of their familiar bond. The text says he runs his hand along Biscuit's ear "the way he had done every night since he was four years old," which shows he is reaching for the ritual that has defined their relationship — seeking normalcy in a frightening moment. The second piece of evidence adds depth: "Her fur was thinner now, but the ear was the same shape, the same softness." The contrast between what has changed (the thinning fur, her age) and what has stayed the same (the shape, the softness) mirrors Kai's internal struggle between accepting change and clinging to what is familiar. Together, these details show someone who is aware of loss approaching but finding comfort in the continuity that remains.

**Mastery = Student cites at least two specific pieces of evidence with quotation marks and provides an explanation that shows how the pieces connect to each other and to the inference.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the difference between single-evidence and multi-evidence responses using a simplified passage.
- Provide a sentence frame: "The text first shows ___ when it states, '___.' This connects to a second detail: '___.' Together, these moments suggest ___ because ___."
- Practice with the Guided Practice passage, asking the student to find a second piece of evidence that adds to (rather than repeats) their first.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 2, which builds on multi-evidence citation by teaching students to support layered inferences.
- Encourage the student to practice the multi-evidence model in their independent reading responses.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Evidence Audit:** The student rereads the "Garden" passage and identifies every piece of evidence that could support the claim "The grandmother finds strength in patience." They then rank the evidence from weakest to strongest and write a paragraph explaining why the top three pieces are more effective than the others.
2. **Cross-Text Evidence:** The student selects a book they are reading independently, forms a claim about the main character, and finds evidence from three different chapters that collectively support the claim. They write a multi-evidence response using the model from this lesson.
3. **Evidence Weaving Practice:** The student takes their Independent Practice response to question 1 and revises it to include one additional piece of evidence. They focus on where in the paragraph the new evidence fits — does it come before, between, or after the existing pieces? — and write transitional reasoning that connects it to the surrounding analysis.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Most common mistake at Grade 7:** Students cite multiple pieces of evidence but treat them as a list rather than a connected argument. Each piece of evidence must be followed by reasoning that links it to the previous evidence and to the overall claim. Watch for responses that read as "evidence dump" without connective tissue.
- **Transitioning from Grade 6:** Sixth graders learned the Evidence Sandwich (claim + one piece of evidence + explanation). Seventh graders are building multi-layer sandwiches. If students struggle, let them start with two pieces of evidence rather than three, and focus on the transitional reasoning between them.
- **Quality over quantity:** Three well-chosen, well-explained pieces of evidence are always better than five pieces that are poorly connected. Emphasize that "several" does not mean "as many as possible" — it means "enough to build a convincing case."
- **Pacing note:** If students grasp the multi-evidence model quickly during Guided Practice, spend extra time on Independent Practice question 3, which requires students to connect a final statement to earlier evidence — a skill that bridges to thematic analysis in Lessons 3-4.
- **Emotional content awareness:** The exit ticket passage involves a pet's declining health. Be sensitive to students who may have recently experienced pet loss. Offer an alternative prompt if needed.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The grandmother's character is revealed through three layered dimensions. First, she is devoted and hardworking: "Every Saturday morning, Amara's grandmother woke at five to tend the garden" and she treats "that strip of earth as if it were the most valuable land in the city." This shows someone who invests care into what she has rather than lamenting what she lacks. Second, she is culturally rooted and spiritually connected to her work: "She talked to the plants in Amharic, the language she had spoken as a girl in Addis Ababa." This detail reveals that the garden connects her to her origins and her first language — it is not just a hobby but a link to her identity. Third, she is profoundly resilient: when the garden is destroyed, she studies "the damage the way she studied the soil each spring — carefully, without rushing to judgment" and says, "The roots are still here." This shows a person who responds to destruction not with despair but with careful assessment and faith in what endures. Together, these three dimensions move from dedication to identity to resilience, building a portrait of someone whose strength comes from deep roots of her own.

2. The contrast between Amara and her grandmother deepens across the passage. In the garden, Amara "wanted the tomatoes to ripen faster" and "pulled weeds aggressively, sometimes yanking out seedlings by mistake," while her grandmother responds without scolding and simply replants. This first contrast establishes a difference in temperament — impatience versus patience. The contrast deepens after the garden is damaged: Amara "expected her grandmother to be angry or sad," projecting her own likely reaction onto her grandmother. Instead, the grandmother responds with calm assessment. This second moment shows that the contrast is not just about gardening style but about how each person processes difficulty. Amara assumes emotional reaction is the natural response; her grandmother demonstrates that careful observation is more productive than immediate emotion.

3. The grandmother's statement "The roots are still here" carries significance beyond the literal garden. Earlier, the text establishes that she talks to the plants "in Amharic, the language she had spoken as a girl in Addis Ababa," connecting the garden to her cultural roots and her life before immigration. When she says the roots are still here, she is speaking both about the plants and about her own resilience — the foundation of who she is remains intact even when the surface is damaged. Her ability to study destruction "without rushing to judgment" confirms that this is a woman who has learned to focus on what survives rather than what is lost. The roots are both literal (the plants can regrow) and metaphorical (her identity, patience, and strength endure through hardship).

### Interleaved Review
4. (RL.6.2) Theme: True resilience comes not from avoiding hardship but from recognizing that the deepest parts of who we are — our roots — survive even when the visible parts of our lives are damaged. (Accept reasonable variations about patience, cultural identity sustaining a person through loss, or the relationship between care and endurance.)

5. (RL.6.6) We experience the events through Amara's perspective. This shapes the reader's understanding because we see the grandmother from the outside — through the eyes of someone who does not fully share her patience or her history. When Amara expects anger or sadness, we feel the surprise of the grandmother's calm response alongside Amara. The grandmother's internal experience remains somewhat mysterious, which makes her final statement more powerful — we must interpret it rather than having it explained.
