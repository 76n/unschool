# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 8

## Topic
Unreliable and Limited Narrators

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Recognize when a narrator's perspective is limited by age, knowledge, bias, or emotional state
- Identify gaps between what a narrator claims and what the text's details suggest
- Analyze how narrative limitations shape the reader's understanding and create layers of meaning

## Prerequisites
- Ability to analyze how authors develop and contrast narrator perspectives (Lessons 6-7)
- Understanding of the four narrator development techniques: diction, sensory focus, selective detail, and tone (Lesson 7)
- Experience explaining how an author develops the point of view of a narrator (RL.6.6)
- Familiarity with building inferences from multiple textual clues (Lesson 2)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Highlighters in two colors (one for what the narrator says, one for what the text suggests)
- Index cards (2-3 per student)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept that narrators do not always tell the whole truth — not necessarily because they are lying, but because their perspective is limited.

**Activity: What the Camera Cannot See**

Say: "In Lessons 6 and 7, we analyzed how authors develop narrator perspective. Today, we are going to explore a specific challenge: what happens when the narrator cannot — or does not — tell the full story? This is not about narrators who lie on purpose. It is about narrators whose understanding is limited by who they are."

Write on the board:
- **A five-year-old narrator** might describe a parent crying at the kitchen table but not understand that the tears are about a job loss.
- **A jealous narrator** might describe a classmate's success as luck, ignoring the hard work the text shows.
- **A grieving narrator** might focus on every detail of a room except the one thing that reminds them of their loss.

Say: "In each case, the narrator is not lying. They are telling the story as they see it. But the reader can see more than the narrator does — because the author plants details that the narrator does not fully understand. Your job as a reader is to notice the gap between what the narrator tells you and what the text actually shows."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify sources of narrative unreliability and analyze the gap between narrator and text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Unreliable narrator:** A narrator whose account of events is incomplete, biased, or inaccurate — not necessarily because they intend to deceive, but because their perspective is shaped by limitations the reader can detect.
- **Limited narrator:** A narrator who does not have access to all the information — they may not know other characters' thoughts, may misinterpret events, or may lack the experience to understand what they witness.
- **Narrative gap:** The space between what the narrator reports and what the reader can infer from the text's details — where the narrator's understanding falls short of the full picture.
- **Textual clue:** A detail in the text that contradicts, complicates, or adds to the narrator's account, revealing information the narrator does not consciously share.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "There are four common sources of narrative limitation."

Write on the board — **Sources of Narrative Limitation:**
1. **Age or experience** — Young narrators may not understand adult situations they witness.
2. **Emotional bias** — A narrator's strong emotions (jealousy, grief, anger, love) may distort their account.
3. **Limited knowledge** — A narrator who does not know the full story may misinterpret events.
4. **Self-deception** — A narrator who does not want to face a truth may describe events in ways that protect themselves.

Say: "The key skill is learning to read on two levels: Level 1 is what the narrator tells you. Level 2 is what the text shows you that the narrator misses or misunderstands."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Best Day" (young narrator)**

> Today was the best day ever. Mom said we were going on an adventure, and we drove for a long time with the car packed full of boxes and suitcases. I got to sit in the front seat because the back seat was full. Mom let me pick the music the whole way.
>
> We stopped at a new apartment. It was smaller than our house, but it had a balcony and I could see the tops of trees from it. Mom said this was our new home. I asked where Dad's stuff was going to go and she said Dad was going to live in the old house for a while so he could take care of the garden. That made sense because Dad loves the garden.
>
> Mom unpacked the kitchen first. She put the coffee maker on the counter and the mugs on the shelf, and then she stood very still for a long time, looking out the window. I thought she was looking at the trees, like I was. Then she wiped her eyes and said, "Let us order pizza for dinner."
>
> Pizza on a weeknight. Like I said — best day ever.

Say: "This passage operates on two levels. Let me trace both."

Write on the board:

**Level 1 — What the narrator tells us:**
The narrator (a young child) reports a fun adventure: car ride with music, a new apartment with a balcony, and pizza on a weeknight. They describe the day as the "best day ever."

**Level 2 — What the text shows the reader:**
The packed boxes and suitcases, the new smaller apartment, the father staying behind "to take care of the garden," and the mother standing very still and wiping her eyes all suggest a family separation or divorce. The mother is crying. The child interprets the tears as looking at trees and the separation as garden care.

**Narrative gap:** The child does not understand that the "adventure" is a family breaking apart. The reader can detect this from the details the child reports but does not interpret: the packed car, the question about Dad's stuff, the mother's stillness and tears. The child's innocence is the source of unreliability — they tell us everything but understand very little.

**Effect on the reader:** The gap between the child's cheerful account and the painful reality creates emotional depth. The reader feels the weight of what is happening precisely because the narrator does not. The contrast between "best day ever" and the implied divorce makes both the child's innocence and the mother's grief more powerful.

Say: "Notice — the narrator is not lying. They are telling the truth as they see it. The unreliability comes from the gap between their understanding and the full picture."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying narrative limitations and gaps with facilitator support.

Say: "Read this passage and use two highlighter colors. One color for what the narrator claims. Another color for textual clues that suggest the narrator's account is incomplete or biased."

**Passage: "The Fair Choice" (emotionally biased narrator)**

> I did not get the solo in the spring concert. Ms. Torres gave it to Priyanka, which was exactly what I expected. Priyanka always gets chosen for things. She is the kind of person who raises her hand for every question, who stays after class to talk to teachers, who volunteers for everything. I do not know why Ms. Torres could not see through that. It is not talent. It is politics.
>
> I sang my audition piece perfectly — every note in tune, every rhythm exact. I know because I practiced it forty times at home with a tuner app. When I finished, Ms. Torres nodded and said, "Thank you, Renata." That was all. She did not say "Good job" or "Well done." Just "Thank you." That told me everything I needed to know.
>
> Priyanka auditioned after me. I could hear her from the hallway. She did not sing the piece the way it was written. She changed the rhythm in the second verse and held the last note longer than the sheet music indicated. She made it her own, which is another way of saying she did not follow the rules.
>
> After the results were posted, my friend Dara said, "Priyanka sounded amazing in the audition." I said, "She sounded different. That is not the same thing."
>
> Dara looked at me for a long time and said, "Maybe it is, though."

Say: "Highlight what Renata claims in one color and the textual clues that suggest a different reality in another."

Allow 2 minutes for highlighting and partner discussion.

Expected findings:
- **Narrator's claims:** Priyanka gets chosen because of politics, not talent. Renata sang perfectly. Ms. Torres's brief response means she was dismissive.
- **Textual clues suggesting otherwise:** Priyanka "made it her own," suggesting artistic interpretation. Renata's focus on technical perfection (tuner app, exact rhythm) suggests she may prioritize accuracy over expression. Dara, an outside observer, says Priyanka "sounded amazing" — an unbiased reaction. Ms. Torres's "Thank you" could be neutral, not negative. Renata's bitter tone throughout suggests jealousy is shaping her account.

Say: "What is the source of Renata's unreliability?"

Expected answer: Emotional bias — specifically jealousy and hurt. Renata interprets every detail through the lens of someone who feels unfairly overlooked. She dismisses Priyanka's creativity as rule-breaking and Dara's honest reaction as irrelevant. Her account is not false, but it is filtered through an emotion that distorts her interpretation.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze unreliable and limited narrators.

**Passage: "The Rescue" (limited-knowledge narrator)**

> I was the one who found the kitten in the storm drain behind the school. It was tiny — barely bigger than my fist — and it was shaking. I wrapped it in my jacket and carried it to the office. Mrs. Gomez, the secretary, said she would call animal control.
>
> When I went back to check on the kitten at lunch, it was gone. Mrs. Gomez said a woman had come to pick it up. "She said it was hers," Mrs. Gomez told me. "She said it had been missing for two days."
>
> I do not believe that. Who loses a kitten for two days and does not look behind the school? The drain is right there. Anyone who cared about their pet would have found it sooner. I think the woman just wanted a free kitten and made up a story.
>
> After school, I walked past the drain again. There was a flyer taped to the fence — a picture of the kitten, the woman's phone number, and the words "MISSING SINCE TUESDAY — PLEASE HELP." The flyer was water-stained and ripped at the corners, like it had been there through the storm.
>
> I pulled the flyer off the fence and folded it into my pocket. I kept walking.

**Directions:** Read the passage carefully and analyze the narrator's limitations.

1. What does the narrator claim about the woman who picked up the kitten? What specific language reveals the narrator's bias?
2. What textual clue contradicts the narrator's interpretation? Cite the evidence and explain what it reveals that the narrator does not acknowledge.
3. What is the source of the narrator's unreliability? Choose from the four sources (age/experience, emotional bias, limited knowledge, or self-deception) and explain your choice using evidence from the passage.
4. Why does the narrator pull the flyer off the fence and pocket it? What inference can you draw about what the narrator is thinking or feeling at that moment?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4, citing evidence for each.
**Stretch:** In your response to question 3, explain whether more than one source of limitation is at work and how they interact.
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph analyzing the passage on two levels: Level 1 (what the narrator tells the reader) and Level 2 (what the text shows the reader that the narrator does not fully grasp). Explain what the narrative gap adds to the passage's meaning.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.6) How does the author develop the narrator's point of view in the first two paragraphs? Identify one specific technique and cite evidence.
6. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.1) Cite two pieces of evidence that reveal the narrator has formed an emotional attachment to the kitten. Explain how each piece supports this inference.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> My older sister got accepted to a college three hundred miles away. Everyone was celebrating — my parents, my grandparents, her friends. They kept saying "Congratulations" and "We are so proud." I said congratulations too. I meant it. She worked hard and she deserved it. But when she went upstairs to pack, I sat on the couch and counted the tiles on the ceiling because I did not want to think about what the house would sound like without her music playing through the walls every night.

1. What does the narrator claim they feel about their sister's acceptance? What does the text suggest they also feel but do not directly state? Cite evidence for each.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The narrator claims to feel genuinely happy for their sister: "I said congratulations too. I meant it. She worked hard and she deserved it." These statements are sincere — the narrator is not being sarcastic or dismissive. However, the text also suggests the narrator feels grief or dread at the coming separation. The evidence is: "I sat on the couch and counted the tiles on the ceiling because I did not want to think about what the house would sound like without her music playing through the walls every night." The act of counting tiles is avoidance behavior, and the specific detail about the sister's music reveals that the narrator is already imagining the absence. The narrative gap is between the publicly expressed happiness and the privately felt loss — the narrator is not unreliable in the sense of being wrong, but their cheerful statements do not capture the full emotional truth.

**Mastery = Student identifies both the narrator's claim and the text's deeper suggestion, cites evidence for each, and explains the gap between the two levels of the narrative.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the concept of two levels (what the narrator says vs. what the text shows) using the warm-up examples.
- Provide a two-row graphic organizer: Row 1 = "What the narrator says," Row 2 = "What the text shows me." Have the student fill in both rows with evidence.
- Practice with the Guided Practice passage using guided support.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9, which shifts to comparing fiction with historical accounts.
- Encourage the student to look for narrative gaps and limitations in their independent reading.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Perspective Flip:** The student rewrites "The Fair Choice" from Priyanka's perspective. How does the audition look from her point of view? The student then writes a paragraph comparing the two versions and analyzing what each narrator cannot see about the other.
2. **Unreliable Narrator Gallery:** The student identifies an unreliable or limited narrator in a book, film, or television show and writes a brief analysis (1 paragraph) explaining the source of the unreliability and what the audience understands that the narrator does not.
3. **Create an Unreliable Narrator:** The student writes a short passage (8-10 sentences) from the perspective of an unreliable narrator. The narrator should believe one thing while the text's details suggest another. The student exchanges passages with a partner, and each identifies the narrative gap.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Sensitive handling required:** "The Best Day" passage depicts a family separation through a child's innocent eyes. This may resonate with students who have experienced divorce or family changes. Approach the passage analytically but be prepared for emotional reactions.
- **Unreliable does not mean bad:** Be explicit that an unreliable narrator is not a flaw in writing — it is a deliberate technique. Some of the greatest works of literature use unreliable narrators. The skill is in recognizing the gap, not in judging the narrator.
- **Common error:** Students may think the narrator is lying. Help them understand the difference between lying (intentional deception) and limitation (a restricted lens). Most unreliable narrators in literature are limited, not dishonest.
- **Scaffolding tip:** The two-highlighter method (one color for narrator's claims, another for textual clues) is the most effective scaffold for this lesson. It physically separates the two levels of the text.
- **Connection to Lesson 2:** Remind students that the inference skills from Lesson 2 are essential here. Recognizing an unreliable narrator requires the reader to infer what is really happening from clues the narrator misses — exactly the skill they practiced in Lesson 2.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The narrator claims the woman "just wanted a free kitten and made up a story." The specific language revealing bias includes: "I do not believe that," "Who loses a kitten for two days and does not look behind the school?" and "Anyone who cared about their pet would have found it sooner." The accusatory questions and the phrase "anyone who cared" impose a standard of care that the narrator uses to judge the woman without knowing her circumstances.

2. The flyer contradicts the narrator's interpretation. It was "water-stained and ripped at the corners, like it had been there through the storm." This detail reveals that the woman had been actively searching for the kitten before the storm — the flyer predates the narrator's rescue. The water stains and tears show the flyer endured the same storm the kitten did, meaning the woman's claim of two days of searching is supported by physical evidence. The narrator does not acknowledge this contradiction — they pull the flyer down and pocket it without revising their opinion.

3. The primary source of unreliability is emotional bias, specifically a sense of possessive attachment. The narrator found the kitten, rescued it, and wrapped it in their own jacket — establishing a bond. When the kitten is given to someone else, the narrator's disappointment manifests as distrust of the woman's story. However, there is also self-deception at work: the flyer provides concrete evidence that the woman searched for the kitten, but the narrator responds by removing the flyer rather than reconsidering their judgment. The act of pocketing the flyer suggests the narrator does not want the evidence to exist — not because they are malicious, but because accepting it would mean accepting that the kitten was never theirs.

4. The narrator pulls the flyer off the fence and pockets it rather than acknowledging what it means. This action suggests the narrator recognizes, on some level, that the flyer contradicts their story — if the woman made up the claim, no flyer would exist. Pocketing it is an act of removing evidence that challenges their narrative. The inference is that the narrator is struggling with the loss of the kitten and is not yet ready to accept that their rescue was a temporary act of care, not an ownership claim. The physical act of folding the flyer and keeping it mirrors their emotional refusal to let go.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.6.6) In the first two paragraphs, the author develops the narrator's point of view through selective detail. The narrator describes finding the kitten in vivid, personal terms: "barely bigger than my fist," "it was shaking," "I wrapped it in my jacket." These details are sensory and intimate, establishing the narrator as someone who felt an immediate, physical connection to the kitten. The emphasis on "I was the one who found" it in the opening sentence signals that the narrator takes ownership of the rescue from the start. This selective focus on the personal bond — rather than, say, the kitten's health or the logistics of the situation — develops the narrator as someone whose perspective is shaped by emotional attachment.

6. (RL.6.1) First, the narrator says "I was the one who found the kitten," emphasizing their role as rescuer with the phrase "the one" — suggesting they see the finding as a claim or a distinction. Second, the narrator "went back to check on the kitten at lunch," showing that they could not wait until after school — the kitten was on their mind during the school day. Together, these details suggest the narrator formed a quick and strong bond with the kitten, which explains why the loss of it to the woman feels personal and unfair.
