# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 2

## Topic
Supporting Inferences with Multiple Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between explicit information and inference in a literary text
- Build layered inferences by combining several pieces of textual evidence that are not directly stated
- Explain the reasoning chain that connects multiple pieces of evidence to a single inference

## Prerequisites
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence to support analysis (Lesson 1)
- Understanding of the difference between explicit statements and inferences (RL.6.1)
- Experience making inferences about characters using single pieces of evidence
- Familiarity with the multi-evidence model introduced in Lesson 1

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Sticky notes (3-4 per student)
- Highlighters in two colors
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish between what a text says directly and what it implies, and activate inferencing skills.

**Activity: Said vs. Suggested**

Say: "In Lesson 1, we practiced citing several pieces of evidence. Today we are going to focus on a particular kind of analysis: inference. An inference is a conclusion the reader draws based on evidence in the text — something the author suggests without saying it directly."

Write on the board:
- **Explicit:** The text says it directly. You can point to the sentence.
- **Inference:** The text implies it. You must read between the lines using clues.

Say: "Here is a quick test. I will read two statements about a character. Tell me which one is explicit and which is an inference."

Write on the board:
- **Statement A:** "Ren walked into the classroom ten minutes after the bell."
- **Statement B:** "Ren does not care about being on time."

Say: "Which is explicit and which is an inference? Talk to a partner for twenty seconds."

Expected answer: Statement A is explicit — the text directly says Ren was late. Statement B is an inference — we might guess Ren does not care, but the text never says so. Ren could have been late for many reasons. This is an important distinction: an inference must be supported by evidence, not just assumed.

Say: "Today, we are going to build inferences that are supported by several pieces of evidence — not guesses, but careful conclusions built on multiple clues the author has planted."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to combine multiple textual clues to build a well-supported inference.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Inference:** A logical conclusion drawn from evidence in the text that is not directly stated by the author.
- **Explicit information:** Details that are stated directly in the text — facts the reader does not need to interpret.
- **Reasoning chain:** The logical sequence of thinking that connects several pieces of evidence to an inference, showing how each piece contributes to the conclusion.
- **Textual clue:** A word, phrase, detail, or pattern in the text that hints at a meaning the author does not state outright.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned to make inferences using evidence. In seventh grade, the challenge is deeper: you are going to build layered inferences — conclusions that require multiple pieces of evidence working together. A single clue might suggest an inference. Several clues working together confirm it."

Write on the board — **The Inference Ladder:**
1. **Clue 1** — What does this detail suggest on its own?
2. **Clue 2** — What does this second detail add? Does it support the same direction?
3. **Clue 3** — Does this third detail deepen, complicate, or confirm the inference?
4. **Inference** — What conclusion can I now draw that no single clue could support alone?

Say: "The key idea is that some inferences cannot be supported by just one piece of evidence. You need multiple clues that point in the same direction. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Audition"**

> Sofia arrived at the theater an hour before the audition was scheduled to begin. She sat in the third row of the empty auditorium and read through her monologue silently, mouthing the words without making a sound. Every few minutes, she closed her eyes and recited a line from memory, then opened her eyes to check if she had gotten it right. She always had.
>
> Other students began arriving. They clustered near the stage, talking and laughing, comparing the monologues they had chosen. A boy named Tyler was performing his piece for a small audience near the curtain, using grand gestures and a booming voice. Several students applauded when he finished.
>
> Sofia did not join them. She stayed in the third row, notebook in her lap, pencil tapping against the armrest. When her name was called, she walked to the stage without looking at the other students. She stood in the center of the light, took a breath, and began. Her voice was quiet — not uncertain, but controlled, as if she were choosing each word with the precision of someone placing stones on a scale.
>
> When she finished, there was no applause. Just silence. The drama teacher, Ms. Farah, leaned forward in her chair and wrote something on her clipboard.
>
> Sofia returned to the third row, picked up her notebook, and left through the side door without waiting for the results.

Say: "The text never directly tells us how confident Sofia is. But we can infer it. Let me build the inference using multiple clues."

Write on the board:

**Clue 1:** "She sat in the third row of the empty auditorium and read through her monologue silently... She always had [gotten it right]." This suggests thorough preparation and self-assurance — she trusts her own memory.

**Clue 2:** "Sofia did not join them" and "she walked to the stage without looking at the other students." This suggests she is not seeking social validation or comparing herself to others. Her preparation is internal, not performative.

**Clue 3:** "Her voice was quiet — not uncertain, but controlled, as if she were choosing each word with the precision of someone placing stones on a scale." The author explicitly distinguishes "quiet" from "uncertain." Her quietness is deliberate control, not nervousness.

**Clue 4:** "Sofia returned to the third row, picked up her notebook, and left through the side door without waiting for the results." She does not need external confirmation. She already knows how she performed.

**Inference:** Sofia possesses a deep, internalized confidence that does not depend on the audience's reaction or social comparison. Her self-assurance comes from preparation and self-knowledge, not from external validation. No single clue proves this — Clue 1 alone could suggest mere diligence, and Clue 4 alone could suggest indifference. But together, the four clues trace a pattern of someone whose confidence is rooted internally rather than externally.

Say: "Notice that I needed all four clues. If I only had Clue 4 — she left without waiting — I might infer that she did not care about the result. But Clue 1 shows she prepared carefully, which contradicts indifference. The multiple clues working together protect us from making a wrong inference."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice building layered inferences with facilitator support.

Say: "Read this next passage and mark at least three textual clues with your sticky notes. Do not jump to an inference yet — first, collect the clues."

**Passage: "The Storm Jar"**

> Every time a thunderstorm rolled through town, Davi's mother took a glass jar from the kitchen shelf, unscrewed the lid, and set it on the windowsill. She did this without explanation, the same way she washed the dishes after dinner or locked the front door before bed — quietly, as routine. Davi had watched her do it since he was small, and he had never asked why.
>
> He asked on the night of his thirteenth birthday. The storm was fierce — wind bending the trees sideways, rain hammering the roof — and his mother placed the jar on the sill as she always did. The jar was empty. It was always empty.
>
> "What is the jar for?" Davi asked.
>
> His mother did not answer right away. She stood at the window and watched the lightning flash across the sky. Then she said, "When I was a girl, storms frightened me. My grandmother told me to put an empty jar on the windowsill during a storm. She said the jar would catch the fear."
>
> "Did it work?" Davi asked.
>
> His mother smiled. "No. But opening the jar and placing it on the sill meant I had to stand up, walk to the window, and do something. By the time I finished, the worst of the fear had passed. The jar did not catch the fear. The doing caught the fear."
>
> Davi looked at the jar on the sill. Rain streaked the glass behind it. He thought about how many storms his mother had stood through — not because she was not afraid, but because she had learned a way to move through the fear rather than sit inside it.

Say: "Collect your clues. What textual details help you understand something about the mother that the text does not state directly?"

Allow 90 seconds for students to annotate.

Say: "Now, with a partner, try building an inference. Use the Inference Ladder: what does each clue suggest, and what conclusion can you draw when you combine them?"

Expected clues and reasoning:
- She performs the routine "without explanation" and "the same way she washed the dishes" — the ritual is so deeply embedded it is automatic, suggesting she has been managing fear for a very long time.
- "The jar was empty. It was always empty." — The jar has no magical power. The emphasis on emptiness suggests the ritual's value is psychological, not physical.
- "The jar did not catch the fear. The doing caught the fear." — She understands that action is the antidote to fear, not the object itself.

**Inference:** The mother has developed a deeply personal strategy for coping with fear that values deliberate action over passive waiting. The text never states that she is brave — in fact, she admits she was frightened as a child. The inference is that her strength comes not from an absence of fear but from a practiced habit of responding to fear with movement and purpose.

Guide students to write a multi-evidence response using the inference ladder format.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently build layered inferences using several pieces of evidence.

**Passage: "The Translation"**

> When Yuki's family moved from Osaka to Portland, she carried a notebook everywhere. It was not for school. It was a translation notebook — two columns on every page. On the left, she wrote English words she did not yet understand. On the right, she wrote her best guess at what they meant, based on context, gesture, and the expressions on people's faces when they said them.
>
> The notebook filled quickly. Some translations were accurate. "Awesome" meant something was good, though people used it for things Yuki did not consider particularly impressive. "My bad" was an apology, though it sounded to Yuki more like a shrug than a sincere expression of regret.
>
> But the entries that interested Yuki most were the ones she could not translate. A girl in her homeroom, Maisie, kept saying "I'm fine" in a voice that clearly meant she was not fine. Yuki wrote "I'm fine" in the left column and, after three weeks of observation, wrote in the right column: "Please ask again."
>
> Her English teacher, Mr. Dietrich, ended every class by saying "You guys are killing it." Yuki knew this was praise, but she could not find the logic in it. She wrote "killing it" in the left column and, after careful thought, wrote: "Doing something so well it appears violent."
>
> By November, the notebook had become something more than a translation tool. It was a record of how language worked differently on the surface and underneath. Yuki began to understand that fluency was not about knowing every word. It was about knowing which words people meant and which words they hid behind.

**Directions:** Read the passage carefully. For each question, build your inference using several pieces of evidence from the text.

1. What can you infer about how Yuki approaches the challenge of learning a new language? Cite at least three pieces of evidence and explain how they collectively support your inference.
2. The text says Yuki wrote "Please ask again" as the translation of "I'm fine." What inference can you draw about Yuki's understanding of human communication? Cite this detail along with at least one other piece of evidence.
3. What does the final paragraph suggest about the kind of understanding Yuki is developing? Cite evidence from the final paragraph and connect it to at least one earlier detail to build your inference.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3, citing at least two pieces of evidence per response.
**Stretch:** In your response to question 1, arrange your evidence in a deliberate order — from the most surface-level observation to the deepest insight — and explain why you chose that order.
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph exploring this inference: Yuki's notebook reveals that understanding a culture requires observing what people mean, not just what they say. Cite at least three pieces of evidence, including at least one from the notebook entries and one from the final paragraph.

### Interleaved Review
4. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.1) Choose one of Yuki's notebook entries ("I'm fine" or "killing it") and explain how the author uses it to develop Yuki's character. Cite the entry as evidence and explain what it reveals.
5. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.5) The passage is structured around the notebook — its creation, its entries, and its transformation. How does this structural choice contribute to the reader's understanding of Yuki's growth?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Elijah always sat in the same seat on the bus — the window seat four rows from the back. He kept his headphones on even when no music was playing. The other students assumed he was listening to something, so they left him alone, which was the point. On the day the new student boarded the bus and sat next to him without asking, Elijah did not remove the headphones. But he moved his backpack off the seat.

1. What can you infer about why Elijah wears headphones with no music playing? Cite at least two pieces of evidence.
2. What does the final detail — moving his backpack — suggest when combined with the headphone detail?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Elijah wears headphones as a social barrier, not for music. The text says he keeps them on "even when no music was playing," which shows the headphones serve a purpose unrelated to listening. The next sentence confirms: "The other students assumed he was listening to something, so they left him alone, which was the point." The phrase "which was the point" makes the inference nearly explicit — the headphones are a deliberate tool for creating distance.
2. The final detail — "he moved his backpack off the seat" — contrasts with the headphone barrier. He does not remove the headphones (maintaining the social wall), but he clears space for the new student (creating a small opening). Combined, these details suggest Elijah is not opposed to connection but is selective about it. He keeps his guard up while quietly making room for someone who approached without being deterred by the barrier. The inference is that Elijah wants connection on his own terms — he will not invite it, but he will not reject it when it arrives.

**Mastery = Student cites at least two pieces of evidence and explains a logical inference that no single piece of evidence could fully support alone.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the difference between explicit statements and inferences using the warm-up format.
- Provide a graphic organizer: three boxes labeled "Clue 1," "Clue 2," and "Clue 3" with arrows pointing to a single "Inference" box. Have the student fill in clues first, then state the inference.
- Practice with the Guided Practice passage using a simpler inference question.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3, which shifts from evidence-based analysis to thematic analysis.
- Encourage the student to practice the Inference Ladder in their independent reading.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Inference Journal:** The student selects a passage from a book they are reading independently and identifies three textual clues that support an inference about a character's motivation. They write the inference and the clue chain in their notebook.
2. **Misleading Clues:** The student rereads the "Audition" passage and considers an alternative inference: Sofia is anxious, not confident. Can any of the same evidence support this different inference? Why or why not? The student writes a paragraph evaluating the strength of each interpretation.
3. **Inference Exchange:** The student writes a short paragraph (5-6 sentences) about a character in which the character's emotion is implied but never stated. They exchange paragraphs with a partner, and each student identifies the textual clues and states the inference with evidence.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Key distinction for this lesson:** Help students understand that a strong inference is not the same as a guess. A guess lacks evidence; an inference is built on specific textual details. If a student cannot point to evidence that supports their inference, they are guessing.
- **Common error:** Students may confuse an inference with an opinion. "I think Elijah is shy" is an opinion. "The text suggests Elijah uses headphones as a social barrier, based on the detail that no music is playing and the narrator's confirmation that isolation 'was the point'" is an inference supported by evidence.
- **Scaffolding tip:** For students who struggle to combine multiple clues, have them first write each clue on a separate sticky note, arrange the notes in order, and then verbally state the inference before writing it. The physical manipulation of evidence can make the reasoning chain more visible.
- **Pacing note:** The Guided Practice passage about the storm jar may spark rich discussion about family traditions and coping strategies. Allow the conversation to develop but redirect to the analytical task — the goal is inference-building, not personal sharing.
- **Connection to Lesson 1:** Remind students that the multi-evidence model from Lesson 1 is the structural framework for today's inferencing work. The difference is that Lesson 1 focused on explicit claims, while Lesson 2 focuses on conclusions the reader must draw from clues.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Yuki approaches language learning as an analytical observer, not just a memorizer. The first clue is that she creates a two-column notebook with English words on the left and "her best guess at what they meant, based on context, gesture, and the expressions on people's faces" on the right. This shows she is decoding language through social observation, not just dictionary definitions. The second clue is her translation of "I'm fine" as "Please ask again" — she has spent three weeks watching Maisie and concluded that the phrase carries the opposite of its literal meaning. This shows Yuki treats language as social behavior, not just vocabulary. The third clue is the final paragraph: "fluency was not about knowing every word. It was about knowing which words people meant and which words they hid behind." This confirms that Yuki has moved from collecting individual translations to understanding the gap between surface language and actual meaning. Together, these three clues build an inference that Yuki approaches language as a system of social communication requiring empathy and observation, not just linguistic knowledge.

2. Yuki's translation of "I'm fine" as "Please ask again" reveals that she understands communication operates on two levels — what people say and what they actually mean. She arrives at this translation after "three weeks of observation," showing that she treats human interaction as something worthy of sustained, careful study. This connects to her method of decoding words "based on context, gesture, and the expressions on people's faces" — she is reading the whole person, not just the words. The inference is that Yuki perceives language as inherently incomplete: words alone do not carry full meaning, and understanding someone requires attending to what goes unsaid as much as what is spoken.

3. The final paragraph suggests Yuki is developing an understanding of language that goes beyond fluency — she is learning that communication involves intentional concealment. The text states the notebook "had become something more than a translation tool. It was a record of how language worked differently on the surface and underneath." The phrase "on the surface and underneath" suggests two layers of meaning. This connects to her earlier translation of "I'm fine" as "Please ask again" — a moment where surface language directly contradicted underlying meaning. The inference is that Yuki is learning a kind of emotional literacy that many native speakers never consciously develop: the ability to perceive and name the gap between what is said and what is felt.

### Interleaved Review
4. (RL.6.1) The "I'm fine" entry develops Yuki's character by showing her as deeply empathetic and observant. The text says she spent "three weeks of observation" before writing "Please ask again" as the translation. This reveals that Yuki does not accept surface-level communication at face value — she invests time in understanding what people truly mean. It shows her as someone who pays careful attention to others' emotional states and recognizes that language can be a mask. The entry characterizes Yuki as a thoughtful interpreter of human behavior, not just a language learner.

5. (RL.6.5) The passage is structured around the notebook's evolution — from its creation as a practical tool, through specific entries that demonstrate Yuki's growing insight, to the final paragraph where the notebook becomes "something more than a translation tool." This structure mirrors Yuki's own growth: she begins with a simple system (two columns, words and definitions) and ends with a philosophical understanding of language. The notebook is the structural spine of the passage, and its transformation is Yuki's transformation. By organizing the text around the notebook rather than around events or time, the author keeps the reader focused on Yuki's intellectual and emotional development.
