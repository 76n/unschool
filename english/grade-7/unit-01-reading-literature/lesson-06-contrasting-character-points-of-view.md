# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 6

## Topic
Contrasting Character Points of View

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify how different characters in the same text perceive the same events, people, or situations differently
- Analyze how an author develops contrasting points of view through dialogue, internal thoughts, actions, and descriptive choices
- Explain what the contrast between characters' perspectives reveals about each character and about the text's themes

## Prerequisites
- Ability to explain how an author develops the point of view of a narrator or speaker (RL.6.6)
- Understanding of first-person, third-person limited, and third-person omniscient narration (Grade 6)
- Experience citing several pieces of evidence to support analysis (Lessons 1-2)
- Familiarity with analyzing how details reveal character traits and motivations

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Highlighters in two colors (one per character's perspective)
- Sticky notes (3-4 per student)
- T-chart or two-column chart template
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept that different characters can witness the same event and understand it entirely differently.

**Activity: Same Event, Different Eyes**

Say: "In sixth grade, you learned how an author develops a narrator's point of view — whose eyes we see through and how that shapes the story. In seventh grade, we are going a step further. We are going to analyze how authors develop and contrast the points of view of different characters within the same text."

Write on the board:
- **Event:** A student receives a trophy at a school assembly.
- **Character A (the student):** Feels embarrassed because she does not think she deserved it.
- **Character B (the student's parent):** Feels proud and emotional.
- **Character C (another student who was not chosen):** Feels jealous and confused about the selection process.

Say: "Same event. Three completely different experiences. In literature, authors use this technique to show that reality is not one thing — it is shaped by who is looking. Today, you are going to learn how to identify and analyze these contrasts."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to identify and analyze contrasting character perspectives.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Contrasting points of view:** The different ways characters perceive, interpret, and respond to the same events, people, or situations in a text.
- **Perspective lens:** The unique combination of a character's experiences, knowledge, emotions, and biases that shapes how they see the world.
- **Dialogue contrast:** A technique in which characters reveal different perspectives through what they say — and what they choose not to say — about the same situation.
- **Situational irony:** When the reader knows something a character does not, often because contrasting perspectives reveal information that individual characters lack.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Authors develop contrasting points of view through four main techniques."

Write on the board — **Four Techniques for Contrasting Perspectives:**
1. **Dialogue** — Characters say different things about the same situation, revealing their different lenses.
2. **Internal thoughts** — The narrator gives access to what characters are thinking, showing different interpretations of the same event.
3. **Actions** — Characters respond to the same situation with different behaviors, revealing their priorities.
4. **Descriptive language** — The author describes the same setting, person, or event in different ways depending on whose perspective is active.

Say: "Let me show you all four techniques in a single passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Empty Chair"**

> The Okafor family sat around the dinner table on Thanksgiving evening. The turkey was carved. The candles were lit. The chair at the head of the table was empty. It had been empty for eleven months, since Grandpa Emeka had passed away the previous January.
>
> Adaeze, the oldest daughter, set a plate in front of the empty chair — turkey, yams, greens, the same portions she served everyone else. She did this without asking anyone's permission. It was her way of saying he was still part of the family.
>
> Her younger brother, Chidi, stared at the plate. "Why did you do that?" he said. His voice was flat, not angry. "He is not here."
>
> "I know he is not here," Adaeze said quietly. "That is why the plate is there."
>
> Their mother, Ngozi, watched both of them. She understood Adaeze's gesture — the plate was an act of love, a refusal to let absence erase presence. She also understood Chidi's reaction — he was thirteen and still learning that grief did not follow a straight line. Seeing the plate reminded him of the loss in a way he was not ready to feel again.
>
> Ngozi picked up her fork. "Let us eat," she said. She did not comment on the plate. She did not ask Chidi to accept it or Adaeze to remove it. She let both responses exist at the same table.
>
> Chidi ate quietly. Halfway through the meal, without looking at anyone, he reached over and moved the salt shaker closer to the empty plate. It was Grandpa Emeka's habit — he had always salted everything too heavily. Adaeze noticed. She did not say anything. She just smiled and passed the rolls.

Say: "Let me analyze the contrasting points of view using the four techniques."

Write on the board:

**Adaeze's perspective (developed through action and dialogue):** She sets a plate at the empty chair — an action that reveals her approach to grief: keeping the absent person present through ritual. Her dialogue confirms this: "That is why the plate is there." She sees the empty chair as an opportunity for connection, not a reminder of loss.

**Chidi's perspective (developed through dialogue and later action):** His question — "Why did you do that?" — and his statement — "He is not here" — reveal that Chidi's grief operates through avoidance. He is not ready to confront the loss symbolically. But his later action — moving the salt shaker — shows that his perspective shifts during the meal. He finds his own way to honor Grandpa Emeka, one that acknowledges the reality of absence while still participating in the ritual.

**Ngozi's perspective (developed through internal thoughts and restraint):** The narrator gives us access to Ngozi's understanding of both children. She "understood Adaeze's gesture" and "also understood Chidi's reaction." Her action — saying "Let us eat" without commenting on the plate — reveals a perspective shaped by parental wisdom: she sees both responses as valid and does not force either child to adopt the other's approach.

**What the contrast reveals:** The three perspectives collectively show that grief is not a single experience — it takes different forms depending on a person's relationship with loss, and a healthy family makes room for multiple forms of mourning.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying and analyzing contrasting perspectives with facilitator support.

Say: "Read this next passage and use your T-chart. In the left column, track one character's perspective. In the right column, track the other's. Note the technique the author uses for each (dialogue, thoughts, actions, or description)."

**Passage: "The Assignment"**

> Mr. Reeves assigned a group project: research a local historical figure and present the findings to the class. He paired Jada with Sam. They had never worked together before.
>
> Jada started a shared document that evening. By ten o'clock, she had outlined the project, divided the research into sections, created a bibliography template, and color-coded the timeline. She texted Sam: "I started the outline. Let me know if you want to change anything."
>
> Sam read the text at midnight. He opened the document and scrolled through it. It was thorough, organized, and completely finished. There was nothing to change — and nothing left for him to do. He typed in the chat box: "Looks good." He did not add anything else.
>
> At school the next day, Jada asked Sam when he could work on his sections. Sam shrugged. "I figured you had it covered."
>
> "That was not the point," Jada said. "I did the outline so we could divide the work. Not so I could do all of it."
>
> Sam leaned back in his chair. "I mean, the outline is already better than anything I would have made. If I redo it, it is going to be worse."
>
> Jada stared at him. "It does not have to be as organized as mine. It has to have your research in it. The point is the research, not the formatting."
>
> Sam was quiet. He had not thought about it that way. He had looked at the document and seen something finished. Jada had looked at the same document and seen something that was only started.

Say: "Fill in your T-chart. On the left, track Jada's perspective: what she says, does, and values. On the right, track Sam's."

Allow 2 minutes for chart work and partner discussion.

Expected responses:
- **Jada's perspective:** She creates the outline as a framework for collaboration, not as the finished product. Her dialogue reveals frustration: "I did the outline so we could divide the work." She values participation and shared effort. She sees the document as a starting point.
- **Sam's perspective:** He sees the completed outline and assumes the work is done. His shrug and "I figured you had it covered" reveal that he interprets competence as completion. His later admission — "If I redo it, it is going to be worse" — reveals that he withdrew not from laziness but from intimidation. He sees the document as a finished product.

Say: "What does the contrast reveal about how each character defines 'contribution'?"

Guide discussion toward: Jada defines contribution as putting in effort and sharing the workload. Sam initially defines contribution as quality of product — if someone can do it better, why should he do it at all? The contrast reveals different understandings of collaboration.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze contrasting character perspectives.

**Passage: "The Fence"**

> The Garcia family and the Kowalski family had lived next door to each other for fifteen years. A wooden fence separated their backyards — six feet tall, weathered gray, with a gate that had rusted shut years ago.
>
> When a storm knocked down a twenty-foot section of the fence one March night, both families walked outside the next morning to survey the damage.
>
> Elena Garcia stood on her side and looked at the gap. Without the fence, she could see the Kowalskis' yard for the first time in years — the herb garden along the back wall, the bird feeder shaped like a tiny barn, the string of lights draped over the patio. She had not known any of this existed. The gap in the fence felt like a window opening.
>
> "We should fix it this weekend," said her husband, Roberto. "Before the weather gets worse."
>
> On the other side, Anna Kowalski studied the fallen boards. She saw her own yard exposed — the herb garden she tended every morning, the bird feeder her daughter had made in shop class, the patio lights she turned on every evening. Without the fence, the yard felt less like hers. The gap felt like a wall removed from around a room that was supposed to be private.
>
> Her son, Tomek, walked through the gap and into the Garcia yard. He had never been in it before. There was a basketball hoop, a fire pit, and a hammock strung between two elms. "This is cool," he said. "Why did we never come over here?"
>
> Elena smiled. Anna said nothing.
>
> That evening, Roberto brought wood and tools to rebuild the section. Elena stopped him. "Maybe we fix the gate instead," she said. "So it opens."

**Directions:** Read the passage carefully. For each question, cite evidence and explain the contrasting perspectives.

1. Using a T-chart or two-column format, compare how Elena and Anna perceive the fallen fence. For each character, cite at least two pieces of evidence and identify which technique (dialogue, action, description, or thought) the author uses.
2. How does Tomek's perspective differ from both Elena's and Anna's? What does his reaction suggest about generational differences in how people view boundaries?
3. What is the significance of Elena's final suggestion — "Maybe we fix the gate instead. So it opens"? How does this line reflect her perspective and contrast with Roberto's initial response?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3, citing at least two pieces of evidence per question.
**Stretch:** In your response to question 1, explain not just how the perspectives differ but what the author achieves by placing them side by side — what does the reader understand because both perspectives are present?
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph analyzing what the fence symbolizes differently for Elena and Anna. Use the concept of "perspective lens" from the vocabulary to explain how each character's lens shapes what the fence means to her.

### Interleaved Review
4. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.6) How does the author develop Elena's point of view across the passage? Identify at least two techniques and cite evidence for each.
5. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.2) What theme does the contrast between Elena and Anna suggest? State it as a complete sentence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> At the end of the school year, the seventh graders cleaned out their lockers. Mariam peeled off the photographs taped inside her locker door — pictures of her friends, her soccer team, a ticket stub from a concert. She handled each one carefully, placing them in a folder. "I do not want to forget this year," she said.
>
> Next to her, James swept crumpled papers and an old sandwich wrapper into a trash bag. "Finally done," he said, slamming the locker shut. "Bring on summer."

1. How do Mariam and James view the end of the school year differently? Cite one piece of evidence for each character and explain what it reveals about their perspective.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Mariam views the end of the year with nostalgia and a desire to preserve memories. She "handled each one carefully, placing them in a folder" and says "I do not want to forget this year," which reveals she sees the school year as something valuable worth holding onto. James views the end of the year as a release — he sweeps "crumpled papers and an old sandwich wrapper into a trash bag" and says "Finally done. Bring on summer," revealing he sees the year as something to move past rather than preserve. The contrast shows two fundamentally different relationships with time: Mariam holds onto the past while James races toward the future.

**Mastery = Student identifies contrasting perspectives for both characters, cites evidence for each, and explains what the evidence reveals about each character's point of view.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Provide a two-column graphic organizer labeled with character names and guide the student to sort evidence into each column.
- Use the warm-up example (trophy at an assembly) to practice identifying how the same event looks different to different people.
- Have the student reread the exit ticket and underline action words for each character in different colors to make the contrast visible.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7, which examines the techniques authors use to develop and shift narrator perspective.
- Encourage the student to notice contrasting character perspectives in their independent reading.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Perspective Rewrite:** The student rewrites the "Empty Chair" passage entirely from Chidi's first-person perspective. They then compare their version to the original and write a paragraph about what information is gained or lost when the perspective narrows.
2. **Real-World Contrast:** The student identifies a recent event at school or in their community and describes it from two different people's perspectives (without using real names). They analyze how the different lenses shape the description.
3. **Character Debate:** The student writes a short dialogue between Elena and Anna about whether to rebuild the fence or fix the gate. Each character must stay true to the perspective the passage establishes.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Key shift from Grade 6:** In sixth grade, students explained how an author develops one narrator's point of view. In seventh grade, the emphasis is on contrast — how do multiple characters' perspectives interact, conflict, or complement each other? Push students to analyze the relationship between perspectives, not just describe each one separately.
- **T-charts are essential:** The two-column chart is the most effective tool for this lesson. Have students physically separate the perspectives into columns to prevent blending them in their analysis.
- **Common error:** Students may describe what happens to each character rather than analyzing how each character perceives what happens. Redirect by asking: "You told me what they did. Now tell me how they see the situation — what does it mean to each of them?"
- **Cultural sensitivity:** "The Empty Chair" passage deals with grief and a family member's death. Preview this with students and offer space for those who may find it personally relevant.
- **Pacing note:** The Guided Practice passage about the group project may generate strong student opinions. Allow brief discussion but keep the focus on textual analysis of the characters rather than personal experiences with group work.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Elena's perspective:** The author uses descriptive language to develop Elena's view: she sees the Kowalskis' yard for the first time — "the herb garden along the back wall, the bird feeder shaped like a tiny barn, the string of lights draped over the patio" — and the narration states "The gap in the fence felt like a window opening." These details (developed through description) reveal that Elena perceives the fallen fence as an opportunity for discovery and connection. She did not know what was on the other side, and the gap gives her access. The author also uses her dialogue at the end: "Maybe we fix the gate instead. So it opens," which confirms her view that the barrier should become a passage.

   **Anna's perspective:** The author uses internal reaction to develop Anna's view: she sees "her own yard exposed" and the narration states the gap "felt like a wall removed from around a room that was supposed to be private." The word "exposed" suggests vulnerability, and the room metaphor suggests she thinks of her yard as an interior, personal space. The author also uses Anna's silence — "Elena smiled. Anna said nothing" — which contrasts with Elena's openness and suggests discomfort with the new visibility.

2. Tomek's perspective differs from both adults because he sees the gap with pure curiosity and no sense of loss or anxiety. He walks through immediately — an action neither adult takes — and says "This is cool. Why did we never come over here?" His question implies he has never considered the fence as meaningful. While Elena sees an opening and Anna sees a breach, Tomek sees an unexplored space. His reaction suggests that for younger people who did not build the fence or establish the boundary, barriers between neighbors are arbitrary rather than meaningful.

3. Elena's suggestion to fix the gate "so it opens" is significant because it reframes the problem. Roberto's instinct is to rebuild the wall as it was — restore the boundary. Elena's instinct is to convert the barrier into a passage — maintain the structure but add the ability to cross it. This reflects her perspective throughout the passage: she has been excited by the gap, by the discovery of the Kowalskis' garden and lights. Her suggestion takes that excitement and makes it permanent — not by tearing down the fence but by making it possible to choose to walk through it. The contrast with Roberto highlights Elena's desire for connection versus Roberto's desire for normalcy.

### Interleaved Review
4. (RL.6.6) The author develops Elena's point of view through two main techniques. First, descriptive language: when Elena looks through the gap, the text provides detailed, warm descriptions of the Kowalskis' yard — "the herb garden along the back wall, the bird feeder shaped like a tiny barn" — which reflects Elena's sense of wonder and discovery. The details are specific and appreciative because Elena is seeing them with fresh eyes. Second, dialogue: Elena's final line — "Maybe we fix the gate instead. So it opens" — develops her perspective as someone who values openness and connection, choosing to transform a barrier into a passageway rather than simply rebuilding it.

5. (RL.6.2) Theme: The boundaries we build between ourselves and others can become so familiar that we forget to question whether they still serve a purpose — and sometimes it takes an unexpected disruption to reveal what we have been missing. (Accept variations about how barriers can prevent connection, how perspective shapes whether a change feels like a loss or an opportunity, or how communities benefit from openness.)
