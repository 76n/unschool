# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 5

## Topic
Objective Summary vs. Personal Response

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write an objective summary of a literary text that captures key events and central ideas without inserting personal opinions
- Identify and eliminate opinion language, evaluative adjectives, and personal judgments from a summary
- Distinguish between an objective summary and a personal response, understanding the purpose and audience for each

## Prerequisites
- Ability to provide a summary of a text distinct from personal opinions or judgments (RL.6.2)
- Experience determining theme and analyzing its development (Lessons 3-4)
- Familiarity with identifying key events and central ideas in a narrative
- Understanding of the difference between factual statements and opinions

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Index cards (3-4 per student)
- Highlighters in two colors (one for objective language, one for opinion language)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students recognize opinion language embedded in writing that appears to be factual.

**Activity: Spot the Opinion**

Say: "In sixth grade, you practiced writing summaries that were distinct from personal opinions. This year, we are going to sharpen that skill. The tricky part about opinion language is that it sometimes hides inside sentences that look factual. Let me show you."

Write on the board:
- **Sentence A:** "The character makes a difficult decision about leaving home."
- **Sentence B:** "The character makes a heartbreaking decision about leaving home."

Say: "Which sentence contains an opinion? Talk to a partner for fifteen seconds."

Expected answer: Sentence B. "Heartbreaking" is an evaluative word — it reflects the writer's emotional response. "Difficult" is closer to objective because the text can support it with evidence of struggle, but "heartbreaking" imposes a specific emotional reaction that not every reader may share.

Write on the board:
- **Opinion signals:** amazing, boring, heartbreaking, best, worst, obviously, unfortunately, sadly, beautifully, should have, the reader will feel...
- **Objective signals:** the text describes, the character chooses, the passage shows, the conflict involves, the resolution occurs when...

Say: "Today, you are going to learn to write summaries that are entirely free of these opinion signals — and you are going to learn why that matters."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students the principles of objective summary and the distinction from personal response.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Objective summary:** A brief, neutral account of a text's key events, characters, and central ideas that does not include the writer's personal opinions, evaluations, or emotional reactions.
- **Personal response:** A written reaction to a text that includes the reader's opinions, feelings, interpretations, and connections — valuable for analysis but distinct from summary.
- **Evaluative language:** Words and phrases that express judgment about quality, value, or emotional impact (e.g., "amazing," "unfortunately," "the reader cannot help but feel").
- **Neutral language:** Words and phrases that describe events and ideas without imposing the writer's judgment (e.g., "the character decides," "the text reveals," "the conflict arises").

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Both objective summary and personal response are important skills. But they serve different purposes. An objective summary tells someone what a text is about — the events, characters, and central idea — without telling them what to think about it. A personal response tells someone what you think about the text. The problem arises when writers mix the two without realizing it."

Write on the board — **Two Tools, Two Purposes:**
| | Objective Summary | Personal Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Inform the reader about the text | Share your reaction to the text |
| **Language** | Neutral, factual | Evaluative, personal |
| **Includes** | Key events, central idea, characters | Opinions, feelings, connections |
| **Excludes** | Personal opinions, evaluative adjectives | N/A — opinions are welcome |

Say: "Let me show you both types using the same passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Last Notebook"**

> For three years, Celia had kept a notebook of observations. Not a diary — she never wrote about herself. She wrote about what she noticed: the way the crossing guard at Oak Street held his stop sign like a sword, the way Mrs. Paulson's dog barked at bicycles but not at cars, the pattern of cracks in the sidewalk outside the library that looked like a river delta if you squinted.
>
> The notebook was almost full. Only a few blank pages remained. Celia began to ration them, writing shorter entries, skipping days, choosing only the observations that felt essential. The crossing guard retired and was replaced by a woman who held the sign with both hands. Celia did not write it down. It was interesting, but it was not essential.
>
> On the last page, she wrote a single observation: "The oak tree in Miller Park has a branch that reaches across the walking path like an arm offering to shake hands. It has been there for years. No one looks up."
>
> She closed the notebook and put it in her desk drawer. She did not buy a new one. Instead, she started walking through the neighborhood without writing anything down. She noticed the same things — the cracks, the dogs, the trees — but she let them pass through her without capturing them. It felt different. Lighter, but also less certain, as if the observations might disappear if she did not pin them to a page.
>
> After a week, she bought a new notebook. On the first page, she wrote: "Starting over. Same eyes, new pages."

Say: "Here is an objective summary."

Write on the board:

**Objective Summary:** Celia keeps a notebook of observations about her neighborhood for three years. As the notebook fills, she becomes selective about which observations she records, writing less frequently and choosing only what she considers essential. After filling the last page, she stops writing and tries to observe without recording. She finds the experience lighter but unsettling, as if her observations might be lost without being written down. She eventually buys a new notebook and begins again.

Say: "Notice what is not in that summary: no words like 'beautiful,' 'meaningful,' 'the reader feels,' or 'she should have.' It tells the reader what happens and what the central situation is. Now here is a personal response."

**Personal Response:** This passage is a moving exploration of what it means to be an observer. Celia's dedication to recording her neighborhood is admirable, and the reader cannot help but feel the weight of her decision to ration the last pages. The final line — "Same eyes, new pages" — is the most powerful moment in the passage because it captures the tension between continuity and fresh starts. I think the passage suggests that we need to record our observations to make them real, which is something I personally relate to as someone who keeps a journal.

Say: "Both of these are well written. But they serve different purposes. The summary tells you about the text. The response tells you about the reader's reaction. For RL.7.2, you need to be able to write the first kind — the objective summary — without accidentally mixing in the second."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying and removing opinion language from summaries.

Say: "I am going to give you a passage and a summary that contains hidden opinion language. Your job is to find the opinion words and rewrite the sentences to make them objective."

**Passage: "The Trade"**

> Diego had collected baseball cards since he was seven. His collection filled three binders — over four hundred cards organized by team, by year, by player position. He knew the value of every card. He knew which ones were rare. He kept his most valuable card — a mint-condition rookie card of a Hall of Fame pitcher — in a plastic sleeve inside a fireproof lockbox in his closet.
>
> When his neighbor, an eight-year-old named Suki, showed him her collection — twelve cards held together by a rubber band, most of them bent or creased — Diego felt a pang of something. Not pity. Something closer to recognition. He remembered being seven with a handful of cards and no binder, sorting them on the floor of his bedroom, imagining the collection they would become.
>
> Suki asked if she could trade one of her cards for one of his. She offered a common outfielder card with a crease down the middle. Diego looked at it. It was worth nothing in terms of market value. But Suki held it out with both hands, carefully, the way Diego held his Hall of Fame card.
>
> He went inside and came back with a card from his duplicates — a solid starting pitcher, no creases, a player with good stats. "Fair trade?" he said.
>
> Suki's eyes went wide. "Really? This one is way better than mine."
>
> "Every collection starts somewhere," Diego said.

**Flawed Summary (with hidden opinion language):**

> Diego is an impressive collector who has carefully built a wonderful collection of over four hundred baseball cards. When he meets Suki, a sweet eight-year-old neighbor with a sadly small collection, he kindly trades her one of his better cards. The heartwarming moment shows that Diego understands the joy of collecting. The beautiful ending — "Every collection starts somewhere" — proves that generosity is more important than value.

Say: "With a partner, highlight every opinion word or evaluative phrase in this summary. Then rewrite it on your own paper using neutral language."

Allow 3 minutes for highlighting and discussion.

Expected opinion words to identify: "impressive," "wonderful," "sweet," "sadly small," "kindly," "heartwarming," "beautiful," "proves that generosity is more important than value."

Say: "Let us write a corrected version together."

Guide students to write: Diego is a dedicated baseball card collector with over four hundred cards organized in three binders. When he meets his eight-year-old neighbor Suki, who has a small collection of twelve bent cards, he recognizes her enthusiasm from his own experience starting out as a young collector. Suki offers to trade a common, creased card for one of his, and Diego gives her a solid duplicate card in return. The exchange suggests that Diego values the experience of building a collection more than the market value of individual cards.

Say: "See the difference? We kept the key events and the central idea. We removed every word that told the reader how to feel about those events."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write objective summaries and distinguish them from personal responses.

**Passage: "The Relay"**

> Anika had trained for the four-hundred-meter relay all season. She was the anchor — the final runner, the one who crossed the finish line. The position suited her. She was fast, competitive, and calm under pressure. The regional meet was on Saturday, and her team had a real chance at qualifying for state.
>
> On Wednesday, the team's second runner, Grace, twisted her knee during practice. The coach pulled Anika aside. "Grace is out. I need you to run second leg instead of anchor."
>
> "Second leg? That is not my position."
>
> "I know. But Tessa can anchor. She has the speed. What I need in the second position is someone who can handle a clean handoff under pressure. That is you."
>
> Anika said nothing. She had spent months preparing for the moment of crossing the finish line — the crowd, the clock, the final surge. Second position was invisible. The second runner handed off the baton and jogged to the infield while someone else finished. No one cheered for the second runner.
>
> She trained for second position for three days. The handoff was different — receiving from the first runner and passing to the third. It required timing, trust, and a kind of awareness that had nothing to do with personal speed. She had to match the first runner's pace perfectly, receive the baton without breaking stride, accelerate into the exchange zone, and release it cleanly to the third runner.
>
> On Saturday, Anika ran second leg. Her handoff was flawless. Tessa anchored and crossed the finish line in first place. The team qualified for state. The crowd cheered Tessa's name.
>
> In the team photo, Anika stood in the back row, smiling. She was not smiling for the camera. She was smiling because she knew something the crowd did not: the race had been won in the exchange zone, not at the finish line.

**Directions:**

1. Write an objective summary of this passage in 4-5 sentences. Include key events and the central situation but no personal opinions or evaluative language.
2. Now write a personal response of 3-4 sentences. Include your opinions, feelings, and connections to the passage.
3. Underline any words in your personal response that would not belong in an objective summary. Explain why they are appropriate in a response but not in a summary.
4. A classmate writes this summary: "Anika selflessly gives up her dream position to help the team. Her amazing handoff wins the race." Identify the opinion language and rewrite the summary using neutral terms.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4.
**Stretch:** In your objective summary, include the theme of the passage stated in neutral language — not as your opinion, but as what the text conveys.
**Challenge:** Write a third version: a one-sentence objective summary that captures the passage's central idea in under thirty words. Then explain what you had to cut and why.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.1) Cite two pieces of evidence that reveal Anika's attitude toward being moved to second position. Explain what each piece suggests about her character.
6. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.6) From whose perspective is this passage told? How does this perspective shape the reader's understanding of the relay?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Owen spent the summer building a treehouse with his father. They worked every weekend, measuring boards, sawing joints, and hammering nails into the old oak in the backyard. When it was finished in August, Owen climbed up alone and sat on the platform. Through the leaves, he could see the whole neighborhood — rooftops, gardens, the school in the distance. He realized the treehouse was not really about having a place to sit above the world. It was about the Saturdays spent building it.

Write an objective summary of this passage in 2-3 sentences.

**Expected Answers:**
Owen and his father spend the summer building a treehouse, working every weekend. When the treehouse is complete, Owen sits in it alone and sees his neighborhood from above. He realizes the value of the project was in the process of building it together, not in the finished structure.

(Deduct credit for opinion language such as "beautiful," "meaningful," "the reader feels," "heartwarming," "it was amazing that," etc.)

**Mastery = Student writes a summary that includes key events and the central idea, uses neutral language throughout, and contains no evaluative adjectives, personal opinions, or emotional directives to the reader.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Have the student highlight their summary in two colors: one for factual statements, one for opinion statements. If they cannot identify the opinion language, review the opinion signals list from the warm-up.
- Provide a sentence frame: "The passage is about ___ who ___. The central situation involves ___. The text conveys the idea that ___."
- Practice with the Guided Practice passage using partner feedback to identify hidden opinion words.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 6, which shifts to analyzing contrasting character points of view.
- Encourage the student to practice objective summaries in their independent reading log.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Summary Swap:** The student writes an objective summary and a personal response for "The Relay" on separate index cards (unlabeled). They exchange cards with a partner, and the partner identifies which is which and highlights the evidence that distinguishes them.
2. **Summary Revision:** The student takes a personal response they have written previously (from any class) and revises it into an objective summary. They highlight every word or phrase they had to change and write a brief reflection on what they learned about their own writing habits.
3. **Summary Challenge:** The student reads a short text from their independent reading and writes three versions: a one-sentence summary, a three-sentence summary, and a five-sentence summary. They reflect on what must be included at every length and what can be cut.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Most common mistake:** Students insert opinion language unconsciously — words like "unfortunately," "amazingly," or "the reader feels" creep in without the student realizing they are editorializing. The two-color highlighting exercise in Guided Practice is the most effective tool for building this awareness.
- **Both skills matter:** Be clear that personal response is not inferior to objective summary — it is simply a different tool. Students should not feel that their opinions are unwelcome. The goal is to help them control when they use each type of writing intentionally.
- **Evaluative verbs:** Watch for evaluative verbs like "proves," "shows how important," and "demonstrates the beauty of." These are subtler than evaluative adjectives but equally opinion-laden.
- **Connection to Lessons 3-4:** When students include the theme in an objective summary, they should state it neutrally: "The text conveys the idea that..." rather than "The passage beautifully reveals that..." This bridges thematic analysis (Lessons 3-4) with the objectivity skill in this lesson.
- **Pacing note:** The Guided Practice exercise of fixing a flawed summary is highly engaging and may take longer than expected. If time runs short, assign question 4 of Independent Practice as homework.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Objective summary: Anika is the anchor runner on her four-hundred-meter relay team, but when the second runner is injured, the coach moves Anika to the second position and puts another runner, Tessa, at anchor. Anika trains for three days to master the handoff required in the new position. At the regional meet, her handoff is executed cleanly, and Tessa anchors the team to a first-place finish that qualifies them for state. Afterward, Anika reflects that the race was decided by the baton exchange, not the finish line — a fact the crowd does not recognize.

2. Personal response (example): I found this passage inspiring because Anika sacrifices personal glory for the team's success. The ending is especially powerful — "the race had been won in the exchange zone, not at the finish line" — because it reminds me that the most important contributions are often invisible. I connected with Anika's frustration because I have been in situations where my role was not recognized even though it mattered. The passage makes me think about how we define success.

3. Opinion words in the personal response: "inspiring," "sacrifices personal glory" (evaluative framing), "especially powerful," "reminds me," "I connected," "makes me think." These words express the reader's emotional and intellectual reaction — they are appropriate in a personal response because the purpose is to share the reader's experience, but they would not belong in an objective summary because a summary reports what the text says, not what the reader feels.

4. Correction of flawed summary: "Selflessly" is evaluative (it assumes a motive the text does not state directly). "Dream position" is an inference presented as fact. "Amazing" is evaluative. "Wins the race" oversimplifies the team dynamic. Corrected version: Anika is moved from the anchor position to second leg after a teammate is injured. She trains for the handoff and executes it cleanly at the regional meet. Her team finishes first, with Tessa crossing the finish line as anchor.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.6.1) First, when the coach tells Anika she will run second leg, "Anika said nothing," and the text reveals she "had spent months preparing for the moment of crossing the finish line." Her silence and the emphasis on months of preparation suggest deep disappointment — she is not just losing a position but losing the culmination of sustained effort. Second, the text says she understood that "no one cheered for the second runner," indicating she is aware that the position lacks visibility. Together, these reveal a character who values recognition but ultimately prioritizes team success over personal visibility.

6. (RL.6.6) The passage is told from Anika's perspective using third-person limited narration. This perspective shapes the reader's understanding by giving access to Anika's internal thoughts — her frustration about losing anchor, her awareness that second position is invisible, and her final satisfaction that "the race had been won in the exchange zone." Without this perspective, the reader would experience the race the way the crowd does — seeing only the finish line. Anika's point of view reveals what the crowd cannot see: the invisible contribution that made the visible success possible.
