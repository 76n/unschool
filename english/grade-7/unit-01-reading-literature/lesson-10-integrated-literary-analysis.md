# English Language Arts — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 10

## Topic
Integrated Literary Analysis

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply all four unit standards (RL.7.1, RL.7.2, RL.7.6, RL.7.9) to a single complex text
- Write a multi-paragraph analytical response that integrates evidence citation, thematic analysis, perspective analysis, and fiction-history comparison
- Demonstrate fluency in moving between analytical skills within a single response

## Prerequisites
- Ability to cite several pieces of textual evidence (Lessons 1-2, RL.7.1)
- Ability to determine and trace theme development and write objective summaries (Lessons 3-5, RL.7.2)
- Ability to analyze contrasting points of view and narrator perspective (Lessons 6-8, RL.7.6)
- Ability to compare fictional portrayals with historical accounts (Lesson 9, RL.7.9)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Highlighters in three colors
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)
- Venn diagram template (optional)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all four standards and prepare students to apply them simultaneously.

**Activity: Standards Toolkit Review**

Say: "Over the past nine lessons, you have built four powerful analytical tools. Today, you are going to use all of them at once — the way a skilled reader does naturally when they sit down with a complex text."

Write on the board:
- **RL.7.1 — Evidence:** Cite several pieces of textual evidence for explicit and inferential analysis.
- **RL.7.2 — Theme:** Determine theme and trace its development; write an objective summary.
- **RL.7.6 — Point of View:** Analyze how the author develops and contrasts characters' or narrators' perspectives.
- **RL.7.9 — Fiction vs. History:** Compare fictional portrayal with historical accounts.

Say: "Think of these as lenses on a camera. In the last nine lessons, you practiced each lens individually. Today, you are going to switch between them as the text demands. A strong reader does not say, 'Now I am doing evidence' and then 'Now I am doing theme.' They weave the skills together."

Say: "Here is the challenge: you will read a longer passage, and I will ask you questions that require you to use multiple standards at once. Ready?"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model integrated analysis using multiple standards on a single passage.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Integrated analysis:** An analytical approach that draws on multiple skills — evidence citation, thematic analysis, perspective analysis, and comparative analysis — within a single response.
- **Analytical fluency:** The ability to move between analytical skills smoothly and apply whichever skill the text demands at a given moment.
- **Synthesis:** Combining insights from different analytical approaches into a unified understanding of a text.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When you read a complex text, the standards do not take turns. They overlap. The same sentence might serve as evidence for a claim about theme AND reveal a character's point of view. Your job is to see those connections."

Write on the board — **The Integration Model:**
1. **Read the text** — Annotate for all four standards simultaneously.
2. **Identify the dominant question** — What does the text most need you to analyze?
3. **Layer your analysis** — Start with one standard, then show how the others connect.
4. **Synthesize** — Pull your observations together into a unified insight.

Say: "Let me model this briefly with a short example before we move to the main passage."

### Worked Examples

**Short Passage for Modeling:**

> During the 1920s, the Harlem neighborhood of New York City became the center of a cultural movement known as the Harlem Renaissance. African American writers, musicians, and artists created works that celebrated Black identity, challenged racial stereotypes, and explored the experience of being Black in America.

> *The following is a fictional scene set during this period:*

> Ruth stood in the doorway of the Crescent Club on Lenox Avenue and listened. The saxophone player was doing something she had never heard before — bending the notes sideways, pulling the melody apart and putting it back together in a shape that did not exist in any songbook. The crowd leaned forward in their chairs. A woman at the bar closed her eyes. A man in a gray hat tapped his fingers against his glass, keeping time with a rhythm that kept changing.
>
> Ruth's mother had told her that music was supposed to follow rules. But the music in this room was writing its own rules — and everyone in the room understood them, as if the rules had been there all along, buried under the ones they had been taught.

Say: "Watch how I integrate standards."

Write on the board:

**Evidence (RL.7.1):** The text says the musician was "bending the notes sideways, pulling the melody apart and putting it back together in a shape that did not exist in any songbook." This is the primary evidence for my analysis.

**Theme (RL.7.2):** The theme is introduced through the contrast between "music was supposed to follow rules" and "this room was writing its own rules." The theme is about creative self-determination — the right to define one's own expression rather than conforming to inherited standards.

**Point of View (RL.7.6):** Ruth's perspective is shaped by what her mother taught her (music follows rules), and the scene challenges that perspective. The crowd's reaction (leaning forward, closing eyes, tapping along) provides a collective point of view that validates the new expression.

**Fiction vs. History (RL.7.9):** The historical account describes the Harlem Renaissance in general terms: "challenged racial stereotypes" and "explored the experience of being Black in America." The fiction shows one specific moment where this happens — a saxophone player redefining the rules of music in real time, and an audience recognizing the new rules as legitimate. The fiction humanizes the historical movement by placing the reader inside the experience.

**Synthesis:** The passage is about how a marginalized community creates its own artistic language — one that the dominant culture did not authorize but that carries its own authority. The fiction makes this abstract historical concept into a lived moment.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice integrated analysis with facilitator support on a substantial passage.

Say: "Now we are going to work with a longer passage. As you read, annotate for all four standards. Use different colors or symbols for evidence, theme, and point of view."

**Historical Context (read aloud before the passage):**

> In the early 1900s, thousands of children in the United States worked in textile mills, coal mines, and factories. Many started as young as eight or nine years old. The work was dangerous — children lost fingers in machinery, developed lung diseases from dust and chemicals, and worked twelve to sixteen hours per day. Reform movements gradually led to child labor laws, but enforcement was slow and uneven.

**Passage: "Thread Count" (fictional portrayal, set in a textile mill, 1908)**

> The loom was twice as tall as Ada and three times as wide. It shook when it ran, a vibration that traveled through the wooden floor, up through Ada's shoes, and into her teeth. She had learned to ignore it the way she had learned to ignore the cotton dust that hung in the air like fog.
>
> Ada was ten. She worked the bobbin station — replacing empty bobbins with full ones when the thread ran out. The machine did not stop for bobbin changes. She had to reach between the moving parts, slide the empty bobbin off the spindle, and snap the new one into place before the next pass of the shuttle. The whole exchange took four seconds. She had gotten it down from seven in her first week.
>
> Her supervisor, Mr. Hamill, watched the floor from a raised platform at the back of the room. He carried a wooden pointer that he tapped against the railing when a worker moved too slowly. He had never hit anyone with it — at least, not when Ada was watching. But the tapping was enough.
>
> Ada's older brother, Eli, worked the carding machines on the other side of the mill. He was thirteen and had been working there for two years. He called the mill "the box" and counted the hours until closing the way a prisoner counts days. When Ada told him she did not mind the work, that she liked the rhythm of the bobbin exchange, Eli looked at her the way adults look at children who do not yet understand the cost of the thing they are describing.
>
> "You will mind it," he said. "Give it time."
>
> On the walk home, Ada's hands shook from the vibrations of the loom. She held them out in front of her and watched them tremble. The shaking usually stopped by dinnertime. Usually.

Say: "With a partner, identify one observation for each standard. You have three minutes."

Expected observations:
- **RL.7.1:** Multiple pieces of evidence about the danger: reaching between moving parts, four-second exchange, supervisor's pointer, hands shaking after work.
- **RL.7.2:** Theme about how exploitation can become normalized, especially for those too young to see it. Ada "liked the rhythm" but Eli knows the cost.
- **RL.7.6:** Contrasting perspectives — Ada does not mind the work; Eli calls the mill "the box" and counts hours like a prisoner. Their perspectives contrast based on experience: Ada has worked there briefly; Eli has worked there two years.
- **RL.7.9:** The historical account describes child labor in general terms (dangerous, long hours, lost fingers). The fiction shows a specific ten-year-old reaching between moving parts in four seconds, and an older brother who already understands the exploitation Ada does not yet recognize.

Guide a whole-class discussion connecting the observations.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently apply all four standards to the passage.

**Directions:** Using the passage "Thread Count" and the historical context provided above, answer the following questions.

1. **(RL.7.1)** Cite at least three pieces of evidence from the passage that reveal the physical dangers and demands of Ada's work. Explain how these pieces of evidence work together to build a picture of the conditions.

2. **(RL.7.2)** What is the theme of this passage? State it as a complete sentence and trace its development from Ada's initial attitude to Eli's warning to the final image of Ada's shaking hands.

3. **(RL.7.6)** How does the author develop and contrast Ada's and Eli's points of view? Identify at least one technique used for each character and explain what the contrast reveals.

4. **(RL.7.9)** Compare the fictional passage with the historical account. Identify two historical facts the fiction preserves and one element the fiction adds. Explain what the fictional addition reveals about the historical period that the factual account cannot.

5. **(Integrated)** Write a paragraph that uses at least three standards to analyze the final image: "The shaking usually stopped by dinnertime. Usually." Address what this detail reveals about the conditions (evidence), how it connects to the theme (theme development), and what it suggests about Ada's awareness compared to Eli's (point of view).

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-5 with cited evidence.
**Stretch:** In your response to question 5, connect the final image to the historical account's mention of long-term health effects on child workers. How does the fiction foreshadow what the history documents?
**Challenge:** Write a full integrated analysis paragraph (8-10 sentences) that addresses all four standards in a single unified response about the passage. Do not answer each standard separately — weave them together the way a skilled reader would.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.5) Analyze the structural choice of ending the passage with Ada's shaking hands. Why does the author choose this image as the final detail rather than ending with Eli's warning?
7. (Grade 6 review — RL.6.1) The passage says Ada had reduced her bobbin exchange time from seven seconds to four. Cite this evidence and explain what inference you can draw about how children adapted to dangerous labor conditions.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> **Historical fact:** During the California Gold Rush (1848-1855), thousands of people migrated to California hoping to find gold. Most did not find significant amounts. Many ended up working in service industries — laundries, general stores, and restaurants — rather than mining.
>
> **Fictional excerpt:** Liang wiped the counter of the general store and watched the miners come in, mud-caked and hollow-eyed. They bought flour, rope, and sometimes a letter's worth of postage to send home. They never looked at him. He was furniture. But Liang knew something the miners did not: in three months, he had saved more money selling flour than any of them had panned from the river.

1. Identify which standards you would use to analyze this paired text. For each standard you identify, write one analytical sentence.

**Expected Answers:**
1. **RL.7.1 (Evidence):** The detail that the miners "never looked at him" and that Liang was "furniture" to them provides evidence of the invisibility and marginalization Liang faces despite his economic success.
**RL.7.2 (Theme):** The theme of the passage is that true success can be invisible to those who define success narrowly — the miners chase gold and fail, while Liang quietly prospers through steady work.
**RL.7.6 (Point of View):** Liang's perspective contrasts with the miners' — he sees them as "hollow-eyed" and unsuccessful, while they do not see him at all, creating a power dynamic the miners are unaware of.
**RL.7.9 (Fiction vs. History):** The historical account states that most migrants ended up in service industries; the fiction adds the emotional and social dimension of that reality — Liang is economically successful but socially invisible.

**Mastery = Student identifies at least three standards and writes an analytical sentence for each that demonstrates understanding of both the standard and the text.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Have the student focus on two standards rather than all four and build from there.
- Provide a graphic organizer with four boxes (one per standard) and have the student place evidence in each box before writing.
- Review the integration model from Direct Instruction and have the student follow its steps.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is prepared for the Unit Assessment.
- Encourage the student to practice integrated analysis in their independent reading.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Full Integrated Essay:** The student writes a one-page essay analyzing "Thread Count" using all four standards. The essay should have an introduction, two to three body paragraphs, and a conclusion that synthesizes insights from all four analytical approaches.
2. **Standards Scavenger Hunt:** The student selects a passage from a book they are reading and identifies one example of each standard at work. They annotate the passage with labels and brief explanations.
3. **Peer Review:** The student exchanges their integrated paragraph (question 5) with a partner. Each student identifies which standards are present in their partner's response and provides feedback on whether any standard is underrepresented.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This is the capstone lesson:** Lesson 10 is designed to bring together everything students have learned. It is intentionally more demanding than previous lessons. Allow extra time (55-60 minutes) and be prepared to provide more scaffolding than usual.
- **Historical sensitivity:** The child labor passage may be emotionally challenging. Provide historical context before reading and remind students that the conditions described were real and affected millions of children. This is an opportunity for both literary analysis and historical awareness.
- **Integration, not compartmentalization:** The most common error in this lesson is students treating each standard as a separate task. Push them toward integration — how do evidence, theme, perspective, and fiction/history comparison work together to create a complete understanding of the text?
- **Flexible assessment:** If time runs short, question 5 is the most important assessment of integration. It can serve as a formative check on its own.
- **Celebrating growth:** This is a good moment to acknowledge how far students have come from Lesson 1. At the start of the unit, they were learning to cite several pieces of evidence. Now they are weaving four complex skills together. Name that growth explicitly.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **(RL.7.1)** Three pieces of evidence about dangers and demands: (a) "She had to reach between the moving parts, slide the empty bobbin off the spindle, and snap the new one into place before the next pass of the shuttle" — this shows the physical risk of working alongside moving machinery without stopping it. (b) "The whole exchange took four seconds. She had gotten it down from seven in her first week" — this shows the pressure to work faster, with speed measured in seconds rather than minutes. (c) "On the walk home, Ada's hands shook from the vibrations of the loom... The shaking usually stopped by dinnertime. Usually" — this shows the physical toll on Ada's body, and the word "usually" implies the damage may be accumulating. Together, these details build from immediate danger (reaching into machinery) through performance pressure (speed expectations) to lasting physical impact (shaking hands), showing that the work harms children at every level.

2. **(RL.7.2)** Theme: When exploitation is experienced as normal, especially by those too young to understand its cost, the damage is not just physical but psychological — because the person being harmed does not yet recognize the harm. The theme is introduced through Ada's attitude: she does not mind the work and "liked the rhythm of the bobbin exchange," showing she has normalized dangerous labor. The theme is complicated by Eli's perspective: he calls the mill "the box" and counts hours "the way a prisoner counts days," revealing that experience transforms the work from tolerable to unbearable. His warning — "You will mind it. Give it time" — deepens the theme by suggesting that Ada's acceptance is temporary and based on ignorance, not resilience. The theme crystallizes in the final image: "The shaking usually stopped by dinnertime. Usually." The repetition of "usually" introduces doubt into Ada's own account — her body is registering the cost even if her mind has not yet caught up. The theme develops from acceptance through warning to the first physical sign that the warning is correct.

3. **(RL.7.6)** Ada's perspective is developed through selective detail and tone: she notices the rhythm of the bobbin exchange, reduces her time from seven to four seconds, and reports that she "did not mind the work." Her tone is matter-of-fact, suggesting she sees the mill as her environment rather than her prison. Eli's perspective is developed through metaphor and dialogue: he calls the mill "the box" (a metaphor for confinement) and "counted the hours until closing the way a prisoner counts days." His dialogue — "You will mind it" — is a prediction, not a complaint, showing he has passed through the stage Ada is in and arrived at understanding. The contrast reveals that perspective on exploitation depends on duration of exposure: Ada's freshness makes the work tolerable, while Eli's two years of experience have made the truth visible. The author develops both perspectives to show that exploitation does not announce itself — it normalizes first and is recognized only later.

4. **(RL.7.9)** The fiction preserves two historical facts: (a) Children worked in textile mills — Ada is ten and works the bobbin station, consistent with the historical description of children starting as young as eight or nine. (b) The work was dangerous — Ada reaches between moving parts, consistent with the historical mention of children losing fingers in machinery. The fiction adds the contrasting sibling perspectives — Ada's acceptance and Eli's understanding. This element reveals something the historical account cannot: the psychological process by which child workers initially normalized their exploitation. The historical account tells us the work was dangerous, but it cannot show us how a ten-year-old experienced that danger as routine. Josephine's — Ada's matter-of-fact tone and Eli's bitter awareness add a human dimension that statistics cannot convey.

5. **(Integrated)** The final image — "The shaking usually stopped by dinnertime. Usually" — functions on multiple analytical levels simultaneously. As evidence (RL.7.1), it provides a concrete physical detail: Ada's hands tremble from the loom's vibrations, showing the bodily impact of factory work on a ten-year-old. As theme development (RL.7.2), the repetition of "usually" introduces the first crack in Ada's acceptance — her body is showing signs that the damage is not always temporary, which foreshadows Eli's warning that "you will mind it." The second "usually" is the moment the theme shifts from normalization to recognition: Ada's narrative control slips, and the reader sees what Ada is only beginning to sense. As a point of view detail (RL.7.6), the final line reveals that Ada's perspective is becoming less reliable — she has been telling us the work is fine, but her own body contradicts her, placing her closer to Eli's understanding than she realizes. The line is also the moment where the reader's perspective diverges from Ada's: we understand the significance of the shaking before she does, creating a narrative gap that deepens the passage's emotional impact.

### Interleaved Review
6. (RL.6.5) The author ends with Ada's shaking hands rather than Eli's warning because the physical image is more powerful and more ambiguous than the verbal one. Eli's warning is explicit — "You will mind it" — and leaves nothing for the reader to interpret. The shaking hands are implicit: they show the cost without naming it, and the word "usually" introduces uncertainty without resolving it. Ending on this image forces the reader to sit with the unresolved question — how long before the shaking does not stop? — rather than the answered one. Structurally, the ending also shifts from dialogue back to the body, reminding the reader that the harm is physical, not just emotional.

7. (RL.6.1) The passage states that Ada "had gotten it down from seven [seconds] in her first week." This detail supports the inference that children adapted to dangerous labor conditions by developing speed and efficiency under pressure — skills that were essential for survival in the mill. The reduction from seven to four seconds suggests that Ada's body and reflexes learned to work within the machine's rhythm, which is both a testament to human adaptability and a troubling sign that children were being trained to accept risk as routine. The fact that she achieved this improvement in her "first week" suggests the adaptation was driven by necessity rather than choice.
