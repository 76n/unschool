# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 4, Lesson 4

## Topic
Possessive Nouns: Showing Ownership with Apostrophe-S

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain that a possessive noun shows that someone or something owns or has something
- Form a possessive noun by adding apostrophe-s ('s) to a singular noun
- Distinguish between a plural noun (ending in -s) and a possessive noun (ending in 's)

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of what a noun is: person, place, thing, animal (Lesson 1)
- Familiarity with common and proper nouns (Lessons 1 and 2)
- Ability to form plural nouns with -s and -es (Lesson 3)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or large whiteboard
- Markers in two colors (one for the noun, one for the apostrophe-s)
- 10 index cards with nouns and 10 index cards with objects (see Direct Instruction)

- Optional: small toys or stuffed animals and objects to pair with them (e.g., a toy dog and a small ball — "the dog's ball")

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the idea of ownership through the student's personal experience.

**Activity: Whose Is It?**
Gather 4-5 items from around the room that clearly belong to someone. For example: the student's pencil, a parent's phone, a sibling's toy, the student's shoe.

Hold up each item and ask: "Whose is this?"

The student will answer things like: "That is my pencil" or "That is Mom's phone."

Write their answers on the board, emphasizing the 's:
- "the student's pencil"
- "Mom's phone"
- "Jake's toy"

Point to the 's on each one. "Look at this little mark right here. See that tiny floating comma followed by an 's'? That is called an apostrophe-s. It is a special signal that means 'this thing belongs to that person.' Today we are going to learn all about this little mark and how to use it."

*Facilitator note: Starting with real objects makes ownership tangible and concrete. The student can physically hold the item and understand "this belongs to someone" before seeing the grammar rule on paper.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to form possessive nouns and how to tell them apart from plural nouns.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Possessive noun:** A noun that shows ownership — it tells us that something belongs to someone or something. Formed by adding 's to the noun.
- **Apostrophe:** A small mark that looks like a floating comma ( ' ). It goes before the 's' in a possessive noun.
- **Ownership:** When something belongs to someone. "The dog's bone" means the bone belongs to the dog.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Teach the rule for forming possessive nouns.**
Write on the chart paper:

**To show that something belongs to someone, add 's (apostrophe-s) to the noun.**

"When we want to show that something belongs to someone, we add a tiny mark called an apostrophe and the letter 's' to the owner's name."

Show examples, writing the apostrophe-s in a different color:
- the dog → the dog**'s** bone (The bone belongs to the dog.)
- Mom → Mom**'s** hat (The hat belongs to Mom.)
- Sam → Sam**'s** bike (The bike belongs to Sam.)

"See that little mark before the 's'? That is the apostrophe. It is like a tiny flag that says: 'The next thing belongs to me!'"

**Step 2: The big difference — plural vs. possessive.**
"Now here is something very important. You just learned in Lesson 3 that we add -s to make nouns plural — more than one. And now I am telling you to add 's to show ownership. How do we tell the difference?"

Write two sentences on the board:
- "The dogs ran fast." (dogs = plural — more than one dog)
- "The dog's bone was big." (dog's = possessive — the bone belongs to the dog)

"Look carefully. In the first sentence, 'dogs' has just an 's' at the end. That means there are many dogs. In the second sentence, 'dog's' has an apostrophe before the 's.' That tiny apostrophe changes everything! Now it means the bone belongs to the dog."

Draw a simple visual:
- dogs = more than one dog (draw two dogs)
- dog's = belonging to the dog (draw one dog with a bone, arrow pointing from bone to dog)

**Step 3: Practice reading the apostrophe.**
"Whenever you see an apostrophe-s, read it as 'belonging to.' The dog's bone = the bone belonging to the dog. The girl's hat = the hat belonging to the girl."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "the cat's tail"
"What is the noun? Cat. What does the cat own? Its tail. So we write: the cat (apostrophe-s) tail. The cat**'s** tail. This means the tail belongs to the cat."

**Example 2:** "the teacher's book"
"Who is the owner? The teacher. What does the teacher have? A book. So we write: the teacher**'s** book. The book belongs to the teacher."

**Example 3 — Tricky comparison:** Read these two sentences:
- "I see the birds." → "How many birds? More than one. 'Birds' is a plural noun."
- "I see the bird's nest." → "How many birds? Just one. But the bird has something — a nest. 'Bird's' is a possessive noun. The apostrophe tells us the nest belongs to the bird."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice forming possessive nouns and distinguishing them from plurals with support.

**Activity 1: Mix and Match Possessives (5 minutes)**
Spread out the 10 noun cards (owners) and 10 object cards (things owned) on the table.

Say: "We are going to play a matching game. Pick an owner card and an object card. Then tell me the possessive phrase."

Model the first one: Pick up "dog" and "bone." Say: "The dog's bone. The bone belongs to the dog."

Have the student pick pairs and form the possessive phrase. They should say the phrase aloud and then write it on paper.

*Facilitator prompt: For each pair, ask: "Who is the owner? What do they own? Now add apostrophe-s to the owner's name."*

**Possible pairings (any combination works):**
- the dog's bone
- the cat's tail
- Mom's hat
- Sam's bike
- the bird's nest
- the teacher's book
- the baby's bottle
- Emma's dress
- the frog's lily pad
- Dad's car

**Activity 2: Plural or Possessive? (5 minutes)**
Say: "Now I am going to show you some sentences. You tell me — is the underlined word plural (more than one) or possessive (belonging to someone)?"

Write these sentences on the board and underline the noun in question:

1. "The cats play outside." — Plural or possessive? (Plural — more than one cat)
2. "The cat's toy is under the couch." — Plural or possessive? (Possessive — the toy belongs to the cat)
3. "I ate two peaches." — Plural or possessive? (Plural — more than one peach)
4. "My sister's room is messy." — Plural or possessive? (Possessive — the room belongs to my sister)
5. "The dogs run in the park." — Plural or possessive? (Plural — more than one dog)
6. "The dog's water bowl is empty." — Plural or possessive? (Possessive — the water bowl belongs to the dog)

*Facilitator prompt: "Look for the apostrophe. If you see that little floating comma before the 's,' it is possessive. If there is no apostrophe, it is just plural."*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices forming and using possessive nouns independently.

**Activity: The Ownership Page**
The student completes two parts:

**Part 1: Form the Possessive**
Write the possessive form of each noun, then write what the person or animal owns.

1. the girl + hat → ___
2. the boy + lunch → ___
3. the rabbit + carrot → ___
4. Dad + keys → ___
5. the fish + tank → ___
6. Grandma + cookies → ___

**Part 2: Write Possessive Sentences**
Write 3 sentences using possessive nouns. Each sentence should show something belonging to someone.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Complete Part 1 (6 possessive phrases). Then write 2 sentences using possessive nouns. Use the phrases from Part 1 as a starting point if needed. (Example: "The girl's hat is red.")

**Stretch:**
Complete Part 1 (6 possessive phrases). Then write 3 sentences using possessive nouns. At least 1 sentence should use a proper noun as the owner (e.g., "Emma's dog is fluffy."). Do not reuse the phrases from Part 1.

**Challenge:**
Complete Part 1 (6 possessive phrases). Then write a 4-sentence mini-story that uses at least 3 possessive nouns. Underline each possessive noun. Also include at least 1 plural noun in your story and circle it.

### Interleaved Review
- "Read your sentences aloud. Can you find the nouns in each sentence?" (Noun identification from Lesson 1)
- "Look at the names in your sentences. Did you capitalize the proper nouns?" (Proper noun capitalization from Lesson 2)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can form possessive nouns and distinguish them from plurals.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What does a possessive noun show?" (Answer: It shows that something belongs to someone or something — ownership.)
2. "How do you make the word 'cat' possessive?" (Answer: Add apostrophe-s → cat's)
3. "Look at these two words: 'dogs' and 'dog's.' What is the difference?" (Answer: "Dogs" means more than one dog — it is plural. "Dog's" means something belongs to the dog — it is possessive.)
4. "Write this phrase: 'the bird's wing.'" (Answer: Student writes "the bird's wing" with the apostrophe in the correct place.)

**Mastery = Student explains what a possessive noun is AND correctly forms at least 2 of 2 possessives AND distinguishes plural from possessive in at least 1 example.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the physical objects from the warm-up. Hold up an item and say: "This is ___'s ___." Have the student repeat and write it.
- Draw the visual again: plural = many (draw multiple items), possessive = belonging to (draw an arrow from the object to the owner).
- Practice 5 more oral examples: say a noun and an object, have the student form the possessive phrase.
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 5.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 5 (Pronouns). Keep the possessive noun chart posted alongside the plural rules chart from Lesson 3.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who grasp possessive nouns quickly:**
- Play "Whose Room?": Imagine different characters (a princess, a pirate, a dinosaur). What would be in their room? Write 3 possessive phrases for each character. (Example: "the pirate's treasure," "the pirate's map," "the pirate's ship.")
- Draw a family portrait. Label each person's belongings using possessive nouns (e.g., "Dad's glasses," "Mom's necklace," "my sister's bow").
- Bonus brain teaser: "What if two dogs own one bone together? How would you write that?" (Answer: "the dogs' bone" — the apostrophe goes after the 's' for plural possessives. This is an advanced concept — just introduce it as a fun puzzle, not a requirement.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**The apostrophe is a brand-new punctuation mark for most first graders.** They have learned periods, question marks, and exclamation points, but the apostrophe is different — it lives inside a word rather than at the end of a sentence. Spend a moment just talking about what an apostrophe looks like and where it goes. Some students will put it after the 's' instead of before it, or forget it entirely. Patient, repeated modeling is the best remedy.

**The plural vs. possessive distinction is the hardest part of this lesson.** Both end in an "s" sound, which is confusing. The visual approach — drawing two dogs for "dogs" and one dog with an arrow for "dog's" — is very effective. Return to this visual whenever the student is unsure. Over time, the apostrophe becomes a visual cue they can spot automatically.

**Do not teach plural possessives in this lesson.** The rule for plural possessives (dogs' bone) adds a layer of complexity that is beyond Grade 1 standards. If a student asks, acknowledge it briefly — "Great question! When more than one person owns something, the apostrophe moves. We will learn that rule when you are older." — and move on.

**Physical objects make this lesson come alive.** If you have a toy dog and a small ball, you can literally place the ball next to the dog and say: "This is the dog's ball." The student can move objects between owners: "Now it is the cat's ball!" This kinesthetic approach is especially powerful for students who struggle with abstract punctuation rules.

**Watch for this common error in future writing:** Students often drop the apostrophe when they write possessives, producing "the dogs bone" instead of "the dog's bone." When you see this in their writing, gently point to the noun and ask: "Does the bone belong to the dog? Then we need our special mark — the apostrophe — to show that." Consistent correction during authentic writing is more effective than isolated drills.
