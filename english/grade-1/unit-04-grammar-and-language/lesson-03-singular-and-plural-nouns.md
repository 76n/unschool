# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 4, Lesson 3

## Topic
Singular and Plural Nouns: Adding -s and -es

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain that singular means "one" and plural means "more than one"
- Apply the rule of adding -s to make most nouns plural
- Apply the rule of adding -es to nouns ending in s, x, z, ch, or sh

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of what a noun is: person, place, thing, animal (Lesson 1)
- Ability to identify common and proper nouns (Lessons 1 and 2)
- Ability to read and write simple words with common spelling patterns (Unit 1 phonics)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or large whiteboard
- Markers in two colors (one for the base word, one for the -s or -es ending)
- 20 index cards or slips of paper with singular nouns (see Direct Instruction)
- Two sorting bins or paper plates labeled: "Add -s" and "Add -es"
- Pencil and paper for the student
- Optional: small objects for counting (blocks, coins, crayons) to make "one vs. many" concrete

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build the concept of "one vs. more than one" using concrete objects.

**Activity: One or Many?**
Place a single crayon on the table. Say: "How many crayons do I have?" (One.)

Now place a handful of crayons on the table. Say: "How many now?" (Many! or a specific number.)

"When we have one of something, we say 'one crayon.' When we have more than one, we say 'crayons.' Listen carefully — crayon... crayons. What tiny sound did I add at the end?" (The sound /z/ — written as the letter 's.')

Repeat with another object — one book, then several books. "Book... books. I added an 's' sound at the end!"

"Today we are going to learn the rules for making nouns mean 'more than one.' There is a fancy word for 'more than one' — it is called PLURAL. Let's say that together: plural. And the word for 'just one' is SINGULAR. Singular means one, plural means more than one."

Practice: hold up one finger — "singular!" Hold up many fingers — "plural!"

*Facilitator note: Using real objects grounds the abstract grammar concept in something tangible. First graders learn best when they can see and touch the difference between one and many before they encounter it in written form.*

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the two rules for making nouns plural: add -s (for most nouns) and add -es (for nouns ending in s, x, z, ch, or sh).

### Key Vocabulary
- **Singular:** One of something. Example: one cat.
- **Plural:** More than one of something. Example: two cats.
- **Suffix:** A letter or letters added to the end of a word to change its meaning. The -s and -es are suffixes that make nouns plural.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Teach Rule 1 — Add -s.**
Write on the chart paper:

**Rule 1: To make most nouns plural, add -s.**

"For most nouns, making them plural is easy. We just add the letter 's' to the end."

Show examples, writing the base word in one color and the -s in another:
- cat → cat**s**
- dog → dog**s**
- book → book**s**
- tree → tree**s**

"See? One cat, two cats. One dog, three dogs. We just stick an 's' on the end. Easy!"

**Step 2: Teach Rule 2 — Add -es.**
"But some words are a little trickier. Listen to this: 'bus.' If I just add an 's,' I would get 'buss.' Say that — 'buss.' That sounds funny and is hard to say! So instead, we add 'e-s' — 'buses.' Much better!"

Write on the chart paper:

**Rule 2: If a noun ends in s, x, z, ch, or sh, add -es.**

"Here is a trick to remember. Say the word out loud. If it already ends with a hissy or buzzy sound — like sss, ks, zz, ch, or shh — you need to add -es because adding just -s would be too hard to say."

Show examples:
- bus → bus**es**
- box → box**es**
- wish → wish**es**
- lunch → lunch**es**

"Let me teach you a little rhyme to remember when to use -es:"

*Chant:*
"S, X, Z, CH, SH — add E-S, you're the best!"

Practice the chant 2-3 times.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "Let's make 'ball' plural. Does 'ball' end in s, x, z, ch, or sh? No — it ends in 'l.' So we use Rule 1 and just add -s. Ball → balls. One ball, five balls!"

**Example 2:** "Let's make 'fox' plural. Does 'fox' end in s, x, z, ch, or sh? Yes — it ends in 'x'! So we use Rule 2 and add -es. Fox → foxes. One fox, two foxes!"

**Example 3:** "Let's make 'dish' plural. Does 'dish' end in s, x, z, ch, or sh? Yes — it ends in 'sh'! Rule 2: add -es. Dish → dishes."

**Example 4:** "Let's make 'star' plural. Ends in 'r' — not on our list. Rule 1: just add -s. Star → stars."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice applying both plural rules with support.

**Activity 1: Sort and Spell (6 minutes)**
Set out the two sorting bins labeled "Add -s" and "Add -es." Give the student the 20 noun cards.

Say: "Read each word. Decide: do we add -s or -es to make it plural? Put the card in the right bin. Then write the plural form on the back of the card."

Go through the first 2-3 cards together, then let the student work with guidance.

*Facilitator prompt: For each card, ask: "What letter or letters does this word end with? Is it one of our special endings — s, x, z, ch, or sh? If yes, add -es. If no, just add -s."*

**Sorting answers:**
- cat → cats (add -s)
- dog → dogs (add -s)
- book → books (add -s)
- tree → trees (add -s)
- ball → balls (add -s)
- cup → cups (add -s)
- bird → birds (add -s)
- star → stars (add -s)
- desk → desks (add -s)
- frog → frogs (add -s)
- bus → buses (add -es)
- box → boxes (add -es)
- fox → foxes (add -es)
- wish → wishes (add -es)
- dish → dishes (add -es)
- lunch → lunches (add -es)
- beach → beaches (add -es)
- dress → dresses (add -es)
- buzz → buzzes (add -es)
- peach → peaches (add -es)

**Activity 2: Plural Sentence Builder (4 minutes)**
Say: "Now let's use some of our plural nouns in sentences. I will start a sentence, and you finish it using the plural form of the noun I give you."

1. "I saw three ___." (Give the word: frog) → "I saw three frogs."
2. "She packed two ___." (Give the word: lunch) → "She packed two lunches."
3. "There are many ___ in the sky." (Give the word: star) → "There are many stars in the sky."
4. "We found four ___ on the beach." (Give the word: shell) → "We found four shells on the beach."
5. "The ___ were swimming in the pond." (Give the word: fish — this is a bonus trick! "Fish" is the same singular and plural.) → "The fish were swimming in the pond."

For the bonus word "fish," say: "That is a tricky one! Some nouns stay the same whether there is one or more than one. 'Fish' is one of those special words. One fish, two fish! You do not need to worry about those yet — just know they exist."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student forgets whether to add -s or -es, point them back to the chart and the chant.*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices forming plurals independently.

**Activity: Singular to Plural Transformer**
The student receives a list of 12 singular nouns and writes the plural form next to each one. Then they choose 4 of the plural nouns and write a sentence using each one.

**Word list:**
1. hat → ___
2. wish → ___
3. pen → ___
4. beach → ___
5. bed → ___
6. glass → ___
7. truck → ___
8. church → ___
9. kite → ___
10. fox → ___
11. lamp → ___
12. brush → ___

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Write the plural form for all 12 words. Then choose 3 plural nouns and use each one in a sentence. Sentences may be simple (e.g., "I have two hats.").

**Stretch:**
Write the plural form for all 12 words. Then choose 4 plural nouns and write a sentence for each. At least 2 sentences should have more than 5 words.

**Challenge:**
Write the plural form for all 12 words. Then write a short 4-sentence paragraph (a mini story) that uses at least 5 different plural nouns from the list. Underline each plural noun.

### Interleaved Review
- "Read your sentences back to me. Sound out any words you are not sure about." (Phonics review from Unit 1)
- "Look at sentence 1. Can you find the noun? Is it a common noun or a proper noun?" (Noun identification from Lessons 1-2)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can form plurals using both rules.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What does 'plural' mean?" (Answer: More than one)
2. "How do you make the word 'cup' plural?" (Answer: Add -s → cups)
3. "How do you make the word 'box' plural?" (Answer: Add -es → boxes)
4. "Which rule do you use for a word that ends in 'ch'?" (Answer: Add -es)

**Mastery = Student correctly defines "plural" AND forms at least 3 of 3 plurals correctly (choosing -s or -es appropriately).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Go back to the two-rule chart. Practice the chant: "S, X, Z, CH, SH — add E-S, you're the best!"
- Give 5 more oral examples. Say a singular noun, have the student say the plural, then check together.
- Use the "try it aloud" strategy: "Say the word with just -s. Does it sound right? If it sounds funny, try -es."
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 4.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 4 (Possessive Nouns). Keep the plural rules chart posted — it will be referenced in Lesson 4 when students learn to distinguish plural nouns from possessive nouns.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who grasp singular and plural nouns quickly:**
- Play "Plural Race": Set a timer for 2 minutes. The student writes as many plural nouns as they can. Count them up! Try to beat the score next time.
- Go on a "Plural Hunt" in a book: open any book and find 5 plural nouns. Write down the singular and plural forms.
- Bonus challenge: Can the student think of any nouns that do NOT follow the -s or -es rule? (Examples: mouse/mice, child/children, man/men, tooth/teeth, goose/geese.) These are called irregular plurals. Just introduce the idea for fun — they will learn these in later grades.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This lesson covers one of the most practical grammar skills in first grade.** Students will use plurals every day in their speaking and writing, so the rules taught here have immediate real-world application. The key is helping students internalize when to use -s versus -es, which is best done through lots of repetition and the auditory "does it sound right?" test.

**The -es rule is the part most students struggle with.** They may try to write "boxs" or "wishs" because adding -s is the more familiar pattern. When this happens, have them say the word aloud: "Say 'boxs.' Does that sound right? Now say 'boxes.' Which one sounds like the word you actually say?" The ear is a powerful tool at this age. Most first graders already say "boxes" and "wishes" correctly in speech — the lesson is about connecting their spoken knowledge to written spelling.

**Do not introduce irregular plurals in this lesson.** Words like "mice," "children," "teeth," and "feet" follow no pattern and will only confuse students who are just learning the regular rules. If a student happens to ask about an irregular plural (which curious students sometimes do), simply say: "Great question! That word is a special case — it does not follow the rule. We will learn about those special words another time." The extension activity introduces the concept lightly for advanced students only.

**Connect to reading and writing.** After this lesson, when reading together, occasionally pause at a plural noun and ask: "Is that word singular or plural? How do you know? Did they add -s or -es?" During writing time, if the student needs to write a plural, prompt them: "Check the ending — do you add -s or -es?" These micro-moments of practice are where the skill truly solidifies.

**The chant is a lifesaver for students who need extra support.** "S, X, Z, CH, SH — add E-S, you're the best!" is quick, catchy, and gives the student a concrete tool to pull out whenever they are unsure. Practice it at the beginning of the next few lessons as a warm-up until it is automatic.
