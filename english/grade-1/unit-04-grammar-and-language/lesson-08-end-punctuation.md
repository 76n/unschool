# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 4, Lesson 8

## Topic
End Punctuation — Periods, Question Marks, and Exclamation Points

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify and name the three types of end punctuation marks (period, question mark, exclamation point)
- Choose the correct end mark based on the purpose of a sentence (telling, asking, strong feeling)
- Read sentences aloud with appropriate expression based on the end punctuation

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of complete sentences with a subject and verb (Unit 3, Lesson 2)
- Familiarity with the three end marks introduced briefly in Unit 3, Lesson 2 (this lesson goes deeper)
- Ability to identify nouns, verbs, and adjectives in sentences (Lessons 1-7)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or whiteboard and marker
- Pencil and lined paper
- Three large signs or cards labeled: **. (Period)**, **? (Question Mark)**, **! (Exclamation Point)** — place these in three corners of the room or on three chairs
- 12 sentence strips (sentences written on strips of paper without end punctuation — see Direct Instruction for sentences)
- Crayons or colored pencils (three colors: one for each punctuation mark)

**Prepare before the lesson:**
Write each sentence below on a separate strip of paper WITHOUT the end punctuation mark. Number them 1-12 on the back for easy reference.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate awareness of how voice changes based on sentence type.

**Activity: Listen to My Voice**
Read three sentences aloud using dramatically different expression. Do NOT show the written sentences yet.

Say: "I am going to read three sentences. Listen carefully to HOW my voice sounds."

1. Read in a calm, flat voice: "The cat is on the mat." (Telling voice — steady, drops slightly at the end.)
2. Read with a rising voice: "Is the cat on the mat?" (Asking voice — pitch goes UP at the end.)
3. Read with excitement and energy: "The cat is on the mat!" (Excited voice — louder, more emphasis.)

Ask: "Did those all sound the same?" (No!)
"What was different?" (Let the student describe what they heard.)

Say: "My voice changed because each sentence has a different JOB. One was TELLING something. One was ASKING a question. And one was showing a STRONG FEELING — like surprise or excitement. Today we are going to learn about the little marks at the end of sentences that tell the reader HOW to read them. These marks are called END PUNCTUATION."

*Facilitator note: Starting with the auditory experience is essential. Students need to HEAR the difference before they learn the written symbol. This warm-up builds the bridge between spoken and written language.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the three end marks, their names, their purposes, and how they affect reading expression.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Period (.):** The end mark for a TELLING sentence. It means "I am sharing information calmly." Your voice stays steady and drops a little at the end.
- **Question mark (?):** The end mark for an ASKING sentence. It means "I want to know something." Your voice goes UP at the end.
- **Exclamation point (!):** The end mark for a sentence that shows STRONG FEELING — excitement, surprise, fear, or happiness. Your voice gets LOUDER and more energetic.
- **End punctuation:** The mark at the very end of a sentence that tells the reader how to read it.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce each end mark with its purpose.**
Draw each punctuation mark LARGE on chart paper:

**The Period (.)**
Draw a large dot.
Say: "This is a period. It looks like a little dot. A period goes at the end of a TELLING sentence — a sentence that shares information."
Write: "The dog is brown."
Read it in a calm, steady voice. "See? I am just telling you something. My voice is calm. Period."

**The Question Mark (?)**
Draw a large question mark.
Say: "This is a question mark. It has a curve and a dot underneath. A question mark goes at the end of an ASKING sentence — a sentence that wants to know something."
Write: "Is the dog brown?"
Read it with a rising voice. "Hear how my voice goes UP at the end? That is the question mark voice."

**The Exclamation Point (!)**
Draw a large exclamation point.
Say: "This is an exclamation point. It is a straight line with a dot underneath — like it is standing up tall and shouting! An exclamation point goes at the end of a sentence that shows STRONG FEELING."
Write: "The dog is so cute!"
Read it with energy and enthusiasm. "I am excited! My voice is LOUDER and full of feeling. That is the exclamation point voice."

**Step 2: Create an anchor chart.**

| End Mark | Name | When to Use It | Voice |
|---|---|---|---|
| . | Period | Telling something | Calm, steady, drops at the end |
| ? | Question mark | Asking something | Goes UP at the end |
| ! | Exclamation point | Strong feeling (excited, surprised, scared, happy) | LOUD and full of energy |

**Step 3: Practice reading with expression.**
Write these three sentences on chart paper:

1. "I like pizza." — Read together in a calm, telling voice.
2. "Do you like pizza?" — Read together with a rising, asking voice.
3. "I LOVE pizza!" — Read together with an excited, strong voice.

Say: "The words are almost the same, but the end mark changes HOW we read them. The end mark is like a tiny instruction for your voice."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "The sun is shining."
- "Is this sentence telling something, asking something, or showing a strong feeling?"
- "It is TELLING us about the sun. So it gets a... period!"
- Read it aloud in a telling voice: "The sun is shining."

**Example 2:** "Where is my backpack?"
- "Is this sentence telling, asking, or showing strong feeling?"
- "It is ASKING — someone wants to know where their backpack is. So it gets a... question mark!"
- Read it aloud with a rising voice: "Where is my backpack?"
- "Clue: sentences that start with who, what, where, when, why, or how are usually questions."

**Example 3:** "Watch out for the bee!"
- "Is this sentence telling, asking, or showing strong feeling?"
- "It is showing a STRONG FEELING — the person is scared or alarmed! So it gets an... exclamation point!"
- Read it aloud with an urgent, loud voice: "Watch out for the bee!"

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice with support.

**Activity 1: Punctuation Corners (5 minutes)**
Place the three large signs (Period, Question Mark, Exclamation Point) in three spots around the room (or on three chairs).

Read each sentence aloud with appropriate expression. The student listens and then MOVES to the correct corner to show which end mark the sentence needs.

Sentences to read:
1. "I have a new puppy." (Period — telling)
2. "What is your name?" (Question mark — asking)
3. "That was amazing!" (Exclamation point — strong feeling)
4. "The sky is blue." (Period — telling)
5. "Can I have a cookie?" (Question mark — asking)
6. "LOOK at that rainbow!" (Exclamation point — strong feeling)
7. "She is six years old." (Period — telling)
8. "Do you want to play?" (Question mark — asking)

After each one, confirm the answer: "Yes! That sentence was [telling/asking/showing strong feeling], so it needs a [period/question mark/exclamation point]."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student is unsure, exaggerate your voice when reading. Make the question voice go way up. Make the exclamation voice very loud. The student's ears will guide them to the right answer.*

**Activity 2: Add the End Mark (5 minutes)**
Give the student the sentence strips (without end marks). Read each sentence together and the student writes the correct end mark at the end.

Sentence strips:
1. "My cat likes to sleep" → .
2. "Where are my shoes" → ?
3. "I won the game" → !
4. "The book is on the table" → .
5. "How old are you" → ?
6. "That spider is huge" → !

After each one, the student reads the sentence aloud using the correct voice for the end mark they chose.

*Facilitator prompt: For sentence 3, the student might choose a period (telling) or an exclamation point (excitement). Both could work! If the student chooses a period, say: "That works if you are calmly sharing the news. But if you just won and you are SUPER excited, what mark would you use?" This shows that the writer gets to CHOOSE based on how they feel.*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Add the correct end mark to each sentence:
   - "I like to read books" (.)
   - "What time is it" (?)
   - "That was the best day ever" (!)
   - "The flower is red" (.)
   - "Can you help me" (?)
   - "We won the game" (! or .)
2. Write 3 sentences of your own — one with a period, one with a question mark, and one with an exclamation point. Read each one aloud using the right voice.

**Stretch:**
Complete the On-level tasks, PLUS:
3. Rewrite each sentence with a DIFFERENT end mark and explain how the meaning or feeling changes:
   - "The dog is outside." → "The dog is outside!" (Now I am excited or surprised.)
   - "You like ice cream." → "You like ice cream?" (Now I am asking.)
4. Write a 3-sentence conversation between two people. Use at least one of each end mark:
   - Example: "Do you want to play? Yes! I love playing outside."

**Challenge:**
Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, PLUS:
5. Write a short story (4-5 sentences) that uses ALL THREE types of end marks. Underline each end mark in a different color (e.g., periods in blue, question marks in green, exclamation points in red).
   Example: "One day, I found a box. What was inside? I opened it slowly. It was a puppy! I was so happy."
6. Read your story aloud to someone, using the correct voice for each sentence. Can they hear the difference?

### Interleaved Review
- "Look at sentence 1: 'I like to read books.' Circle the VERB." (Lesson 6 review — answer: like or read)
- "Look at sentence 4: 'The flower is red.' What is the ADJECTIVE?" (Lesson 7 review — answer: red)
- "Look at sentence 4: 'The flower is red.' What is the NOUN?" (Lessons 1-3 review — answer: flower)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What are the three end marks called?" (Answer: Period, question mark, exclamation point.)
2. "When do you use a period?" (Answer: At the end of a telling sentence.)
3. "When do you use a question mark?" (Answer: At the end of an asking sentence / a question.)
4. "Add the correct end mark: 'Wow, that cake is delicious___'" (Answer: ! — exclamation point, because it shows strong feeling.)
5. "Read this sentence with the right voice: 'Where did the cat go?'" (Answer: Student reads with a rising, questioning voice.)

**Mastery = Student correctly answers at least 4 of 5 questions, including choosing the correct end mark and reading with appropriate expression.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to Punctuation Corners and read 10 more sentences. Focus on the sentences the student found hardest.
- Use color coding: write periods in blue, question marks in green, exclamation points in red. The visual distinction helps some students remember.
- Read a page from a favorite book together. Stop at each sentence and have the student point to the end mark and name it. Then read the sentence with the right voice.
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 9.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 9 (Spelling Patterns). Continue to check end punctuation in all future writing activities.

## Extension (Optional)
- **Punctuation Hunt:** Read a page from any book. Count how many periods, question marks, and exclamation points you can find. Which end mark appears the most? (Usually periods!)
- **Voice Acting:** Write 5 sentences about a topic and read them aloud with dramatic expression. Try reading an exclamation point sentence in a whisper — does it feel wrong? That is because the exclamation point wants ENERGY!
- **Punctuation Song:** Sing to the tune of "Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star":
  "Period, period, little dot — you end a sentence, like it or not.
  Question mark, you curve and ask — answering questions is your task.
  Exclamation, tall and loud — you show big feelings and stand out proud!"

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This lesson deepens what was introduced briefly in Unit 3, Lesson 2.** In that earlier lesson, students learned that sentences end with punctuation as part of the "four-finger check" (subject, verb, capital letter, end mark). This lesson zooms in on end punctuation specifically, teaching students to CHOOSE the right mark and to READ with expression based on the mark. It is normal for students to have some familiarity with end marks — this lesson makes that knowledge explicit and active.

**Reading expression is just as important as writing the correct mark.** When students can hear the difference between "The dog is here." and "The dog is here!" and "Is the dog here?" they understand punctuation as a communication tool, not just a rule to follow. Practice reading aloud frequently. If the student reads every sentence in the same flat voice, pause and point to the end mark: "What mark is this? How should your voice sound?"

**The "strong feeling" category is the trickiest.** Students often want to put exclamation points on everything because first-graders feel strongly about most things. This is developmentally appropriate! Gently guide: "An exclamation point is for when you are REALLY excited, surprised, or scared — like 'Watch out!' or 'I love it!' If you are just sharing information calmly, a period is the right choice." But do not overcorrect — enthusiasm in writing is a good thing at this age.

**Common question: "Can I use an exclamation point and a question mark together?!"** Some students will want to write "?!" or "!?" for excited questions. This is actually used in informal writing! For first grade, gently say: "In first-grade writing, we pick ONE end mark for each sentence. If you are mostly asking, use a question mark. If you are mostly showing a big feeling, use an exclamation point." This is sufficient for now.

**Connecting to real reading:** After this lesson, whenever you read together, occasionally stop and ask: "What end mark does this sentence have? How should we read it?" This reinforces the reading-writing connection. Punctuation is not just about writing — it is about understanding what you read, too.
