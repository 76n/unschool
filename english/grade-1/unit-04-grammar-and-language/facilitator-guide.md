# Facilitator Guide — Grade 1 ELA, Unit 4: Grammar & Language

**Grade:** 1
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 4 — Grammar & Language
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)

---

## Unit Overview

In Units 1 through 3, your student learned to decode words, comprehend texts, and write opinion, informative, and narrative pieces. Those are enormous accomplishments. Now this unit adds the final layer of Grade 1 literacy: the grammar and language conventions that make writing clear, correct, and easy to read. Your student will learn how nouns work (common, proper, possessive), how to choose the right punctuation at the end of a sentence, and how to spell common words using patterns they can rely on. They will also meet pronouns, action verbs, and adjectives — the building blocks that make sentences interesting and precise.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Identify and use common nouns (person, place, thing, animal) in sentences
- Recognize and capitalize proper nouns (names of specific people, places, and things)
- Form singular and plural nouns correctly using -s and -es
- Use possessive nouns with an apostrophe and s ('s) to show ownership
- Replace nouns with pronouns (he, she, it, they, we, I) to avoid repetition
- Identify and use action verbs and descriptive adjectives in sentences
- Choose the correct end punctuation (period, question mark, exclamation point) based on the type of sentence
- Spell words with common patterns (short vowel CVC, long vowel CVCe) and high-frequency sight words

**Why this matters:** Grammar is not a set of rules to memorize — it is the system that holds language together. When a student understands that "the dog" and "The Dog" and "the dog's bone" all mean different things, they are learning to communicate with precision. When they know that a sentence ending in a period sounds different from one ending with an exclamation point, they are learning that punctuation carries meaning. These are not abstract skills. Every time your student writes a sentence, tells a story, or reads a book, they are using grammar. This unit makes that invisible system visible, so the student can use it on purpose.

**A note on grammar instruction:** Research is clear on one point: isolated grammar drills do not improve student writing. Filling in blanks on a worksheet about nouns will not help a child write better sentences. Grammar is best learned in the context of real reading and real writing. Throughout this unit, every grammar concept is taught through sentences the student reads and writes — not through abstract exercises. When your student learns about possessive nouns, they will write sentences using them. When they learn about end punctuation, they will read sentences aloud and listen for the difference. This unit connects directly back to the writing skills from Unit 3. The student is not learning grammar for its own sake — they are learning grammar so their writing becomes clearer and more powerful.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Decode one-syllable words and read Grade 1 text with reasonable accuracy (Unit 1 phonics skills)
- [ ] Answer who, what, where, when, why, and how questions about a text (Unit 2 comprehension)
- [ ] Write complete sentences with a subject, verb, capital letter, and end punctuation (Unit 3 writing skills)
- [ ] Write a short piece (opinion, informative, or narrative) of at least 2-3 sentences (Unit 3 writing skills)
- [ ] Hold a pencil with a functional grip and write legibly on lined paper
- [ ] Speak in complete sentences and express ideas orally with some detail

If the student is not yet writing complete sentences, return to Unit 3 Lesson 2 (Writing Complete Sentences) and practice until the student can reliably produce a sentence with a capital letter, a subject, a verb, and end punctuation. Grammar instruction depends on the student being able to write and read sentences — it is very difficult to teach about nouns if the student cannot yet produce a sentence that contains one.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Pencils and erasers** — Every lesson involves writing. Keep sharpened pencils available.
- **Lined paper** (wide-ruled or primary-ruled with a midline) — The student will write sentences in every lesson. Prepare at least 25 sheets.
- **The student's Writing Portfolio from Unit 3** — Several lessons ask the student to revisit their own writing and apply new grammar skills to it.
- **Index cards or small cards** — For word sorting, noun labeling, and punctuation practice. At least 40 blank cards.
- **Crayons or colored pencils** — For color-coding parts of speech (e.g., nouns in blue, verbs in red, adjectives in green) and for illustrations.
- **Chart paper or a large whiteboard** — For modeling sentences during direct instruction. A plain sheet of paper works if you do not have chart paper.
- **A few simple books or printed texts** — Any age-appropriate books your student can read. These are used for "grammar hunts" where the student finds nouns, verbs, adjectives, and punctuation in real text.
- **Anchor charts** (created during lessons):
  - Common nouns vs. proper nouns (Lessons 1-2)
  - Singular and plural nouns rules (Lesson 3)
  - Possessive nouns (Lesson 4)
  - Parts of speech — nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives (Lessons 5-7)
  - End punctuation guide — period, question mark, exclamation point (Lesson 8)
  - Spelling patterns — CVC and CVCe (Lesson 9)

No workbooks or grammar programs are needed. Everything is built into the lessons.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Common Nouns (person, place, thing, animal) | 1 session |
| 2 | Proper Nouns and Capital Letters | 1-2 sessions |
| 3 | Singular and Plural Nouns (-s and -es) | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Possessive Nouns ('s) | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Pronouns (he, she, it, they, we, I) | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Action Verbs | 1 session |
| 7 | Adjectives — Describing Words | 1 session |
| 8 | End Punctuation (periods, question marks, exclamation points) | 1-2 sessions |
| 9 | Spelling Patterns (short vowel CVC, long vowel CVCe, common sight words) | 1-2 sessions |
| 10 | Grammar Review and Application | 1-2 sessions |
| -- | Unit Assessment | 1 separate session (30-40 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lessons 1-2 (common and proper nouns) form a natural pair. If the student grasps common nouns quickly in Lesson 1, move to Lesson 2 in the same session. If the student struggles to distinguish "a dog" from "Buddy," take two sessions on Lesson 2 — capitalizing proper nouns is a skill they will use in every piece of writing going forward.
- Lesson 3 (plural nouns) involves spelling changes (-s vs. -es), which can be tricky. If the student needs more practice, add a short session of sorting words into -s and -es groups before moving on.
- Lesson 4 (possessive nouns) introduces the apostrophe, which is a new punctuation mark. Expect this to take two sessions for most students. The concept of ownership is intuitive, but placing the apostrophe correctly takes repetition.
- Lessons 5-7 (pronouns, verbs, adjectives) move quickly through parts of speech. These lessons are designed to build awareness, not mastery. If the student can identify examples and use the words in a sentence, that is sufficient for Grade 1.
- Lesson 8 (end punctuation) connects to Unit 3 sentence writing. If the student already uses periods consistently from Unit 3, this lesson may move quickly. Focus on question marks and exclamation points, which are typically newer.
- Lesson 9 (spelling patterns) may need two sessions if the student has not yet solidified CVC and CVCe patterns from Unit 1 phonics work.
- Lesson 10 (review and application) is where everything comes together. The student will write a short piece and apply grammar skills learned throughout the unit. Do not rush this lesson — it is where the real learning shows.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student capitalizes random words in the middle of a sentence | The student knows that capital letters are "important" but has not yet learned the rule — capitals go at the start of a sentence and on proper nouns only | Point to each capitalized word and ask: "Is this the first word in the sentence? Is this the name of a specific person, place, or thing?" If both answers are no, it should be lowercase. Practice by giving the student sentences with incorrect capitalization and asking them to fix them. |
| Student adds 's to every plural noun ("two dog's") | The student is confusing the plural -s with the possessive 's — both involve adding "s" to a noun, so this is a very logical error | Separate the two concepts clearly: "When there is more than one, just add s — no apostrophe: two dogs. When something belongs to someone, use apostrophe s: the dog's bone." Practice sorting examples: "Is this more than one, or does something belong to someone?" |
| Student cannot tell the difference between a common and proper noun | The distinction between "a city" and "Denver" is abstract for a six-year-old who is still developing categorization skills | Make it concrete and personal: "A girl is any girl. But Maya is one specific girl — your friend. We capitalize names of specific people, places, and things." Use the student's own life: their name, their school, their street, their pet's name. |
| Student puts a period at the end of every sentence, even questions | The student learned periods in Unit 3 and is applying that rule universally — a sign of learning, not confusion | Read the sentence aloud together: "Does that sound like you are asking something or telling something? If you are asking, we use a question mark." Practice by having the student read sentences with different punctuation and notice how their voice changes. |
| Student writes "Him went to the store" or "Her is my friend" | The student is using objective pronouns (him, her) in subject position — a common pattern in spoken English, especially in certain dialects | Do not frame this as wrong. Say: "In writing, we say 'He went to the store.' Try reading it both ways — which one sounds like a book?" Provide gentle modeling: "He, she, and I go at the beginning. Him, her, and me come after the verb." |
| Student cannot think of adjectives beyond "big," "small," "good," and "bad" | The student's descriptive vocabulary is still developing, which is perfectly normal at age 6-7 | Build a word wall of adjectives together. When reading books, pause and point out interesting adjectives: "Crunchy leaves — crunchy is a describing word! It tells us how the leaves sound." Play games: pick an object and see how many words you can use to describe it. |
| Student spells CVC words correctly but guesses at CVCe words | The student has mastered short vowel patterns but has not yet internalized the "silent e" rule that changes the vowel sound | Review the pattern explicitly: "When we add an e to the end, the vowel says its name. C-A-P is cap. C-A-P-E is cape. The e is silent, but it changes the vowel." Practice with word pairs: hop/hope, kit/kite, cub/cube, tap/tape. |
| Student treats grammar lessons as separate from "real" writing | The student sees grammar as a school exercise rather than a tool for their own writing | Consistently connect grammar back to the student's own writing. Pull a sentence from their Writing Portfolio and ask: "Can you find the nouns in your sentence? Can you add an adjective to make this more interesting?" When grammar lives inside their own writing, it becomes meaningful. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to build grammar awareness in everyday life:

- "Look at that sign — it says 'Elm Street.' Why do you think Elm and Street have capital letters? Are those proper nouns?"
- "I see three birds on that branch. How would you write that? What do you add to 'bird' to make it more than one?"
- "Whose backpack is this? It's Maya's backpack. Did you hear the 's? That means it belongs to Maya."
- "Can you tell me what you did at the park today using action words? You ran, you climbed, you swung — those are all verbs!"
- "Let's describe this apple. What words can you think of? Red, shiny, crunchy, round — those are all adjectives!"
- "I'm going to say a sentence, and you tell me what punctuation goes at the end: 'What time is it' — period, question mark, or exclamation point?"
- "Can you find five nouns in this room right now? How about a proper noun?"
- "Listen to these two sentences: 'I like dogs.' 'I LOVE dogs!' Which one sounds more exciting? What punctuation mark do you think the second one has?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- The student consistently cannot identify nouns in a sentence even after extensive modeling, practice, and concrete examples over multiple lessons (possible difficulty with categorization or language processing)
- The student cannot produce a complete sentence orally or in writing despite completing Units 1-3 (possible language development concern that was not apparent during reading-focused units)
- The student shows persistent confusion between letters and punctuation marks, or cannot consistently locate the end of a sentence (possible visual processing difficulty)
- The student's spelling shows no phonetic logic — the letters bear no relationship to the sounds of the word, even for simple CVC words covered in Unit 1 (possible phonological processing difficulty)
- The student becomes extremely frustrated or anxious during any grammar or writing task, despite encouragement and scaffolding, and this pattern persists across multiple lessons

If you see these signs, consider:
- Revisiting Unit 1 phonics lessons to strengthen the sound-letter foundation that spelling depends on
- Consulting with a speech-language pathologist if the student struggles to form grammatically correct sentences even in speech
- Exploring whether the student may need vision or hearing screening (if they have difficulty distinguishing between similar-looking punctuation marks or similar-sounding words)
- Providing extra processing time — some students understand grammar concepts but need longer to apply them in writing

Remember: Grade 1 grammar is about building awareness, not achieving perfection. A student who can identify a noun most of the time and remembers to capitalize names some of the time is making excellent progress. Mastery develops over years of reading and writing, not in four weeks.

## What Comes Next

This is the final unit in the Grade 1 English Language Arts sequence. By completing all four units, your student has built a strong foundation across the full spectrum of early literacy: decoding and phonics (Unit 1), reading comprehension (Unit 2), writing in three genres (Unit 3), and grammar and language conventions (Unit 4).

In **Grade 2**, the curriculum builds on every skill your student has learned this year. Reading fluency and comprehension deepen — your student will read longer texts with expression, recount stories, and determine the central message. Writing becomes more developed, with greater focus on organization, revision, and editing. Grammar expands to include collective nouns, irregular plurals, reflexive pronouns, and apostrophes for possessives in more complex forms.

Between now and Grade 2, the best thing you can do is keep your student reading and writing every day. Let them write letters to family members, make lists, keep a journal, or write stories for fun. When you notice them capitalizing a proper noun or using an exclamation point on purpose, point it out: "You used an exclamation point because that sentence is exciting — that is exactly right!" Every time they see grammar working in their own writing, it sticks a little more.
