# Facilitator Guide — Grade 1 ELA, Unit 1: Phonics and Beginning Reading

**Grade:** 1
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 1 — Phonics and Beginning Reading
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit builds directly on the phonological awareness and letter-sound knowledge your student developed in kindergarten. In kindergarten, the student learned to recognize letters, connect them to sounds, and read simple CVC (consonant-vowel-consonant) words like "cat" and "dog." Now we take the next step: combining letters into more complex patterns that unlock hundreds of new words.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Read and spell CVC words with short vowels fluently
- Recognize and read words with consonant digraphs (sh, ch, th, wh)
- Decode words with beginning and ending consonant blends
- Read words with the CVCe (silent e) long vowel pattern
- Read words with common vowel teams (ai, ay, ee, ea, oa, ow, igh)
- Read short decodable passages with accuracy and basic fluency
- Use punctuation marks as cues for reading expression

**Why this matters:** Phonics is the bridge between knowing letters and actually reading. A student who can decode words — break them apart and put them together — does not have to memorize every word individually. They have a *system* for reading any word they encounter. This unit gives your student that system, starting with the most common and regular English spelling patterns. Every pattern learned here opens a door to dozens of new words.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, informally check that the student can:

- [ ] Name all 26 uppercase and lowercase letters
- [ ] Say the most common sound for each consonant letter (b = /b/, c = /k/, d = /d/, etc.)
- [ ] Say the short vowel sound for each vowel (a = /a/ as in "apple," e = /e/ as in "egg," i = /i/ as in "igloo," o = /o/ as in "octopus," u = /u/ as in "umbrella")
- [ ] Blend three sounds together to read a simple CVC word (given /c/ /a/ /t/, says "cat")
- [ ] Segment a simple CVC word into individual sounds (given "dog," says /d/ /o/ /g/)
- [ ] Recognize at least 10-15 high-frequency sight words (the, a, is, and, to, in, it, he, she, was)

If the student cannot name most letters and their sounds, return to kindergarten phonics materials before beginning this unit. If the student knows letter sounds but cannot blend them into words, spend a few sessions on oral blending practice (you say the sounds slowly, student puts them together) before starting Lesson 1.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

Most materials are common household items. Gather these before starting:

- **Index cards or cardstock cut into cards** — You will make word cards, letter cards, and sound cards throughout the unit. Prepare at least 60-80 blank cards.
- **Pencils and paper** — For writing practice in every lesson.
- **Crayons or colored pencils** — For sorting activities and marking vowel patterns.
- **A pocket chart or flat surface for sorting** — A table works fine. You can also use paper plates, bowls, or sections of a large sheet of paper as sorting bins.
- **Magnetic letters or letter tiles** (optional but helpful) — For building and manipulating words physically. If you do not have these, writing letters on individual small cards works.
- **A simple timer** (optional) — For timed fluency activities in later lessons.
- **Decodable texts** (provided within lessons) — Short passages are included in Lessons 9 and 10. No separate books are needed.

You do NOT need workbooks, phonics kits, or reading programs. Everything is built into the lessons.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Short Vowel Review (CVC words) | 1 session |
| 2 | Consonant Digraphs: sh, ch | 1 session |
| 3 | Consonant Digraphs: th, wh | 1 session |
| 4 | Beginning Consonant Blends | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Ending Consonant Blends | 1 session |
| 6 | Long Vowel Patterns: Silent e (CVCe) | 1-2 sessions |
| 7 | Vowel Teams: ai, ay, ee, ea | 1-2 sessions |
| 8 | Vowel Teams: oa, ow, igh | 1 session |
| 9 | Reading Connected Text | 1 session |
| 10 | Fluency and Expression | 1 session |
| -- | Unit Assessment | 1 separate short session |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 1 is a review lesson. If your student reads CVC words easily and confidently, move through it briskly and advance to Lesson 2.
- Lessons 2-3 (digraphs) are conceptually similar. If the student grasps sh/ch quickly, they will likely pick up th/wh quickly too. However, th is tricky for many students — allow extra time if needed.
- Lesson 4 (beginning blends) introduces many patterns at once. If the student needs more practice, split it across two sessions: one for l-blends (bl, cl, fl, gl, pl, sl) and one for r-blends (br, cr, dr, fr, gr, tr).
- Lessons 6-8 (long vowels and vowel teams) are the most conceptually demanding in this unit. Do not rush. If the student needs an extra session for silent e or vowel teams, take it.
- Lessons 9-10 bring everything together. If the student is still shaky on individual patterns, spend more time on those patterns before moving to connected text.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student reads "ship" as "sip" — ignores the h in digraphs | Not yet recognizing that two letters can make one sound | Return to the digraph lesson. Emphasize: "These two letters are a team. They make ONE sound together." Use physical cards — tape sh together as a single unit. |
| Student reads "bl" as "bul" — adds a vowel between blend consonants | Trying to separate the blend into two syllables | Model the blend slowly: "/b/ /l/ — bllll." Have the student feel their mouth making both sounds in quick succession. Do NOT say "buh-luh." |
| Student confuses blends and digraphs (says "sh" makes two sounds) | The distinction between blends and digraphs is not clear | Use contrasting pairs. "In 'ship,' sh makes ONE sound: /sh/. In 'slip,' you hear TWO sounds: /s/ then /l/." Sort words into two columns repeatedly. |
| Student reads "cape" as "cap" — ignores the silent e | Not yet applying the CVCe rule | Go back to word pairs: cap/cape, hop/hope, kit/kite. Build them with letter cards and physically add/remove the e to hear the change. This is a high-frequency pattern that needs lots of repetition. |
| Student reads "rain" as "ran" — treats ai as two separate sounds | Not yet recognizing vowel teams | Underline or highlight the vowel team as a unit. Say: "These two vowels work together. The first one says its name: /ay/." Practice with word families: rain, main, pain, gain. |
| Student can decode individual words but stumbles when reading sentences | Decoding is not yet automatic enough for connected text | This is normal and expected. Return to word-level practice for the specific patterns causing difficulty. Fluency develops over time with repeated reading. |
| Student guesses at words based on the first letter instead of decoding | Over-relying on context or picture cues rather than phonics | Cover up pictures. Point to the word and say: "Look at all the letters. What sound does the word start with? What comes next?" Guide them through the whole word. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or in everyday moments to build phonics awareness:

- "I spy something that starts with the /sh/ sound. Can you find it?"
- "Can you think of a word that rhymes with 'cake'? What pattern does it use?" (CVCe)
- "Let's look at this cereal box. Can you find a word with a blend in it?"
- "I'm going to say a word slowly: /ch/ /i/ /p/. Can you put those sounds together?"
- "What happens to the word 'hop' if we add an e at the end?"
- "Look at this sign. Can you read any of the words? Which sounds do you recognize?"
- "Let's play 'change the vowel.' If 'cat' has /a/, what happens if we use /i/ instead?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support than these materials provide. They are NOT cause for alarm — they are signals to investigate further:

- After consistent practice over 3-4 weeks, the student cannot reliably blend three sounds into a CVC word
- The student consistently reverses letters (b/d, p/q) beyond what is typical for age 6-7 (occasional reversals are normal; persistent, frequent reversals warrant attention)
- The student can decode words in isolation but shows extreme difficulty with even the simplest connected text after multiple attempts
- The student appears to have difficulty hearing the differences between similar sounds (e.g., cannot distinguish /sh/ from /ch/ even after focused practice)
- The student shows persistent frustration, anxiety, or avoidance during reading activities despite encouragement, patience, and appropriate scaffolding

If you see these signs, consider:
- Consulting with the student's pediatrician about a reading or language evaluation
- Requesting a screening for dyslexia or other reading difficulties (early identification leads to better outcomes)
- Exploring whether the student might benefit from speech-language therapy (for sound discrimination concerns)
- Looking into structured literacy tutoring programs in your area

Remember: children develop reading skills at different rates. Many early struggles resolve with patient, systematic instruction. The goal is to catch genuine difficulties early, not to create anxiety about normal variation.

---

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 2** (planned), which will focus on reading comprehension and vocabulary development — applying the decoding skills from this unit to understand and respond to what they read. The strong phonics foundation built here makes comprehension instruction much more effective, because the student can actually *read* the words on the page instead of guessing.

Between units, continue to reinforce phonics patterns in everyday life. Point out words on signs, cereal boxes, and book covers. Play word games in the car. Read together every day — even after the student can decode independently, reading aloud together builds vocabulary and comprehension skills that go far beyond what the student can read alone.
