# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 1, Lesson 3

## Topic
Consonant Digraphs: th and wh

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Produce the /th/ sound (both voiced and voiceless) and the /wh/ sound
- Read at least 8 words containing th or wh
- Distinguish between all four digraphs learned so far (sh, ch, th, wh)
- Write at least 3 words containing th or wh from dictation

## Prerequisites
- Short vowel CVC word reading (Lesson 1 mastery)
- Consonant digraphs sh and ch (Lesson 2 mastery)

## Materials Needed
- Index cards with "th" and "wh" written on them (one card each)
- Index cards with "sh" and "ch" from Lesson 2
- Word cards (prepare before lesson — see word list below)
- Letter cards or magnetic letters
- Paper and pencil
- Four sorting labels: "sh," "ch," "th," "wh"

**Word cards to prepare:**
this, that, them, then, the, thin, thick, with, bath, math, path, moth, when, what, which, while, whip, white, where, why, wheel

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review digraphs from Lesson 2 and short vowels from Lesson 1.

**Spaced Review:**
1. Read these words: "ship, chop, fish, chin" (Lesson 2 — sh/ch review)
2. Read these CVC words: "bat, hem, kid, fox, rug" (Lesson 1 — short vowel review)
3. "I'll say a word. Tell me if you hear /sh/ or /ch/: shell (/sh/), chest (/ch/), wish (/sh/), much (/ch/)."

**Bridge to new learning:**
"You already know two digraph teams: sh and ch. Today you're going to meet two MORE teams. By the end of today, you'll know FOUR digraphs. That's a lot of reading power!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce th and wh digraphs with attention to mouth position and voicing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Voiced sound:** A sound where your voice box buzzes (you can feel it if you put your hand on your throat). The th in "this" is voiced.
- **Voiceless sound:** A sound where air comes out but your voice box does NOT buzz. The th in "thin" is voiceless.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the th digraph.**
Hold up the "th" card. Say:
"Here is another letter team: t and h. Together they say /th/."

Model the sound: stick your tongue slightly between your teeth and blow air.
"Watch my mouth. See how my tongue peeks out between my teeth? That is how we make the /th/ sound."

Have the student try. "Put your tongue between your teeth and blow gently. /th/."

**Step 2: Two kinds of th.**
"Here's something interesting. The /th/ sound has two versions. Listen carefully."

Say "thin." Put your hand on your throat. "When I say 'thin,' my voice box is quiet. Feel yours." (Voiceless /th/)

Now say "this." Hand on throat. "When I say 'this,' my voice box buzzes! Feel the difference." (Voiced /th/)

"Both sounds use th. You do NOT need to worry about which is which when reading — your mouth will figure it out naturally. Just remember: t-h together = /th/."

*Facilitator note: Do not spend too much time on voiced vs. voiceless. Mention it so the student is not confused when "this" and "thin" sound slightly different, but move on. They will produce both correctly through natural speech.*

**Step 3: Introduce the wh digraph.**
Hold up the "wh" card. Say:
"Our last digraph team for today: w and h. Together they say /wh/."

Model: "Listen: 'when.' /wh/... /e/... /n/. When."

"You'll notice that /wh/ sounds a lot like /w/. That is because in modern English, most people say them almost the same way. But in reading, when you see w-h together, you know it is the /wh/ digraph."

"A fun thing about wh: almost all wh words are QUESTION words! When, what, where, which, while, why. Wh helps you ask questions!"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Read the word "then."
- "I see t-h at the beginning. That's the th digraph: /th/."
- "Then e: /e/. Then n: /n/."
- "Blend: /th/... /e/... /n/... then!"

**Example 2:** Read the word "with."
- "w: /w/. i: /i/. Then t-h at the end: /th/."
- "Blend: /w/... /i/... /th/... with!"

**Example 3:** Read the word "when."
- "I see w-h at the beginning: /wh/."
- "Then e: /e/. Then n: /n/."
- "Blend: /wh/... /e/... /n/... when!"

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice reading th and wh words, then review all four digraphs together.

**Activity 1: Read th Words (3 minutes)**
Show these word cards: this, thin, bath, math, them.

For each word:
- "Where is the digraph — beginning or end?"
- "Read the word."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student says /t/ /h/ instead of /th/, say: "Remember the tongue-between-the-teeth sound? These letters are a team: /th/." Model and have the student repeat.*

**Activity 2: Read wh Words (3 minutes)**
Show these word cards: what, when, whip, which, while.

"All of these start with wh. Read each one."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles, help them with the vowel sounds. Many wh words have irregular vowel sounds (like "what" where the a sounds like /o/). For "what," say: "This is a tricky word — the a in 'what' sounds like /o/. What." For "where" and "why," these are high-frequency words that may need to be taught as sight words alongside their phonics pattern.*

**Activity 3: Four-Way Digraph Sort (4 minutes)**
Place all four digraph labels on the table: sh, ch, th, wh.

Give the student these 8 word cards: ship, chin, this, when, fish, much, bath, whip.

"Read each word. Listen for the digraph. Sort it under the right team."

*If the student confuses two digraphs: isolate those two. "Let's just listen to /sh/ and /th/. Watch my mouth — /sh/ (lips rounded, air blowing) vs. /th/ (tongue between teeth). Which do you hear in 'ship'?" Practice the confused pair before adding the others back.*

---

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student reads and sorts all four digraph types independently.

**★ On-level (10 words):**
Sort these words under sh, ch, th, or wh:
that, shop, chip, whip, dish, math, chat, when, shell, with

Then write these 3 words from dictation:
1. "then" (t-h-e-n)
2. "when" (w-h-e-n)
3. "bath" (b-a-t-h)

**★★ Stretch (5 additional words + writing):**
Add to the sort: thick, lunch, rush, while, path

Write 2 more from dictation:
4. "this" (t-h-i-s)
5. "whip" (w-h-i-p)

**★★★ Challenge:**
Write a sentence that uses at least one sh, one ch, and one th word. Example: "The ship had a chest with thick shells."

Bonus: "How many question words can you write that start with wh?" (what, when, where, which, while, why, who)

### Interleaved Review
- Read these CVC words: sun, bed, fog, zip, mat (Lesson 1 review)
- "What digraph do you hear in 'cherry'?" (ch)

---

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can read th and wh words and distinguish all four digraphs.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Read these words aloud: **thin, what, path, while** (Student reads all 4)
2. "What two letters make the /th/ sound?" (t and h)
3. "Sort these words by digraph: shed, chat, them, when" (shed = sh, chat = ch, them = th, when = wh)

**Mastery = Student reads at least 3 of 4 words correctly AND sorts at least 3 of 4 words correctly.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If th is the problem: practice with the tongue-between-teeth physical cue. Use a mirror so the student can watch their own mouth.
- If wh is the problem: start with just "when" and "whip" — these are the most phonetically regular wh words.
- If the student confuses digraphs with each other: work with just two at a time (e.g., sh vs. th) until firm, then add the others.
- Repeat this lesson next session.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 4 (Beginning Consonant Blends). Continue to include all four digraphs in warm-ups.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who master th and wh quickly:**
- Write all the "question words" that start with wh: what, when, where, which, while, why, who. Use each in a sentence.
- Play "Digraph Detective": read a simple picture book or look at signs around the house. Every time you find a digraph word, say "Detective found one!" and identify the digraph.
- Practice "th" tongue twisters: "Three thin things think thick thoughts." (This is hard — make it silly and fun, not a test!)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**th is physically challenging.** The /th/ sound requires the tongue to go between the teeth, which is unusual. Many young children (and English language learners) substitute /f/ or /d/ for /th/. This is developmentally normal at ages 5-7. Focus on helping the student HEAR the sound and READ the pattern correctly. Perfect pronunciation will develop over time.

**wh words are often irregular.** Words like "what," "where," and "who" do not follow simple phonics rules. "What" sounds like "wot." "Where" has an r-controlled vowel. "Who" does not even use the /wh/ sound. Introduce these as words the student will "learn by heart" — their wh spelling helps identify them as question words, even if the pronunciation is not perfectly decodable.

**Voiced vs. voiceless th:** Do not test students on this distinction. It is included in instruction only so the facilitator understands why "the" and "thin" sound different at the beginning. Students do not need to label sounds as voiced or voiceless — they just need to recognize th and read it.

**Four digraphs at once may feel like a lot.** If the student is overwhelmed by keeping sh, ch, th, and wh straight, that is normal. These four digraphs will appear in warm-ups for the rest of the unit, providing plenty of additional practice. Mastery of all four will build over time, not in a single session.

**Growth mindset note:** "You now know FOUR digraph teams! That means you can read a whole bunch of new words. Every new pattern you learn makes you a stronger reader."
