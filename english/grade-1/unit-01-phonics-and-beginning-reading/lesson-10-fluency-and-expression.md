# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 1, Lesson 10

## Topic
Fluency and Expression

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read a short passage with improved accuracy and appropriate pace (not word-by-word but not rushing)
- Use punctuation as reading cues: period = full stop, question mark = voice goes up, exclamation point = show excitement
- Demonstrate basic reading expression (not monotone) when reading dialogue or exclamatory sentences
- Re-read a familiar passage with greater smoothness than the first reading

## Prerequisites
- Reading connected text (Lesson 9 mastery)
- All phonics patterns from Lessons 1-8

## Materials Needed
- This lesson plan (contains practice passages)
- Paper and pencil
- A bookmark or index card (reading guide)
- Optional: a timer or stopwatch (for the re-reading fluency activity)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review phonics patterns and practice quick word reading to build automaticity.

**Spaced Review — Quick Word Reading:**
Read each word as fast as you can while still being accurate:
1. "shop, frog, best, cape, rain, tree, boat, night" (one word from each pattern)
2. Read this sentence: "The cat ran to the red shed." (CVC + digraph review)

**Bridge to new learning:**
"Yesterday you read whole passages — and you did a great job! Today we're going to work on reading even MORE smoothly. Great readers don't just read the words correctly — they also read with feeling. They know when to stop, when to ask a question, and when to sound excited. Let me show you what I mean."

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the role of punctuation in reading aloud and model expressive reading.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fluency:** Reading smoothly, at a good pace, without lots of stopping and starting. Not too fast, not too slow.
- **Expression:** Making your voice match what the words are saying. If someone is excited, your voice sounds excited. If someone is asking a question, your voice goes up at the end.
- **Punctuation:** The marks at the end of sentences that tell you HOW to read them.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the three end-of-sentence punctuation marks.**

Write on paper:
- **Period (.)** — "A period means STOP. It is like a red light. You pause before starting the next sentence."
- **Question mark (?)** — "A question mark means someone is asking a question. Your voice goes UP at the end, like you're curious."
- **Exclamation point (!)** — "An exclamation point means strong feelings — excitement, surprise, or shouting. Your voice gets BIGGER and more energetic."

**Step 2: Model the difference.**

Write these three sentences:
1. "The dog is big."
2. "Is the dog big?"
3. "The dog is so big!"

Read sentence 1 in a flat, calm voice: "The dog is big. (Period. I stop. My voice is level.)"

Read sentence 2 with your voice rising at the end: "Is the dog big? (Question mark. My voice goes UP because I'm asking something.)"

Read sentence 3 with energy and emphasis: "The dog is so big! (Exclamation point. I'm amazed! My voice shows it!)"

Have the student read all three sentences, mimicking the expression.

**Step 3: Demonstrate fluent vs. non-fluent reading.**

Write: "The rain came down on the road. The goat ran to the big oak tree."

Read it first in a choppy, word-by-word style: "The... rain... came... down... on... the... road. The... goat... ran... to... the... big... oak... tree."

"That was hard to listen to, right? Now listen to this:"

Read it smoothly, grouping words naturally: "The rain came down on the road. The goat ran to the big oak tree."

"Hear the difference? The second time, I grouped the words together and it sounded more like talking. That's what fluent reading sounds like."

**Step 4: Explain that re-reading builds fluency.**
"Here's a secret about fluency: the BEST way to get smoother is to read the same thing more than once. The first time you read something, your brain is working hard to decode every word. The second time, it's easier. The third time, it's even smoother. Re-reading is not 'going backwards' — it's training your brain!"

### Worked Examples

**Example: Expressive reading with dialogue.**
Write: "Look at that!" said the boy. "What is it?" asked the girl. "It is a big, bright ship," said the boy.

Model reading with expression:
- "Look at that!" — excited voice, emphasis on "that"
- "What is it?" — curious, questioning voice
- "It is a big, bright ship," — calm, explanatory voice

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice reading with fluency and expression together.

**Activity 1: Punctuation Practice (3 minutes)**
Student reads these sentences with appropriate expression:

1. "I see a bee." (calm, period)
2. "Is that a bee?" (questioning, voice goes up)
3. "That bee is on my nose!" (excited/surprised)
4. "The boat went slow." (calm)
5. "Can the boat go fast?" (questioning)
6. "The boat went so fast!" (excited)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student reads everything in the same flat tone, say: "Look at the end of the sentence. What mark do you see? A question mark! That means your voice should go up, like you're curious. Listen to me, then try again."*

*If the student exaggerates wildly (screaming the exclamation sentences): this is fine and fun! Better to over-express than under-express. Gently dial it back: "I love the energy! Try it one more time just a little quieter, like you're surprised but not shouting."*

**Activity 2: Echo Reading (3 minutes)**
Use this passage. Facilitator reads one sentence; student immediately echoes it with the same expression.

> "What is in the shed?" asked Mom. "I will look," said the boy. He went in. "It is a goat!" he said. The goat was white with black spots. "Can we keep it?" asked the boy. "Well," said Mom, "we need to think about that."

Read each sentence with clear expression. Student echoes. After the full passage, have the student try reading the whole thing independently.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student needs to decode a word, pause and help. Then re-read the whole sentence smoothly before moving on.*

**Activity 3: Re-Read for Fluency (4 minutes)**
Use Passage 1 from Lesson 9 ("The Goat on the Road"):

> A goat went down the road. The road was long. The goat saw a big oak tree. "I will rest," said the goat. The goat sat in the shade and ate a green leaf. "This is a fine day," said the goat.

**First read:** Student reads the passage aloud. Time it if possible (or just note the pace). Note any errors.

**Second read:** Student reads the same passage again. It should be noticeably smoother.

"Did you hear the difference? The second time was smoother because your brain already knew the words. That's the power of re-reading!"

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices reading new passages with fluency and expression.

**Passage A: "The Bright Kite"**

> One day, a girl made a kite. The kite was bright and green. "I will fly it today!" she said. She ran to the big green hill. The wind came and took the kite up high. "Look at it go!" said the girl. The kite went left. Then it went right. "This is the best day!" she said.

**★ On-level:**
1. Read the passage aloud. Try to read smoothly — not word by word.
2. Find and circle (or point to) three punctuation marks: one period, one question mark or exclamation point, and one set of quotation marks.
3. "What color was the kite?" (Bright and green)

**★★ Stretch:**
4. Read the passage a second time. Try to be smoother and more expressive than the first time.
5. "Where did the girl take the kite?" (To the big green hill)
6. "How did the girl feel? How do you know?" (Happy/excited — she said "This is the best day!")

**★★★ Challenge:**
7. Read the passage to a family member or stuffed animal. Use your best expression.
8. Write your own 2-3 sentence mini-story. Include at least one question mark and one exclamation point.

**Passage B (additional practice): "Snow Day"**

> "Is it snowing?" asked the boy. He looked out. Yes! The road was white. The trees had snow on them. "May I go out?" he asked Mom. "Yes, but get your coat!" she said. He put on his coat and ran out. The snow was cold and soft. "This is so fun!" he said.

### Interleaved Review
- Write the word "night" from memory. (igh pattern review)
- "What is a vowel team?" (Two vowels that work together to make one sound)
- Read these words: "chess, plant, grape, stream" (digraph, blend, CVCe + blend, vowel team + blend)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student can read connected text with basic fluency and expression.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this mini-passage aloud:

> "Can you see the boat?" asked the man. "Yes!" said the girl. "It has a bright red sail. It is the best boat on the sea!"

Assessment criteria:
1. **Accuracy:** Student reads with 2 or fewer errors.
2. **Punctuation awareness:** Student's voice rises for at least one question. Student shows emphasis or excitement for at least one exclamation.
3. **Pacing:** Student reads most of the passage in phrases (not exclusively word-by-word).

**Mastery = Student meets criteria 1 AND at least one of criteria 2 or 3.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If accuracy is the issue: return to specific phonics patterns that caused errors. The student needs more word-level practice before focusing on fluency.
- If expression is the issue: this develops with practice. Continue echo reading (facilitator reads, student repeats) daily. Use silly voices and exaggerated tones to make it playful.
- If pacing is the issue (very slow, word-by-word): focus on re-reading familiar passages. The student needs more practice with the specific words so that decoding becomes automatic. Fluency is built on accuracy — do not rush.
- Repeat this lesson with Passage B next session.

### If Mastery Is Met
The student is ready for the Unit Assessment. Celebrate: "You are READING! With expression! That is something to be very proud of."

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who read fluently and expressively:**
- Partner reading: student and facilitator each take a character in the echo reading passage. Read it like a play!
- Create a "reading theater": student reads a passage to a family audience with their best expression.
- Try reading something new — a simple book, a note, a recipe, or a sign. Apply the decoding and fluency skills from this unit.
- Record the student reading (phone or voice recorder). Let them listen to themselves. Ask: "What sounded good? What would you change?"

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Fluency has three components:** accuracy (reading the words correctly), rate (reading at an appropriate pace), and prosody (reading with expression). This lesson focuses on all three, but prosody is the new skill. Accuracy and rate have been building throughout the unit.

**Do not time the student competitively.** Timed reading can build fluency but can also create anxiety. If you use a timer, frame it as: "Let's see how smooth your second reading sounds compared to your first." Never compare the student's reading speed to a standard or another child at this stage.

**Monotone reading is normal.** Many beginning readers use all their mental energy on decoding, leaving nothing for expression. As decoding becomes more automatic, expression naturally improves. If the student reads accurately but flatly, that is perfectly fine for now. Celebrate the accuracy and gently encourage expression: "You read every word right! Now let's try it again, and this time, make the exclamation parts really exciting."

**Echo reading is one of the most powerful fluency activities.** The facilitator provides a model of fluent reading, and the student immediately practices it. This is especially helpful for students who have never heard much expressive reading. If you do nothing else for fluency, do echo reading.

**Quotation marks** are introduced implicitly in this lesson (through dialogue in the passages). The student does not need to explain what quotation marks mean — they just need to notice that the words inside quotation marks are what someone is saying, and saying things out loud usually sounds different from narration.

**Growth mindset note:** "Reading out loud with expression takes courage. It can feel silly at first. But the more you practice, the more natural it feels. Even famous actors have to practice reading their lines many times before they sound perfect."
