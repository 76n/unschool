# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 3, Lesson 6

## Topic
Informative Writing — Adding Details and a Closing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write an informative piece with three parts: a topic sentence, facts with supporting details, and a closing sentence
- Add a specific detail to a fact sentence (a size, color, number, or description)
- Write a closing sentence that wraps up the informative piece

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a topic sentence that introduces a subject (Lesson 5)
- Ability to write fact sentences about a topic (Lesson 5)
- Ability to write complete sentences with capitals and end punctuation (Lesson 2)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or whiteboard
- Pencil and lined paper
- The "Three Types of Writing" anchor chart from Lesson 1
- The student's informative piece from Lesson 5 (in their writing folder)
- Crayons for illustrating
- 3 colored markers (for highlighting the three parts)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review informative writing from Lesson 5 and introduce the concept of adding details.

**Activity: Make It Better**
Write this informative sentence on the board: "Birds have feathers."

Say: "This is a true fact. But it is a little plain. Let's make it MORE interesting by adding a detail. What KIND of feathers? What COLOR? How do they FEEL?"

Together, expand it. Examples:
- "Birds have soft, colorful feathers."
- "Birds have feathers that keep them warm."
- "Birds have feathers in many colors, like red, blue, and brown."

"See how adding a detail makes the sentence more interesting? The reader learns MORE. Today we are going to add details to our informative writing AND learn how to end our piece with a closing sentence."

Pull out the student's informative piece from Lesson 5. Say: "We are going to make this piece even better today."

*Facilitator note: Starting with the student's own work from Lesson 5 provides continuity and shows that writing is a process that develops over time.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the student how to add details to facts and write a closing sentence for informative writing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Detail:** Extra information that makes a fact more interesting — a size, color, number, shape, or description.
- **Closing sentence:** The last sentence that wraps up the writing and reminds the reader of the topic.
- **Informative piece:** A complete piece of writing with a topic sentence, facts with details, and a closing.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Teach how to add details.**
Write on chart paper:

**To add a detail, ask yourself:**
- What color is it?
- How big or small is it?
- How many are there?
- What does it look/feel/sound like?
- Where does it happen?

"A detail answers one of these questions. Let me show you."

Write a plain fact: "Elephants have trunks."
Add a detail: "Elephants have long, strong trunks."

Write another: "Fish live in water."
Add a detail: "Fish live in oceans, lakes, and rivers."

"The detail makes the sentence teach the reader more."

**Step 2: Introduce the closing sentence for informative writing.**
"Just like in opinion writing, informative writing needs a closing sentence. It is the last sentence that wraps everything up."

Write closing sentence starters on the anchor chart:

| Informative Closing Starters |
|---|
| "Now you know about ___." |
| "There is a lot to learn about ___." |
| "___ is/are really interesting!" |
| "That is what makes ___ special." |

**Step 3: Model a complete informative piece with details and a closing.**
Revisit the frog piece from Lesson 5 and improve it:

Original (from Lesson 5):
> "Frogs are amazing animals. Frogs live near ponds and lakes. Frogs can jump very far."

Improved with details and a closing:
> "Frogs are amazing animals. Frogs live near ponds and lakes where the water is calm. Frogs can jump up to 20 times their own body length! They also eat bugs like flies and mosquitoes. Now you know some cool things about frogs."

Point out the changes:
- Added a detail to fact 1: "where the water is calm"
- Added a detail to fact 2: "up to 20 times their own body length"
- Added a new fact: "They also eat bugs like flies and mosquitoes"
- Added a closing: "Now you know some cool things about frogs"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: About the rain.**
> Rain is water that falls from the sky. Raindrops are actually shaped like tiny hamburger buns, not teardrops. Rain helps plants and flowers grow tall. Puddles form on the ground when it rains a lot. Now you know about rain!

- Topic sentence: Rain is water that falls from the sky.
- Fact with detail: Raindrops shaped like tiny hamburger buns
- Fact with detail: helps plants grow tall
- Fact with detail: puddles form when it rains a lot
- Closing: Now you know about rain!

**Example 2: About teeth.**
> There are many things to know about teeth. Kids have 20 baby teeth that fall out. Adults have 32 teeth. You should brush your teeth two times every day. That is what keeps teeth healthy and strong!

- Topic sentence: There are many things to know about teeth.
- Fact with number detail: 20 baby teeth
- Fact with number detail: 32 teeth for adults
- Fact with number detail: brush two times every day
- Closing: That is what keeps teeth healthy and strong!

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Revise the student's Lesson 5 piece to add details and a closing, or co-write a new improved piece.

**Activity: Make Your Piece Better**
Pull out the student's informative piece from Lesson 5.

**Step 1: Add details to existing facts.**
Read each fact sentence. For each one, ask: "Can we add a detail? What color? How big? How many? Where?"

Help the student rewrite each fact with a detail added. The student writes on a new piece of paper (keep the original for comparison).

**Step 2: Add a closing sentence.**
"Now let's wrap it up. Which closing starter should we use?"

Point to the closing starters on the anchor chart. Help the student choose one and write a closing sentence.

**Step 3: Read and compare.**
Read the original Lesson 5 piece, then read the new version. Ask: "Which one teaches the reader more? Which one is more interesting?"

*Facilitator prompt: If the student cannot think of details, offer two choices: "Should we say 'Dogs have fur' or 'Dogs have soft, warm fur'? Which tells the reader more?"*

*Facilitator prompt: If the student resists rewriting ("I already wrote this!"), frame it positively: "Authors always come back to make their writing better. You are being a real author right now."*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes a new informative piece with details and a closing.

**Activity: My Expert Writing**
Say: "You are going to be an EXPERT today. Pick a topic you know a lot about and write a full informative piece."

The student writes a complete informative piece with:
- A topic sentence
- At least two facts with details
- A closing sentence

The student draws a picture to match their writing.

Topics to choose from (or the student picks their own):
- An animal from a book they have read
- Their favorite food (facts about it, NOT opinions)
- A sport or game (rules or facts about it)
- Something about their home or family (facts, not opinions)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level (4 sentences):**
Write a topic sentence, two facts (with at least one detail), and a closing sentence.

Example:
> Bikes are fun to ride. Bikes have two round wheels. You push the pedals with your feet to go. Now you know how bikes work!

**Stretch (5 sentences):**
Write a topic sentence, three facts (each with at least one detail), and a closing sentence.

Example:
> There are many things to know about apples. Apples grow on trees in orchards. They can be red, green, or yellow. Apple seeds are inside the core in the middle. Apples are really interesting fruits!

**Challenge (6+ sentences):**
Write a topic sentence, at least four facts with details, and a closing sentence. Include at least one number detail and one comparison.

Example:
> Butterflies are amazing insects. Butterflies start as tiny caterpillars before they change. They have four colorful wings that help them fly. Some butterflies are as small as a penny. Others can be as big as your hand! Butterflies drink nectar from flowers using a long tongue. There is a lot to learn about butterflies.

### Interleaved Review
- "Check each sentence: Is it a fact or an opinion? Informative writing uses facts." (Lesson 3 review)
- "Does your piece have a closing? What kind of writing also has a closing?" (Lesson 4 review — opinion writing also has a closing)
- "Read your piece to me. Does every sentence start with a capital and end with a period?" (Lesson 2 review)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can write a complete informative piece with details and a closing.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What are the three parts of an informative piece?" (Answer: Topic sentence, facts with details, closing sentence)
2. "Listen to this fact: 'Cats have tails.' How could you add a detail to make it more interesting?" (Answer: Any detail added, e.g., "Cats have long, fluffy tails." or "Cats have tails that help them balance.")
3. "What does a closing sentence do?" (Answer: It wraps up the writing / reminds the reader of the topic / says goodbye to the reader)

**Mastery = Student answers all 3 items correctly, including adding a detail to the fact sentence (#2).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model informative piece. Point to each detail and ask: "What extra information does this word give us?"
- Practice adding details orally: say a plain fact, the student adds a detail, then switch roles.
- Provide the closing sentence starters on a card for reference.
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 7.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 7 (Narrative Writing — Introduction). Save the student's informative piece in their writing folder.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who write informative pieces easily:**
- Write a "Did You Know?" page: three surprising facts about any topic, each with a detail.
- Write an informative piece about a family member — interview them first to gather facts.
- Compare the informative piece to the opinion piece from Lesson 4: "How are they the same? How are they different?" Both have three parts, but the content is different (facts vs. opinions).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This lesson completes the informative writing mini-unit.** The student now has the tools to produce a basic informative piece with a topic sentence, facts with details, and a closing. The parallel structure to the opinion writing lessons (Lessons 3-4) is intentional — students build confidence when the process is familiar even as the content changes.

**Adding details is the key new skill in this lesson.** Many first graders write bare-bones facts ("Dogs have fur. Dogs eat food.") that are correct but uninteresting. The detail-adding questions (What color? How big? How many?) give the student a concrete tool for making their writing richer. This is an early version of the "show, don't tell" skill that will develop over years.

**The closing sentence for informative writing** is simpler than for opinion writing because the student does not need to restate an argument — they just need to signal that the piece is finished. "Now you know about ___" is the easiest frame and works well for most first graders.

**If the student mixes facts and opinions,** that is a teaching opportunity, not a failure. Say: "This sentence tells me what you THINK. In informative writing, we stick to what is TRUE. Can you change this to a fact?" This reinforces the fact-opinion distinction from Lesson 3.

**Save both pieces (original from Lesson 5 and revised from today).** The comparison between the two shows the student that revision makes writing better — a concept they will explore more deeply in Lesson 9.

## Answer Key

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Topic sentence, facts with details, closing sentence
2. Any detail added (e.g., "Cats have long, fluffy tails." or "Cats have tails that help them balance.")
3. It wraps up the writing / reminds the reader of the topic / says goodbye to the reader
