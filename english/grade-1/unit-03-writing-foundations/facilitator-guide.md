# Facilitator Guide — Grade 1 ELA, Unit 3: Writing Foundations

**Grade:** 1
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 3 — Writing Foundations
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)

---

## Unit Overview

In Units 1 and 2, your student learned to decode words and comprehend texts — skills that belong to the reading side of literacy. Now we cross to the other side: writing. This unit teaches the student that writing is a powerful tool for sharing what they think, what they know, and what has happened to them. They will learn three distinct types of writing that form the backbone of all written communication: opinion, informative, and narrative.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Explain the purpose of opinion, informative, and narrative writing
- Write complete sentences with a subject, verb, capital letter, and end punctuation
- Write a short opinion piece that states an opinion, gives a reason, and ends with a closing sentence
- Write a short informative text that introduces a topic, provides facts, and ends with a closing sentence
- Write a short narrative that tells a story with a beginning, middle, and end using sequence words
- Re-read their own writing and make simple revisions (adding details, fixing capitals and periods)

**Why this matters:** Writing is thinking made visible. When a student writes, they must organize their ideas, choose words carefully, and put thoughts in a logical order. These are the exact skills that build strong thinkers — not just strong writers. At this stage, the goal is NOT perfect spelling or flawless grammar. The goal is for the student to understand that they have ideas worth sharing and that writing is how those ideas reach other people. Every child who believes "I am a writer" will write more, and the more they write, the better they get.

**A note on invented spelling:** Grade 1 students are still learning to spell. Throughout this unit, accept invented spelling (writing words the way they sound) as long as the writing is readable. A student who writes "becuz I lik dogs" is demonstrating solid phonics application AND clear opinion writing. Correcting every spelling error at this stage kills motivation. Focus on the writing skills — the spelling will improve with continued reading and phonics work.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Read Grade 1 text with reasonable accuracy (Unit 1 phonics skills)
- [ ] Answer who, what, where, when, why, and how questions about a text (Unit 2 comprehension)
- [ ] Identify key details and main topic in a short text (Unit 2 comprehension)
- [ ] Hold a pencil with a functional grip and write all 26 letters legibly (Kindergarten handwriting)
- [ ] Write their first name and several high-frequency words (the, is, and, a, I, my, like)
- [ ] Speak in complete sentences when talking about ideas

If the student cannot write letters legibly, spend a few sessions on handwriting practice before beginning. If the student is very reluctant to write, start with dictation — they say the words and you write them down, then gradually shift more writing responsibility to the student.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Pencils and erasers** — Every lesson involves writing. Have sharpened pencils ready.
- **Lined paper** (wide-ruled or primary-ruled with a midline) — The student will write on paper in every lesson. Prepare at least 30 sheets.
- **Crayons or colored pencils** — For illustrations that accompany writing.
- **Index cards or sentence strips** — For sentence-building activities and vocabulary words.
- **A writing folder or binder** — The student will save all writing pieces across the unit. This becomes their "Writing Portfolio."
- **Chart paper or a large whiteboard** — For modeling writing during direct instruction. A plain sheet of paper works if you do not have chart paper.
- **Anchor charts** (created during lessons):
  - Three types of writing (Lesson 1)
  - Complete sentence checklist (Lesson 2)
  - Opinion writing frame (Lessons 3-4)
  - Informative writing frame (Lessons 5-6)
  - Narrative writing frame (Lessons 7-8)
  - Revision checklist (Lesson 9)

No workbooks or special writing programs are needed. Everything is built into the lessons.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | What Is Writing For? (Three writing types) | 1 session |
| 2 | Writing Complete Sentences | 1-2 sessions |
| 3 | Opinion Writing — Introduction | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Opinion Writing — Adding Reasons and a Closing | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Informative Writing — Introduction | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Informative Writing — Adding Details and a Closing | 1-2 sessions |
| 7 | Narrative Writing — Introduction | 1-2 sessions |
| 8 | Narrative Writing — Middle and End | 1-2 sessions |
| 9 | Revision Basics | 1 session |
| 10 | Writing Celebration | 1 session |
| -- | Unit Assessment | 1 separate session (30-40 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 1 is an overview. Move through it efficiently so the student gets to actual writing quickly.
- Lesson 2 (complete sentences) is foundational for everything that follows. If the student is not yet writing complete sentences with capitals and periods, take two sessions here.
- Lessons 3-4 (opinion writing) and 5-6 (informative writing) each form a two-lesson sequence. If the student needs more time, add a practice session between them where they write another piece of the same type.
- Lessons 7-8 (narrative writing) are often the most engaging for students. Allow extra time if the student wants to develop their story.
- Lesson 9 (revision) may feel tedious to a first grader. Keep it upbeat and brief. The goal is to introduce the concept, not achieve perfection.
- Lesson 10 (celebration) is motivational and important. Do not skip it.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student draws a picture but writes nothing | The student may not yet see themselves as a writer, or the physical act of writing feels overwhelming | Say: "Your picture tells a great story! Now let's write one sentence about it." Start with dictation if needed — the student says the sentence, you write it, then the student copies it. Gradually release responsibility. |
| Student writes a string of words without spaces | The student has not yet internalized word boundaries in writing (even if they read words separately) | Teach "finger spacing." After each word, the student places a finger on the page to mark the space before writing the next word. Practice daily until habitual. |
| Student writes only one sentence when asked for a full piece | The student may be unsure what else to say, or fatigued by the physical effort of writing | Encourage oral rehearsal first: "Tell me your whole piece out loud." Then: "Great — you said three things! Now write down what you just told me." This separates the thinking from the writing. |
| Student confuses opinion and informative writing | The distinction between "what I think" and "what is true" is subtle for age 6-7 | Use concrete anchors: "An opinion is something not everyone agrees with. A fact is something you can check and prove." Practice sorting statements: "Pizza is the best food" (opinion) vs. "Pizza has cheese on it" (fact). |
| Student writes a narrative with no clear sequence | The student may be thinking of events simultaneously rather than in order | Use physical sequencing first. Give the student three pictures and ask them to put the pictures in order. Then: "Now write what happens first, what happens next, and what happens last." |
| Student resists revision — "It's already done!" | Revision is a new concept and may feel like criticism of their work | Frame revision positively: "Authors always go back and make their writing even better. Let's see if we can add one cool detail." Start with adding, not fixing — adding a word or sentence feels creative, while correcting errors feels punitive. |
| Student's spelling makes the writing unreadable | The student is applying phonics but inconsistently, or the words are too advanced for their spelling level | Do NOT correct every word. Ask: "Can you read this back to me?" If they can read it, the message is there. If neither of you can read a word, help them stretch the sounds: "What sounds do you hear in that word? Write those sounds down." |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to build a writing mindset:

- "If you could write a letter to anyone in the world, who would it be? What would you say?"
- "What is your favorite food? Can you tell me why you like it? That is an opinion — you could write about that!"
- "What do you know a lot about? You could teach someone about that by writing it down."
- "Tell me about something funny that happened to you. That is a story — a narrative!"
- "I notice the cereal box has writing on it. What kind of writing is that — opinion, informative, or narrative?"
- "If you could write a book, what would it be about?"
- "What is one thing you learned today? You could write an informative sentence about it."

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- The student consistently cannot produce any written output despite oral ability to express ideas clearly (possible fine motor difficulty or written expression disorder)
- The student's writing is completely illegible even when copying letters, after 3-4 weeks of practice (possible handwriting or motor planning concern)
- The student shows extreme frustration, anxiety, or complete avoidance of all writing tasks despite encouragement, scaffolding, and dictation support
- The student cannot generate any ideas for writing even with prompts, topic choices, and oral rehearsal support (possible language formulation difficulty)
- The student can write individual words but cannot combine them into a sentence, even orally, after repeated modeling and practice

If you see these signs, consider:
- Consulting with an occupational therapist for fine motor and handwriting concerns
- Exploring speech-language evaluation for students who struggle to formulate sentences
- Providing assistive technology (speech-to-text tools) as a bridge while building writing skills
- Checking whether the student may need vision screening (if they have difficulty tracking lines on paper or copying from a model)

Remember: Grade 1 writing is messy, inventive, and imperfect. That is exactly how it should be. The signs above describe persistent struggles that go beyond normal developmental variation.

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 4** (planned), which will build on these writing foundations with more extended writing, vocabulary development, and grammar skills. The three writing types introduced here — opinion, informative, and narrative — will be revisited with greater complexity: longer pieces, more detailed reasons and facts, and stronger organization.

Between units, keep the writing habit alive. Encourage the student to write grocery lists, birthday cards, journal entries, signs for their room, or stories for fun. The single most important thing for a developing writer is volume — the more they write, the better they get. Celebrate every piece of writing, no matter how short or imperfectly spelled.
