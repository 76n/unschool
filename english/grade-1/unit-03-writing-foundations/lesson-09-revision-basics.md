# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 3, Lesson 9

## Topic
Revision Basics (Re-reading Your Writing, Adding Details, Checking for Capitals and Periods)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Re-read their own writing to check if it makes sense
- Add at least one detail (word, phrase, or sentence) to improve a piece of writing
- Check for and fix missing capital letters and end punctuation
- Use a simple revision checklist independently

## Prerequisites
- Experience writing opinion, informative, and narrative pieces (Lessons 3-8)
- Ability to write complete sentences with capitals and end punctuation (Lesson 2)
- A writing folder with at least 3 pieces of writing from the unit

## Materials Needed
- The student's writing folder with all pieces from this unit
- Pencil and eraser
- A different color pencil or pen for revision marks (e.g., blue or green — not red, which can feel punitive)
- Chart paper or whiteboard
- Lined paper for rewriting (if needed)
- Crayons (optional, for adding illustrations to revised pieces)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept of revision by connecting it to something familiar.

**Activity: The Second Try**
Say: "Have you ever built something with blocks and then made it even better? Or drawn a picture, looked at it, and added more details? That is what we are going to do today — but with WRITING."

Read this short piece aloud (write it on the board):

> "I went to the park. It was fun. The end."

Ask: "Is this a story? Sort of. But does it feel finished? Does it tell you very much?"

Listen to the student's thoughts. Then say: "What if we made it better? What could we add?"

Together, brainstorm improvements:
- WHO went? Add a name. "My friend Tia and I went to the park."
- WHAT was fun? Add details. "We played on the swings and went down the big slide."
- HOW did it end? Add a real ending. "We did not want to go home because we were having so much fun."

Write the improved version:
> "My friend Tia and I went to the park. We played on the swings and went down the big slide. We did not want to go home because we were having so much fun!"

"See the difference? Same story, but now the reader can picture it. That is what revision does — it makes your writing BETTER. Today, you are going to revise YOUR writing."

*Facilitator note: The warm-up deliberately uses a "bad" example that is not the student's own work. This prevents the student from feeling criticized and shows that ALL writing gets better with revision.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach a simple revision process and introduce the revision checklist.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Revision:** Going back to your writing and making it better. Adding details, fixing mistakes, making it clearer.
- **Re-read:** Reading your own writing again to see if it makes sense.
- **Detail:** A word or sentence that gives the reader more information (a color, size, number, feeling, or description).
- **Edit:** Fixing the small things — capital letters, end punctuation, and spelling of common words.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain what revision is (and what it is NOT).**
"Revision is what ALL real authors do. They write something, then they go back and make it even better. It does not mean your writing was bad — it means you are making it GREAT."

"Revision is NOT about fixing every spelling mistake. It is about making your ideas clearer and more interesting."

**Step 2: Introduce the Revision Checklist.**
Create an anchor chart:

**My Revision Checklist**

| Check | Question | What to Fix |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Re-read | Does my writing make sense? Can I read it out loud? | If something sounds confusing, change it or add words. |
| 2. Details | Can I add a detail to make it more interesting? | Add a color, size, feeling, or description. |
| 3. Missing parts | Does my writing have all its parts? | Opinion: opinion + reason + closing. Informative: topic + facts + closing. Narrative: beginning + middle + end. |
| 4. Capitals | Does every sentence start with a capital letter? | Fix any lowercase sentence starters. |
| 5. End marks | Does every sentence end with . or ? or ! ? | Add missing periods, question marks, or exclamation points. |

"These are the five things you check every time you revise. Let's practice."

**Step 3: Model revising a piece of writing.**
Write this opinion piece on chart paper (intentionally imperfect):

> "i think summer is the best. it is warm you can swim"

"Let me use my checklist."

*Check 1 — Re-read:* Read it aloud. "Hmm, it kind of makes sense, but 'it is warm you can swim' sounds like two ideas stuck together."

*Check 2 — Details:* "Can I add a detail? What kind of swimming? Where?" Revise: "You can swim in the pool."

*Check 3 — Missing parts:* "Is this an opinion piece? Let me check — opinion, reason, closing. I have an opinion ('summer is the best'). I have a reason ('it is warm' and 'you can swim'). But I don't have a closing!" Add: "That is why summer is the best season."

*Check 4 — Capitals:* "Does every sentence start with a capital? No! 'i' and 'it' need capitals." Fix: "I" and "It."

*Check 5 — End marks:* "Every sentence needs a period." Add periods.

Revised version:
> "I think summer is the best. It is warm. You can swim in the pool. That is why summer is the best season."

"Look at the difference! Five checks, and the piece is much better."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Revising an informative piece.**

Original: "cats are pets they have fur"

Revision process:
- Re-read: Two ideas need to be separate sentences.
- Details: What kind of fur? Add "soft."
- Missing parts: No topic sentence or closing. Add both.
- Capitals: "cats" needs a capital C.
- End marks: Add periods.

Revised: "Cats are popular pets. They have soft fur. Cats also purr when they are happy. Now you know about cats!"

**Example 2: Revising a narrative.**

Original: "I went to the store. I got candy"

Revision process:
- Re-read: Makes sense but very short.
- Details: What store? What kind of candy? Add specifics.
- Missing parts: No real middle or end. Expand.
- Capitals: Both are correct.
- End marks: Second sentence needs a period.

Revised: "Last week, I went to the grocery store with my mom. First, we got apples and bread. Then I saw the candy aisle! My mom let me pick one candy bar. I chose chocolate. I was so happy!"

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Walk through the revision checklist with one of the student's own pieces.

**Activity: Revise Together**
Have the student pick one piece from their writing folder. It can be any type — opinion, informative, or narrative. The student should pick a piece they are proud of but feel they could improve.

Walk through each checklist item together:

**Check 1: Re-read.**
"Read your piece out loud to me. Does everything make sense? Is there any part that sounds funny or confusing?"

If something sounds off, help the student rephrase it.

**Check 2: Details.**
"Let's look for a place to add a detail. Is there a sentence that could use a color, a size, a feeling, or a description?"

Help the student choose one sentence and add a detail. They can write the new word(s) above the line with a caret (^) mark, or rewrite the sentence on a new line.

**Check 3: Missing parts.**
"What type of writing is this? Let's check that it has all its parts." Count the parts together based on the type.

If a part is missing (most often the closing), add it together.

**Check 4: Capitals.**
"Let's check every sentence. Does it start with a capital letter?"

The student points to the first letter of each sentence. If any are lowercase, they fix it with their colored pencil.

**Check 5: End marks.**
"Does every sentence end with a mark?"

The student points to the end of each sentence. If any are missing punctuation, they add it.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student gets discouraged by the number of things to fix, say: "This is great! Finding things to improve means you are learning. Real authors find things to fix every time they look at their work."*

*Facilitator prompt: Limit revision to 2-3 changes per piece. The goal is to learn the process, not to achieve perfection.*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently revises a second piece from their folder.

**Activity: My Revision**
The student picks a DIFFERENT piece from their writing folder and revises it independently using the checklist.

Post the revision checklist where the student can see it. They go through each step on their own.

The student should:
1. Re-read the piece aloud (to themselves or to the facilitator)
2. Add at least one detail
3. Check that all parts are there
4. Fix any missing capitals
5. Fix any missing end marks

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Revise one piece using the checklist. Make at least 2 improvements (any combination of adding a detail, fixing a capital, fixing an end mark, or adding a missing part).

**Stretch:**
Revise one piece using the checklist. Make at least 3 improvements. Then rewrite the entire piece neatly on a clean sheet of paper with all corrections included.

**Challenge:**
Revise TWO pieces from the folder. For each piece, make at least 3 improvements. Rewrite both pieces neatly. Then compare: "Which piece is your BEST writing? Why?"

### Interleaved Review
- "What type of writing is this piece? What parts should it have?" (Lessons 1, 3-8 review — identifying writing type and structure)
- "Read your revised piece aloud. Does it make sense from beginning to end?" (Unit 2 comprehension — monitoring understanding)
- "Count your sentences. How many did you write?" (Lesson 2 review — sentence awareness)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can use the revision checklist to improve writing.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What does revision mean?" (Answer: Going back to your writing and making it better / adding details and fixing mistakes)
2. "Name two things on the revision checklist." (Answer: Any two from: re-read, add details, check for all parts, check capitals, check end marks)
3. "Look at this sentence: 'my dog is nice' — What two things need to be fixed?" (Answer: Capital M for "My" and add a period at the end)
4. "How could you add a detail to make this sentence more interesting: 'I have a cat.'" (Answer: Any detail added, e.g., "I have a fluffy gray cat." or "I have a cat named Whiskers.")

**Mastery = Student answers at least 3 of 4 correctly AND made at least 2 meaningful revisions to their own writing during the lesson.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on the simplest revision skill first: checking capitals and periods. Have the student practice just those two checks on 5 sentences you write (some correct, some missing capitals/periods).
- For adding details, practice orally: you say a plain sentence, the student adds a detail.
- Keep the revision checklist posted for every future writing session.
- Revisit revision informally before moving to Lesson 10 — the celebration lesson does not depend on revision mastery.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 10 (Writing Celebration). The student should use the remaining time to decide which piece from their folder they are most proud of — that will be their "published" piece for Lesson 10.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who revise their work confidently:**
- Revise ALL pieces in their writing folder — create a "best version" of each.
- Read a piece to a family member and ask: "What part do you want to know more about?" Use their feedback to add another detail.
- Practice being a "revision partner" — the facilitator writes a short piece with 3 intentional errors, and the student uses the checklist to find and fix them.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Revision is an advanced concept for Grade 1.** Most first graders believe that once they have written something, it is done. The purpose of this lesson is NOT to produce perfect writing. It is to introduce the HABIT of going back and looking at your own writing with fresh eyes. Even if the student makes only one small improvement, they have begun to think like a writer.

**Frame revision as making writing STRONGER, not fixing what is WRONG.** Language matters. Say: "Let's make this even better!" not "Let's fix your mistakes." The emotional tone of this lesson determines whether the student will embrace or resist revision going forward.

**The revision checklist is intentionally simple.** Five items is the right number for a first grader. Professional writers have more elaborate revision processes, but this checklist covers the essentials and is memorable. The student should be able to recite the five checks after this lesson.

**Do not expect the student to catch every error.** If they find and fix 2-3 things, that is an excellent outcome. Over time, the revision habit will sharpen and they will catch more. For now, the PROCESS matters more than the PRODUCT.

**Common resistance:** "But it is already good!" Validate this: "You are right — it IS good. And we are going to make it even better. Even your favorite author goes back and adds things to their stories." Use the warm-up example as proof.

**Using a different-colored pencil** for revisions makes the changes visible and gives the student a concrete sense of what they improved. It also makes the revision process feel special — like a real editor's work.

**This is the second-to-last lesson.** After this, the student moves to the Writing Celebration. Encourage them to pick their favorite piece tonight so they are ready and excited for next session.

## Answer Key

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Going back to your writing and making it better / adding details and fixing mistakes
2. Any two: re-read, add details, check for all parts, check capitals, check end marks
3. Capital M for "My" and add a period at the end
4. Any detail added (e.g., "I have a fluffy gray cat." or "I have a cat named Whiskers.")
