# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 3, Lesson 7

## Topic
Narrative Writing — Introduction (Telling a Story with a Beginning)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain that narrative writing tells a story about something that happened
- Identify the "beginning" of a story (who is in the story and what is happening at the start)
- Write a story beginning with at least two sentences that introduce a character and a situation

## Prerequisites
- Ability to distinguish between opinion, informative, and narrative writing (Lesson 1)
- Ability to write complete sentences with capitals and end punctuation (Lesson 2)
- Experience writing opinion and informative pieces (Lessons 3-6)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or whiteboard
- The "Three Types of Writing" anchor chart from Lesson 1
- Pencil and lined paper
- Crayons for illustrating
- 3 index cards labeled "Beginning," "Middle," "End"
- A familiar picture book or short story (optional — if available, choose a story with a clear beginning, middle, and end such as a Frog and Toad story or an Elephant & Piggie book)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate storytelling skills by having the student retell something that happened to them.

**Activity: Tell Me About Your Day**
Say: "Something happened to you recently — maybe yesterday, maybe this morning. Tell me about it. What happened?"

Listen as the student tells a short story about something real. As they talk, listen for:
- Who was involved
- What happened
- When and where it happened

After they finish, say: "You just told me a narrative! A narrative is a story about something that happened. It can be a true story — like yours — or a made-up story. Today we are going to learn how to WRITE stories like that."

If the student has trouble thinking of something, offer prompts:
- "What did you do this morning before our lesson?"
- "Did anything funny or surprising happen recently?"
- "Tell me about the last time you played outside."

*Facilitator note: Oral storytelling is the bridge to written storytelling. A student who can tell a story can learn to write one.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the student that stories have three parts and focus on writing a strong beginning.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Narrative:** A story about something that happened — real or made up.
- **Character:** The person or animal the story is about. (Who is in the story?)
- **Beginning:** The start of the story — it tells WHO and WHAT IS HAPPENING.
- **Middle:** The exciting or important part — what happens next, or the problem.
- **End:** How the story finishes — what happens last or how the problem is solved.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the three parts of a story.**
Place the three index cards ("Beginning," "Middle," "End") in a row.

"Every story has three parts — just like a sandwich has a top bun, a filling, and a bottom bun."

| Beginning | Middle | End |
|---|---|---|
| WHO is in the story? | WHAT happens? | HOW does it end? |
| WHAT is happening at the start? | Is there a problem or something exciting? | Is the problem solved? What happens last? |

"Today we are going to focus on writing a GREAT beginning. The middle and end come in the next lesson."

**Step 2: Teach what goes in a beginning.**
Write on chart paper:

**A good beginning tells the reader:**
1. WHO is in the story (the character)
2. WHAT is happening at the start (the situation)

"A beginning introduces the character and gets the story started. Let me show you."

**Step 3: Model writing a story beginning.**
Think aloud and write on chart paper:

"I want to write a story about a boy who finds something. Let me start my beginning."

> "Sam was walking to school on a sunny morning. He saw something shiny on the sidewalk."

Point to the parts:
- "WHO is in my story? Sam." (Underline "Sam.")
- "WHAT is happening? He was walking to school and saw something shiny."
- "Now the reader wants to know MORE. What is the shiny thing? That is what makes someone want to keep reading."

"A good beginning makes the reader say: 'What happens next?' That is the sign of a strong start."

**Step 4: Show a beginning that needs improvement.**
Write: "I went to a place."

"Is this a good beginning? Let's check. Who is in the story? 'I' — but we do not know much about what is going on. Where did I go? What was happening? Let's make it better."

Rewrite: "One Saturday, I went to my grandma's house for a big family dinner."

"Now we know WHO (I), WHEN (Saturday), WHERE (grandma's house), and WHAT (family dinner). Much better!"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: A made-up story.**
> Lily loved to climb trees more than anything. One day, she found the tallest tree in the park.

- Character: Lily
- Situation: She loves climbing and found a very tall tree
- The reader wonders: Will she climb it? What will happen?

**Example 2: A true story (personal narrative).**
> Last summer, my family went to the beach. I had never seen the ocean before.

- Character: "I" (the writer) and family
- Situation: Going to the beach for the first time
- The reader wonders: What was the ocean like? What happened at the beach?

**Example 3: An animal story.**
> A little frog named Pip lived by a big blue pond. One morning, Pip woke up and the pond was gone!

- Character: Pip the frog
- Situation: The pond has disappeared
- The reader wonders: Where did the pond go? What will Pip do?

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Co-write a story beginning with the student.

**Activity: Our Story Beginning**
"Let's write a story beginning together. First, we need to decide two things."

**Step 1: Choose a character.**
"Who will our story be about? It can be you, or a made-up character, or an animal."

Let the student decide. Write the character's name on a sticky note.

Prompt if needed: "How about a kid your age? What is their name? Or maybe an animal — a dog, a cat, a bunny?"

**Step 2: Choose a situation.**
"Now, what is happening at the start of the story? Where is our character? What are they doing?"

Offer choices if the student is stuck:
- "Maybe [character] is going somewhere special."
- "Maybe [character] finds something surprising."
- "Maybe [character] wakes up and something is different."
- "Maybe [character] is getting ready for something exciting."

**Step 3: Write the beginning together.**
The student says the sentences aloud first. Help them shape the sentences, then the student writes them on paper. Aim for 2-3 sentences.

Example (if the student chose a dog named Max):
> "Max was a little brown dog who lived on Maple Street. One day, Max saw the gate open. He ran outside to explore!"

Read it aloud together. Ask: "Does the reader know WHO is in the story? Does the reader know WHAT is happening? Does the reader want to know WHAT HAPPENS NEXT? If yes — this is a great beginning!"

*Facilitator prompt: If the student writes a very long beginning with many events, say: "This is great storytelling! But some of these things sound like the MIDDLE of the story. Let's save the exciting part for next time. A beginning just sets things up."*

*Facilitator prompt: If the student cannot think of a situation, use the story from their warm-up. "Remember the story you told me at the beginning? Let's write that down."*

---

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes their own story beginning independently.

**Activity: My Story Starts Here**
The student writes a story beginning (just the beginning — not the whole story). It should have at least 2 sentences that tell WHO is in the story and WHAT is happening.

The student draws a picture of the beginning of their story.

Story starters to choose from (or the student creates their own):
- "One morning, [name] woke up and found a ___."
- "It was a rainy day, and [name] was bored until ___."
- "[Name] was [the student's age] years old. One day, something amazing happened."
- "Deep in the forest, a little [animal] was looking for ___."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level (2 sentences):**
Write 2 sentences that introduce a character and a situation. Use a story starter from the list above.

Example:
> One morning, Emma woke up and found a tiny kitten on her porch. The kitten was orange with big green eyes.

**Stretch (3 sentences):**
Write 3 sentences that introduce a character, a setting (where and when), and a situation. Do not use the provided story starters — come up with the beginning independently.

Example:
> Ben loved playing soccer at the park every Saturday. One Saturday, he kicked the ball so hard it flew over the fence. He could hear it bouncing on the other side.

**Challenge (4 sentences):**
Write 4 sentences that include a character, a setting, a situation, and a hint about what might happen next. Include at least one detail about how the character feels.

Example:
> Mia and her brother Jake walked to the old library after school. The library was very quiet and kind of dusty. Mia noticed a door she had never seen before. She felt curious and a little bit scared.

### Interleaved Review
- "Is your story beginning an opinion, informative, or narrative?" (Lesson 1 review — it is narrative!)
- "Do the four-finger check on each sentence." (Lesson 2 review — complete sentences)
- "Who is the character in your story? That is like asking a WHO question about a book." (Unit 2 comprehension review)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can write a story beginning that introduces a character and situation.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What does narrative writing do?" (Answer: It tells a story about something that happened)
2. "What are the three parts of a story?" (Answer: Beginning, middle, end)
3. "What does the beginning of a story tell the reader?" (Answer: Who is in the story and what is happening at the start)
4. Read the student's story beginning aloud: "Can you tell me who is in your story and what is happening?" (Answer: Student identifies their character and situation)

**Mastery = Student answers at least 3 of 4 correctly AND their written beginning includes a character and a situation.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model story beginnings. Point to the character and situation in each one.
- Have the student tell a story orally first, then write just the first part.
- Use the story starter frames as scaffolding — the student fills in the blanks rather than generating the beginning from scratch.
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 8.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 8 (Narrative Writing — Middle and End). Save the student's story beginning in their writing folder — they will add the middle and end in Lesson 8.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who write story beginnings easily:**
- Write TWO different story beginnings and let a family member pick which one they want to hear the rest of.
- Write a story beginning where the character is an animal in a surprising place (e.g., a penguin in a grocery store, a bear at school).
- Look at the beginning of a picture book together. Identify: Who is the character? What is happening at the start?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This is where many students come alive.** Narrative writing is often the most engaging type for first graders because they love stories. They have been listening to stories their whole lives, and now they get to CREATE one. Capitalize on this energy.

**The most common issue** is that students try to write the entire story at once, ending up with a long, rambling piece that does not have clear parts. This is why we deliberately limit today to the BEGINNING only. Resist the urge to let the student finish the whole story today — the pacing matters. By writing just the beginning, they build anticipation for the next lesson and practice the skill of crafting a strong opening.

**If the student writes in first person ("I went to the park"),** that is a personal narrative and it is perfectly valid. If they write in third person ("Max went to the park"), that is also valid. Do not require one or the other. Both are narrative writing.

**The story does not need to be "exciting" in an adult sense.** A story about walking to the store, finding a rock, or eating breakfast is fine. What matters is that the student identifies a character, establishes a situation, and writes it in complete sentences. The dramatic arc will develop over time.

**Keep the student's beginning for Lesson 8.** They will literally continue the same story by adding the middle and end. Make sure the paper goes into the writing folder.

**Connection to Unit 2 comprehension:** The story elements here (character, events, sequence) mirror what the student learned to identify as a READER in Unit 2. Now they are using those same elements as a WRITER. Point this out: "Remember when we asked 'Who is this story about?' when we read? Now YOU are deciding who your story is about."

## Answer Key

**Exit Ticket:**
1. It tells a story about something that happened
2. Beginning, middle, end
3. Who is in the story and what is happening at the start
4. Student identifies the character and situation from their own writing (answers will vary)
