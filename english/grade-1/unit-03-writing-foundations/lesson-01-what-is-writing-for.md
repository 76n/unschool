# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
What Is Writing For? (Introduction to Three Writing Types)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Name three purposes of writing: to share an opinion, to teach or inform, and to tell a story
- Sort simple example sentences into three categories: opinion, informative, and narrative
- Explain in their own words what each type of writing does

## Prerequisites
- Ability to read simple sentences (Unit 1 phonics skills)
- Understanding of key details and main topic (Unit 2 comprehension)
- Ability to hold a pencil and write letters legibly (Kindergarten handwriting)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or large whiteboard
- 3 colored markers or crayons (one color per writing type — e.g., red for opinion, blue for informative, green for narrative)
- 9 sentence strips or index cards with pre-written example sentences (see Direct Instruction)
- Pencil and paper for the student
- Crayons for drawing

**Sentence cards to prepare before the lesson:**
1. "I think chocolate ice cream is the best." (opinion)
2. "Cats have four legs and a tail." (informative)
3. "One day, a little bird fell out of its nest." (narrative)
4. "I like summer more than winter." (opinion)
5. "Fish live in water." (informative)
6. "First, I woke up. Then, I ate breakfast." (narrative)
7. "Dogs are the best pets." (opinion)
8. "The sun is a star." (informative)
9. "Last week, my mom took me to the store." (narrative)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about writing by connecting to the student's everyday experience.

**Activity: Where Do We See Writing?**
Say to the student: "Writing is everywhere! Let's think of all the places we see writing."

Prompt the student to name places where they see writing. Accept all answers. If they need help, offer these:
- Books
- Signs (stop signs, store signs)
- Cereal boxes
- Text messages
- Grocery lists
- Birthday cards
- Instructions (recipes, directions)

Write each answer on the board or paper as they say it.

Then ask: "Why do people write all these things? What are they trying to do?"

Listen for answers like: tell someone something, share an idea, remember something, tell a story. Accept all reasonable answers.

"Today we are going to learn about three special reasons people write. By the end of today, you will be a writing detective — you will be able to look at any piece of writing and figure out what type it is!"

*Facilitator note: This warm-up builds motivation by showing that writing is practical and present in the student's world — not just a school activity.*

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the three types of writing that students will learn throughout this unit.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Opinion:** What you think or feel about something. Not everyone agrees with an opinion.
- **Informative:** Writing that teaches the reader something true — real facts.
- **Narrative:** Writing that tells a story about something that happened (real or made up).
- **Purpose:** The reason someone writes something — what they are trying to do.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the three types with a simple anchor chart.**
Draw three columns on chart paper. Label them with the student:

| Opinion Writing | Informative Writing | Narrative Writing |
|---|---|---|
| Tells what I THINK | Teaches something TRUE | Tells a STORY |

Use the colored markers — one color per column. This visual anchor will be used throughout the unit.

"There are three main types of writing. Let me tell you about each one."

**Type 1: Opinion Writing (red)**
"Opinion writing is when you tell someone what you THINK about something. You share what you like, what you believe, or what you feel is best. Not everyone will agree with your opinion, and that is okay!"

Write on the chart: "Key words: I think, I like, I believe, the best, my favorite"

**Type 2: Informative Writing (blue)**
"Informative writing is when you TEACH the reader something true. You share real facts — things you can check and prove. Like a little teacher!"

Write on the chart: "Key words: facts, true, teaches, real"

**Type 3: Narrative Writing (green)**
"Narrative writing is when you tell a STORY. It could be a true story about something that happened to you, or a made-up story. Narratives have things that happen in order — a beginning, a middle, and an end."

Write on the chart: "Key words: story, first, then, last, one day"

**Step 2: Read example sentences and sort them.**
Hold up each prepared sentence card. Read it aloud with the student. Ask: "Which type of writing is this?"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "I think chocolate ice cream is the best."
- "Hmm — does this teach me a fact? No, not everyone thinks chocolate is best. Does it tell a story? No, nothing happened. It tells me what someone THINKS. This is an opinion!"
- Place under the Opinion column.

**Example 2:** "Cats have four legs and a tail."
- "Is this what someone thinks? No — it is a true fact about cats. You can check it. Does it tell a story? No. It teaches us something true. This is informative writing!"
- Place under the Informative column.

**Example 3:** "One day, a little bird fell out of its nest."
- "Does this tell us someone's opinion? No. Does it teach a fact? Well, it tells us something that happened — there is a character (the bird) and an event (falling out of the nest). This is the beginning of a story. This is narrative writing!"
- Place under the Narrative column.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice identifying writing types with support, then try producing one sentence of each type.

**Activity 1: Sort the Remaining Cards (5 minutes)**
Give the student the remaining 6 sentence cards (cards 4-9). Read each one together.

For each card, ask:
- "Does this tell what someone thinks? Does it teach a fact? Does it tell a story?"
- Have the student place it in the correct column on the anchor chart.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student is unsure, ask: "Could everyone agree with this, or would some people disagree?" If people could disagree, it is likely an opinion. "Can you check if this is true?" If yes, it is informative. "Did something happen — is there a story?" If yes, it is narrative.*

**Sorting answers:**
- Card 4 ("I like summer more than winter") — Opinion
- Card 5 ("Fish live in water") — Informative
- Card 6 ("First, I woke up. Then, I ate breakfast.") — Narrative
- Card 7 ("Dogs are the best pets") — Opinion
- Card 8 ("The sun is a star") — Informative
- Card 9 ("Last week, my mom took me to the store") — Narrative

**Activity 2: Say One of Each (5 minutes)**
"Now it is YOUR turn. Can you say one sentence of each type? You do not need to write them yet — just tell me."

- "Tell me an opinion — something you think." (Example response: "I think pizza is yummy.")
- "Tell me a fact — something true you could teach someone." (Example response: "Dogs can run fast.")
- "Tell me the start of a story — something that happened." (Example response: "Yesterday I played outside.")

Praise each response. If the student's response fits a different category, gently redirect: "That is a great sentence! But is it telling me what you think, or is it teaching me a fact? Let's see..."

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student draws and writes to represent all three writing types.

**Activity: My Three-Panel Writing Page**
Fold a sheet of paper into three sections (or use three separate pieces). Label each section:
- Section 1: "My Opinion" (draw a heart or thumbs-up symbol)
- Section 2: "A Fact I Know" (draw a lightbulb symbol)
- Section 3: "A Little Story" (draw a star symbol)

The student draws a picture and writes at least one sentence in each section.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Write one sentence for each type. Invented spelling is expected and accepted.
- Opinion: "I think ___ is the best."
- Fact: "___ have/are ___."
- Story: "One day, I ___."

**Stretch:**
Write one sentence for each type WITHOUT using the sentence starters above. Come up with the sentence independently.

**Challenge:**
Write two sentences for each type. For the opinion, include a reason ("I think ___ because ___"). For the story, include what happened next.

### Interleaved Review
- "Read your opinion sentence back to me out loud." (Fluency review from Unit 1)
- "Point to the capital letter at the beginning of your sentence." (Handwriting/conventions review from Kindergarten)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can distinguish the three types of writing.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What are the three types of writing we learned today?" (Answer: opinion, informative, narrative)
2. The facilitator reads: "I think recess is the best part of school." "What type of writing is that?" (Answer: opinion)
3. The facilitator reads: "Frogs can jump very far." "What type of writing is that?" (Answer: informative)
4. The facilitator reads: "Last Saturday, we went to Grandma's house." "What type of writing is that?" (Answer: narrative)

**Mastery = Student names all three types AND correctly identifies at least 3 of 3 example sentences.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the anchor chart together. Point to the key words for each type.
- Use the student's own sentences from Independent Practice to re-sort: "You wrote this — is it an opinion, a fact, or a story?"
- Practice again with 3-4 more oral examples before the next lesson.
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 2.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 2 (Writing Complete Sentences). Keep the anchor chart posted for reference in every future lesson.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who grasp the three types quickly:**
- Go on a "writing type hunt": look through books, cereal boxes, signs, or other print materials in the house and identify whether each piece is opinion, informative, or narrative.
- Dictate a short letter to a family member. Decide together: is this letter an opinion, informative, or narrative? (Many real letters mix types — that is a great discussion!)
- Draw a picture of a "writing workshop" where three characters are each doing a different type of writing. Label each character with their writing type.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This lesson is foundational but should be light and energetic.** The student does not need to master all three types today — they will spend two full lessons on each type in the coming weeks. The purpose here is simply to plant three clear categories in the student's mind: writing to share what I think, writing to teach, and writing to tell a story.

**The most common confusion** is between opinion and informative. A student might say "Dogs are the best" is a fact because they genuinely believe it is true. This is developmentally normal. Gently explain: "You believe that, and it might be true for you, but not everyone agrees. Your friend might think cats are the best. When people can disagree, it is an opinion." Use lots of examples.

**The sorting activity is the key to this lesson.** Students who physically sort sentences into categories build stronger mental categories than students who just hear about them.

**Do not worry about** complete sentences, capitals, periods, or spelling today. Those come in Lesson 2. Today is about understanding the purpose of writing, not the mechanics. If the student writes a sentence fragment or misspells words, that is perfectly fine for today.

**Save the anchor chart.** You will reference it in every single lesson for the rest of this unit.
