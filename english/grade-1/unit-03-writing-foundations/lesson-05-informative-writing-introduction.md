# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Informative Writing — Introduction (Introducing a Topic and Stating Facts)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain that informative writing teaches the reader something true
- Write a topic sentence that introduces what the writing is about
- Write at least one fact sentence about the topic
- Distinguish between a fact and an opinion when writing about a topic

## Prerequisites
- Ability to distinguish between opinion, informative, and narrative writing (Lesson 1)
- Ability to write complete sentences with capitals and end punctuation (Lesson 2)
- Ability to write an opinion piece with an opinion, reason, and closing (Lessons 3-4)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or whiteboard
- The "Three Types of Writing" anchor chart from Lesson 1
- Pencil and lined paper
- Crayons for illustrating
- Index cards or sticky notes
- A simple picture of an animal the student is familiar with (e.g., a dog, cat, fish, or frog) — draw one if needed

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate the student's knowledge about a familiar topic to prepare for informative writing.

**Activity: What Do You Know About...?**
Choose a topic the student knows well. Good options: dogs, cats, the sun, rain, or their own school.

Say: "I want you to be my teacher for one minute. Tell me everything you know about [topic]. I am going to write down what you say."

As the student talks, jot down each fact they share on separate sticky notes or index cards. Aim for 3-6 facts.

For example, if the topic is dogs:
- "Dogs have four legs."
- "Dogs can bark."
- "Some dogs are big and some are small."
- "Dogs like to play fetch."
- "Dogs wag their tails when they are happy."

After the student finishes, say: "Wow — you just taught me all these things about dogs! This is exactly what informative writing does. You share what you KNOW so the reader can learn. Today, we are going to put these facts into real writing."

*Facilitator note: Writing the student's facts on cards validates their knowledge and creates material they can use later in the lesson.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the student how to write an informative topic sentence and fact sentences.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Informative writing:** Writing that teaches the reader true facts about a topic.
- **Topic:** What the writing is about — the main subject.
- **Topic sentence:** The first sentence that tells the reader what you are going to teach them about.
- **Fact:** Something that is true and can be checked or proven.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Review informative writing.**
Point to the Informative column on the anchor chart: "Remember — informative writing teaches something TRUE. It is different from opinion writing because it uses facts, not opinions."

Quick check: "Is this informative or opinion? 'Frogs live near water.'" (Informative — it is a fact.) "'Frogs are the coolest animals.'" (Opinion — not everyone agrees.)

**Step 2: Introduce the topic sentence.**
"Every piece of informative writing starts with a TOPIC SENTENCE. The topic sentence tells the reader what you are going to teach them about."

Write on chart paper:

**Topic sentence frame: "[Topic] is/are ___. OR There are many things to know about [topic]."**

Examples:
- "Dogs are interesting animals."
- "There are many things to know about the sun."
- "Fish are animals that live in water."

"The topic sentence introduces the topic. It does not give all the facts yet — it just tells the reader: 'Here is what I am going to teach you about.'"

**Step 3: Model writing informative sentences.**
"Watch me write an informative piece about frogs. I am going to start with a topic sentence, then add facts."

Think aloud and write on chart paper:

*Topic sentence:*
"Frogs are amazing animals."

"That tells the reader my topic. Now I need to add facts — true things about frogs."

*Fact 1:*
"Frogs live near ponds and lakes."

*Fact 2:*
"Frogs can jump very far."

Read the piece aloud:
> "Frogs are amazing animals. Frogs live near ponds and lakes. Frogs can jump very far."

"See? I introduced my topic and gave two facts. The reader just learned something!"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: About the sun.**
> The sun is a very important star. It gives us light during the day. It also keeps the Earth warm.

- Topic sentence: The sun is a very important star.
- Fact 1: It gives us light during the day.
- Fact 2: It also keeps the Earth warm.

**Example 2: About cats.**
> Cats are popular pets. Cats have soft fur and whiskers. They can see very well in the dark.

- Topic sentence: Cats are popular pets.
- Fact 1: Cats have soft fur and whiskers.
- Fact 2: They can see very well in the dark.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Co-write an informative piece using the facts from the warm-up.

**Activity: Write About Our Topic Together**
Return to the sticky notes from the warm-up. Spread them out.

**Step 1: Write the topic sentence together.**
"Let's write about [topic from warm-up]. First, we need a topic sentence. What could we say to introduce this topic?"

Help the student form a topic sentence. Write it on paper. Example: "Dogs are amazing animals."

**Step 2: Choose the best facts.**
Look at the sticky notes. Ask: "Which facts are the most interesting? Which ones would teach the reader the most?"

Help the student pick 2-3 facts. For each one, turn it into a complete sentence and write it down.

Example:
- "Dogs have four legs and a tail."
- "Dogs can bark to tell you things."
- "Some dogs are big and some dogs are small."

**Step 3: Read the piece aloud.**
Read the full piece together:
> "Dogs are amazing animals. Dogs have four legs and a tail. Dogs can bark to tell you things. Some dogs are big and some dogs are small."

"We just wrote an informative piece! Anyone who reads this will learn facts about dogs."

*Facilitator prompt: If a student's "fact" is actually an opinion (e.g., "Dogs are the best"), say: "That is a great opinion! But for informative writing, we need facts that everyone agrees with. What is something TRUE about dogs that we can check?"*

*Facilitator prompt: If the student repeats the same fact in different words, say: "You already told me about that. Can you think of something DIFFERENT about [topic]?"*

---

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes their own informative piece.

**Activity: My Informative Writing**
The student picks a topic they know a lot about and writes an informative piece with a topic sentence and at least two fact sentences.

Topics to choose from (or the student picks their own):
- An animal they like (not their favorite — just one they know about)
- A sport or game they play
- A food they know how to make or describe
- The weather outside today
- Their school or home

The student draws a picture to go with their writing.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level (3 sentences):**
Write one topic sentence and two fact sentences. Use the topic sentence frame from the anchor chart.

Example:
> Fish are animals that live in water. Fish have fins to help them swim. Some fish are very colorful.

**Stretch (4 sentences):**
Write one topic sentence and three fact sentences. At least one fact should include a specific detail (a number, a color, a size).

Example:
> Birds are animals that can fly. Birds have feathers and wings. Some birds are red and some are blue. Baby birds live in nests.

**Challenge (5 sentences):**
Write one topic sentence and four fact sentences. Include at least one comparison or contrast. No sentence starts the same way as another sentence.

Example:
> There are many things to know about rabbits. Rabbits have long ears and fluffy tails. They eat plants like carrots and lettuce. Wild rabbits live in holes called burrows. Pet rabbits live in cages or inside houses.

### Interleaved Review
- "Is your writing informative or opinion? How can you tell?" (Lesson 1 and 3 review — informative has facts, not opinions)
- "Do the four-finger check on each sentence." (Lesson 2 review — complete sentences)
- "Read your topic sentence. Does it tell the reader what your writing is about?" (Unit 2 main topic skill)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can write informative text with a topic sentence and facts.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What does informative writing do?" (Answer: It teaches the reader something true / shares facts)
2. "What does a topic sentence do?" (Answer: It tells the reader what the writing is about / introduces the topic)
3. "Listen to these two sentences. Which one is a fact for informative writing, and which one is an opinion? 'Spiders have eight legs.' 'Spiders are scary.'" (Answer: "Spiders have eight legs" is a fact; "Spiders are scary" is an opinion)
4. "Tell me a topic sentence about a topic you know about." (Answer: Any sentence that introduces a topic, e.g., "Cars are machines that take people places.")

**Mastery = Student answers at least 3 of 4 correctly, including producing a topic sentence (#4).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model informative piece about frogs. Point to the topic sentence and each fact. Have the student identify them.
- Practice generating facts about a very familiar topic (the student's own pet, their house, their family) — familiar topics make fact-generation easier.
- Provide the topic sentence frame on a card for reference.
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 6.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6 (Informative Writing — Adding Details and a Closing). Save the student's informative piece in their writing folder.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who write informative pieces easily:**
- Write a second informative piece about a DIFFERENT topic.
- Write 3 facts about themselves: "My name is ___. I am ___ years old. I have ___ hair." — this is an informative piece about ME!
- Find an informational text (a book, a cereal box, a sign) and identify the topic sentence and facts in it.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This lesson mirrors Lesson 3 (opinion introduction) but for informative writing.** The structure is deliberately parallel: introduce the type, model it, co-write one, then the student writes independently. This repetition of the lesson structure helps the student feel confident because the process is familiar even though the content type is new.

**The biggest challenge for first graders** with informative writing is distinguishing facts from opinions when writing about something they care about. A student writing about dogs might write "Dogs are the best" right alongside "Dogs have four legs." Gently redirect opinions without discouraging enthusiasm: "That sounds like an opinion — you really love dogs! For this piece, let's stick to facts. What is something TRUE about dogs that even someone who doesn't have a dog would agree with?"

**The warm-up is strategically important.** By having the student brainstorm facts orally BEFORE writing, you separate the cognitive demands: first generate ideas, then put them on paper. This dramatically reduces the "I don't know what to write" problem.

**Topics the student knows well produce the best writing.** If the student wants to write about something obscure that they do not actually know many facts about, gently steer toward a more familiar topic. Informative writing requires actual knowledge — unlike opinion writing, where the student just needs a preference.

**We intentionally do not add a closing sentence in this lesson.** That comes in Lesson 6. Keeping the lesson focused on topic sentences and facts prevents cognitive overload.

## Answer Key

**Exit Ticket:**
1. It teaches the reader something true / shares facts
2. It tells the reader what the writing is about / introduces the topic
3. "Spiders have eight legs" = fact; "Spiders are scary" = opinion
4. Any sentence that introduces a topic (e.g., "Cars are machines that take people places.")
