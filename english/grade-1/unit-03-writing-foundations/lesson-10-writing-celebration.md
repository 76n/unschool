# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 3, Lesson 10

## Topic
Writing Celebration (Choose Best Piece, Create Final Copy, Share)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Select their best piece of writing from the unit and explain why they chose it
- Create a clean final copy of their chosen piece with correct capitalization and end punctuation
- Read their writing aloud to an audience with expression and confidence
- Identify what type of writing they chose (opinion, informative, or narrative) and name its parts

## Prerequisites
- A writing folder with at least 3 pieces from the unit (opinion, informative, and narrative)
- Experience with the revision checklist (Lesson 9)
- Ability to read their own writing aloud

## Materials Needed
- The student's writing folder with all unit pieces
- "Special" paper for the final copy — use paper with a drawing box at the top and lines at the bottom, or any nicer paper that feels different from everyday writing paper
- Pencil and eraser
- Colored pencils or crayons for a final illustration
- The Revision Checklist from Lesson 9
- Optional: a small audience (family member, sibling, pet, or stuffed animal)
- Optional: a "certificate" or sticker to celebrate (see Extension)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build excitement and help the student reflect on their growth as a writer.

**Activity: My Writing Journey**
Spread out ALL of the student's writing from the unit on the table. Look at them together.

Say: "Look at all the writing you have done! Let's see what you have."

Point to each piece and name it together:
- "This is your three-panel writing from Lesson 1 — opinion, fact, and story."
- "This is your sentence page from Lesson 2."
- "Here are your opinion pieces from Lessons 3 and 4."
- "Here is your informative writing from Lessons 5 and 6."
- "Here is your narrative from Lessons 7 and 8."
- "And here are your revised pieces from Lesson 9."

"Wow. You wrote ALL of this. You wrote opinions, you wrote facts, you wrote stories. You are a WRITER."

Then ask: "Which piece are you most proud of? Which one do you want to share with someone else?"

Give the student a minute to look through their pieces and choose one. They do not need to decide instantly — they will confirm their choice during Guided Practice.

*Facilitator note: This moment of reflection is powerful. Seeing their collected work gives the student a tangible sense of accomplishment. Let them take their time looking through everything.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the student how to create a "published" final copy and prepare to share their writing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Final copy:** The clean, neat version of a piece of writing — like the version in a real book.
- **Publish:** To share your writing with others. Real authors publish books. Today, you publish your best piece!
- **Author:** A person who writes. Today, YOU are the author.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain what a final copy is.**
"When a real author finishes a book, they do not give the reader their messy first try. They make a clean, beautiful version. That is called a FINAL COPY or a PUBLISHED piece."

"Today, you are going to make a final copy of your best writing. This is the version you will share with your audience."

**Step 2: Model creating a final copy.**
Use a short example. Write a messy version on the board with cross-outs and additions:

> "I think ~~apels~~ apples are the best froot because they are sweet ^and crunchy^. That is why I lik apples."

"This is a good opinion piece, but it is messy. Let me make a final copy."

On a clean sheet of paper, write neatly:

> "I think apples are the best fruit because they are sweet and crunchy. That is why I like apples."

Point out the differences:
- Neat handwriting
- No cross-outs
- Correct spelling of common words (fix a few if you can — but remind the student that invented spelling is still okay for hard words)
- Capital letters and periods in place
- Space between words

"Then I would add a beautiful picture at the top to go with my writing."

**Step 3: Teach how to share writing with expression.**
"After you make your final copy, you are going to READ IT to someone. When authors read their work, they use their best reading voice."

Model reading the apple opinion piece aloud with expression — not in a flat voice, but with enthusiasm and feeling.

Then model reading it in a flat, boring voice. Ask: "Which sounded better?" (The first one!)

"Here are the tips for reading your writing aloud:"

Write on the board:
1. Hold your paper so you can see it clearly.
2. Read slowly — not too fast.
3. Use expression — let your voice show feeling.
4. Look up at your audience sometimes.
5. At the end, say the title and "by [your name]" — just like a real author!

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: A final copy of a narrative.**

Draft version:
> "one day my cat noked over a cup. milk went everywhere. i had to cleen it up. it was funny"

Final copy:
> "One day, my cat knocked over a cup. Milk went everywhere! I had to clean it up. It was so funny."

Changes: Capital letters added, period/exclamation point added, "noked" corrected to "knocked" (common word), detail added ("so funny" instead of just "funny").

**Example 2: A final copy of an informative piece.**

Draft version:
> "fish live in water. they have fins. some fish are red and blue. fish are cool animals"

Final copy:
> "Fish are interesting animals. Fish live in water. They have fins to help them swim. Some fish are red and blue. Fish are really cool animals!"

Changes: Capital letters, periods, topic sentence added, detail added ("to help them swim"), closing strengthened.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Support the student in selecting their piece, doing a final revision, and starting the final copy.

**Activity: Choose, Revise, Publish**

**Step 1: Confirm the choice (2 minutes).**
"Which piece did you choose as your best? Tell me why you chose it."

Listen to the student's reasoning. Valid reasons include: "I like the topic," "It is my longest one," "It has good details," "It is funny," "I worked hard on it." Any reason is fine.

If the student cannot choose, offer guidance: "Which one would you most want to read to [family member's name]?" This audience-awareness question often helps.

**Step 2: Final revision (4 minutes).**
"Before we make the final copy, let's do one more checklist check."

Go through the revision checklist quickly:
1. Re-read: Read it aloud. Does it make sense?
2. Details: Any last detail to add?
3. Parts: Are all the parts there?
4. Capitals: Every sentence starts with one?
5. End marks: Every sentence ends with one?

Make any final corrections on the draft.

**Step 3: Begin the final copy (4 minutes).**
Give the student the special paper. They begin writing their final copy. Guide them:
- "Write neatly — this is your best handwriting."
- "Remember to leave spaces between words."
- "Copy from your revised draft. Don't skip any words."

If the student cannot finish in this time, they will continue in Independent Practice.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student wants to change their piece significantly (add a whole new paragraph, change the topic), gently redirect: "Those are great ideas for your NEXT piece of writing. For today, let's publish what you have. It is already wonderful."*

*Facilitator prompt: Help with spelling of 3-5 common words if the student asks. For harder words, encourage the student to spell them the best they can — invented spelling is still acceptable in a Grade 1 final copy.*

---

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student completes the final copy, adds an illustration, and prepares to share.

**Activity: Finish and Illustrate**
The student completes their final copy and adds a detailed illustration at the top of the page.

Then the student practices reading their piece aloud 2-3 times.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Complete the final copy of the chosen piece. Add a drawing. Practice reading it aloud once.

**Stretch:**
Complete the final copy. Add a detailed, colored illustration. Practice reading aloud twice — once quietly and once with expression. Add a title and "by [name]" at the top.

**Challenge:**
Complete the final copy with a title and "by [name]." Add a detailed, colored illustration. On the back of the paper, write a note to the reader: "I wrote this because ___. I hope you like it because ___." Practice reading aloud three times, each time with more expression.

### Interleaved Review
- "What type of writing is your piece — opinion, informative, or narrative?" (Lesson 1 review)
- "Can you name the parts of your piece?" (Lessons 3-8 review — opinion: opinion + reason + closing; informative: topic + facts + closing; narrative: beginning + middle + end)
- "Did you use the revision checklist before you made the final copy?" (Lesson 9 review)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can identify their writing type and share their work.

This lesson's "check" is the sharing itself. Rather than a traditional exit ticket, the student presents their published piece.

**Sharing Protocol:**

1. The student holds up their paper and says: "This is my [opinion/informative/narrative] piece. It is called [title or topic]. I wrote it by [student's name]."

2. The student reads the piece aloud with expression.

3. The facilitator (and any other audience) responds:
   - "Thank you for sharing!"
   - "My favorite part was ___."
   - "I learned [or felt] ___ from your writing."

4. The facilitator asks: "What type of writing is this?" (Student answers: opinion, informative, or narrative.)

5. The facilitator asks: "What makes you proud of this piece?" (Student shares something they like about their own work.)

**Mastery = Student reads their piece aloud clearly, identifies the writing type correctly, and names at least one thing they are proud of.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
This is a celebration lesson — mastery issues should be rare. If the student cannot identify the writing type, gently review: "Does this tell what you think, teach facts, or tell a story?" If the student is too shy to share, offer alternatives: read it to a stuffed animal, record it as a voice memo, or have the facilitator read it while the student holds the paper.

### If Mastery Is Met
Celebrate! The student has completed Unit 3: Writing Foundations. They can write opinions, informative texts, and narratives. Display the published piece prominently — on the refrigerator, a bulletin board, or in a frame.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who want to do more:**
- Create a "book cover" for their piece: a front cover with the title, their name as author, and an illustration; a back cover with a one-sentence summary.
- Write a dedication: "This book is for ___." (Just like in a real book!)
- Make a class or family "library" — collect published pieces from all students or write another piece to add to the collection.
- Create a simple "About the Author" page: draw a self-portrait and write 2-3 sentences about themselves.
- Choose a SECOND piece to publish and share with a different audience.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This is the most important lesson for motivation.** Everything in this unit leads to this moment: the student sees themselves as a WRITER who creates real pieces that other people read and enjoy. The emotional payoff of sharing their work is what makes them want to keep writing. Do not skip this lesson or rush through it.

**Make it special.** Even small touches matter: using nicer paper, letting the student sit in a special chair for the reading, clapping after they share, displaying the piece in a place of honor. These signals communicate: "Your writing matters. YOU matter."

**The audience matters.** If possible, have at least one person other than the facilitator present for the sharing — a parent, sibling, grandparent, neighbor, or friend (even over video call). Writing exists to be read by others, and sharing with a real audience is the ultimate validation.

**If the student is shy about sharing,** do not force it. Offer alternatives:
- The facilitator reads the piece aloud while the student listens
- The student reads to a pet or stuffed animal
- The student records themselves reading and listens to the recording
- The piece is displayed without an oral reading

Any form of "publishing" counts. The goal is for the student to experience their writing reaching an audience.

**Do not over-correct the final copy.** If the student's final copy has some invented spelling or a missed capital, that is fine. A first grader's published piece is not expected to look like a professionally edited document. The important thing is that the student went through the process: writing, revising, and publishing.

**This lesson is lighter on new academic content** than other lessons in the unit. That is intentional. The purpose is celebration, reflection, and motivation. These are legitimate educational goals. A student who finishes this unit feeling proud and capable will carry that identity into future writing.

**After today, the student has completed three writing types.** Their writing folder should contain work from across the unit. Save this folder — it is evidence of growth and can be referenced in parent conferences, portfolios, or future units.

## Answer Key

There is no traditional answer key for this lesson. The "answers" are the student's own published writing, their ability to identify the writing type, and their confidence in sharing their work.

**Sharing Protocol Expected Responses:**
1. Student correctly identifies writing type (opinion, informative, or narrative)
2. Student reads aloud clearly enough for the audience to understand
3. Student names at least one thing they are proud of (any authentic response)
