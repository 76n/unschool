# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 3, Lesson 8

## Topic
Narrative Writing — Middle and End (Sequencing Events with "First, Then, Last")

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Add a middle and an end to a story that already has a beginning
- Use sequence words (first, then, next, after that, last, finally) to order events
- Write a complete narrative with a beginning, middle, and end

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a story beginning that introduces a character and situation (Lesson 7)
- Ability to write complete sentences with capitals and end punctuation (Lesson 2)
- Understanding of connecting events in a story from reading (Unit 2, Lesson 7)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or whiteboard
- Pencil and lined paper
- The student's story beginning from Lesson 7 (in their writing folder)
- Crayons for illustrating
- 6 index cards with sequence words: "First," "Then," "Next," "After that," "Last," "Finally"
- 3 index cards labeled "Beginning," "Middle," "End" (from Lesson 7)
- 3 simple pictures that tell a story in sequence (draw these: a child sees a puddle, the child jumps in the puddle, the child is wet and smiling)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review story structure and introduce the concept of putting events in order.

**Activity: Picture Sequence**
Lay the three pictures on the table in SCRAMBLED order. Say: "These pictures tell a story, but they are all mixed up. Can you put them in order?"

The student arranges the pictures in the correct order.

Then ask:
- "What happens FIRST?" (The child sees a puddle.)
- "What happens NEXT?" (The child jumps in the puddle.)
- "What happens LAST?" (The child is wet and smiling.)

Place the "Beginning," "Middle," "End" cards under the pictures.

"See? Every story has things that happen in ORDER — first, then, last. Today we are going to add the middle and end to your story from last time. Your character started a story, and now we are going to finish it!"

Pull out the student's story beginning from Lesson 7.

*Facilitator note: Physical sequencing with pictures prepares the student to think about sequencing with words. Hands-on before abstract.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach sequence words and model writing the middle and end of a story.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sequence:** The order things happen in — what comes first, next, and last.
- **Sequence words:** Words that tell the reader the order: first, then, next, after that, last, finally.
- **Middle:** The main part of the story — what happens, the exciting part, or the problem.
- **End:** How the story wraps up — what happens last, how the problem is solved, or how the character feels.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce sequence words.**
Lay out the sequence word cards. Read them together: "First... Then... Next... After that... Last... Finally."

"These are magic words for storytelling. They tell the reader WHEN things happen. They keep the story in order so the reader does not get confused."

Write on chart paper:

| Sequence Words for the MIDDLE | Sequence Words for the END |
|---|---|
| Then, | Last, |
| Next, | Finally, |
| After that, | At the end, |

"The middle uses words like 'then,' 'next,' and 'after that.' The end uses words like 'last,' 'finally,' and 'at the end.'"

**Step 2: Review the model story beginning from Lesson 7.**
Write (or recall) the Sam story beginning:
> "Sam was walking to school on a sunny morning. He saw something shiny on the sidewalk."

"This is the BEGINNING. We know WHO (Sam) and WHAT (walking to school, sees something shiny). Now let's write the middle and end."

**Step 3: Model writing the middle.**
Think aloud: "What happens next? I need to tell the reader what happened with the shiny thing."

Write:
> "Sam picked up the shiny thing. It was a gold coin! Then he put it in his pocket. Next, he showed it to his friend at school."

"I used 'Then' and 'Next' to show the order of events. The middle tells what HAPPENED."

**Step 4: Model writing the end.**
Think aloud: "How should the story end? I need to wrap it up."

Write:
> "Finally, Sam took the coin home and put it in his treasure box. He smiled because it was the best walk to school ever."

"I used 'Finally' to signal this is the end. I also told how Sam felt — happy. That is a satisfying ending."

**Step 5: Read the complete story.**
> "Sam was walking to school on a sunny morning. He saw something shiny on the sidewalk. Sam picked up the shiny thing. It was a gold coin! Then he put it in his pocket. Next, he showed it to his friend at school. Finally, Sam took the coin home and put it in his treasure box. He smiled because it was the best walk to school ever."

"Beginning, middle, end. Sequence words hold it all together."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: A true story.**
> *Beginning:* Last Saturday, my dad and I went to the store to buy eggs.
> *Middle:* First, we found the eggs. Then my dad let me carry the carton. Next, I tripped and almost dropped them!
> *End:* Last, we got home safe with all the eggs. I was so proud I did not break any.

**Example 2: A made-up animal story.**
> *Beginning:* A little turtle named Dot wanted to see the top of the hill.
> *Middle:* First, Dot started walking up the hill very slowly. Then a rabbit hopped by and said, "You are too slow!" After that, Dot kept going and did not stop.
> *End:* Finally, Dot made it to the top. She could see the whole forest! She felt happy and brave.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help the student add a middle and end to their Lesson 7 story beginning.

**Activity: Finish Your Story**
Pull out the student's story beginning from Lesson 7. Read it aloud together.

**Step 1: Plan the middle orally.**
"Okay, your story has started. What happens next? Tell me — don't write yet, just tell me."

Listen as the student describes 2-3 events. Help them put events in order:
- "What happens FIRST after the beginning?"
- "Then what?"
- "Anything else before the end?"

**Step 2: Write the middle.**
Guide the student to write the middle, using sequence words. Offer sequence word cards to place next to their writing as reminders.

"Start your next sentence with 'Then' or 'Next.' Write what happens."

The student writes 2-3 sentences for the middle. Help with phrasing if needed, but let the student generate the ideas.

**Step 3: Plan and write the end.**
"How does your story end? What happens LAST? How does your character feel?"

The student says the ending aloud, then writes 1-2 sentences. Encourage use of "Last," "Finally," or "At the end."

**Step 4: Read the complete story.**
Read the entire story aloud — beginning, middle, and end. Celebrate: "You wrote a WHOLE story! Beginning, middle, and end. You are an author!"

*Facilitator prompt: If the student's middle has no clear events (just feelings or descriptions), ask: "What HAPPENED? What did [character] DO?" Narratives need events — things that happen.*

*Facilitator prompt: If the student wants to keep going and never end, say: "Every story needs an ending. What is the LAST thing that happens? Let's write that and finish strong."*

---

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes a NEW complete narrative (beginning, middle, and end).

**Activity: My New Story**
The student writes a brand-new short story with all three parts. This is separate from the story they just completed in Guided Practice.

Story ideas (or the student creates their own):
- A time you were surprised
- A pet that goes on an adventure
- A rainy day when something unexpected happened
- A kid who makes a new friend

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level (5 sentences):**
Write a story with:
- Beginning: 2 sentences (who and what is happening)
- Middle: 2 sentences using at least one sequence word
- End: 1 sentence using a sequence word

Example:
> My friend Kai came to my house. We wanted to play outside. First, we played tag in the yard. Then we found a big frog by the tree. Last, we put the frog near the garden and said goodbye.

**Stretch (6-7 sentences):**
Write a story with:
- Beginning: 2 sentences
- Middle: 3 sentences using at least two different sequence words
- End: 1-2 sentences with a feeling or reaction

Example:
> One day, my dog Biscuit got out of the yard. I was so worried! First, I looked in the front yard but he was not there. Next, I called his name really loud. Then I heard barking from the neighbor's yard. Finally, I found Biscuit playing with the neighbor's cat! I was so happy to find him.

**Challenge (8+ sentences):**
Write a story with:
- Beginning: 2-3 sentences including a detail about the setting
- Middle: 3-4 sentences with a problem and at least three sequence words
- End: 2 sentences that solve the problem and tell how the character feels

Example:
> It was a cold, windy day at the beach. Ava and her little brother Leo were building a sandcastle. It was the biggest one they had ever made. Then a huge wave came and knocked part of it down! Next, Ava said, "Let's build it even bigger this time." After that, they added a moat to protect it from the waves. Finally, the sandcastle was done and it was even better than before. Ava and Leo were proud of their teamwork.

### Interleaved Review
- "Is your story an opinion, informative, or narrative?" (Lesson 1 review — narrative!)
- "Point to your sequence words. Can you find 'first,' 'then,' or 'last'?" (New skill check)
- "Read your story. Does it make sense in order? Does the ending feel like it wraps things up?" (Unit 2 comprehension — connecting events)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can write a complete narrative with sequenced events.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What are the three parts of a story?" (Answer: Beginning, middle, end)
2. "Name three sequence words." (Answer: Any three from: first, then, next, after that, last, finally)
3. "Listen to these sentences. Put them in order: 'Last, we ate the cake. First, we mixed the batter. Then, we baked it in the oven.'" (Answer: First we mixed the batter. Then we baked it. Last we ate the cake.)
4. "Read me your story. Can you point to where the beginning ends and the middle starts? Where does the end begin?" (Answer: Student identifies the three parts of their own story.)

**Mastery = Student answers at least 3 of 4 correctly AND their written story has a recognizable beginning, middle, and end.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the picture sequencing from the warm-up. Practice telling the story with sequence words: "First... then... last..."
- Co-write another short story together with heavy scaffolding: you ask the questions, the student answers, and you write together.
- Focus on the weakest area: if sequence words are missing, provide a word bank card. If the middle is missing, practice generating "what happens" events orally.
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 9.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 9 (Revision Basics). Save both the student's completed story (from Guided Practice) and their new story (from Independent Practice) in their writing folder.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who write narratives easily:**
- Write a sequel to their story — what happens AFTER the end?
- Write the same story from a different character's point of view. How does the story change?
- Draw a comic strip version of their story: 3 panels for beginning, middle, and end, with speech bubbles.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This lesson completes the narrative writing mini-unit.** The student now has all three writing types in their repertoire — opinion, informative, and narrative. Each one has the same basic three-part structure (opening, body, closing), which is intentional. This parallel structure helps students understand that ALL writing has organization.

**The sequence words are the most important tool in this lesson.** First graders often write events out of order or without connectors, leaving the reader confused. "First, then, last" is a simple framework that creates coherent narratives. If the student uses these three words consistently, they are on track.

**Common issue: The middle is too short or missing.** Some students write a beginning and jump straight to the end: "I went to the park. I had fun. The end." Coach them to think about what HAPPENED: "What did you do at the park? Tell me step by step." Each step becomes a middle sentence.

**Common issue: The end is abrupt or missing.** Some students do not know how to end a story. Teach two reliable strategies: (1) Tell what happened LAST. (2) Tell how the character FELT. These two approaches work for almost any story.

**If the student did not complete a story beginning in Lesson 7,** spend the first few minutes of this lesson writing a quick beginning together before moving to the middle and end. Do not skip the beginning — the story needs all three parts.

**Accept invented spelling.** A student who writes "Finulee, we got hoem and I was hapy" has demonstrated: a sequence word (finally), a story ending (got home), and a character emotion (happy). That is excellent narrative writing for Grade 1.

**This lesson has TWO writing products** (the completed Lesson 7 story and a new story). Both should go in the writing folder. The student now has at least one piece of each type to choose from for Lesson 10's Writing Celebration.
