# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 3, Lesson 2

## Topic
Writing Complete Sentences (Subject + Verb, Capitalization, End Punctuation)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain that a complete sentence needs a "who or what" (subject) and a "does or is" (verb)
- Write a complete sentence that begins with a capital letter and ends with a period, question mark, or exclamation point
- Identify and fix incomplete sentences by adding missing parts

## Prerequisites
- Ability to name the three types of writing (Lesson 1)
- Ability to write all 26 letters legibly (Kindergarten handwriting)
- Knowledge of capital and lowercase letters (Kindergarten)

## Materials Needed
- Chart paper or whiteboard
- Pencil and lined paper
- Index cards with sentence parts (see Direct Instruction)
- Red and green crayons or colored pencils
- The "Three Types of Writing" anchor chart from Lesson 1

**Prepare before the lesson:**
Write each word or phrase on a separate index card:
- Subject cards: "The dog," "My mom," "The fish," "I," "A bird," "The ball"
- Verb/predicate cards: "runs fast.", "likes to cook.", "swims in the pond.", "can read books.", "flew away.", "is red."

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the three writing types from Lesson 1 and activate thinking about sentences.

**Activity: Quick Sort**
Say three sentences aloud. The student holds up fingers to show the type: 1 finger = opinion, 2 fingers = informative, 3 fingers = narrative.

- "I think apples are better than oranges." (1 — opinion)
- "Spiders have eight legs." (2 — informative)
- "Yesterday, my cat knocked over a cup." (3 — narrative)

Then say: "Great! Today we are going to learn how to build perfect sentences. Every type of writing uses sentences, so this is a super important skill."

"Listen to this: 'The dog.' Is that a sentence?" (No.) "What about 'runs fast'? Is that a sentence?" (No.) "What about 'The dog runs fast'? Is THAT a sentence?" (Yes!) "Let's find out what makes a sentence complete."

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the two essential parts of a complete sentence and the rules for capitalization and end punctuation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sentence:** A group of words that tells a complete thought. It has a subject and a verb.
- **Subject:** The "who" or "what" the sentence is about. (Who is doing something?)
- **Verb:** The "action word" — what the subject does or is. (What are they doing?)
- **Capital letter:** A big, uppercase letter. Every sentence starts with one.
- **End punctuation:** The mark at the end of a sentence — a period (.), question mark (?), or exclamation point (!).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the two parts of a sentence.**
Draw a simple chart:

| Every sentence needs: |
|---|
| 1. A WHO or WHAT (subject) |
| 2. A DOES or IS (verb) |

"A sentence is like a two-piece puzzle. You need both pieces or it is not complete."

Hold up the card "The dog." Ask: "Does this tell us WHO? Yes — the dog. Does it tell us what the dog DOES? No. This is not a sentence yet. It is missing the second piece."

Hold up "runs fast." Ask: "Does this tell us what someone does? Yes — runs fast. But WHO runs fast? We don't know. Missing the first piece."

Now put them together: "The dog runs fast." Ask: "Now do we know WHO? (The dog.) And what the dog DOES? (Runs fast.) YES! Now it is a complete sentence."

**Step 2: Teach capitalization and end punctuation.**
Write on the chart paper: "the dog runs fast"

"This sentence has both parts, but it is missing two important things. Can you see what is wrong?"

Point to the t in "the" — "The first letter of every sentence must be a CAPITAL letter." Change it to T.

Point to the end — "Every sentence must end with a PUNCTUATION MARK." Add a period.

Write the rule:
- Sentences START with a capital letter.
- Sentences END with a . or ? or !

**Step 3: Introduce three end marks.**
- Period (.) — "I use a period when I am telling something." Write: "The cat is soft."
- Question mark (?) — "I use a question mark when I am asking something." Write: "Is the cat soft?"
- Exclamation point (!) — "I use an exclamation point when I am excited or something is surprising." Write: "The cat is so soft!"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Build a sentence from parts.
- Subject card: "My mom"
- Verb card: "likes to cook."
- Together: "My mom likes to cook."
- Check: Who? My mom. Does what? Likes to cook. Capital letter? Yes — M. End punctuation? Yes — period. This is a complete sentence.

**Example 2:** Fix an incomplete sentence.
- Write: "A bird"
- "Is this complete? No — it tells us WHO but not WHAT. Let's add what the bird does."
- Student suggests an action. Write: "A bird sings in the tree."
- Check all four things: subject, verb, capital letter, end punctuation.

**Example 3:** Fix a sentence with missing conventions.
- Write: "the ball is red"
- "Are both parts here? Yes — the ball (who) is red (is what). But what is missing?"
- Fix: "The ball is red." (Capital T, add period.)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice building and checking sentences with support.

**Activity 1: Sentence Puzzle (5 minutes)**
Lay out all the subject cards and verb/predicate cards face up. The student matches a subject card to a verb card to make a complete sentence. Read each sentence together.

Possible combinations:
- "The dog runs fast."
- "My mom likes to cook."
- "The fish swims in the pond."
- "I can read books."
- "A bird flew away."
- "The ball is red."

After each sentence, do the "Sentence Check" together. Count on four fingers:
1. Does it have a WHO or WHAT? (Hold up one finger.)
2. Does it have a DOES or IS? (Hold up two fingers.)
3. Does it start with a capital letter? (Three fingers.)
4. Does it end with . or ? or ! ? (Four fingers.)

"Four fingers up = complete sentence!"

*Facilitator prompt: If the student makes a silly combination like "The fish likes to cook," laugh together and say: "That is a funny sentence, but it IS complete — it has all four parts!"*

**Activity 2: Fix the Sentences (5 minutes)**
Write these on paper. The student finds and fixes the errors using a red crayon:

1. "the cat is big" (Missing: capital T, period)
2. "My sister" (Missing: verb — student adds one)
3. "likes to run" (Missing: subject — student adds one)
4. "I like dogs" (Missing: period)

*Facilitator prompt: For items 2 and 3, if the student is unsure what to add, offer two choices: "Should we add 'My sister dances' or 'My sister reads'? You pick!"*

---

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes original complete sentences independently.

**Activity: My Sentence Page**
The student writes 4 complete sentences on lined paper. After each sentence, they do the four-finger check on their own.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level (4 sentences):**
Write one sentence for each prompt. Use the four-finger check after each.
1. Write a sentence about an animal.
2. Write a sentence about your favorite food.
3. Write a sentence that asks a question.
4. Write a sentence that shows excitement (use !).

**Stretch (4 sentences + fix 2):**
Complete the On-level sentences, PLUS fix these two broken sentences by rewriting them correctly:
- "my dad" → (Add a verb and end punctuation.)
- "is very tall" → (Add a subject and a capital letter.)

**Challenge (6 sentences):**
Write 6 original sentences — two telling sentences (period), two asking sentences (question mark), and two excited sentences (exclamation point). Each must pass the four-finger check.

### Interleaved Review
- "Read your first sentence to me. Now — is that sentence an opinion, informative, or narrative?" (Lesson 1 review)
- "Circle all the capital letters in your sentences." (Kindergarten letter knowledge review)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can write and identify complete sentences.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "What two parts does every sentence need?" (Answer: a who/what AND a does/is — or subject and verb)
2. "What does every sentence start with?" (Answer: a capital letter)
3. "What goes at the end of every sentence?" (Answer: a period, question mark, or exclamation point)
4. Write this on paper and ask: "Is this a complete sentence? 'Runs very fast.'" (Answer: No — missing a subject/who.)
5. "Fix it — make it a complete sentence." (Answer: Any sentence with a subject added, e.g., "The horse runs very fast.")

**Mastery = Student answers at least 4 of 5 items correctly, including fixing the incomplete sentence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the sentence puzzle activity. Build 5 more sentences using the cards.
- Focus on the weakest area: if the student forgets capitals/periods, practice circling them in printed text. If the student writes fragments, practice the two-part check (who + does what) with oral sentences first.
- Repeat this lesson next session before moving to Lesson 3.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 3 (Opinion Writing — Introduction). Continue the four-finger sentence check in every future writing activity.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who write complete sentences with ease:**
- Play "Sentence or Not?" Read a mix of complete and incomplete sentences. The student gives a thumbs-up for complete and thumbs-down for incomplete, then fixes the incomplete ones.
- Write a "Silly Sentence" by combining a random subject and verb: "The banana flew to the moon." Check: Is it a complete sentence? (Yes! Silly, but complete.)
- Write 3 sentences about what they did today. Underline the subject in red and the verb in blue in each sentence.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This lesson establishes the mechanical foundation for all writing in this unit.** Every lesson from here forward depends on the student knowing what a complete sentence is and how to write one. If the student does not internalize this lesson, their opinion, informative, and narrative pieces will be weaker. Take time here.

**The four-finger check is the most important tool in this lesson.** Make it a habit. In every future lesson, whenever the student finishes a sentence, ask: "Four-finger check?" Eventually the student will do it automatically.

**Common issue: Students who speak in fragments.** Some students say things like "My dog. He runs." This is natural oral language. In writing, gently combine: "Can we put that into one sentence? 'My dog runs.'" Model the difference between talking and writing.

**Do not overcorrect spelling.** Today is about sentence structure (subject + verb, capitals, periods). If the student writes "My kat is flufee." — that sentence has a subject (My kat), a verb (is), a capital letter, and a period. It is a PERFECT sentence for today's lesson. The spelling will improve over time.

**The sentence puzzle with cards** is tactile and effective. Students who struggle with writing sentences from scratch often succeed when they can physically move parts together. Keep these cards for future lessons — you can add new subjects and verbs as the student's vocabulary grows.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice Activity 2 (Fix the Sentences):**
1. "The cat is big." (Capital T, add period)
2. "My sister [any verb]." — e.g., "My sister plays." or "My sister reads books."
3. "[Any subject] likes to run." — e.g., "The boy likes to run." or "My dog likes to run."
4. "I like dogs." (Add period)

**Exit Ticket:**
1. A who/what (subject) and a does/is (verb)
2. A capital letter
3. A period, question mark, or exclamation point
4. No — missing a subject (who runs fast?)
5. Any complete sentence, e.g., "The cheetah runs very fast."
