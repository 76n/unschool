# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 10

## Topic
Comprehension Strategies Review

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply multiple comprehension strategies to both fiction and informational text: asking questions, finding key details, identifying the main topic, and making connections
- Demonstrate readiness for the unit assessment by using strategies independently with a new passage
- Explain which comprehension strategy they are using and why

## Prerequisites
- Asking and answering questions using question words (Lessons 1-2)
- Finding key details in fiction and informational text (Lessons 3-4)
- Identifying the main topic and supporting details (Lessons 5-6)
- Identifying cause and effect in fiction (Lesson 7)
- Identifying sequence and connections in informational text (Lesson 8)
- Comparing fiction and informational text (Lesson 9)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copies of both passages: "A Day at the Beach" (fiction) and "All About the Ocean" (informational)
- Comprehension Strategy Cards (5 cards labeled: Ask Questions, Find Key Details, Main Topic and Details, Cause and Effect, Sequence/Connections)
- Graphic organizer worksheet with sections for each strategy
- Pencils or crayons
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Sticky notes (optional, for marking text)

## Standards Alignment
- **RL.1.1:** Ask and answer questions about key details in a text.
- **RI.1.2:** Identify the main topic and retell key details of a text.
- **RI.1.3:** Describe the connection between two individuals, events, ideas, or pieces of information in a text.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all comprehension strategies learned in the unit and build confidence before applying them.

**Activity: Strategy Recall Poster**

Before the lesson, write the following headings on chart paper or the whiteboard:

1. Ask Questions
2. Find Key Details
3. Main Topic and Supporting Details
4. Cause and Effect
5. Sequence and Connections
6. Fiction vs. Informational

Say to students:
> "We have learned SO many reading strategies in this unit! Today we are going to use ALL of them. But first, let's remember what each one means. I am going to point to a strategy and I want you to tell me what it means."

Point to each heading and elicit responses:

1. **Ask Questions:** "What do we do?" (We ask who, what, where, when, why, and how questions about the text.)
2. **Find Key Details:** "What are key details?" (Important pieces of information in the text that help us understand it.)
3. **Main Topic and Supporting Details:** "What is the main topic?" (What the whole text is mostly about.) "What are supporting details?" (Facts that tell us more about the main topic.)
4. **Cause and Effect:** "What is cause and effect?" (A cause is why something happens. An effect is what happens because of it. We use the word "because.")
5. **Sequence and Connections:** "What are sequence words?" (First, then, next, last — words that tell us the order.)
6. **Fiction vs. Informational:** "What is fiction?" (A made-up story.) "What is informational text?" (Writing with real facts.)

Say:
> "You remembered everything! Today we will practice using these strategies with two new passages — one fiction and one informational. Think of this as a warm-up for our unit assessment. Let's show what we know!"

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model applying multiple comprehension strategies to a single passage, showing students how to use their full toolkit.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Comprehension:** Understanding what you read.
- **Strategy:** A plan or tool to help you do something. A comprehension strategy helps you understand what you read.
- **Review:** Looking back at what you have learned to make sure you remember it.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Read the fiction passage aloud (2 minutes)**

Say:
> "First, I am going to read a fiction story. Listen carefully — we are going to use several strategies with this one passage!"

Read the following passage aloud (display it for students to see):

> **A Day at the Beach**
>
> Mia and her dad went to the beach on a hot summer day. Mia ran to the water and splashed in the waves. She found a pretty pink shell in the sand. "Look, Dad!" she called. "I found a treasure!" Dad smiled and said, "That is a beautiful shell." Then the wind started to blow hard. Dark clouds filled the sky. Mia and her dad packed up their things and hurried to the car. "We can come back another day," said Dad. Mia held her shell tight and smiled all the way home.

**Step 2: Model applying multiple strategies (8 minutes)**

Say:
> "Now I am going to show you how I use our strategies one at a time with this passage. Watch me think through each one."

**Strategy 1: Identify — Fiction or Informational?**

> "First, is this fiction or informational? Let me think. It has characters — Mia and her dad. It tells a story about something that happened. It could be made up. I think this is FICTION because it is a story about characters."

**Strategy 2: Ask Questions**

> "Now I will ask some questions about the text. WHO is in the story? Mia and her dad. WHERE did they go? To the beach. WHAT did Mia find? A pretty pink shell. WHY did they leave? Because the wind blew hard and dark clouds came."

Write on the board:
- Who? Mia and her dad.
- Where? The beach.
- What? Mia found a pink shell.
- Why did they leave? Storm was coming.

**Strategy 3: Find Key Details**

> "What are the key details — the most important pieces of information? Let me find them. One key detail is that Mia found a pink shell. Another is that the wind started to blow and dark clouds filled the sky. A third is that they packed up and hurried to the car."

**Strategy 4: Cause and Effect**

> "Can I find cause and effect? Yes! The wind blew hard and dark clouds came — that is the CAUSE. Mia and her dad packed up and left — that is the EFFECT. I can say: 'Mia and her dad left the beach BECAUSE the wind blew hard and dark clouds came.'"

Write: Cause: Storm coming. -> Effect: They packed up and left.

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Applying sequence to the fiction passage**

Say:
> "Can I find a sequence in this story? Yes! Let me put the events in order."

> "FIRST, Mia and her dad went to the beach. THEN, Mia splashed in the waves and found a shell. NEXT, the wind started to blow and clouds came. LAST, they packed up and went home."

> "See? I just used ANOTHER strategy — sequence — on the same passage. We can use more than one strategy with the same text!"

**Worked Example 2: Combining strategies on one detail**

Say:
> "Watch how I can use TWO strategies on the same detail. The story says Mia found a pretty pink shell. I can ASK A QUESTION: 'Where did Mia find the shell?' And I can FIND THE KEY DETAIL to answer it: 'She found it in the sand.' I used asking questions AND finding key details together!"

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply comprehension strategies to a new informational passage with facilitator support.

**Activity: Strategy Stations**

Read the following informational passage aloud (display it for students to see):

> **All About the Ocean**
>
> The ocean is a very large body of salt water. It covers most of the Earth. Many animals live in the ocean, like fish, whales, and sea turtles. The water near the top of the ocean is warm because the sun heats it. The water deep down is very cold and dark. Waves are made when the wind blows across the water. People visit the ocean to swim, surf, and look at sea life. The ocean is an important part of our world.

Say:
> "Now it is your turn! We are going to use our strategies with this informational passage. I will guide you through each one."

Give students the graphic organizer worksheet with sections for each strategy (or use a blank paper divided into sections).

**Station 1: Fiction or Informational? (1 minute)**

Ask: "Is this passage fiction or informational? How do you know?"
(Informational — it gives real facts about the ocean, no characters, no made-up story.)
Students write "Informational" and one reason on their paper.

**Station 2: Ask Questions (2 minutes)**

Say: "Write one question about this passage using a question word."

**Facilitator Prompts:**
- "What is something you wonder about the ocean after reading this?"
- "Try starting with WHERE or WHY."
- "Can you ask a question that the passage answers?"

Students write one question.

**Station 3: Main Topic and Supporting Details (2 minutes)**

Ask: "What is the main topic?" (The ocean.)
"Write the main topic and two supporting details."

**Facilitator Prompts:**
- "What is this whole passage about?"
- "Find a sentence that tells you more about the ocean."

**Station 4: Cause and Effect (2 minutes)**

Ask: "Can you find a cause and effect in this passage?"
Guide students to: "The water near the top is warm BECAUSE the sun heats it." and "Waves are made BECAUSE the wind blows across the water."

Students write one cause-and-effect pair using "because."

**Station 5: Connections (1 minute)**

Ask: "How are the ideas in this passage connected?"
Guide students to see: The passage tells us about the ocean in a sequence — what it is, what lives there, what the water is like, how waves are made, and why it is important.

**Supporting Struggling Students:**
- Focus on just 2-3 strategies instead of all of them.
- Provide sentence frames for each strategy:
  - Question: "___ is ___?"
  - Main topic: "This passage is mostly about ___."
  - Detail: "One detail is ___."
  - Cause/Effect: "___ BECAUSE ___."
- Work one-on-one or in a small group, reading the passage together sentence by sentence.

After completing the stations, share as a group. Ask students to share one thing they wrote for each station.

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently apply comprehension strategies to demonstrate readiness for the unit assessment.

Students choose ONE of the two passages from today's lesson ("A Day at the Beach" or "All About the Ocean") and complete the following tasks independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Answer these questions about the passage you chose:
1. Is your passage fiction or informational? How do you know? (Write one reason.)
2. Write one question about the passage using a question word (who, what, where, when, why, or how).
3. What is one key detail from the passage?
4. Write one cause-and-effect sentence using the word "because."

**Stretch:**
Complete all four questions above. Then:
5. If you chose the fiction passage, what is the main topic or "big idea" of the story? If you chose the informational passage, name the main topic and two supporting details.
6. Put two events or ideas from the passage in order using the words FIRST and THEN.

**Challenge:**
Complete all six questions above. Then:
7. Think about BOTH passages. Write 2-3 sentences comparing them. How are they the same? How are they different? (Hint: both are connected to the ocean/beach.)
8. Write one question about the passage that is NOT answered in the text — something you still wonder about.

### Interleaved Review
After completing the strategy practice, students answer one cumulative review question:
- "What is the difference between a main topic and a supporting detail?" (Review of Lessons 5-6. The main topic is what the whole text is about. A supporting detail is a piece of information that tells more about the main topic.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Name two comprehension strategies we learned in this unit. (Accept any two: asking questions, finding key details, main topic and details, cause and effect, sequence/connections, fiction vs. informational.)

2. Read this sentence: "The ice cream melted BECAUSE it was a hot day." What is the cause? (It was a hot day.) What is the effect? (The ice cream melted.)

3. Is this sentence from a fiction text or an informational text? "Bees make honey inside their hives." (Informational — it is a real fact.)

4. What question word would you use to ask about WHERE something happened? (Where.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Identify which specific strategy or strategies the student struggled with based on their exit ticket responses.
- Provide targeted review:
  - If struggling with fiction vs. informational: Return to Lesson 9 warm-up "Real or Made Up?" and practice with simple sentences.
  - If struggling with cause and effect: Return to Lesson 7 examples and practice 2-3 simple "because" sentences.
  - If struggling with main topic/details: Return to Lesson 6 graphic organizer and practice with a familiar passage.
  - If struggling with question words: Return to Lesson 1 and review the question word chart.
- Consider a brief review session before the unit assessment.

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for the Unit 2 Assessment.
- Celebrate their progress! They have learned and practiced five major comprehension strategies.
- Encourage continued practice: "When you read at home tonight, try to use one of our strategies. Ask a question, find a detail, or notice cause and effect!"

## Extension (Optional)
- **Strategy Bookmark:** Students create a bookmark listing all the comprehension strategies they learned. They can use it as a reference when reading independently.
  - Side 1: Ask Questions / Find Key Details / Main Topic and Details
  - Side 2: Cause and Effect / Sequence and Connections / Fiction vs. Informational
- **Partner Quiz:** Students pair up. One student reads a sentence from a passage, and the other student names which strategy they could use with that sentence.
- **Strategy Journal:** Students choose a book from the classroom library, read one page (or have it read to them), and write about which strategy they used and what they learned. For example: "I used asking questions. I asked, 'Why did the bear go to sleep?' The answer is because it was winter."

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Pacing:** This is a review lesson, so the pace should be slightly faster than a teaching lesson. Students have seen all of these strategies before. The goal is to build fluency and confidence, not to introduce new concepts. If students are breezing through, spend more time on independent practice. If students are struggling, slow down during guided practice and provide more modeling.
- **Assessment Preparation:** This lesson directly prepares students for the unit assessment. Pay close attention during the exit ticket to identify students who may need additional review before the assessment. Consider pulling small groups for targeted review if needed.
- **Common Misconceptions:**
  - Students may think they can only use ONE strategy per passage. Emphasize that good readers use MANY strategies with the same text.
  - Some students may mix up "main topic" and "key detail." Remind them: the main topic is the BIG idea — what the whole text is about. A key detail is a smaller piece of information that supports it.
  - Students may struggle to articulate which strategy they are using. The strategy cards and labels are helpful visual supports.
- **Preparation:** Pre-make strategy cards if possible. Having them displayed on a wall or board throughout the lesson provides a helpful reference. The graphic organizer for guided practice should be prepared ahead of time with labeled sections.
- **Differentiation Note:** For the independent practice, allowing students to CHOOSE which passage they work with gives them agency and can increase engagement. Students who struggle with informational text may choose the fiction passage, and that is okay — the goal is strategy application, not text type mastery.
- **Connection to Standards:** This lesson integrates all three unit standards (RL.1.1, RI.1.2, RI.1.3) in a single session, modeling how comprehension strategies work across text types.
- **Methodology Note:** This review lesson follows the spiral review approach. Rather than introducing new content, it revisits and consolidates all previous learning, giving students the chance to strengthen any weak areas before the summative assessment. See the Teaching Methodology Framework for more on spiral review and cumulative practice.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice — "All About the Ocean"

**Fiction or Informational?**
Informational. It gives real facts about the ocean. There are no characters or made-up events.

**Sample Questions:**
- "What animals live in the ocean?"
- "Why is the water near the top warm?"
- "How are waves made?"
- "Where do people go to see sea life?"
(Accept any question using a question word that relates to the passage.)

**Main Topic and Supporting Details:**
- Main topic: The ocean.
- Supporting details (any two):
  - The ocean is a large body of salt water.
  - It covers most of the Earth.
  - Many animals live in the ocean (fish, whales, sea turtles).
  - The water near the top is warm; the water deep down is cold and dark.
  - Waves are made when wind blows across the water.
  - People visit the ocean to swim, surf, and look at sea life.
  - The ocean is important.

**Cause and Effect:**
- "The water near the top is warm BECAUSE the sun heats it."
- "Waves are made BECAUSE the wind blows across the water."
(Either is acceptable.)

**Connections:**
The passage connects ideas by telling us about the ocean from general (what it is) to specific (animals, water temperature, waves, human visitors) to a conclusion (it is important). It also uses cause and effect (sun causes warm water, wind causes waves).

### Independent Practice

**"A Day at the Beach" (Fiction):**
1. Fiction. It is a story with characters (Mia and her dad) and events that are part of a made-up narrative.
2. Sample questions: "Where did Mia find the shell?" (In the sand.) "Why did they leave the beach?" (The wind blew hard and dark clouds came.)
3. Key details: Mia found a pink shell. The wind started to blow and clouds came. They packed up and left.
4. Cause/effect: "Mia and her dad left the beach BECAUSE dark clouds filled the sky." OR "Mia smiled all the way home BECAUSE she had her shell."
5. Big idea: A girl has fun at the beach and finds a special shell, but they have to leave because of a storm.
6. FIRST, Mia splashed in the waves. THEN, she found a pink shell.

**"All About the Ocean" (Informational):**
1. Informational. It tells real facts about the ocean. No characters or story.
2. Sample questions: See guided practice section above.
3. Key details: See guided practice section above.
4. Cause/effect: See guided practice section above.
5. Main topic: The ocean. Details: Many animals live in the ocean. Waves are made when wind blows.
6. FIRST, the passage tells us the ocean is a large body of salt water. THEN, it tells us about animals that live there.

**Challenge — Comparing both passages:**
Sample: "Both passages are about the water. 'A Day at the Beach' is fiction because it tells a made-up story about Mia. 'All About the Ocean' is informational because it gives real facts. They are different because one is a story and one teaches us things, but they are the same because they are both about the ocean."

**Challenge — Unanswered question:**
Sample: "How deep is the ocean?" / "What kind of shell did Mia find?" / "Do sea turtles swim fast?"
(Accept any reasonable question not directly answered in the text.)

### Interleaved Review
- The main topic is what the WHOLE text is about — the big idea.
- A supporting detail is a smaller piece of information that tells more about the main topic.
- Example: "If the main topic is 'the ocean,' a supporting detail is 'many animals live in the ocean.'"

### Exit Ticket
1. Accept any two: asking questions, finding key details, main topic and supporting details, cause and effect, sequence/connections, fiction vs. informational.
2. Cause: It was a hot day. Effect: The ice cream melted.
3. Informational — it is a real fact about bees.
4. Where.
