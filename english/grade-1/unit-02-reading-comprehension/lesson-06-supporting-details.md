# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 6

## Topic
Supporting Details in Informational Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain that supporting details give more information about the main topic
- Identify at least three supporting details in a short informational passage
- Use a graphic organizer to connect supporting details to the main topic

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify the main topic of a short informational text (Lesson 5)
- Familiarity with question words: who, what, where, when, why, how (Lessons 1-2)
- Ability to locate key details in informational text (Lessons 3-4)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "All About Frogs"
- Main Topic and Details graphic organizer (one circle in the center with four lines branching outward to four outer circles)
- Pencils or crayons
- Sticky notes (optional, for marking details in the text)
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers

## Standards Alignment
- **RI.1.2:** Identify the main topic and retell key details of a text.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about main topic and build curiosity about supporting details.

**Activity: Main Topic Quick Recall**

Say to students:
> "Yesterday we learned how to find the main topic of a text. The main topic tells us what the whole text is mostly about. Let's practice! I am going to read you three sentences. Tell me what they are mostly about."

Read aloud:
> "Cats like to nap. Cats like to play with string. Cats like to drink milk."

Ask: "What is the main topic? What are all three sentences about?" (Cats — or what cats like to do.)

Then say:
> "Great! Those three sentences all told us more about cats. Each sentence gave us a DETAIL — a small piece of information. Today we are going to learn that details SUPPORT the main topic. That means the details help us understand more about it. Details are like helpers — they give the main topic extra information!"

Have students turn to a partner and share one detail they remember about cats from the sentences.

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the concept of supporting details and model how to identify them using a graphic organizer.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Main topic:** What the whole text is mostly about.
- **Supporting detail:** A piece of information that tells more about the main topic. It "supports" (holds up, helps) the main topic.
- **Graphic organizer:** A picture or chart that helps us sort our ideas.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce supporting details (3 minutes)**

Write "Main Topic" on the board inside a large circle. Draw three or four lines coming out of the circle, each ending in a smaller circle labeled "Detail."

Say:
> "This is called a graphic organizer. It helps us see how a text is put together. The big circle in the middle is for the main topic. The smaller circles are for the supporting details. Supporting details are facts or pieces of information that tell us MORE about the main topic. They support it — like legs on a table. Without the legs, the table would fall! Without details, we would not know very much about the topic."

**Step 2: Read the passage aloud (3 minutes)**

Say:
> "Now I am going to read a passage about frogs. Listen carefully for the main topic and the details that tell us more about it."

Read the following passage aloud (display it for students to see):

> **All About Frogs**
>
> Frogs are small animals that live near water. They have smooth, wet skin. Frogs have long back legs that help them jump very far. They use their big eyes to look for bugs to eat. Most frogs start life as tiny tadpoles that swim in ponds. As they grow, tadpoles get legs and lose their tails. Frogs can be many colors like green, brown, and red. Some frogs make loud sounds at night called croaking. Frogs are amazing animals!

**Step 3: Model finding the main topic and supporting details (4 minutes)**

Say:
> "What is this whole passage about? It is about frogs! So I will write FROGS in my main topic circle."

Write "Frogs" in the center circle on the board.

> "Now I need to find the details that tell me MORE about frogs. Let me look at the first sentence: 'Frogs are small animals that live near water.' Does this tell me more about frogs? Yes! It tells me where they live. I will write 'live near water' in one of my detail circles."

Write "live near water" in one detail circle.

> "Let me look at another sentence: 'Frogs have long back legs that help them jump very far.' This tells me more about frogs — it tells me about their bodies and what they can do. I will write 'long back legs — jump far' in the next detail circle."

Write "long back legs — jump far" in a second detail circle.

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Finding a detail about what frogs eat**

Say:
> "I want to find a detail about what frogs eat. Let me look back at the text. I see: 'They use their big eyes to look for bugs to eat.' This is a supporting detail! It tells me more about frogs — it tells me what they eat. I will write 'eat bugs' in my next detail circle."

Write "eat bugs" in a third detail circle.

Ask students: "Does this detail tell us more about frogs? Yes! That is how we know it is a supporting detail."

**Worked Example 2: Finding a detail about how frogs grow**

Say:
> "Let me find one more detail. I see: 'Most frogs start life as tiny tadpoles that swim in ponds.' and 'As they grow, tadpoles get legs and lose their tails.' These sentences tell me how frogs grow and change. I will write 'start as tadpoles, then grow legs' in my last detail circle."

Write "start as tadpoles, then grow legs" in the fourth detail circle.

Say:
> "Look at our graphic organizer! The main topic is FROGS, and all four details tell us more about frogs. The details SUPPORT the main topic. They help us learn more."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying supporting details with facilitator guidance.

**Activity: Find the Detail Hunt**

Give each student a copy of the "All About Frogs" passage and a blank graphic organizer (main topic circle in the center with four branching detail circles).

Say:
> "We already found the main topic together: Frogs. Write 'Frogs' in the big circle in the middle of your paper."

Pause and check that all students have written the main topic.

> "Now it is your turn to find supporting details! Look at the passage. I want you to find details that we did NOT already talk about. There are still more details in this text! Read each sentence and ask yourself: 'Does this tell me more about frogs?' If it does, it is a supporting detail."

Give students 3-4 minutes to work. Circulate and support.

**Facilitator Prompts:**
- "Read this sentence out loud to me. Does it tell you something new about frogs?"
- "Point to a sentence that tells you what frogs look like."
- "What does this sentence teach you about frogs?"
- "Does this sentence give you MORE information about the main topic?"

**Supporting Struggling Students:**
- If a student cannot find details, read each sentence with them one at a time and ask: "What does this sentence tell us about frogs?"
- Help them underline or use a sticky note to mark sentences that give information about frogs.
- Offer a sentence frame: "One detail about frogs is ___."

After 3-4 minutes, bring the group together and share. Ask 2-3 students to read a detail they found. Possible details students may identify:
- Frogs have smooth, wet skin.
- Frogs can be many colors (green, brown, red).
- Some frogs make loud sounds (croaking) at night.
- Frogs are small animals.

Say:
> "Wonderful! All of those details SUPPORT the main topic. They each tell us something more about frogs. When we read informational text, we can use a graphic organizer to find and sort the main topic and the supporting details."

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify the main topic and supporting details in a new short text.

Read the following passage aloud, then have students work independently. Display the passage for students to reference.

> **All About Ants**
>
> Ants are tiny insects. They have six legs and two feelers on their heads. Ants live together in big groups called colonies. They build their homes under the ground. Ants work very hard to find food. They can carry things that are much bigger than they are!

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
- Write the main topic in the center of a new graphic organizer.
- Find and write at least two supporting details in the detail circles.

**Stretch:**
- Write the main topic in the center of a new graphic organizer.
- Find and write three supporting details in the detail circles.
- Write one sentence telling what the passage is mostly about using this frame: "This passage is mostly about ___ because it tells us ___."

**Challenge:**
- Complete the graphic organizer with the main topic and four supporting details.
- Write two sentences: one that tells the main topic and one that uses a detail from the text. Example: "This passage is about ants. One detail is that ants can carry things bigger than they are."

### Interleaved Review
After completing the graphic organizer, students answer one review question from a previous lesson:
- "Write one question you could ask about this passage. Use a question word (who, what, where, when, why, or how)." (Review of Lessons 1-2)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read each sentence. Circle YES if it is a supporting detail about ants. Circle NO if it is NOT.

1. Ants have six legs. (YES / NO)
2. Dogs like to run. (YES / NO)
3. Ants live in colonies. (YES / NO)
4. Fill in the blank: A supporting detail tells more about the _________. (main topic)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Work with the student one-on-one using the "All About Frogs" passage again.
- Re-read each sentence together and sort them: "Does this tell us about frogs, or about something else?"
- Practice with a very simple three-sentence passage (e.g., "Apples are a fruit. They can be red or green. Apples taste sweet.") and have the student name the topic and one detail.
- Revisit the graphic organizer with hand-over-hand support.

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 7, which introduces connecting events in a story through cause and effect.
- Encourage the student to practice finding details in books they read at home.

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Draw and Label:** Students draw a picture of a frog and label it with three supporting details from the passage (e.g., "wet skin," "long back legs," "eats bugs").
- **Detail Detective at Home:** Students choose a short informational book or page and find the main topic and one supporting detail to share with the class the next day.
- **Create Your Own:** Students think of an animal they know about and draw a graphic organizer with the animal as the main topic and 2-3 details they know about it.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Pacing:** The graphic organizer is the key tool in this lesson. Spend enough time modeling it so students understand the relationship between the center (main topic) and the branches (details). If students struggle with writing, they may draw their details instead.
- **Common Misconceptions:** Some students may confuse a detail with a completely unrelated fact. Emphasize: "A supporting detail MUST be about the main topic. If it is about something else, it is not a supporting detail for this text."
- **Preparation:** Pre-draw or print the graphic organizer. Having a large version on chart paper for modeling is very helpful.
- **Scaffolding Tip:** For emergent readers, read the passage aloud multiple times. Students can point to sentences rather than writing them.
- **Connection to Standards:** This lesson directly supports RI.1.2 by teaching students to identify not just the main topic, but the details that develop it. This is foundational for retelling and summarizing skills.
- **Methodology Note:** This lesson follows the I Do / We Do / You Do gradual release model. The Direct Instruction section models the strategy (I Do), the Guided Practice section lets students try with support (We Do), and the Independent Practice section gives students ownership (You Do). See the Teaching Methodology Framework for more on this approach.

### Guided Practice — Additional Details from "All About Frogs"
Students may identify any of the following:
1. Frogs have smooth, wet skin.
2. Frogs can be many colors like green, brown, and red.
3. Some frogs make loud sounds at night called croaking.
4. Frogs are small animals.
5. Frogs use their big eyes to look for bugs.

(Any detail from the passage that tells more about frogs is acceptable.)

### Independent Practice — "All About Ants"
- **Main topic:** Ants
- **Supporting details (any of the following):**
  1. Ants are tiny insects.
  2. They have six legs and two feelers on their heads.
  3. Ants live together in big groups called colonies.
  4. They build their homes under the ground.
  5. Ants work very hard to find food.
  6. They can carry things that are much bigger than they are.

- **Stretch sentence frame example:** "This passage is mostly about ants because it tells us what ants look like and how they live."
- **Challenge sentence examples:** "This passage is about ants. One detail is that ants have six legs and two feelers."

### Interleaved Review
Acceptable questions include:
- "What do ants eat?"
- "Where do ants live?"
- "How many legs do ants have?"
- "Why do ants live in colonies?"
(Any question using a question word that relates to the passage is acceptable.)
