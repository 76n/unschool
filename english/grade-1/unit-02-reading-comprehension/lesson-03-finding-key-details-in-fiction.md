# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 3

## Topic
Finding Key Details in Fiction

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define "key details" as the most important pieces of information in a story
- Identify at least 3 key details in a short fiction passage
- Distinguish between key details and minor details in a story

## Prerequisites
- Ability to ask and answer who, what, where, when, why, and how questions about a text (Lessons 1-2)
- Ability to decode Grade 1 connected text with at least 90% accuracy (Unit 1)
- Understanding that stories have characters, settings, and events

## Materials Needed
- The reading passage "Max's Big Trick" (provided below — print or write it for the student)
- 6 index cards or paper strips, each with one detail from the story written on it (prepared before the lesson; see Guided Practice)
- 2 sorting labels: "Key Detail" and "Not a Key Detail" (write on index cards or paper)
- Paper and pencil
- Crayons or colored pencils (optional)
- Sticky notes (optional, for marking details in the text)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect the concept of "important information" to the student's everyday life before applying it to reading.

**Activity: What's Important?**
Say: "Imagine you went to a birthday party, and your grandma calls and asks, 'How was the party?' You cannot tell her every single thing that happened — that would take all day. You have to pick the most important parts. What would you tell her?"

Let the student respond. They might say things like: "It was my friend's party. We had cake. I played games."

Say: "Those are the BIG things — the important parts. You did not tell her that you tied your shoe or that you drank a cup of water. Those things happened, but they were not the most important parts of the party."

"When we read a story, it is the same thing. Some parts of the story are really important — we need them to understand what the story is about. Other parts are small details that are not as important. Today we are going to learn how to find the important parts. We call them KEY DETAILS."

*Facilitator note: Use a real scenario from the student's life if possible. "Remember when we went to the library yesterday? If you told a friend about it, what would be the most important parts?" Real connections make abstract concepts concrete.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach what key details are, how to find them, and how to tell the difference between key details and minor details.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Key detail:** An important piece of information in a story. Key details help you understand who the story is about, what happens, where it happens, and why things happen. Without key details, the story would not make sense.
- **Minor detail:** A small piece of information that is in the story but is NOT needed to understand the main events. The story would still make sense without it.
- **Retell:** To tell the story again in your own words, using the most important parts.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Define key details.**
Say: "A KEY DETAIL is an important piece of information in a story. Think of it like a key that opens a door — without it, you cannot get in. Without key details, you cannot understand the story."

"Key details answer the big questions we learned: Who is in the story? What happened? Where did it happen? Why did it happen? If a detail helps answer one of these big questions, it is probably a key detail."

**Step 2: Define minor details.**
Say: "A MINOR DETAIL is a small piece of information. It might be interesting, but the story would still make sense without it. For example, if a story says, 'The girl wore a red hat and went to the store,' the fact that she went to the store is a key detail — it tells us what she did. The fact that her hat was red is a minor detail — it is nice to know, but it does not change what happened."

**Step 3: Read the passage together.**
Say: "Now let's read a story. While we read, I want you to think about which parts are really important — the parts you would NEED to tell someone about the story."

Read the following passage aloud with the student.

---

**Max's Big Trick**

Max had a big brown dog named Buster. Buster could do lots of tricks. One Saturday, there was a pet show at the town park. Max wanted to take Buster. Max's mom drove them in the blue van. At the show, many pets did tricks. A cat jumped through a hoop. A bird sang a song. Then it was Buster's turn. Max said, "Buster, play dead!" Buster lay down and closed his eyes. He did not move at all. Then Max said, "Wake up!" and Buster jumped up and wagged his tail. Everyone laughed and clapped. Buster won a shiny ribbon!

---

After reading, say: "Great reading! Now let's find the key details."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Identifying a key detail.**
Say: "Let me find a key detail. I ask myself: Who is this story about? The story is about Max and his dog Buster. That is a key detail — without knowing about Max and Buster, the story does not make sense."

Write on paper:
- Key detail: Max has a dog named Buster.

"Now I ask: What happened in the story? Buster did a trick at a pet show and won a ribbon. That is another key detail — it is the main event of the story."

Write on paper:
- Key detail: Buster did a trick at the pet show and won a ribbon.

**Example 2: Identifying a minor detail.**
Say: "Now let me find a minor detail. The story says, 'Max's mom drove them in the blue van.' Is the color of the van important? Let me think... If the van were red instead of blue, would the story change? No! The color of the van does not change what happened. So 'the van was blue' is a minor detail."

Write on paper:
- Minor detail: The van was blue.

"Here is another minor detail: 'A bird sang a song.' This is interesting, but it is about a different pet, not about Max and Buster. If we took this sentence out, we would still understand the story about Buster's trick."

Write on paper:
- Minor detail: A bird sang a song.

Say: "See the difference? Key details are the parts you NEED. Minor details are extra."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice sorting details from the story into key details and minor details with facilitator support.

**Activity: Detail Sort**
Before this activity, prepare 6 index cards with these details written on them (one per card):

1. Max had a dog named Buster.
2. Max's mom drove them in the blue van.
3. There was a pet show at the town park.
4. A cat jumped through a hoop.
5. Buster did a trick called "play dead."
6. Buster won a shiny ribbon.

Place the two sorting labels on the table: "Key Detail" and "Not a Key Detail."

Say: "Here are six details from the story. Let's sort them together. For each one, we will ask: Does this detail help us understand the main story about Max and Buster? If yes, it is a key detail. If no, it is a minor detail."

**Card 1: "Max had a dog named Buster."**
Ask: "Is this important to the story? Could we understand the story without it?"
Guide: "This tells us WHO the story is about. Without this, we would not know the main character. It is a KEY DETAIL."
Place under "Key Detail."

**Card 2: "Max's mom drove them in the blue van."**
Ask: "Is this important? If we take it out, do we still understand the story?"
Guide: "We know Max got to the show, but the blue van is not important to what happened. It is a MINOR DETAIL — not a key detail."
Place under "Not a Key Detail."

**Card 3: "There was a pet show at the town park."**
Ask the student: "What do you think? Key detail or not?"
If the student is unsure: "This tells us WHERE the story takes place and what the big event was. Without this, we would not know why Max took Buster somewhere. It is a KEY DETAIL."
Place under "Key Detail."

**Card 4: "A cat jumped through a hoop."**
Ask the student: "Key detail or minor detail?"
Guide if needed: "This is about a different pet, not about Max and Buster. If we took this out, would Max's story change? No. It is NOT a key detail."
Place under "Not a Key Detail."

**Card 5: "Buster did a trick called 'play dead.'"**
Ask the student: "What do you think?"
Guide if needed: "This is WHAT HAPPENED — the main event! Without this, we would not know what Buster did at the show. It is a KEY DETAIL."
Place under "Key Detail."

**Card 6: "Buster won a shiny ribbon."**
Ask the student: "Key detail or minor detail?"
Guide if needed: "This is how the story ends — the result. It tells us what happened because of Buster's trick. It is a KEY DETAIL."
Place under "Key Detail."

After sorting, review the results:
- Key Details: Max had a dog named Buster. / There was a pet show at the town park. / Buster did a trick called "play dead." / Buster won a shiny ribbon.
- Not Key Details: Max's mom drove them in the blue van. / A cat jumped through a hoop.

Say: "Look at the key details. If I used ONLY those four details, I could retell the whole story. Let me try: 'Max had a dog named Buster. They went to a pet show at the park. Buster did a trick called play dead. Buster won a ribbon.' That IS the story! The key details are the bones of the story."

*Facilitator note: If the student places a card in the wrong category, do not simply correct them. Ask: "If we took this out of the story, would the story still make sense?" This question helps the student reason through the distinction rather than guessing.*

---

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently identifies key details in the passage.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):**
Answer these questions about "Max's Big Trick."

1. Name two key details from the story.
   - Key detail 1: _______________
   - Key detail 2: _______________

2. Name one minor detail from the story: _______________

3. Why is the detail "Buster did a trick" a key detail? _______________

**★★ (Stretch):**
Answer the On-level questions PLUS:

4. Read this detail: "It was Saturday." Is this a key detail or a minor detail? Explain why. _______________

5. Retell the story in 3-4 sentences using only key details. _______________

**★★★ (Challenge):**
Answer all the above PLUS:

6. Find one more minor detail in the story that we did not talk about. Write it down and explain why it is a minor detail. _______________

7. Imagine you are telling a friend about this story on the phone. You only have time to say two sentences. What would you say? _______________

8. Draw a picture that shows the most important part (the key moment) of the story. Write one sentence under your picture explaining what is happening.

### Interleaved Review
After finishing the key detail questions, have the student complete these brief review items:
- Ask the student one "who" question and one "why" question about the story (Lessons 1-2 review):
  - "Who won the ribbon?" (Buster.)
  - "Why did everyone laugh and clap?" (Because Buster's trick was funny / impressive.)
- Read these words aloud: trick, brown, clapped, ribbon, shiny (decoding review — blends and digraphs from Unit 1)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to determine if the student can identify key details and distinguish them from minor details.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short passage to the student (or have them read it):

"Anna got a new red bike. She rode it to the lake. She saw three ducks swimming."

1. Name one key detail in this passage. (Anna got a new bike. / She rode it to the lake. Accept either.)
2. Name one minor detail. (The bike was red. / She saw three ducks. / There were three ducks.)
3. Is "Anna rode her bike to the lake" a key detail or a minor detail? Why? (Key detail — it tells what Anna did and where she went.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies at least 1 key detail (item 1), at least 1 minor detail (item 2), and correctly classifies the detail in item 3 with a reasonable explanation.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Re-teach the concept using a very simple two-sentence story: "Tom has a cat. The cat is black." Ask: "Which part tells us about Tom? Which part just describes the cat's color?" Build from simple examples to longer passages.
- Use the "retell test": "If you could only say ONE sentence about this story, what would it be? The parts in your one sentence are the key details."
- Practice with one more short passage before moving on. Repeat this lesson with a different story if needed.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 4: Finding Key Details in Informational Text. The student will apply the same skill to nonfiction passages. Continue to ask "What are the key details?" during any reading throughout the day.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Key Detail Stars:** Re-read "Max's Big Trick." The student puts a star (or sticky note) next to each sentence that is a key detail. Count them up. Then try to retell the story using only the starred sentences.
- **Make It Up:** The student makes up their own 4-5 sentence story. Then they identify which sentences are key details and which are minor details. This reversal (creating content and then analyzing it) deepens understanding.
- **Key Detail Chain:** On a strip of paper, write each key detail of the story in order, like links in a chain. Connect them. Read the chain aloud — this is the retelling!
- **Detail Detective at Home:** During a read-aloud at bedtime or any other time, pause and ask: "Was that a key detail? How do you know?" The student becomes the Detail Detective in everyday reading.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pedagogical context:** This lesson addresses standard RL.1.1 by teaching students to identify key details — the specific pieces of textual evidence that support comprehension. Key details are the foundation for retelling (RL.1.2) and for making connections between events (RL.1.3), both of which are addressed later in this unit. A student who can reliably identify key details can answer comprehension questions, summarize a story, and eventually analyze texts at higher levels.

**The key detail vs. minor detail distinction:** This is a genuinely difficult concept for 6-7 year olds. Many students will initially say every detail is important (because it is all in the story, so it must matter). The "retell test" is the most useful tool: "If you were telling the story to someone who never read it, would you NEED this detail? Or could you skip it and they would still understand?" This makes the abstract concept concrete and functional.

**Why fiction before nonfiction:** Students are generally more familiar with stories (fiction) than informational text at this age. Starting with fiction allows them to learn the concept of key details in a familiar context before transferring it to nonfiction in Lesson 4. The progression from familiar to unfamiliar reduces cognitive load.

**Pacing notes:** This lesson is designed for approximately 35-40 minutes. The Guided Practice Detail Sort is the most important part of the lesson — this is where the student actively practices the target skill with support. If time is limited, shorten the Independent Practice to the On-level tier and ensure the student completes the Exit Ticket. If the student needs more time, this lesson can easily span two sessions: Direct Instruction and Guided Practice in session 1, Independent Practice and Exit Ticket in session 2.

**Common issues:**
- Students may insist that every detail is a key detail. Use the van color example repeatedly: "If the van were green instead of blue, would anything in the story change? No. So the color is not a key detail."
- Students may confuse "interesting" with "important." A cat jumping through a hoop IS interesting. But it is not important to Max and Buster's story. Help the student see: "Interesting does not always mean important."
- When students retell, they often include minor details and leave out key details. This is developmentally normal. Gently redirect: "That is a detail from the story! But is it one of the BIG important parts, or one of the small extra parts?"

**Connection to the methodology framework:** This lesson uses the gradual release model with a concrete sorting activity during Guided Practice to make the abstract concept of "importance" tangible. Tiered independent practice provides differentiation as defined in the [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md).

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice — Detail Sort:**
- Key Details: (1) Max had a dog named Buster. (3) There was a pet show at the town park. (5) Buster did a trick called "play dead." (6) Buster won a shiny ribbon.
- Not Key Details: (2) Max's mom drove them in the blue van. (4) A cat jumped through a hoop.

**Independent Practice — On-level (★):**
1. Key details (accept any two): Max had a dog named Buster. / There was a pet show at the park. / Buster did a trick called "play dead." / Buster won a ribbon. / Max took Buster to the pet show.
2. Minor detail (accept any one): The van was blue. / It was Saturday. / A cat jumped through a hoop. / A bird sang a song. / The dog was brown. / Max's mom drove them.
3. "Buster did a trick" is a key detail because it tells us what happened — it is the main event of the story. Without it, we would not know what Buster did at the show. (Accept any reasoning that connects to "it is important to understanding the story.")

**Independent Practice — Stretch (★★):**
4. "It was Saturday" is a minor detail. It tells us when the show happened, but if it had been on Sunday or any other day, the story would be the same. The day does not change what happened. (Accept reasoning that identifies it as non-essential to the main events.)
5. Retelling should include approximately: Max had a dog named Buster. They went to a pet show. Buster did a trick called "play dead." Buster won a ribbon. (Accept any retelling that covers the main character, the event, the trick, and the outcome.)

**Independent Practice — Challenge (★★★):**
6. Additional minor details: "The dog was big and brown." / "Max's mom drove them." / "A bird sang a song." / "Everyone laughed and clapped." (Some of these are borderline — accept the student's reasoning if it is logical. "Everyone laughed and clapped" could be argued as a key detail showing the audience reaction; accept it either way if the student provides reasoning.)
7. Two-sentence summary: Possible answer: "Max took his dog Buster to a pet show. Buster did a play dead trick and won a ribbon." (Accept any two sentences that capture the main character and main event.)
8. (Drawing and sentence — accept any response showing the trick or the ribbon, with a descriptive sentence.)

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Key detail: "Anna got a new bike" or "She rode it to the lake." (Both are key details — one tells what she got, the other tells what she did with it.)
2. Minor detail: "The bike was red." or "She saw three ducks." or "There were three ducks." (The bike color and the ducks are not essential to the core event.)
3. "Anna rode her bike to the lake" is a key detail because it tells us what Anna did (rode her bike) and where she went (the lake). Without this, we would not know what happened in the story.

**Interleaved Review:**
- "Who won the ribbon?" — Buster.
- "Why did everyone laugh and clap?" — Because Buster's trick was funny. / Because Buster did a good trick. / Because the play dead trick was impressive.
- Decoding: trick, brown, clapped, ribbon, shiny (student reads aloud — check accuracy).
