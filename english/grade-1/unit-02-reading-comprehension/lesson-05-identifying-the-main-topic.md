# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 5

## Topic
Identifying the Main Topic

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define "main topic" as what the text is mostly about
- Use the "one sentence" strategy to identify the main topic of an informational passage
- Identify the main topic of two different informational passages
- Explain how key details connect to the main topic

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify key details in both fiction and informational text (Lessons 3-4)
- Understanding of informational text and text features such as headings and bold words (Lesson 4)
- Ability to ask and answer all six question words (Lessons 1-2)

## Materials Needed
- Reading passage 1: "Busy Bees" (provided below — print or write it for the student)
- Reading passage 2: "What Makes It Rain?" (provided below — print or write it for the student)
- Paper and pencil
- Crayons or colored pencils (optional)
- 3 index cards or paper strips for the "one sentence" activity

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build the concept of "mostly about" using familiar, everyday examples before applying it to text.

**Activity: What Is It Mostly About?**
Say: "Today we are going to learn how to figure out what a text is MOSTLY about. Let's practice that idea first with something fun."

"I am going to tell you a few things about a topic. You tell me what I am mostly talking about."

**Round 1:**
"It has four legs. It barks. It wags its tail. You can play fetch with it. It is a pet."
Ask: "What am I mostly talking about?" (A dog.)

**Round 2:**
"You go there to learn. A teacher is there. You sit at a desk. You read books and do math."
Ask: "What am I mostly talking about?" (School.)

**Round 3:**
"It is cold and white. It falls from the sky. You can make a snowman with it. It melts when it gets warm."
Ask: "What am I mostly talking about?" (Snow.)

Say: "Great! You figured out what I was MOSTLY talking about each time. That is exactly what we are going to do with informational text today. We call it finding the MAIN TOPIC."

*Facilitator note: If the student answers with a full sentence (like "You are talking about what dogs do"), that is fine! The concept of "mostly about" is more important than the format of the answer at this stage.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the concept of "main topic" and introduce the "one sentence" strategy for identifying it.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Main topic:** What the text is mostly about. The main topic is the BIG idea — the one thing the whole text is about. If you had to describe the text in one word or one short phrase, that would be the main topic.
- **Supporting details:** The key details that tell more about the main topic. They give facts, examples, or descriptions that connect to the main topic.
- **One sentence strategy:** A way to find the main topic by asking: "If I could only say ONE thing about this text, what would it be?"

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Define main topic.**
Say: "The MAIN TOPIC is what the whole text is mostly about. Think of it like the title of a box. If you put all the facts from a text into a box, what would you write on the outside of the box? That label is the main topic."

"For example, if a text tells you that apples are red, apples grow on trees, apples taste sweet, and you can make pie with apples — what is the main topic? APPLES! Every fact is about apples. Apples is the main topic."

**Step 2: Teach the "one sentence" strategy.**
Say: "Here is a trick to find the main topic. It is called the ONE SENTENCE strategy. Ask yourself this question:"

Write on paper in big letters: **If I could only say ONE thing about this text, what would it be?**

"That one thing is the main topic. Let me show you."

"Imagine a text that says: 'Cats have soft fur. Cats say meow. Cats like to nap. Some cats chase mice.' If I could only say ONE thing, I would say: 'This text is about cats.' CATS is the main topic."

**Step 3: Connect key details to the main topic.**
Say: "Remember key details from our last two lessons? Key details are the important facts. They all CONNECT to the main topic. They tell us MORE about the main topic."

Write on paper:
- Main topic: Cats
- Key detail: Cats have soft fur. (tells about cats)
- Key detail: Cats say meow. (tells about cats)
- Key detail: Cats like to nap. (tells about cats)

"See? Every key detail gives us more information about the main topic. They are all connected."

**Step 4: Read the first passage together.**
Say: "Let's read our first informational text and find the main topic. Remember to think: What is this text mostly about?"

Read the following passage aloud with the student.

---

**Busy Bees**

**Bees** are very busy insects. They live together in a home called a **hive**. Inside the hive, every bee has a job. Some bees find flowers and bring back food. The food they bring back is a sweet liquid called **nectar**. Other bees take care of baby bees. The **queen bee** is the most important bee in the hive. She lays all the eggs. Bees also make honey! They turn the nectar into honey and store it in the hive.

---

After reading, say: "Now let's find the main topic."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Finding the main topic of "Busy Bees."**
Say: "Let me use the one sentence strategy. If I could only say ONE thing about this text, what would it be?"

Model thinking aloud: "This text told me that bees live in hives. Bees have jobs. Bees find food. Bees take care of babies. Bees make honey. Every single fact is about... BEES! And not just bees — it is about what bees do and how they live. So the main topic is: bees and what they do."

Write on paper:
- Main topic: Bees (what they do and how they live)

"Now let me check: Do all the key details connect to this main topic?"
- Bees live in a hive. -- about bees? Yes!
- Every bee has a job. -- about bees? Yes!
- Bees find flowers and bring back nectar. -- about bees? Yes!
- The queen bee lays eggs. -- about bees? Yes!
- Bees make honey. -- about bees? Yes!

"Every key detail is about bees. That confirms it: the main topic is BEES."

**Example 2: What the main topic is NOT.**
Say: "Could the main topic be 'honey'? Let me check. Is every fact about honey? No — the text mentions honey once at the end, but most of the facts are about what bees do, not about honey. So honey is a detail, not the main topic."

"Could the main topic be 'the queen bee'? Let me check. Is every fact about the queen bee? No — only one sentence mentions the queen. The rest is about all the bees. So the queen bee is a detail, not the main topic."

"The main topic covers the WHOLE text — not just one part."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice finding the main topic of a second passage with facilitator support, and connect key details to the main topic.

**Activity: Main Topic Detectives**
Say: "Let's read another informational text. This time, YOU are going to find the main topic. I will help you."

Read the following passage aloud with the student.

---

**What Makes It Rain?**

Did you know that **rain** comes from clouds? Here is how it works. The sun heats up water in lakes, rivers, and oceans. The warm water turns into tiny drops that float up into the sky. These tiny drops form **clouds**. Inside the clouds, the drops bump into each other and get bigger. When the drops get too big and heavy, they fall back down to the ground. That is rain! Rain gives water to plants, animals, and people. Without rain, nothing could grow.

---

After reading, guide the student through the process:

**Step 1: Ask the "one sentence" question.**
Say: "If you could only say ONE thing about this text, what would it be?"

Give the student time to think. If they struggle, offer options:
- "Is this text mostly about clouds, rain, or the sun?"
- "Think about what almost every sentence is talking about."

The answer: Rain / What makes it rain / How rain works.

**Step 2: Check with key details.**
Say: "Good! Now let's check. Let's name some key details and see if they connect to the main topic."

Ask: "What is one important fact from this text?"
Help the student name 3-4 key details:
- The sun heats water and it goes up into the sky.
- Tiny drops form clouds.
- The drops get big and fall down as rain.
- Rain gives water to plants, animals, and people.

For each one, ask: "Does this fact connect to rain?" (Yes — each detail tells us something about how rain forms or why it matters.)

**Step 3: Practice the "one sentence" strategy with cards.**
Give the student 3 index cards. On each card, write a possible "one sentence" summary:

- Card A: "This text is about rain and how it forms."
- Card B: "This text is about the sun."
- Card C: "This text is about lakes and rivers."

Ask: "Which card is the best one sentence for this text?"

The answer: Card A. The text IS mostly about rain and how it forms. The sun, lakes, and rivers are mentioned, but they are just parts of the process — not the main topic.

*Facilitator note: If the student picks Card B or C, do not just say "wrong." Ask: "Is EVERY fact in the text about the sun? Or is the sun just mentioned in one part?" Guide them to see that the main topic covers the whole text.*

**Step 4: Compare the two passages.**
Say: "We read two texts today. Let's compare their main topics."
- "What was the main topic of 'Busy Bees'?" (Bees / what bees do.)
- "What was the main topic of 'What Makes It Rain?'" (Rain / how rain forms.)
- "Both texts are informational. Both have key details. But the main topics are completely different — because they are about different things!"

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently identifies the main topic and supporting key details.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):**
Answer these questions.

**About "Busy Bees":**
1. What is the main topic of "Busy Bees"? _______________
2. Name two key details that tell more about the main topic.
   - Detail 1: _______________
   - Detail 2: _______________

**About "What Makes It Rain?":**
3. What is the main topic of "What Makes It Rain?" _______________
4. Name two key details that tell more about the main topic.
   - Detail 1: _______________
   - Detail 2: _______________

**★★ (Stretch):**
Answer the On-level questions PLUS:

5. Use the "one sentence" strategy. Write one sentence that tells what "Busy Bees" is mostly about. _______________
6. Use the "one sentence" strategy. Write one sentence that tells what "What Makes It Rain?" is mostly about. _______________
7. Is "The queen bee lays eggs" a key detail or the main topic of "Busy Bees"? Explain. _______________

**★★★ (Challenge):**
Answer all the above PLUS:

8. Find two bold words in "Busy Bees." For each one, explain why the author made it bold. _______________
9. Think about this: Could the main topic of "What Makes It Rain?" be "clouds"? Why or why not? _______________
10. Imagine you are writing a short informational text about YOUR favorite animal. Write the main topic. Then write 3 key details (important facts) about that animal.
   - Main topic: _______________
   - Key detail 1: _______________
   - Key detail 2: _______________
   - Key detail 3: _______________

### Interleaved Review
After finishing the main topic questions, have the student complete these brief review items:
- Key detail vs. minor detail review (Lessons 3-4):
  - "Bees make honey." (Key detail from the bee text.)
  - Read this sentence: "The bee was sitting on a yellow flower." Is "yellow" a key detail or a minor detail? (Minor detail — the color of the flower does not change the important information.)
- Read these words aloud: nectar, queen, clouds, heavy, ground (decoding practice)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to determine if the student can identify the main topic of a short informational passage.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short passage to the student (or have them read it):

"Frogs are small animals that live near ponds. They have long back legs for jumping. Frogs eat bugs like flies and ants. Baby frogs are called tadpoles. Tadpoles live in the water before they grow into frogs."

1. What is the main topic of this passage? (Frogs.)
2. Use the "one sentence" strategy: If you could only say ONE thing about this text, what would it be? (This text is about frogs. / This text tells about frogs and how they live.)
3. Name one key detail that supports the main topic. (Accept any: Frogs live near ponds. / They have long legs for jumping. / Frogs eat bugs. / Baby frogs are called tadpoles.)
4. Is "tadpoles" a key detail or the main topic? (A key detail — it tells about one part of a frog's life, but the whole text is about frogs, not just tadpoles.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the main topic (item 1), produces a reasonable "one sentence" statement (item 2), names at least 1 key detail (item 3), AND correctly distinguishes between key detail and main topic (item 4).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the "one sentence" strategy with very simple examples. Use 2-3 sentence passages: "Dogs bark. Dogs wag their tails. Dogs like treats." Main topic? Dogs. Build up to longer passages.
- Use the "box label" analogy: "If I put all these facts in a box, what would I write on the box?" This concrete image helps many students.
- Confusion between main topic and key detail is common. Keep emphasizing: "The main topic covers the WHOLE text. A key detail is just one fact." Practice with the question: "Is EVERY sentence about [student's answer]? Or just one sentence?"
- Repeat this lesson with a different pair of short passages in the next session.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6: Supporting Details. The student will deepen their understanding of how key details support and explain the main topic. Continue to ask "What is the main topic?" and "What are the key details?" during any nonfiction reading.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Main Topic Match:** Write 3 main topics on cards (e.g., "Dogs," "The Ocean," "Pizza") and 9 key details on separate cards (3 per topic). Mix up the details. The student sorts each detail under the correct main topic. This reinforces the connection between main topic and supporting details.
- **Write a Mini-Text:** The student picks a topic they know about (their pet, a sport, a food) and writes 3-4 sentences of key details. Then they write a "main topic" label at the top. This reverses the process — creating text instead of analyzing it.
- **Main Topic in Fiction:** Challenge the student: "Stories have a main topic too — we sometimes call it 'what the story is mostly about.' What was 'Mia's Lost Bunny' mostly about?" (Mia losing and finding her stuffed bunny.) "What was 'Max's Big Trick' mostly about?" (Max's dog Buster doing a trick at a pet show.) This bridges informational and fiction comprehension.
- **Two-Text Comparison:** Ask: "Both of our texts today were informational. How are they the same? How are they different?" (Same: both give facts, both have a main topic and key details, both have bold words. Different: different topics, different kinds of information.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pedagogical context:** This lesson directly addresses standard RI.1.2 — identify the main topic and retell key details of a text. "Main topic" is one of the most fundamental comprehension concepts in early elementary. It is the basis for summarizing, for identifying the author's purpose, and eventually for finding the main idea (a more nuanced version of main topic that emerges in Grades 2-3). Getting this concept solid in Grade 1 pays dividends for years.

**Main topic vs. main idea:** In Grade 1, we use the term "main topic" rather than "main idea." The main TOPIC is the subject — what the text is about (e.g., "bees"). The main IDEA is the point — what the author wants you to understand about the topic (e.g., "bees are important because they help plants grow"). Main idea is a Grade 2-3 concept. For now, focus on topic identification. If the student naturally states a main idea rather than just a topic ("The text is about how bees work really hard"), accept and praise it — they are ahead.

**The "one sentence" strategy:** This strategy is referenced in the Unit 2 facilitator guide as the recommended approach for main topic identification. It works because it forces the student to synthesize all the information into one statement. The constraint of "one sentence" prevents them from retelling every detail and pushes toward the big picture. Use this strategy consistently across lessons so it becomes automatic.

**Why two passages:** Using two passages in one lesson serves two purposes. First, it gives the student more practice. Second, it shows that the strategy works with ANY text, not just one specific passage. If the student can apply the "one sentence" strategy to two different texts in one lesson, they are building a transferable skill.

**The bee passage:** This passage is approximately 75 words at an early Grade 1 reading level. Bold words (bees, hive, nectar, queen bee) serve as both vocabulary and text feature practice, reinforcing Lesson 4 concepts. The passage covers multiple aspects of bee life (home, jobs, food, babies, honey) to provide rich key details.

**The rain passage:** This passage is approximately 80 words and introduces a process (the water cycle in simplified form). It is slightly more complex than the bee passage because it describes a sequence of events rather than a list of facts. The bold words (rain, clouds) reinforce text feature recognition.

**Pacing notes:** This lesson is designed for approximately 35-40 minutes. If the student grasps the main topic concept quickly from the first passage, spend less time on Guided Practice and more time on Independent Practice. If the concept is challenging, focus the full Guided Practice on the second passage and reduce Independent Practice to the On-level tier. This lesson can span two sessions if needed: passage 1 in session 1, passage 2 in session 2.

**Common issues:**
- The most common error is confusing a key detail with the main topic. A student might say the main topic of "Busy Bees" is "honey" because honey was mentioned. Redirect: "Is EVERY sentence about honey? Or is honey just one fact? The main topic is what ALL the sentences are about."
- Some students state the main topic as a full retelling: "The bees live in a hive and they find flowers and the queen lays eggs and they make honey." Gently redirect: "That is a great retelling! But can you say it in just ONE word or one short sentence? What is it ALL about?"
- Students may not understand why finding the main topic matters. Connect it to real life: "When someone asks you 'What is that book about?' they want the main topic — the short answer, not the whole book!"

**Connection to the methodology framework:** This lesson uses the gradual release model with explicit strategy instruction (the "one sentence" strategy), scaffolded practice across two passages, and tiered independent work. The use of a named, repeatable strategy aligns with the metacognitive skill-building approach defined in the [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md).
