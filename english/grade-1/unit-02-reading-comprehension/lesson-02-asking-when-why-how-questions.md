# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 2

## Topic
Asking When, Why, and How Questions

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the three question words: when, why, and how
- Ask when, why, and how questions about a short fiction passage
- Answer when, why, and how questions about a short fiction passage, using evidence from the text and simple inference

## Prerequisites
- Ability to ask and answer who, what, and where questions about a text (Lesson 1)
- Ability to decode Grade 1 connected text with at least 90% accuracy (Unit 1)
- Understanding that questions end with a question mark

## Materials Needed
- 3 new index cards or strips of paper with the words WHEN, WHY, and HOW written in large letters
- The 3 question word cards from Lesson 1 (WHO, WHAT, WHERE)
- The reading passage "Lily's Garden" (provided below — print or write it for the student)
- Paper and pencil
- Crayons or colored pencils (optional)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review who, what, and where questions from Lesson 1 and prepare to learn three new question words.

**Activity: Quick Review**
Place the WHO, WHAT, and WHERE cards on the table.

Say: "Last time, we learned three question words. Let's see if you remember them."

Hold up each card and ask: "What kind of answer does this question word need?"
- WHO = a person or animal
- WHAT = a thing or something that happened
- WHERE = a place

"Great! Now let's practice. I will say a question. You point to the right question word card."

- "Who is your best friend?" (WHO)
- "What is your favorite game?" (WHAT)
- "Where do birds make their nests?" (WHERE)

Say: "You remember those well! Today we are going to learn three MORE question words. After today, you will know all six question words. That means you will be able to ask questions about anything you read!"

*Facilitator note: If the student does not remember Lesson 1 content well, spend a few extra minutes reviewing before introducing new material. You can re-read the sentence from the Lesson 1 warm-up ("A boy named Sam ate a sandwich at school") and practice asking who, what, and where questions about it.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the three new question words — when, why, and how — and explain that why and how often require thinking beyond what the text says directly.

### Key Vocabulary
- **When:** A question word that asks about a time. When did it happen? The answer is a time — morning, night, Tuesday, last week, after lunch.
- **Why:** A question word that asks about a reason. Why did it happen? The answer tells a reason — because she was hungry, because it was raining.
- **How:** A question word that asks about the way something happens. How did it happen? The answer describes the way or the steps — she did it by digging a hole, he ran fast.
- **Inference:** Using clues from the text plus what you already know to figure something out. Sometimes the answer is not written in the exact words — you have to think about it.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the three new question words.**
Hold up each new card as you teach it.

Say: "We already know who, what, and where. Today we are adding three more: when, why, and how."

Hold up the WHEN card.
"WHEN asks about a time. If I ask, 'When do you eat breakfast?' — the answer is a time. You might say 'in the morning.' When = time."

Hold up the WHY card.
"WHY asks about a reason. If I ask, 'Why do you wear a coat?' — the answer is a reason. You might say 'because it is cold.' Why = reason. The answer to a why question often starts with the word 'because.'"

Hold up the HOW card.
"HOW asks about the way something happens. If I ask, 'How do you get to school?' — the answer tells the way. You might say 'I ride in a car' or 'I walk.' How = the way it happens."

**Step 2: Explain that why and how are harder.**
Say: "Here is something important. Who, what, and where questions are usually easy to find in the story. The answer is right there in the words. But why and how questions are a little different. Sometimes you have to think about the story and use clues to figure out the answer. The words might not say the answer exactly, but you can figure it out. That is called making an inference — using clues to figure something out."

Give a quick real-life example:
"Imagine you see your friend crying. You ask, 'Why are you crying?' Nobody wrote the answer on a page. But you look for clues — maybe your friend's knee is scraped. So you figure out: she is crying because she fell down and hurt her knee. You used clues to figure out the reason. That is an inference."

**Step 3: Read the passage together.**
Say: "Now let's read a story. After we read, we are going to ask all six kinds of questions — who, what, where, when, why, and how."

Read the following passage aloud with the student.

---

**Lily's Garden**

Lily wanted to grow a flower. One morning, she went to the yard with her mom. Lily dug a small hole in the dirt. She put a tiny seed in the hole. Then she put dirt on top. Every day, Lily gave the seed water. She waited and waited. After many days, a green stem popped up! Lily was so happy. She jumped up and down. Her little seed was growing into a plant!

---

After reading, say: "What a nice story! Let's talk about it."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: A "when" question.**
Say: "Let me ask a 'when' question. When did Lily go to the yard?"

Model thinking aloud: "I need to find a time. Let me look at the story. The second sentence says, 'One morning, she went to the yard with her mom.' It says 'one morning.' That is a time. So the answer is: Lily went to the yard one morning."

Write on paper:
- Question: When did Lily go to the yard?
- Answer: One morning.

Say: "See? 'When' asked about time, and I found the time in the story: one morning."

**Example 2: A "why" question (inference needed).**
Say: "Now a 'why' question. Why did Lily give the seed water every day?"

Model thinking aloud: "Hmm, the story says, 'Every day, Lily gave the seed water.' But it does not say exactly why. I have to think about it. I know that plants need water to grow. Lily wanted to grow a flower. So she gave it water because seeds need water to grow. That is my inference — I used what I know about plants plus what the story told me."

Write on paper:
- Question: Why did Lily give the seed water?
- Answer: Because seeds need water to grow, and Lily wanted to grow a flower.

Say: "The answer was not written in the exact words. I had to think about it. That is what makes 'why' questions harder — and more interesting!"

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice asking and answering when, why, and how questions about the passage, with facilitator support.

**Activity: Six-Question Challenge**
Say: "Let's do a Six-Question Challenge! We are going to ask questions about Lily's Garden using all six question words."

Place all six question word cards on the table: WHO, WHAT, WHERE, WHEN, WHY, HOW.

**Round 1 — Facilitator asks, student answers (5 minutes):**

Ask these questions one at a time. Encourage the student to look back at the text for answers.

1. "Who wanted to grow a flower?" (Lily.)
   - If the student struggles: "Look at the first sentence."

2. "What did Lily put in the hole?" (A tiny seed.)
   - If the student struggles: "Read the fourth sentence."

3. "Where did Lily dig the hole?" (In the yard / in the dirt.)
   - If the student struggles: "The story says Lily went to the yard and dug a hole. Where was the hole?"

4. "When did a green stem pop up?" (After many days.)
   - If the student struggles: "Find the sentence about the green stem. What does it say before 'a green stem popped up'?"

5. "Why was Lily happy?" (Because her seed was growing. / Because a green stem popped up.)
   - If the student struggles: "What happened right before Lily was happy? The stem popped up. So why do you think she was happy?"

6. "How did Lily take care of her seed?" (She gave it water every day.)
   - If the student struggles: "The story tells us something Lily did for the seed every day. What was it?"

For each answer, ask: "Can you show me where in the story you found that?" For questions 5 and 6, acknowledge if the student uses an inference: "Good thinking! You had to figure that out — it was not written in the exact words."

**Round 2 — Student creates questions (5 minutes):**

Say: "Now pick any three question word cards. For each one, ask me a question about the story."

Help the student form questions as needed. Provide sentence starters:
- "When ___?"
- "Why ___?"
- "How ___?"

Possible student questions:
- "When did Lily give the seed water?" (Every day.)
- "Why did Lily dig a hole?" (To put the seed in it.)
- "How did Lily feel when the plant grew?" (She was happy — she jumped up and down.)
- "Who helped Lily?" (Her mom.)

*Facilitator note: Students often find "how" the hardest question word to use. If the student cannot form a "how" question, offer a completed one and have them read it to you: "How did Lily show she was happy?" (She jumped up and down.)*

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently answers questions that include all six question words.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):**
Answer these questions about "Lily's Garden." Write or dictate your answers.

1. Who went to the yard with Lily? _______________
2. What popped up from the ground? _______________
3. Where did Lily plant her seed? _______________
4. When did Lily water the seed? _______________

**★★ (Stretch):**
Answer the On-level questions PLUS these:

5. Why did Lily jump up and down? _______________
6. How did Lily plant the seed? (Describe the steps.) _______________
7. Write your own "why" question about the story: _______________

**★★★ (Challenge):**
Answer all the above questions PLUS:

8. Write one question for each question word (who, what, where, when, why, how) about the story. Then write the answer to each.
9. Think about this: Why do you think Lily's mom went to the yard with her? (This is not in the text — use your own thinking!) _______________

### Interleaved Review
After finishing the comprehension questions, have the student complete these brief review items from Lesson 1:
- Read this sentence aloud: "The cat sat on a soft bed." (Decoding review)
- "I am going to say a question. Tell me what question word it starts with."
  - "Who made dinner?" (Who)
  - "Where is the dog?" (Where)
  - "What fell off the table?" (What)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to determine if the student understands all six question words.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this sentence to the student (or have them read it): "Last night, Ben ran home fast because it started to rain."

1. Answer: When did Ben run home? (Last night.)
2. Answer: Why did Ben run home? (Because it started to rain.)
3. Answer: How did Ben run? (Fast.)
4. Match each question word to what it asks about:
   - WHEN = (a) a time
   - WHY = (b) a reason
   - HOW = (c) the way something happens

**Mastery = Student correctly answers at least 2 of 3 questions (items 1-3) AND correctly matches at least 2 of 3 question words (item 4).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on whichever question words the student missed. If "why" is the problem, practice with real-life situations: "Why do we brush our teeth? Because ___." "Why do we eat food? Because ___."
- If "when" is the problem, review time words: morning, night, today, yesterday, after, before, then, last week.
- If "how" is the problem, practice with everyday activities: "How do you make a sandwich? First you ___, then you ___." "How do you tie a shoe?"
- Repeat this lesson with a different passage in the next session.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 3: Finding Key Details in Fiction. Continue to reinforce all six question words in warm-ups and during read-alouds. The question words are a tool the student will use for the rest of this unit and beyond.

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Question Word Dice:** Write the six question words on a cube (or slips in a bag). The student picks a question word at random, then asks a question about the story using that word. Repeat 4-6 times.
- **Question Match:** Write 6 questions on strips and 6 answers on separate strips. Mix them up. The student matches each question to its answer.
- **My Own Story Questions:** The student draws a picture of something they did today (or makes up a short story). Then they write or say one when, one why, and one how question about their picture. They answer their own questions.
- **Compare Easy and Hard Questions:** Discuss with the student: "Which questions were easy to answer — the ones you could find in the words? Which ones were harder — the ones you had to think about?" This builds metacognitive awareness.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pedagogical context:** This lesson continues to address standard RL.1.1 — asking and answering questions about key details in a text. It builds on Lesson 1 by adding the three question words that require deeper thinking. Together, Lessons 1 and 2 give the student a complete set of six question words that will be used throughout the rest of this unit.

**Why when, why, and how are harder:** "When" requires understanding of time markers, which can be implicit (e.g., the story might not say "on Tuesday" but might say "after lunch"). "Why" requires understanding cause and effect — the student must connect a reason to an event, and that reason may not be stated directly. "How" requires understanding of process or manner. These are abstract concepts for a 6-7 year old. Expect that mastery of "why" and "how" will develop gradually across this unit and the rest of Grade 1.

**Inference is a big concept:** This lesson introduces the idea of inference very gently. Do not expect the student to use the word "inference" independently. The goal is simply for the student to understand that sometimes the answer is right there in the words, and sometimes you have to think about it. Use the phrase "use clues from the story" rather than formal inference terminology if the student is confused.

**The word "because":** Teaching students to start their "why" answers with "because" is a powerful scaffold. It forces them to articulate a reason rather than simply restating the event. Encourage this throughout the unit: "Start your answer with 'because.'"

**Pacing notes:** This lesson is designed for approximately 35 minutes. The Direct Instruction section may take a bit longer than Lesson 1 because the concepts are more abstract. If time is short, prioritize the Guided Practice Round 1 (student answering questions) over Round 2 (student asking questions). If the student finds "why" and "how" particularly challenging, it is perfectly fine to spread this lesson across two sessions.

**Common issues:**
- Students may confuse "when" and "where" because they sound similar. Emphasize: "wheN = time (N for Now). wheRE = place (RE for Right hERE)." Use hand motions: point to a pretend clock for "when" and point around the room for "where."
- For "why" questions, some students will say "because" and then stop. Prompt: "Because what? What is the reason?"
- For "how" questions, students may give a one-word answer. Prompt: "Can you tell me more? What did they do first? Then what?"

**Connection to the methodology framework:** This lesson follows the gradual release model (I Do / We Do / You Do) with explicit vocabulary instruction, scaffolded practice, and tiered independent work as defined in the [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md).
