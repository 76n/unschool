# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic
Comparing Fiction and Informational Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define fiction as a made-up story and informational text as writing that gives real facts
- Identify whether a given passage is fiction or informational text and explain their reasoning
- Describe at least one thing they can learn from each type of text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to find key details in fiction (Lesson 3) and informational text (Lesson 4)
- Understanding of main topic and supporting details (Lessons 5-6)
- Familiarity with cause and effect (Lesson 7) and sequence connections (Lesson 8)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copies of both passages: "Buster's Big Day" (fiction) and "All About Dogs" (informational)
- Comparison chart (two columns: "Fiction" and "Informational Text")
- Pencils or crayons
- Two different colored highlighters or crayons (optional, for marking clues)
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Labels or signs: one that says "FICTION" and one that says "INFORMATIONAL"

## Standards Alignment
- **RL.1.1:** Ask and answer questions about key details in a text.
- **RI.1.2:** Identify the main topic and retell key details of a text.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about different types of text by connecting to students' reading experiences.

**Activity: Real or Made Up?**

Say to students:
> "I am going to say some sentences. For each one, I want you to stand up if you think it is REAL and sit down if you think it is MADE UP."

Read these sentences one at a time:
1. "Dogs have four legs." (Real — stand up!)
2. "A dog flew to the moon in a rocket ship." (Made up — sit down!)
3. "Fish live in water." (Real — stand up!)
4. "A fish put on a hat and went to school." (Made up — sit down!)
5. "Birds have feathers." (Real — stand up!)

After the activity, say:
> "You did great! Some of those sentences told us real facts, and some were silly and made up. When we read books, some books tell us real facts and some books tell us made-up stories. Today we are going to learn the difference between these two kinds of text."

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the difference between fiction and informational text and model how to identify each type.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fiction:** A story that is made up. It comes from the author's imagination. It has characters, a setting, and events that are not real.
- **Informational text:** Writing that gives us real facts and true information about a topic. It teaches us about real things.
- **Compare:** To look at two things and find what is the same and what is different.
- **Characters:** The people or animals in a story.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Teach fiction and informational text (3 minutes)**

Draw a T-chart on the board:

| Fiction (Made-Up Stories) | Informational Text (Real Facts) |
|---|---|
| Made up by the author | Tells true facts |
| Has characters (people/animals in the story) | Teaches about real things |
| Has a setting (where/when) | Has a main topic and details |
| Things happen that may not be real | Everything is real and true |
| Entertains us (makes us laugh, feel, wonder) | Teaches us something new |

Say:
> "FICTION is a made-up story. It comes from the author's imagination. Fiction has characters — like a boy, a girl, or a talking animal. Things happen in fiction that may not happen in real life. Fiction entertains us — it makes us laugh, feel happy, or wonder what will happen next."

> "INFORMATIONAL TEXT tells us real facts. It is about real things in the real world. It teaches us something new. It has a main topic and supporting details — just like we learned in our last lessons!"

> "Both kinds of text are important. We can enjoy fiction AND learn from informational text. Sometimes we can even learn things from both!"

**Step 2: Read both passages aloud (4 minutes)**

Say:
> "Now I am going to read two short passages. Both are about the same topic: DOGS. But one is fiction and one is informational. Listen carefully and see if you can figure out which is which!"

Read Passage 1 (display it for students):

> **Buster's Big Day**
>
> Buster was a little brown dog who loved to dig. One day, Buster dug a hole so deep that he found a shiny gold bone! "Wow!" said Buster. "This is the best bone in the whole world!" Buster put on his red cape and ran to show all his friends. The cats, the birds, and even the fish in the pond all clapped for Buster. It was the best day ever!

Read Passage 2 (display it for students):

> **All About Dogs**
>
> Dogs are popular pets. There are many kinds of dogs. Some dogs are big and some are small. Dogs need food, water, and a safe place to live. They like to run, play, and go for walks. Dogs can learn tricks like sit, stay, and shake. A dog wags its tail when it is happy. Dogs are good friends to people.

**Step 3: Model comparing the two texts (3 minutes)**

Say:
> "Let me think about these two passages. Are they the same kind of text? Let me check."

> "Passage 1, 'Buster's Big Day' — Does it have a character? Yes, Buster the dog. Does Buster do things that could really happen? Well, dogs DO dig holes. But would a dog find a gold bone? No, that is made up. Would a dog put on a red cape? No! And would cats, birds, and fish all clap? No — that is silly and made up. So this is FICTION."

> "Passage 2, 'All About Dogs' — Does it have a character? No, it does not tell a story about one dog. Does it tell us facts? Yes! It tells us dogs need food, water, and a safe place. It tells us dogs can learn tricks. These are all TRUE facts. So this is INFORMATIONAL TEXT."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: What clues tell us "Buster's Big Day" is fiction?**

Say:
> "Let me find the clues that tell me 'Buster's Big Day' is fiction. I will look for things that could NOT happen in real life."

Write on the board under "Fiction Clues":
- Buster found a gold bone (not real).
- Buster said "Wow!" — dogs cannot talk.
- Buster put on a red cape — dogs do not wear capes.
- Cats, birds, and fish clapped — animals cannot clap.

> "All of these things are made up. They are clues that this is fiction."

**Worked Example 2: What can we learn from each text?**

Say:
> "Even though 'Buster's Big Day' is fiction, can we learn anything from it? Hmm. Well, we can learn that dogs like to dig. That IS true about real dogs! So sometimes fiction includes some true things, but the story itself is made up."

> "From 'All About Dogs,' we can learn real facts: dogs need food and water, they can learn tricks, and they wag their tails when happy. These are all true."

Write on the board:
- From fiction (Buster): Dogs like to dig. (This is true, even in a made-up story!)
- From informational text (All About Dogs): Dogs need food, water, and shelter. Dogs can learn tricks. Dogs wag their tails when happy.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying fiction and informational text with facilitator support.

**Activity: Sort It Out!**

Give each student copies of both passages and a comparison chart with two columns: "Fiction — Buster's Big Day" and "Informational — All About Dogs."

Say:
> "Now it is your turn! I am going to read some sentences from the passages. I want you to tell me: Is it from the fiction story or the informational text? Then write it in the correct column on your chart."

**Sentence 1:** "Buster put on his red cape."
Ask: "Is this fiction or informational?" (Fiction — dogs do not wear capes.)
Students write it in the Fiction column.

**Sentence 2:** "Dogs can learn tricks like sit, stay, and shake."
Ask: "Is this fiction or informational?" (Informational — this is a real fact.)
Students write it in the Informational column.

**Sentence 3:** "The cats, the birds, and even the fish in the pond all clapped."
Ask: "Is this fiction or informational?" (Fiction — animals cannot clap.)
Students write it in the Fiction column.

**Sentence 4:** "Dogs need food, water, and a safe place to live."
Ask: "Is this fiction or informational?" (Informational — this is a true fact.)
Students write it in the Informational column.

Now ask students to work with a partner for 2-3 minutes to find ONE more sentence from each passage and add it to their chart.

**Facilitator Prompts:**
- "Could this really happen? Or is it made up?"
- "Does this sentence teach us a real fact, or is it part of a story?"
- "How do you know this is fiction? What is the clue?"
- "What does this sentence teach us that is true about dogs?"

**Supporting Struggling Students:**
- Use the "Could this really happen?" test. Read each sentence and ask: "Could this happen in real life?" If yes, it is informational. If no, it is fiction.
- Place the FICTION and INFORMATIONAL signs on the table. Have the student physically place sentence strips under the correct sign.
- Focus on the most obvious clues first (talking dog, wearing a cape) before moving to subtler differences.

After partner work, share out. Ask 2-3 pairs what sentences they found and where they placed them.

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify fiction and informational text and explain their reasoning.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Read (or listen to) each passage below. Write "F" for Fiction or "I" for Informational next to each one. Then write one reason for your answer.

> **Passage A:** "Rabbits live in holes called burrows. They eat grass, clover, and vegetables. Baby rabbits are called kittens."

> **Passage B:** "Rosie the rabbit put on her glasses and read a book by the fire. 'What a lovely story!' she said."

1. Passage A is: ___ Reason: ___
2. Passage B is: ___ Reason: ___

**Stretch:**
Complete the activity above. Then answer: "What is one real fact you learned from Passage A?" and "What is one thing in Passage B that could NOT happen in real life?"

**Challenge:**
Complete all of the above. Then write two sentences of your own — one that could be in a fiction story about a rabbit and one that could be in an informational text about rabbits. Label each one.

### Interleaved Review
After completing the fiction/informational activity, students answer one review question:
- "Read Passage A again. What is the main topic? Name two supporting details." (Review of Lessons 5-6.)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Fiction is a story that is ___. (made up / real) — Circle one.
2. Informational text tells us ___. (made-up stories / real facts) — Circle one.
3. Listen to this sentence: "The pig cooked dinner for his family." Is this fiction or informational? (Fiction.) How do you know? (Pigs cannot cook.)
4. Name one thing you learned from the informational text "All About Dogs." (Accept any fact from the passage.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the warm-up "Real or Made Up?" activity and do more examples.
- Use two very short, obvious passages (e.g., "The cat went to school and did math" vs. "Cats have whiskers and soft fur") and sort them together.
- Create a simple anchor chart with pictures: fiction side shows a cartoon animal doing something silly, informational side shows a real animal photo with facts.
- Ask the "Could this really happen?" question repeatedly until the student can apply it independently.

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 10, which is a cumulative review of all comprehension strategies from the unit.
- Encourage the student to sort their home reading into "fiction" and "informational" piles and share with a family member.

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Same Topic, Two Ways:** Students choose an animal and write (or dictate) two sentences — one that could be in a fiction story and one that gives a real fact. They illustrate both.
- **Text Type Walk:** Walk around the classroom library. Students point to books and say whether they think each one is fiction or informational based on the cover and title.
- **Venn Diagram:** Draw a Venn diagram. Students write what is ONLY true of fiction on one side, what is ONLY true of informational text on the other side, and what is true of BOTH in the middle (e.g., both have words, both can be about animals, both have a topic).

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Pacing:** Students at this age love silly fiction, so they tend to enjoy identifying the "impossible" clues. Use their enthusiasm to reinforce the concept. The warm-up is quick and energizing — let them move!
- **Common Misconceptions:**
  - Students may think that if a fiction story includes ANY true fact (e.g., "dogs like to dig"), the whole text is informational. Clarify: "A fiction story CAN include true things, but if the story is made up — if it has made-up characters doing made-up things — it is still fiction."
  - Some students may think informational text is "boring" and fiction is "fun." Address this gently: "Informational text can be really exciting too! Learning about real animals, space, or the ocean is amazing!"
- **Preparation:** Print both passages clearly. Consider putting them on different colored paper (e.g., fiction on yellow, informational on blue) to create a visual association during the lesson.
- **Building Toward Assessment:** This lesson is the second-to-last before the unit assessment. Make sure students feel confident distinguishing the two text types, as the assessment will include both.
- **Connection to Standards:** This lesson supports both RL.1.1 (working with fiction text and its details) and RI.1.2 (identifying the main topic and details in informational text). Comparing the two types strengthens both skills.
- **Methodology Note:** Comparing two texts on the same topic is a high-leverage strategy that builds critical thinking. The comparison chart provides a visual scaffold. See the Teaching Methodology Framework for more on comparison-based learning activities.

### Independent Practice

**Passage A:** I (Informational)
- Reason: It tells real facts about rabbits. (Accept any version of "it has true information" or "it teaches facts.")

**Passage B:** F (Fiction)
- Reason: Rabbits cannot wear glasses, read books, or talk. (Accept any identification of a made-up element.)

**Stretch answers:**
- Real fact from Passage A: Baby rabbits are called kittens. / Rabbits live in burrows. / Rabbits eat grass, clover, and vegetables. (Any fact from the passage.)
- Made-up thing from Passage B: A rabbit putting on glasses / reading a book / talking. (Any made-up element.)

**Challenge sample answers:**
- Fiction: "Rosie the rabbit baked a cake and had a party with her friends." (or any made-up story sentence about a rabbit)
- Informational: "Rabbits have long ears and short tails." (or any true fact about rabbits)

### Interleaved Review
- **Main topic of Passage A:** Rabbits.
- **Supporting details (any two):** Rabbits live in holes called burrows. They eat grass, clover, and vegetables. Baby rabbits are called kittens.

### Exit Ticket
1. made up
2. real facts
3. Fiction. Pigs cannot cook. (Accept any explanation that identifies the sentence as impossible/made up.)
4. Accept any fact from "All About Dogs": dogs need food and water, dogs can learn tricks, dogs wag their tails when happy, dogs are popular pets, there are many kinds of dogs, dogs like to run and play, etc.
