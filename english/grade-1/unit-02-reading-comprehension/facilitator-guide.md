# Facilitator Guide — Grade 1 ELA, Unit 2: Reading Comprehension

**Grade:** 1
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 2 — Reading Comprehension
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4-5 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding throughout)

---

## Unit Overview

In Unit 1, your student developed the phonics skills to decode words — consonant digraphs, blends, vowel teams, and connected text reading. Now it is time to focus on what those decoded words mean. This unit shifts from "learning to read" to "reading to learn," building the comprehension skills that turn word-reading into genuine understanding.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Ask and answer who, what, where, when, why, and how questions about a text
- Identify key details in both fiction and informational text
- Identify the main topic of an informational text
- Describe how details support the main topic
- Describe connections between events in a story (what happened and why)
- Describe connections between ideas, steps, or pieces of information in an informational text
- Use basic comprehension strategies: predicting, visualizing, questioning, and retelling

**Why this matters:** Decoding is only half of reading. A student who can read every word on a page but cannot tell you what the text is about is not truly reading. Comprehension is the purpose of reading — and it is a skill that must be explicitly taught, not just assumed. This unit builds the mental habits that strong readers use automatically: asking questions, noticing important details, connecting ideas, and checking understanding. These habits, established now, will serve the student through every grade and every subject.

---

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Decode words with consonant digraphs (sh, ch, th, wh) and common blends
- [ ] Decode words with long vowel patterns (silent-e, common vowel teams)
- [ ] Read grade-level connected text with reasonable accuracy (not perfect — 90%+ word accuracy is sufficient)
- [ ] Track print left to right, pointing to words as they read
- [ ] Retell a simple story with characters, setting, and major events (Kindergarten Unit 2 skill)

If the student is still struggling to decode most words, spend more time on Unit 1 phonics skills before beginning this unit. Comprehension instruction works best when the student is not exhausting all their cognitive energy on decoding.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Grade-appropriate fiction texts:** 3-5 short stories or picture books with clear characters, settings, and events. Look for books with:
  - Simple but engaging plots
  - Clear cause-and-effect relationships
  - Illustrations that support but don't replace the text
  - Good options: Frog and Toad series, Henry and Mudge series, Elephant & Piggie books, or any beginning reader chapter books

- **Grade-appropriate informational texts:** 3-5 short nonfiction texts or books about topics interesting to the student. Look for:
  - Clear main topic with supporting details
  - Text features like headings, bold words, and photographs
  - Topics the student is curious about (animals, space, weather, sports, etc.)
  - Good options: National Geographic Readers, DK Readers, or any "all about ___" nonfiction books for early readers

- **Sticky notes or small paper strips** (for marking important parts)
- **A reading journal or blank paper** (for drawing and writing responses)
- **Question word cards:** Index cards with WHO, WHAT, WHERE, WHEN, WHY, HOW written on them

If you do not have access to published books, you can use the reading passages provided within the lessons. These passages are designed to be age-appropriate and to teach the target skills.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Asking Who, What, and Where Questions | 1 session |
| 2 | Asking When, Why, and How Questions | 1 session |
| 3 | Finding Key Details in Fiction | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Finding Key Details in Informational Text | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Identifying the Main Topic | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Supporting Details | 1-2 sessions |
| 7 | Connecting Events in a Story | 1-2 sessions |
| 8 | Connecting Ideas in Informational Text | 1-2 sessions |
| 9 | Comparing Fiction and Informational Text | 1 session |
| 10 | Comprehension Strategies Review | 1-2 sessions |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session (25-30 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lessons 1-2 (question words) are foundational. If the student grasps the question types quickly, these can be brief. If the student struggles to formulate questions, spend an extra session on each.
- Lessons 3-6 (key details and main topic) are the core of this unit. Take as many sessions as needed — these skills are worth building thoroughly.
- Lessons 7-8 (connections) are more cognitively demanding. If the student needs extra time, that is expected and appropriate.
- Always use a variety of texts. Each lesson can be repeated with different stories or informational passages to build the skill with diverse content.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student can decode every word but cannot answer "What was this about?" | The student is focused entirely on word-level processing and not building meaning | Pause reading after every 2-3 sentences. Ask: "What just happened?" or "What did we just learn?" Build the habit of checking comprehension as you go, not just at the end. |
| Student answers every question by pointing to a picture rather than using the text | The student relies on illustrations for comprehension (a valid strategy in kindergarten but needs to transition to text-based answers in Grade 1) | Say: "The picture does show that! Now let's find where the words tell us the same thing." Guide the student to locate the specific sentence that answers the question. Pictures support comprehension but should not replace text engagement. |
| Student confuses fiction and nonfiction | The distinction between "made up" and "real" is still developing | Before reading, always say: "This is a story someone made up" (fiction) or "This is about something real" (informational). Use a simple anchor: "Fiction = fake (made up). Informational = info (real facts)." |
| Student answers "why" questions with "because" but cannot explain further | Understanding causation is a higher-order skill that develops throughout Grade 1 | Model complete "because" answers: "The dog ran away BECAUSE the gate was open." Practice with everyday situations: "Why do we wear coats? Because it is cold outside." Accept partial causal reasoning and build from it. |
| Student can retell events but cannot identify the "main topic" of informational text | "Main topic" is an abstract concept — the student is used to thinking about stories (events) but not ideas (topics) | Use the "one sentence" strategy: "If you could only say ONE thing about this text, what would it be?" That one thing is usually the main topic. Practice with familiar subjects first: "What is this book mostly about? Dogs. Yes — the main topic is dogs." |
| Student says "I don't know" when asked comprehension questions | This may indicate genuine confusion, lack of confidence, or the question being too abstract | Rephrase the question more concretely. Instead of "What is the main idea?" try "What is this page mostly telling us about?" Instead of "Why did the character do that?" try "What happened right before she ran? What made her want to run?" |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day:

- "What do you think will happen next in the story? Why?"
- "Who is your favorite character? Why do you like them?"
- "If you could ask the author one question, what would it be?"
- "What did you learn from that book that you didn't know before?"
- "Does this story remind you of anything in your own life?"
- "What was the most important thing that happened in the story?"
- "Can you tell me three facts you learned from this text?"
- "What is this book mostly about? Can you say it in one sentence?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- The student can decode words accurately but consistently cannot answer any comprehension questions about what was read — even with simple, direct questions like "Who was the story about?"
- The student appears to forget what happened at the beginning of a short text by the time they reach the end (working memory concern)
- The student shows no interest in or curiosity about the content of texts, despite being offered topics they normally enjoy
- The student can answer questions about read-alouds (when someone reads TO them) but not about texts they read themselves (indicating decoding is consuming too much cognitive energy)
- After 3-4 weeks of comprehension instruction, the student shows no improvement in answering basic who/what/where questions

If you see these signs, consider:
- Dropping back to easier texts where decoding is automatic — comprehension improves when the student doesn't have to work hard on word reading
- Having the student's working memory and processing speed evaluated (sometimes comprehension difficulty has a cognitive basis)
- Using more read-alouds to build comprehension skills in the absence of decoding demands — comprehension strategies can be taught through listening before transferring to reading
- Consulting with a reading specialist

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 3: Writing Foundations**, where they write their own opinion pieces, informative texts, and narratives. The comprehension skills from this unit directly support writing: a student who can identify key details in a text can include key details in their own writing. A student who understands main topic can write informative texts with a clear topic. A student who can retell events in sequence can write narratives in sequence.

Between units, continue daily reading with comprehension conversations. The single best thing you can do for reading comprehension is read with your student every day and talk about what you read. "What happened? Why do you think that? What will happen next? What did you learn?" — these questions, asked consistently, build powerful reading habits.
