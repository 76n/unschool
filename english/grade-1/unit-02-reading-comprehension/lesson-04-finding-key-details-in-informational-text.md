# English Language Arts — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 4

## Topic
Finding Key Details in Informational Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify key details in a short informational (nonfiction) passage
- Recognize basic text features — bold words and headings — and explain their purpose
- Distinguish between key details and minor details in informational text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify key details and minor details in fiction (Lesson 3)
- Ability to ask and answer who, what, where, when, why, and how questions (Lessons 1-2)
- Ability to decode Grade 1 connected text with at least 90% accuracy (Unit 1)

## Materials Needed
- The reading passage "All About Penguins" (provided below — print or write it for the student, preserving the bold words and heading)
- Paper and pencil
- Crayons or colored pencils (optional, for illustrating)
- Sticky notes or small paper strips (for marking details in the text)
- 2 sorting labels: "Key Detail" and "Minor Detail" (from Lesson 3, or make new ones)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about the difference between fiction (made-up stories) and informational text (real facts), and review key details from Lesson 3.

**Activity: Fiction or Facts?**
Say: "Last time, we found key details in a made-up story — a fiction story. Today we are going to find key details in a different kind of text. This kind of text is called INFORMATIONAL TEXT. Informational text tells us real facts about real things. It is not a made-up story."

"Let me give you some examples. I will say something, and you tell me if it sounds like fiction or informational text."

- "Once upon a time, a frog flew to the moon." (Fiction — frogs cannot fly to the moon!)
- "Frogs live near ponds and lakes." (Informational — that is a real fact.)
- "A magic cat baked a cake and invited all the animals to a party." (Fiction — cats do not bake cakes!)
- "Cats have soft fur and sharp claws." (Informational — that is a real fact.)

Say: "Good! Today we are going to read an informational text about real animals. It will teach us real facts. And we are going to find the key details — the most important facts."

"Remember from last time: a key detail is an important piece of information that you NEED to understand the text. A minor detail is a small extra piece that is nice to know but not necessary."

*Facilitator note: If the student is unsure about the fiction vs. nonfiction distinction, keep it simple: "Fiction = fake, made up. Informational = real information, real facts." This distinction is addressed more formally later in the unit but is helpful to establish here.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the student to find key details in informational text and introduce text features (bold words and headings).

### Key Vocabulary
- **Informational text:** A text that gives real facts and information about a topic. Also called nonfiction.
- **Text feature:** A special part of the text that helps the reader find or understand information. Examples: headings, bold words, pictures with labels.
- **Heading:** A title or label at the top of a section. It tells you what that section is about. Headings are usually bigger or bolder than the rest of the text.
- **Bold word:** A word printed in thick, dark letters to show that it is important. Bold words often name something the reader should pay special attention to.
- **Fact:** Something that is true and can be checked. Example: "Penguins are birds."
- **Key detail (in informational text):** An important fact that helps the reader understand the main topic.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain how informational text is different from fiction.**
Say: "In a fiction story, we look for key details about characters and events — who did what, where, and why. In informational text, we look for key details about the TOPIC — the real thing the text is teaching us about. The key details are the most important facts."

"For example, if a text is about dogs, the key details would be the most important facts about dogs — like what they eat, where they live, and what they do. A minor detail might be what color one dog is."

**Step 2: Introduce text features.**
Say: "Informational text often has special features that help us read. Today we are going to learn about two of them: headings and bold words."

Write the word **Heading** at the top of a piece of paper in big letters.
"A HEADING is like a title for a section. It tells you what you are about to read about. When you see a heading, you know: the next part is going to be about THIS topic."

Write the word **bold** in thick dark letters.
"A BOLD WORD is a word that is printed in thick, dark letters — like this. When a word is bold, it means: this word is important! Pay attention to it! Bold words are often vocabulary words or key ideas."

**Step 3: Read the passage together.**
Say: "Now let's read an informational text about penguins. As we read, look for the heading and the bold words. Also think about which facts are key details — the most important facts about penguins."

Read the following passage aloud with the student.

---

**All About Penguins**

## What Are Penguins?

**Penguins** are birds, but they cannot fly. They live in very cold places near the ocean. Penguins have black and white **feathers** that keep them warm. They are very good swimmers. Penguins use their wings like flippers to move through the water. They eat fish and other small sea animals. Penguins live in big groups called **colonies**. A colony can have thousands of penguins! Baby penguins are covered in soft, fluffy gray down. The parents take turns keeping the baby warm.

---

After reading, say: "Great! Now let's look at this text closely."

Point to the heading: "See this? 'What Are Penguins?' — that is the HEADING. It tells us this section is going to answer the question: What are penguins?"

Point to the bold words: "And see these thick, dark words? 'Penguins,' 'feathers,' and 'colonies' — those are BOLD WORDS. The author made them bold to show that they are important. Let's make sure we know what they mean."
- **Penguins** — the animal the text is about.
- **Feathers** — the soft covering on a bird's body.
- **Colonies** — big groups that penguins live in.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Identifying a key detail.**
Say: "Let me find a key detail. I ask myself: What is one of the most important facts about penguins? The text says, 'Penguins are birds, but they cannot fly.' That is a key detail! It tells us the most basic, important thing about what penguins are. If someone asked me, 'What are penguins?' I would need that fact."

Write on paper:
- Key detail: Penguins are birds, but they cannot fly.

**Example 2: Identifying a minor detail and comparing it to a key detail.**
Say: "Now let me find a minor detail. The text says, 'A colony can have thousands of penguins!' That is an interesting fact. But if I were telling someone the most important things about penguins, would I NEED to say exactly how many are in a colony? Probably not. The important fact is that they live in groups called colonies. The exact number is a minor detail."

Write on paper:
- Key detail: Penguins live in big groups called colonies.
- Minor detail: A colony can have thousands of penguins.

Say: "See the difference? 'Penguins live in colonies' is a key detail — it tells us how they live. 'Thousands of penguins' is extra information — interesting, but not essential."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice finding key details in the informational passage with facilitator support.

**Activity: Fact Finder**
Say: "Let's be Fact Finders! We are going to go through the penguin text and pick out the key details — the most important facts."

Give the student sticky notes (or have them use a pencil to underline). Re-read the passage one sentence at a time. For each sentence, discuss whether it contains a key detail.

**Sentence 1: "Penguins are birds, but they cannot fly."**
Ask: "Is this a key detail?" (Yes — it tells us the most important thing about what penguins are.)
Student marks it.

**Sentence 2: "They live in very cold places near the ocean."**
Ask: "Is this a key detail?" (Yes — it tells us WHERE penguins live.)
Student marks it.

**Sentence 3: "Penguins have black and white feathers that keep them warm."**
Ask: "Is this a key detail or a minor detail?"
Guide: "It tells us two things — what penguins look like AND that their feathers keep them warm. The 'keep them warm' part is a key detail because it explains how penguins survive in cold places. The colors (black and white) are a minor detail."
Student marks it as a key detail (the feathers keeping them warm is important).

**Sentence 4: "They are very good swimmers."**
Ask: "Key detail or minor detail?"
Guide: "This tells us something important about what penguins CAN do, especially since they cannot fly. It is a key detail."
Student marks it.

**Sentence 5: "Penguins use their wings like flippers to move through the water."**
Ask: "Key detail or minor detail?"
Guide: "This explains HOW they swim. It gives more detail about swimming. This one is a bit tricky — it supports the fact that they are good swimmers. You could call it a key detail because it explains how they move." (Accept either answer if the student explains their reasoning.)

**Sentence 6: "They eat fish and other small sea animals."**
Ask: "Key or minor?"
(Key — it tells what they eat, which is important for understanding an animal.)

**Sentence 7: "Penguins live in big groups called colonies."**
Ask: "Key or minor?"
(Key — it tells how they live together.)

**Sentence 8: "A colony can have thousands of penguins!"**
Ask: "Key or minor?"
(Minor — the exact number is extra information. The key fact is that they live in colonies.)

**Sentence 9: "Baby penguins are covered in soft, fluffy gray down."**
Ask: "Key or minor?"
(This is borderline. The fact that babies look different is interesting but not essential. Accept either answer with reasoning.)

**Sentence 10: "The parents take turns keeping the baby warm."**
Ask: "Key or minor?"
(Key — it tells us about penguin behavior and how they care for their babies.)

After sorting, say: "Look at all the key details we found! If we put them together, we could tell someone almost everything important about penguins without reading the whole text. That is the power of key details."

*Facilitator note: In informational text, the line between "key" and "minor" can be blurrier than in fiction. If the student makes a reasonable argument for a detail being key or minor, accept it. The goal is the THINKING PROCESS — asking "Is this important to understanding the topic?" — not getting a perfect answer.*

## Independent Practice (5-10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently identifies key details and text features in the passage.

### Difficulty Tiers

**★ (On-level):**
Answer these questions about "All About Penguins."

1. Name two key details (important facts) from the passage.
   - Fact 1: _______________
   - Fact 2: _______________

2. Name one minor detail from the passage: _______________

3. What is the heading of this passage? _______________

4. Name one bold word from the passage. Why do you think the author made it bold? _______________

**★★ (Stretch):**
Answer the On-level questions PLUS:

5. Name two MORE key details from the passage.
   - Fact 3: _______________
   - Fact 4: _______________

6. What does the heading "What Are Penguins?" tell you the passage will be about? _______________

7. Write one "what" question and one "where" question about penguins. Then answer them.
   - Question: _______________  Answer: _______________
   - Question: _______________  Answer: _______________

**★★★ (Challenge):**
Answer all the above PLUS:

8. List as many key details as you can find in the passage. Try to find at least 5. _______________

9. Imagine you are writing a book about penguins. What heading would you write for a section about what penguins eat? _______________

10. Why do you think the author put the word "colonies" in bold? _______________

### Interleaved Review
After finishing the key detail questions, have the student complete these brief review items:
- Identify whether each detail is key or minor (Lesson 3 review, using the fiction story from memory):
  - "Buster won a ribbon." (Key detail.)
  - "The van was blue." (Minor detail.)
- Read these words aloud: penguins, feathers, ocean, colonies, flippers (decoding practice with multisyllabic words)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to determine if the student can identify key details in informational text and recognize text features.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short informational passage to the student (or have them read it):

"**Rabbits** are small animals with long ears. They eat plants like grass and carrots. Rabbits can hop very fast. Some rabbits are brown and some are white."

1. Name one key detail from this passage. (Acceptable: Rabbits are small animals with long ears. / They eat plants. / Rabbits can hop very fast.)
2. Name one minor detail. (Acceptable: Some rabbits are brown and some are white.)
3. Which word is bold in this passage? (Rabbits.) Why is it bold? (Because it is the topic — what the passage is about. / Because it is an important word.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies at least 1 key detail (item 1), identifies 1 minor detail (item 2), AND identifies the bold word with a reasonable explanation for why it is bold (item 3).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the difference between key details and minor details using a familiar topic. Ask the student: "If I want to tell someone about cats, what are the MOST important things? What would be extra, less important things?"
- Practice with one more short informational passage (2-3 sentences) before moving on.
- If the student struggles specifically with text features, do a "text feature hunt" — look through any nonfiction book and point out headings, bold words, and other features. This builds familiarity.
- Repeat this lesson with a different informational topic in the next session.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 5: Identifying the Main Topic. The student will build on key detail identification to determine what the whole text is mostly about. Continue to ask "What are the key details?" during nonfiction reading throughout the day.

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Penguin Expert:** The student draws a picture of a penguin and writes 3-4 key facts around the picture (like a mini poster). This reinforces the key details while integrating writing.
- **Text Feature Hunt:** Give the student any nonfiction book or text. Have them find and point to: headings, bold words, pictures, captions, labels. How many text features can they find? This builds awareness of nonfiction text structures.
- **Compare Fiction and Nonfiction Details:** Ask the student: "In the Buster story (fiction), the key details were about a character and events. In the penguin text (nonfiction), the key details were about real facts. How are they different?" This develops the ability to compare genres.
- **Write Your Own Informational Sentence:** The student picks an animal they know about and writes 2-3 sentences with key details about that animal. Challenge them to include one bold word.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pedagogical context:** This lesson addresses standard RI.1.2 (identify the main topic and retell key details of a text) by teaching the student to find key details in informational text. It also introduces foundational nonfiction text features (headings and bold words), which support comprehension of informational text across all grade levels. This lesson builds directly on Lesson 3 (key details in fiction) by transferring the same skill to a new genre.

**Fiction vs. informational key details:** In fiction, key details typically relate to character, setting, and plot (what happened). In informational text, key details are the most important facts about the topic. This is a subtle but important shift in thinking. The student must move from "What happened in the story?" to "What are the most important facts?" Explicitly naming this shift helps the student adjust.

**Text features are a big deal in nonfiction:** Headings and bold words are just the beginning. Throughout Grades 1-3, students will encounter tables of contents, glossaries, indexes, diagrams, captions, labels, maps, and more. This lesson plants the seed by introducing the two most basic text features. Do not overwhelm the student — just headings and bold words for now.

**The penguin passage:** This passage was written at an early Grade 1 reading level with mostly decodable words and common sight words. The bold words (penguins, feathers, colonies) serve as both vocabulary instruction and text feature practice. "Colonies" is the most challenging word — pre-teach it if needed. The passage contains approximately 85 words, which is an appropriate length for a Grade 1 informational text.

**Pacing notes:** This lesson is designed for approximately 35-40 minutes. The Guided Practice sentence-by-sentence analysis is the core of the lesson. If time is short, you do not need to analyze every sentence — focus on 5-6 sentences that provide clear examples of key vs. minor details. If the student needs more time with the text feature concepts, consider splitting this lesson into two sessions: key details in session 1, text features in session 2.

**Common issues:**
- Students may be less engaged with nonfiction than fiction. Choose topics the student finds interesting. If penguins do not excite this particular student, supplement with a short text about their favorite animal.
- The concept of "bold" may be new if the student has not encountered nonfiction books with text features. Show physical examples if possible — any children's nonfiction book will have bold vocabulary words.
- Some students will try to memorize the passage rather than analyze it. Redirect to the text: "Let's look at what the words say" rather than "Tell me what you remember."

**Connection to the methodology framework:** This lesson follows the gradual release model and uses concrete sorting activities to scaffold the abstract skill of evaluating importance. Tiered independent practice and interleaved review follow the structure defined in the [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md).
