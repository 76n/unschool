# Unit Assessment — Grade 1 ELA, Unit 2: Reading Comprehension

**Grade:** 1
**Unit:** 2 — Reading Comprehension
**Assessment Type:** Summative (unit-level)
**Administration:** One-on-one with facilitator (oral reading, oral responses, and brief written/drawn responses)
**Estimated Duration:** 25-30 minutes
**Materials:** Printed copies of the two assessment passages (one fiction, one informational), pencil, paper for drawing/writing, question word cards (optional support)

---

## Assessment Overview

This assessment covers all Unit 2 learning objectives:

| Section | Skill Assessed | Lesson Source | Items |
|---------|---------------|---------------|-------|
| A | Question words (who, what, where, when, why, how) | Lessons 1-2 | 6 |
| B | Key details in fiction | Lesson 3 | 4 |
| C | Key details in informational text | Lesson 4 | 4 |
| D | Main topic and supporting details | Lessons 5-6 | 4 |
| E | Connecting events (cause and effect) | Lesson 7 | 3 |
| F | Connecting ideas in informational text | Lesson 8 | 2 |
| G | Comparing fiction and informational text | Lesson 9 | 2 |
| -- | **Total** | -- | **25** |

**Mastery threshold:** 85% overall (21 of 25 points) AND at least 1 correct item in each section.

---

## Administration Instructions

Read the passages aloud to the student, or have the student read them aloud (depending on reading level). For students who can read independently, let them read first, then ask the questions. For students who are still developing fluency, read the passage aloud while the student follows along, then ask the questions.

Read each question aloud. The student may answer orally, by pointing to the text, or by writing/drawing — accept any mode that demonstrates understanding.

There are no time limits. If the student is stuck for more than 15 seconds, say: "That's okay. Let's try the next one."

Do NOT provide hints or lead the student to answers. If the student asks for help, say: "Think about what the text said. What do you remember?"

Before beginning, say: "We're going to read two short texts today and I'm going to ask you some questions about them. Some might be easy and some might make you think hard. Just do your best!"

## Assessment Passage 1: Fiction

Read or have the student read the following story:

---

**Lily and the Lost Kite**

Lily got a new red kite for her birthday. She was so happy. On Saturday, Lily went to the park with her dad to fly the kite.

The wind was strong that day. Lily held the string tight. But then a big gust of wind came. The string slipped out of her hands! The kite flew up, up, up into a tall tree.

Lily felt sad. "My kite is stuck!" she said. Her dad said, "Let me try to get it." He reached up high, but the tree was too tall.

Then a kind man with a ladder walked by. "I can help!" he said. He climbed up the ladder and got the kite down. Lily smiled big. "Thank you!" she said. She held the string very tight after that.

---

## Section A: Question Words (Lessons 1-2)

Ask these questions about "Lily and the Lost Kite":

**A1.** "WHO is this story about?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Names Lily (and optionally her dad and/or the kind man) | 1 point |
| Cannot name any character | 0 points |

**A2.** "WHAT did Lily get for her birthday?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| A red kite / a kite | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**A3.** "WHERE did Lily go to fly her kite?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| The park | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**A4.** "WHEN did Lily go to the park?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| On Saturday / on her birthday / after she got the kite (any reasonable time reference) | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**A5.** "WHY did the kite fly into the tree?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Because the wind was strong / a big gust of wind came / the string slipped out of her hands (any causal explanation) | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**A6.** "HOW did Lily get her kite back?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| A kind man with a ladder climbed up and got it / a man helped / someone used a ladder | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

## Section B: Key Details in Fiction (Lesson 3)

Continue with "Lily and the Lost Kite":

**B1.** "What are the most important things that happen in this story? Tell me three important things."

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Names 3 key events (e.g., Lily got a kite, the kite got stuck in a tree, a man helped get it down) | 2 points |
| Names 2 key events | 1 point |
| Names 0-1 key events | 0 points |

**B2.** "How did Lily feel when her kite got stuck in the tree?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Sad / upset / worried (any appropriate emotion) | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**B3.** "Why couldn't Lily's dad get the kite?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| The tree was too tall / he couldn't reach it | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

## Assessment Passage 2: Informational Text

Read or have the student read the following passage:

---

**All About Ladybugs**

Ladybugs are small insects. Most ladybugs are red or orange with black spots. But some ladybugs are yellow or even pink!

Ladybugs are helpful to farmers and gardeners. They eat tiny bugs called aphids. Aphids hurt plants by eating their leaves. One ladybug can eat up to 50 aphids in a single day!

Ladybugs have a special way to stay safe. When a bird or other animal tries to eat them, ladybugs make a bad-tasting liquid come out of their legs. This makes the animal spit them out and leave them alone. Their bright red color also warns animals: "Don't eat me! I taste bad!"

In the winter, ladybugs huddle together in big groups to stay warm. Sometimes thousands of ladybugs will gather in one spot!

---

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|

| Partially correct (says red OR orange but not both, or mentions spots) | 0.5 points |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**C2.** "What do ladybugs eat?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Aphids / tiny bugs / bugs that eat plants | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**C3.** "How many aphids can one ladybug eat in a day?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| 50 / up to 50 | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**C4.** "What do ladybugs do in the winter?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Huddle together / gather in big groups to stay warm | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

## Section D: Main Topic and Supporting Details (Lessons 5-6)

**D1.** "What is this text mostly about? What is the main topic?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Ladybugs / all about ladybugs / facts about ladybugs | 1 point |
| A specific detail rather than the main topic (e.g., "eating aphids") | 0.5 points |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**D2.** "The text says ladybugs are helpful. What detail from the text supports that — what makes them helpful?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| They eat aphids / they eat bugs that hurt plants / they help farmers and gardeners | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**D3.** "The text tells us how ladybugs stay safe. What is one detail about how they protect themselves?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| They make a bad-tasting liquid / their red color warns animals / they taste bad (any detail from the safety paragraph) | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**D4.** "Tell me two facts you learned about ladybugs from this text."

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| States 2 distinct facts from the text | 1 point |
| States only 1 fact or repeats the same fact twice | 0.5 points |
| Cannot state any facts | 0 points |

## Section E: Connecting Events — Cause and Effect (Lesson 7)

Return to "Lily and the Lost Kite":

**E1.** "The kite flew into the tree. What CAUSED that to happen?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| The wind / a big gust of wind / the string slipped out of Lily's hands | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**E2.** "What happened BECAUSE the kite was stuck in the tree?" (What was the effect?)

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Lily felt sad / her dad tried to get it / a man helped with a ladder (any consequence) | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**E3.** "At the end, Lily held the string very tight. WHY did she do that?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Because she didn't want to lose the kite again / so it wouldn't fly away / she learned from what happened | 1 point |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

---

## Section F: Connecting Ideas in Informational Text (Lesson 8)

Return to "All About Ladybugs":

**F1.** "The text tells us that aphids hurt plants. Then it tells us ladybugs eat aphids. How are these two ideas connected?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Ladybugs help plants by eating the bugs that hurt them / ladybugs protect plants from aphids / because aphids are bad for plants, ladybugs eating them is good (any connection showing understanding) | 1 point |
| Restates both facts without connecting them | 0.5 points |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

**F2.** "The text says ladybugs are bright red AND that they taste bad. How do these two things work together to keep ladybugs safe?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| The red color warns animals that ladybugs taste bad / animals see the red and know not to eat them / the color is like a warning sign (any explanation connecting color to safety) | 1 point |
| Mentions one element but doesn't connect them | 0.5 points |
| Incorrect or no response | 0 points |

---

## Section G: Comparing Fiction and Informational Text (Lesson 9)

**G1.** "We read two texts today — 'Lily and the Lost Kite' and 'All About Ladybugs.' Which one is fiction (a made-up story) and which one is informational (real facts)?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Correctly identifies "Lily" as fiction and "Ladybugs" as informational | 1 point |
| Reverses them or says both are the same type | 0 points |

**G2.** "How can you tell which one is fiction and which one is informational? What makes them different?"

| Response | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Gives at least 1 valid reason (e.g., Lily is a made-up character / the ladybug text teaches real facts / the Lily story has things that could happen but someone made it up / the ladybug text has true information about real animals) | 1 point |
| Cannot explain the difference | 0 points |

## Rubric and Mastery Determination

| Level | Score Range | Meaning | Next Steps |
|-------|-----------|---------|------------|
| **Mastered** | 21-25 (85-100%) with at least 1 correct in each section | Solid reading comprehension across fiction and informational text | Advance to Unit 3 (Writing Foundations). Continue daily reading with comprehension conversations. |
| **Approaching** | 18-20 (70-84%) | Most comprehension skills are developing; specific areas need reinforcement | Identify weak sections and reteach (1-2 sessions each). The student may begin Unit 3 while continuing comprehension practice. |
| **Developing** | Below 18 (<70%) | Significant comprehension gaps | Return to the weakest section and reteach from that lesson. Increase read-aloud time with comprehension questions. Do not advance to Unit 3 until "Approaching" level. |

---

## Section-Specific Reteaching Guide

| If the student struggles in... | What it tells you | Reteach with... |
|-------------------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Section A (Question Words)** | Cannot formulate or answer basic comprehension questions | Return to Lessons 1-2. Practice with very simple, short texts (2-3 sentences). Use question word cards as visual cues. Ask one question type at a time before mixing them. |
| **Section B (Key Details — Fiction)** | Reads the story but cannot identify important information | Return to Lesson 3. Teach the "important vs. interesting" distinction. After each page, ask: "What is the most important thing that happened?" Use sticky notes to mark key details while reading. |
| **Section C (Key Details — Info)** | Cannot recall facts from informational text | Return to Lesson 4. Use shorter passages (3-4 sentences). Pause after each sentence: "What did we just learn?" Build up to recalling multiple facts. |
| **Section D (Main Topic)** | Cannot identify the overall topic or distinguish it from details | Return to Lessons 5-6. Practice the "one sentence" strategy with familiar topics. Use the main topic/details graphic organizer with every informational text read. |
| **Section E (Cause & Effect)** | Cannot connect events or explain why things happened | Return to Lesson 7. Practice with everyday cause-and-effect: "Why did you put on your coat? Because it's cold." Use the sentence frame: "___ happened because ___." |
| **Section F (Connecting Ideas)** | Cannot see relationships between pieces of information | Return to Lesson 8. Use sequence-focused texts (how-to, life cycles) where connections are explicit. Practice with graphic organizers showing arrows between connected ideas. |
| **Section G (Fiction vs. Info)** | Cannot distinguish text types | Return to Lesson 9. Read pairs of texts on the same topic (one fiction, one nonfiction) repeatedly. Create a T-chart: "Made Up" vs. "Real Facts." |

## Detailed Rubric by Skill

### Questioning (Section A)

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Answers literal questions (who, what, where, when) | Answers all literal questions correctly using information from the text | Answers most literal questions but occasionally gives vague or incomplete responses | Cannot reliably answer basic literal questions about what was read |
| Answers inferential questions (why, how) | Provides reasonable explanations that show understanding of causation and process | Attempts to explain but answers are incomplete or partially incorrect | Cannot provide explanations; may say "I don't know" or give unrelated responses |

### Key Details (Sections B-C)

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Identifies key details in fiction | Names 3+ important events/details without prompting | Names 1-2 key details; may include minor details alongside important ones | Cannot identify key details; retells random fragments or nothing |
| Identifies key details in informational text | Recalls 3+ specific facts from the text accurately | Recalls 1-2 facts; some details may be inaccurate or vague | Cannot recall specific facts from the text |

### Main Topic and Connections (Sections D-F)

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Identifies main topic | States the main topic accurately and concisely | States a related but overly specific or overly broad topic | Cannot identify the main topic; confuses it with a single detail |
| Connects supporting details to main topic | Explains how specific details support the main topic | Identifies supporting details but cannot explain the connection | Cannot identify or connect supporting details |
| Explains cause and effect | Clearly states cause-and-effect relationships using "because" or equivalent language | Identifies either the cause or effect but not the connection between them | Cannot identify causal relationships in the text |

### Text Comparison (Section G)

| Criterion | Mastered (3) | Approaching (2) | Developing (1) |
|-----------|-------------|-----------------|-----------------|
| Distinguishes fiction from informational | Correctly identifies both text types and explains the difference with clear reasoning | Correctly identifies both types but cannot clearly explain why | Cannot reliably distinguish fiction from informational text |

## Facilitator Observations (Record During Assessment)

- Does the student refer back to the text when answering questions, or answer entirely from memory?
- Which question types are easiest (who/what/where) vs. hardest (why/how)?
- Does the student show different comprehension levels for fiction vs. informational text?
- Does the student make connections between the text and their own experience?
- Does the student show confidence or hesitation when answering?
- Is decoding still effortful, and if so, does it seem to interfere with comprehension?

These observations inform differentiation for Unit 3 and help identify whether comprehension challenges stem from text difficulty, working memory, vocabulary, or metacognitive strategy gaps.
