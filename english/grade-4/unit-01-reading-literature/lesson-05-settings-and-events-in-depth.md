# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 5

## Topic
Settings and Events in Depth

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Describe a story's setting in depth using specific details from the text (time, place, atmosphere)
- Explain how the setting influences characters' actions and feelings
- Analyze how the setting shapes the events of a story

## Prerequisites
- Ability to describe characters in depth using text evidence (Lesson 3)
- Understanding of how characters respond to challenges (Lesson 4)
- Ability to identify key events in a story (Lesson 2)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils and colored pencils
- Sticky notes (2-3 per student)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students understand how setting is more than just a backdrop — it shapes everything that happens.

**Activity: Same Character, Different Setting**

Say: "I want you to imagine a character named Alex. Alex is ten years old and loves adventure. Now imagine Alex in two different settings."

Write on the board:
- Setting A: A dark, snowy forest at night
- Setting B: A sunny beach on a summer afternoon

Say: "Turn to your partner. How would Alex feel in Setting A? What might happen? Now how about Setting B? How do the feelings and events change just because the setting changed?"

Allow 60 seconds for partner discussion, then call on 2-3 pairs.

Say: "See what happened? The same character had completely different experiences depending on where and when the story takes place. That is the power of setting. Today we are going to learn how to describe settings in depth and explain how settings shape what happens in a story."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify and describe the three dimensions of setting and analyze how setting influences characters and events.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Setting:** Where and when a story takes place. Setting includes the physical location, the time period, and the mood or atmosphere.
- **Atmosphere/Mood:** The feeling a setting creates for the reader (e.g., cozy, spooky, exciting, lonely). Authors create atmosphere through descriptive details.
- **Influence:** When the setting affects what characters do, how they feel, or what events happen. The setting pushes the story in a certain direction.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When we describe a setting in depth, we look for three things."

Write on the board:
1. **Where?** — The physical place (a city, a farm, a classroom, the ocean)
2. **When?** — The time (morning, winter, 1850, the future)
3. **What does it feel like?** — The atmosphere or mood (peaceful, dangerous, mysterious, warm)

Say: "Most students can tell me the 'where.' Fewer students notice the 'when.' And almost nobody starts with 'what does it feel like.' But that third one — the atmosphere — is where the best analysis happens."

Say: "Let me show you what I mean."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Cabin"**

> Rain hammered against the windows of the old cabin. Inside, the fire had burned down to a few orange embers. The only light came from a single candle on the table. Shadows stretched across the wooden walls like long fingers.
>
> Mia pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulders. The wind howled through a crack under the door. Somewhere outside, a branch snapped.
>
> "It is just the wind," she whispered to herself. But she scooted her chair closer to the dying fire anyway.
>
> Her grandfather had said he would be back before dark. The clock on the mantle read 9:15. Mia stared at the door and listened.

Say: "Let me describe this setting in depth."

Write on the board:
- **Where:** An old cabin in a remote area (probably in the woods, based on the branch snapping)
- **When:** Nighttime — past 9:15 PM. It is also during a rainstorm.
- **Atmosphere:** Isolated, tense, a little scary. The details that create this mood are the rain hammering, shadows like long fingers, the wind howling, and the branch snapping.

Say: "Now, how does the setting influence the character? Let us look at what Mia does."

Write on the board:
- Mia pulls the blanket tighter (the cold, dark cabin makes her uncomfortable).
- She whispers "It is just the wind" to comfort herself (the eerie sounds from the storm make her nervous).
- She scoots closer to the dying fire (the dark, isolated setting makes her seek warmth and safety).
- She stares at the door (she is worried about her grandfather because it is dark and stormy outside).

Say: "Every one of Mia's actions is shaped by the setting. If this story took place on a sunny afternoon in a park, Mia would not be huddled by a fire whispering to herself. The setting creates the feelings and drives the actions. That is what we mean when we say the setting influences the characters."

Say: "And what about events? What events could happen in this setting that could not happen somewhere else?"

Guide students: A storm could knock a tree down, the fire could go out, her grandfather could get lost in the dark. The setting makes these events possible.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students work with the teacher to describe a setting in depth and analyze its influence.

Say: "Let us try one together. I will read a passage, and you will help me fill in the three parts of setting plus the influence on characters and events."

**Passage: "Market Day"**

> The morning sun warmed the cobblestone streets of the village market. Vendors called out from their stalls, holding up baskets of ripe peaches, bundles of fresh herbs, and jars of golden honey. The air smelled like baking bread and roasted nuts. A fiddle player stood on the corner, tapping his foot as he played a lively tune.
>
> Tomás walked through the crowd with his mother's shopping list in one hand and a handful of coins in the other. He had never been to the market alone before. There was so much to see that he kept stopping to stare — at the juggler tossing oranges, at the woman painting tiny portraits, at the cart full of puppies.
>
> "Tomás! Over here!" called old Mr. Salazar from the fruit stand. "Your mother's usual — three pounds of peaches?"
>
> Tomás grinned. He liked being treated like a grown-up. He handed over the coins and carefully placed the peaches in his bag. Then he looked at the list. Five more items. He took a deep breath and headed into the crowd.

Say: "Let us fill in our chart. Where does this take place?"

Guide toward: A village market — an outdoor market with vendor stalls, cobblestone streets, and a crowd of people.

Say: "When?"

Guide toward: Morning — the "morning sun" is mentioned. It also seems to be a warm season because there are ripe peaches.

Say: "What is the atmosphere? What does this place feel like?"

Guide toward: Lively, warm, friendly, bustling, cheerful. Details: people calling out, music playing, smells of bread and roasted nuts, bright colors.

Say: "Now the important question. How does this setting influence Tomás?"

Guide students to identify:
- The busy, exciting market makes Tomás keep stopping to stare — he is distracted by all the sights (juggler, painter, puppies).
- The friendly atmosphere (Mr. Salazar knowing his mother) makes Tomás feel welcomed and proud — "He liked being treated like a grown-up."
- The crowded, bustling setting also creates a small challenge: Tomás has to navigate through the crowd to find five more items, which requires confidence.

Say: "Compare this setting to the cabin from before. How are the characters affected differently?"

Guide toward: The cabin made Mia feel scared and isolated. The market makes Tomás feel excited and confident. Setting shapes the character's entire experience.

Have students write a brief paragraph in their notebooks: "Describe the market setting in depth (where, when, atmosphere) and explain how it influences Tomás."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently describe a setting and analyze its influence on characters and events.

**Passage: "The Frozen Pond"**

> The January wind bit at Suki's cheeks as she stood at the edge of the pond. Everything was white — the frozen water, the snow-covered banks, the pale sky. The trees around the pond were bare and black, their branches clicking together like bony fingers in the wind.
>
> Suki laced up her ice skates with stiff, cold fingers. She had not skated since last winter, and she was nervous. The pond was smooth and glassy, stretching out wide and empty. No one else had come yet.
>
> She stepped onto the ice. Her first few strides were shaky. The cold air stung her lungs. But as she pushed forward, her legs remembered the rhythm. Glide, push, glide, push. The wind that had felt harsh a moment ago now rushed past her face like a friend racing beside her.
>
> She spun in a wide circle, leaving curved lines in the fresh ice. The bare trees seemed to lean in and watch. For a moment, the whole world was silent except for the whisper of her blades.
>
> When her mother called from the car, Suki skated one more slow loop. She did not want to leave. Out here, alone on the frozen pond, she felt completely free.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences.

1. Describe the setting in depth using the three dimensions: Where, When, and Atmosphere. Include at least two specific details from the text for each dimension.
2. How does the setting influence how Suki feels? Trace how her feelings change from the beginning to the end of the passage, and explain how the setting plays a role in each change.
3. The author writes that the bare trees "seemed to lean in and watch." What kind of atmosphere does this detail create? Why do you think the author included it?
4. How does the setting make the events of this story possible? What could NOT happen in a different setting (for example, a summer day in a city)?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 as written.
**Stretch:** After completing questions 1-4, write a paragraph describing how the story would change if it took place on a crowded outdoor ice rink in a big city instead of a frozen pond in the countryside. How would Suki's feelings and actions be different?
**Challenge:** After completing all questions, write your own short passage (6-8 sentences) set in a place you know well (your backyard, a park, your kitchen). Include specific details about where, when, and atmosphere. Then write two sentences explaining how the setting in your passage influences the character.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Lesson 3-4 review) Identify one character trait for Suki based on this passage. What is the evidence? What challenge does she face, and how does she respond?
6. (Lesson 1-2 review) What is the theme of "The Frozen Pond"? How do the key events build toward that theme?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following mini-passage:

> The library was quiet except for the soft hum of the heater. Warm light fell across rows of books. Aiden sat in the big armchair by the window, reading. Snow drifted past the glass outside. He pulled his legs up under him and turned the page. He never wanted to leave.

1. Describe the setting using Where, When, and Atmosphere.
2. How does the setting influence Aiden?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Where: A library, in a big armchair by the window. When: Winter (snow outside), daytime (light through the window). Atmosphere: Warm, quiet, cozy, peaceful. Details: soft hum of the heater, warm light, snow drifting past.
2. The cozy, warm setting makes Aiden feel comfortable and content. He pulls his legs up (getting more comfortable) and "never wanted to leave" — the peaceful atmosphere encourages him to relax and enjoy his reading.

**Mastery = Student correctly describes the setting using all three dimensions and explains at least one way the setting influences the character.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three dimensions of setting (Where, When, Atmosphere) using a chart.
- Give the student a picture of a familiar place (a playground, a kitchen, a forest) and have them describe it using all three dimensions orally before trying written text analysis.
- Provide the sentence frame: "The setting influences the character because _______________."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 6, which introduces point of view.
- Encourage the student to pay attention to settings in their independent reading and note how settings influence characters.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Setting Sketch:** The student draws the setting from "The Frozen Pond" or "The Cabin," labeling specific details from the text. Below the drawing, they write how the setting creates a mood.
2. **Setting Swap:** The student takes "The Frozen Pond" passage and rewrites the first paragraph, changing the setting to a tropical beach. Then they explain how the new setting would change Suki's feelings and actions.
3. **Setting Journal:** The student describes a real place they visited recently using the three-dimension framework (where, when, atmosphere) and writes about how the setting affected their mood or actions.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Atmosphere is the key skill:** Most students can identify where and when. The real learning in this lesson is atmosphere — the feeling a setting creates. Spend extra time on this if needed. Ask: "If you were standing in this place, how would you feel? What details make you feel that way?"
- **Personification in setting:** The passages use figurative language to describe settings ("shadows like long fingers," "branches clicking like bony fingers," trees that "lean in and watch"). Note these for students and explain that authors use this kind of language to create atmosphere. This connects to figurative language skills they will study later in the year.
- **Connection to character lessons:** Setting analysis is not separate from character analysis — they work together. The setting creates conditions that reveal character traits. The cabin reveals Mia's nervousness and self-reliance; the market reveals Tomás's excitement and growing independence; the pond reveals Suki's determination and love of freedom.
- **Anchor chart:** Add a "Setting Analysis" section to the classroom anchor chart with the three dimensions and the key question: "How does the setting influence the characters and events?"
- **Pacing tip:** The Worked Example and Guided Practice passages are rich with descriptive detail. If students are engaged in discussion, it is fine to let the Guided Practice run a few minutes longer and shorten Independent Practice to questions 1-2 in class.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Setting description:**
   - **Where:** At the edge of a frozen pond in a natural area surrounded by bare trees. It is outdoors, away from other people (no one else had come yet).
   - **When:** January, wintertime. It is cold — the wind "bit" at Suki's cheeks, her fingers were stiff, and everything was covered in snow and ice.
   - **Atmosphere:** At first, the atmosphere is cold, empty, and slightly intimidating (bare trees with branches "clicking like bony fingers," the wide empty pond, the stinging wind). By the end, the atmosphere shifts to peaceful, free, and magical (the world is silent except for her blades, the trees seem to watch, she feels free).

2. At the beginning, the cold, empty setting makes Suki feel nervous — she has not skated since last winter, the pond is wide and empty, and her fingers are stiff. As she starts skating, the setting transforms from harsh to welcoming. The wind changes from something that "bit" her cheeks to "a friend racing beside her." The silence that seemed lonely at first becomes peaceful — "the whole world was silent except for the whisper of her blades." By the end, the setting makes her feel completely free. The setting's influence on Suki's feelings shifts as she overcomes her initial nervousness.

3. The detail about the trees leaning in to watch creates a feeling of intimacy and magic — as if nature is paying attention to Suki and approving of her skating. It makes the atmosphere feel alive and enchanted, turning the empty pond into a special, almost sacred space. The author included it to show that Suki feels connected to the natural world around her.

4. The frozen pond is essential to the story. Suki could not ice skate on a summer day or in a city without a pond. The cold weather creates the initial nervousness (stiff fingers, biting wind), the ice allows the skating and the feeling of freedom, and the emptiness of the setting allows the solitude that makes Suki feel free. In a different setting — like a hot summer day in a city — none of these events or feelings would be possible.

### Interleaved Review
5. **Trait:** Determined / brave. **Evidence:** Even though she was nervous and cold, Suki laced up her skates and stepped onto the ice. Her first strides were shaky, but she kept going until she found her rhythm. **Challenge:** She had not skated since last winter and was nervous; the pond was empty and intimidating. **Response:** She pushed forward, and as her body remembered the rhythm, she gained confidence and eventually felt free.

6. **Theme:** Pushing past fear and discomfort can lead to moments of joy and freedom. **Key events that build the theme:** (1) Suki is nervous and cold at the edge of the pond. (2) She steps onto the ice and her first strides are shaky. (3) She keeps going and finds her rhythm. (4) By the end, she feels completely free and does not want to leave. Each step shows her moving from fear to freedom.
