# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 7

## Topic
Comparing Points of View

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare and contrast how the same event looks different when told from two different characters' perspectives
- Explain what information changes and what stays the same when the narrator changes
- Analyze why an author might choose one character's perspective over another's

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of first-person and third-person point of view (Lesson 6)
- Ability to identify narrator POV using pronoun clues (Lesson 6)
- Understanding that different characters have different feelings and motivations (Lessons 3-4)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate the idea that two people can experience the same event very differently.

**Activity: Two Sides of the Story**

Say: "Imagine two people are at the same baseball game. One person's team is winning. The other person's team is losing. They are watching the exact same plays, but how do they feel?"

Allow students to share responses briefly.

Say: "The person whose team is winning might describe the game as thrilling and exciting. The person whose team is losing might describe it as frustrating or unfair. Same game, different perspectives."

Say: "In stories, the same thing happens. When we change which character is telling the story, the whole feeling and focus of the story can shift. Today, we are going to read the same event from two different characters' viewpoints and compare what we notice."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate how the same event told from different perspectives changes what the reader knows, feels, and focuses on.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Perspective:** A character's way of seeing and understanding events, based on their own experiences, feelings, and knowledge.
- **Compare:** To find similarities — what is the same in both versions.
- **Contrast:** To find differences — what changes between the two versions.
- **Bias:** When a narrator's feelings or opinions shape the way they describe events. Every first-person narrator has some bias because they can only share their own view.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In Lesson 6, we learned that first-person narrators can only share what THEY see, think, and feel. That means if two characters are in the same scene, they will tell the story differently. Let me show you exactly how."

Say: "I am going to read two versions of the same event. The first version is told by a character named Ava. The second version is told by a character named Jordan. Listen carefully for what is the same and what is different."

### Worked Examples

**Version A — Ava's Perspective (First Person):**

> I could not believe it. I had been working on my volcano model for a whole week, and now Jordan was standing in front of the class with a volcano that looked almost exactly like mine. Same shape, same colors, same baking-soda-and-vinegar eruption.
>
> "That is my idea!" I whispered to my friend Mei.
>
> Mei shrugged. "Maybe it is just a coincidence."
>
> But I was not so sure. Jordan sat behind me in class. He could have easily seen my sketches in my notebook. When Jordan's volcano erupted and the whole class cheered, I felt my face get hot. That should have been MY moment.
>
> After class, I walked right up to Jordan. "Did you copy my project?" I asked.
>
> He looked confused. "What? No. I found the idea online."
>
> I crossed my arms. I did not believe him.

**Version B — Jordan's Perspective (First Person):**

> I was really proud of my volcano project. My older sister helped me find a great tutorial online, and I spent the whole weekend building it. I even added red food coloring to make the lava look real.
>
> When I presented it in class, everyone cheered during the eruption. I grinned. It was the best moment of my whole week.
>
> But after class, Ava marched up to me looking angry. "Did you copy my project?" she demanded.
>
> I was so confused. Copy? I had never even seen her project. "What? No. I found the idea online," I said.
>
> Ava crossed her arms and stared at me. She clearly did not believe me. I felt my stomach sink. I had done all this work, and now someone thought I was a cheater. It was not fair.

Say: "Now let us compare these two versions."

Draw a Venn diagram on the board. Label one circle "Ava" and the other "Jordan." Label the overlap "Both."

Fill in together:

**Ava Only:**
- Thinks Jordan copied her idea
- Feels angry and cheated
- Focuses on the similarities between the two projects
- Believes Jordan could have seen her sketches
- Sees Jordan's success as something stolen from her

**Jordan Only:**
- Found the idea from an online tutorial with his sister's help
- Feels proud of his hard work
- Is confused and hurt when accused of copying
- Thinks the accusation is unfair
- Has no idea about Ava's project

**Both:**
- Presented volcano projects in class
- The volcanoes looked similar
- Ava asked Jordan if he copied
- Jordan said he found the idea online
- Ava did not believe him

Say: "The facts are the same. They both made volcanoes. They both had the confrontation after class. But the feeling is completely different. Ava's version makes the reader sympathize with Ava — she feels cheated. Jordan's version makes the reader sympathize with Jordan — he feels wrongly accused."

Say: "This is why point of view matters so much. The narrator shapes how the reader sees the story. Each narrator has a bias — they can only share their own side."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice comparing two perspectives of the same event with teacher support.

Say: "Let us try another pair of passages. This time, I want you to help me build the Venn diagram."

**Version A — Mia's Perspective (First Person):**

> I had been saving my seat at the lunch table for weeks. Every day, I put my backpack on the chair next to mine so Layla and I could sit together. It was our spot.
>
> But today, when I came back from getting my lunch tray, someone was sitting in Layla's chair. A new girl. She was talking to Layla like they had been friends forever.
>
> I stood there holding my tray, not sure where to sit. Layla waved at me. "Mia, this is Fatima. She just moved here. Sit across from us!"
>
> Across from them. Not next to Layla. Across. I sat down and poked at my food. Fatima was telling a funny story and Layla was laughing so hard she snorted. I tried to laugh too, but it came out wrong.
>
> I was not mad at Fatima. She seemed nice enough. But I felt like I was losing my best friend, and there was nothing I could do about it.

**Version B — Fatima's Perspective (First Person):**

> I had been at my new school for three days and still had not made a single friend. Every lunch period, I sat alone at the end of a long table, eating my sandwich as fast as I could so I could escape to the library.
>
> Today, a girl named Layla sat down next to me. "Hi! I am Layla. You are new, right? Sit with us!"
>
> I could have cried. Someone had finally noticed me. Layla asked where I was from, and I told her about my old city and my old school. She actually listened.
>
> Then another girl came over with a lunch tray. She looked at me and her face fell. Layla introduced us. "Mia, this is Fatima."
>
> Mia sat across from us and barely said a word the whole lunch period. I tried telling a funny story to include her, but she just poked at her food. My stomach twisted. Was she upset because of me? Had I taken her seat?
>
> I wanted to say something, but I did not know what. I had finally found someone to sit with, and now I was worried I had already ruined it.

Say: "Draw a Venn diagram in your notebook. With your partner, fill in what belongs in Mia's circle, what belongs in Fatima's circle, and what belongs in both."

Allow 3-4 minutes for partner work, then discuss as a class.

Guide toward:

**Mia Only:**
- Has been saving the seat for weeks — it is "their spot"
- Feels like she is being replaced by Fatima
- Focuses on what she is losing (her seat, her closeness with Layla)
- Feels unable to laugh genuinely

**Fatima Only:**
- Has been alone at school for three days
- Is relieved and grateful someone finally noticed her
- Tried to include Mia by telling a funny story
- Worries that she has ruined something or taken Mia's place
- Fears the one friendship she found is already damaged

**Both:**
- Sat at the same lunch table
- Layla introduced them
- Mia was quiet during lunch
- Both felt anxious or uncomfortable by the end

Say: "Whose side do you sympathize with more? This is not about who is right or wrong — both characters have real feelings. But notice how the story FEELS different depending on who tells it."

Say: "Now think about this: If someone reading only Mia's version might think Fatima is pushy. If someone reading only Fatima's version might think Mia is cold. But reading BOTH, you understand that neither is the villain. That is the power of seeing multiple perspectives."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently compare two perspectives of the same event.

**Version A — Kai's Perspective (First Person):**

> I practiced my speech twenty times. I knew every word by heart. When my name was called, I walked to the front of the classroom and started speaking. My voice was steady. My hands were not even shaking.
>
> Halfway through, someone in the back row started whispering. I glanced over and saw Ellie leaning toward her neighbor, her hand covering her mouth. She was definitely not paying attention.
>
> I lost my place. I stammered, looked down at my notes, and had to start the paragraph over. My face burned.
>
> After I finished, the class clapped, but all I could think about was the moment I messed up. That was Ellie's fault. If she had just listened like everyone else, I would have been fine.

**Version B — Ellie's Perspective (First Person):**

> Kai's speech was really good. He spoke clearly and seemed so confident. I was impressed because I would be way too nervous to stand up there.
>
> Halfway through his speech, I felt a sneeze coming. My nose started to tickle. I turned to my neighbor and whispered, "Do you have a tissue?" She shook her head. I covered my nose with my hand and tried to hold the sneeze in.
>
> When I looked up, Kai was staring at me. He stumbled over his words and had to look at his notes. I felt terrible. Had I distracted him? I had not meant to.
>
> After the speech, I wanted to go tell him he did a great job. But he walked right past me without looking at me. My chest felt tight. He was probably angry. I wished I could explain that I was just trying not to sneeze.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences.

1. What event do both passages describe? What facts stay the same in both versions?
2. Create a Venn diagram comparing Kai's perspective and Ellie's perspective. Include at least two items in each section (Kai only, Ellie only, Both).
3. In Kai's version, he says Ellie's whispering was her "fault." Based on Ellie's version, is that fair? Explain using evidence from both passages.
4. How does reading both versions change your understanding of the event compared to reading only one?
5. If this story were told by a third-person narrator who could see both characters' thoughts, what would the reader understand that neither Kai nor Ellie understands alone?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-5 as written.
**Stretch:** After completing questions 1-5, write a third version of this event from the perspective of the teacher watching from the side of the room. Use first person ("I") as the teacher. What would the teacher notice?
**Challenge:** After completing all questions, think of a real-life situation where two people might see the same event differently (e.g., a disagreement with a sibling or friend). Write two short paragraphs — one from each person's perspective — and explain what each person does not know about the other's experience.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Lesson 6 review) Identify the point of view of each passage (Version A and Version B). What pronoun clues told you?
7. (Lesson 3-4 review) Identify one character trait for Kai based on Version A. Identify one character trait for Ellie based on Version B. Support each with evidence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read these two mini-passages about the same event:

**Lin's version:** "I spent an hour building my sandcastle. It was my best one ever. Then my little brother ran right through it and knocked it down. He did not even care."

**Her brother's version:** "I was chasing a seagull on the beach and tripped. I fell right into Lin's sandcastle. I felt so bad. I wanted to help her rebuild it, but she yelled at me and ran away."

1. What is one thing Lin believes that is different from what her brother experienced?
2. What does the reader understand by reading both versions that neither character knows alone?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Lin believes her brother "did not even care" and intentionally ran through her castle. But her brother tripped while chasing a seagull — it was an accident, and he actually felt bad about it.
2. The reader understands that it was an accident and that the brother wanted to help rebuild the castle. Lin does not know it was an accident, and the brother does not know that Lin thinks he did it on purpose. Reading both versions shows a misunderstanding that neither character can see from their own perspective.

**Mastery = Student identifies a difference between the two perspectives and explains what the reader gains by seeing both sides.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the Venn diagram strategy. Give the student a simple two-version scenario and have them physically sort the details into three categories: Character A only, Character B only, Both.
- Ask guiding questions: "What does Character A think happened? What does Character B think happened? Are they both right? Are they both wrong?"
- Practice with one additional pair before Lesson 8.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8, which introduces the structural elements of poems, drama, and prose.
- Encourage the student to look for moments in their reading where they wonder what a different character would say about the same event.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Perspective Swap Writing:** The student chooses a scene from a book they are reading and rewrites it from a different character's first-person perspective.
2. **Double Diary:** The student writes two diary entries about the same event — one from Mia (Guided Practice) and one from Fatima — set one week later. How have their feelings changed? Do they understand each other better?
3. **Perspective in the News:** The student finds a simple news story and writes two brief first-person accounts of the same event from two different people involved (e.g., a firefighter and a neighbor during a rescue, or two players in a championship game).

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Empathy is the deeper lesson:** While the standard (RL.4.6) focuses on comparing narrative POV, the real payoff of this lesson is empathy building. Students learn that people experience the same events differently and that understanding multiple perspectives leads to a fuller picture. Let this emerge naturally from the texts.
- **No villain:** Deliberately, none of the paired passages have a clear villain. Both characters have understandable feelings. If students try to declare one character "right" and the other "wrong," push back gently: "Both characters have real feelings. Can both be telling the truth?"
- **Connecting to earlier lessons:** Point out that comparing perspectives builds on everything students have learned: traits (what kind of person is each narrator?), challenges (what problem does each narrator face?), and settings (are both narrators in the same physical place but experiencing it differently?).
- **Venn diagram consistency:** The Venn diagram is the anchor tool for this lesson. Keep it consistent across Worked Example, Guided Practice, and Independent Practice so students internalize the comparison strategy.
- **Looking ahead:** This lesson focuses on first-person perspective swaps. In later grades, students will analyze unreliable narrators and POV as a tool for building suspense. For now, the goal is recognizing that different narrators produce different stories.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. Both passages describe Kai giving a speech in class. Facts that stay the same: Kai was giving a speech; someone in the back whispered during the speech; Kai lost his place and had to look at his notes; he finished the speech and the class clapped.

2. **Venn Diagram:**
   - **Kai only:** Thinks Ellie was not paying attention and was whispering because she did not care. Blames Ellie for his mistake. Feels angry. Walks past Ellie without looking at her.
   - **Ellie only:** Was actually impressed by Kai's speech. Was whispering because she needed a tissue and was about to sneeze. Felt terrible when Kai stumbled. Wanted to tell Kai he did a great job but could not because he walked past her. Felt guilty.
   - **Both:** Were in the same classroom. There was whispering during the speech. Kai stumbled and lost his place. The class clapped at the end. Both felt bad after the speech.

3. Kai's claim that it was Ellie's "fault" is not entirely fair. In Ellie's version, she was not whispering because she was bored or being rude — she was asking her neighbor for a tissue because she was about to sneeze. She tried to be quiet about it. She did not intend to distract Kai at all and felt terrible when he stumbled. Kai assumed the worst because he could only see the whispering, not the reason behind it.

4. Reading only Kai's version, the reader might dislike Ellie and think she was rude. Reading only Ellie's version, the reader might think Kai overreacted. Reading both versions together, the reader understands that it was a misunderstanding — neither character did anything wrong, but each interpreted the situation based on their own perspective. This gives a much more complete and fair picture.

5. A third-person narrator would be able to show that Kai was nervous and hyper-focused on doing well (so any distraction felt huge), AND that Ellie was genuinely trying to hold in a sneeze and admired Kai's speech. The reader would understand the whole misunderstanding from the start — that Kai's anger was based on a wrong assumption and Ellie's guilt was based on an accident she could not control.

### Interleaved Review
6. Both versions are in first-person point of view. Version A uses "I" (referring to Kai) — "I practiced my speech," "I walked to the front," "I lost my place." Version B uses "I" (referring to Ellie) — "I felt a sneeze coming," "I covered my nose," "I wanted to go tell him."

7. **Kai trait:** Hardworking / dedicated — he practiced his speech twenty times and knew every word by heart. He set high expectations for himself. (Also acceptable: self-critical, as he focused on the one mistake instead of the overall success.) **Ellie trait:** Thoughtful / considerate — she tried to whisper quietly so she would not distract Kai, she felt terrible when he stumbled, and she wanted to tell him he did a great job. She worried about his feelings.

See expected answers above.
