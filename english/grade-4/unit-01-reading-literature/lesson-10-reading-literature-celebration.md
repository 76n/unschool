# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 10

## Topic
Reading Literature Celebration

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply all four unit skills — theme, character/setting/event analysis, point of view, and literary structure — to a single text
- Complete a culminating project analyzing a self-selected or provided text
- Reflect on growth as a reader across the unit

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of theme and how events build theme (Lessons 1-2)
- Ability to describe characters, settings, and events in depth (Lessons 3-5)
- Understanding of first-person and third-person point of view and perspective comparison (Lessons 6-7)
- Knowledge of structural elements of poems, drama, and prose (Lessons 8-9)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils and colored pencils
- Self-selected books or stories (students should choose in advance if possible)
- Printed "Literary Analysis Project" template (provided below)
- Optional: poster paper or construction paper for presentations

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all four skill areas from the unit and build excitement for the culminating project.

**Activity: Skills Showdown**

Say: "We have spent nine lessons building powerful reading skills. Let us see how many you can remember. I am going to give you a clue, and you tell me which skill it connects to."

Read aloud:
1. "I am a full sentence that tells the lesson or message of the story." — (Theme)
2. "I help you figure out WHO is telling the story by looking at pronouns." — (Point of view)
3. "I am a lasting quality like 'brave' or 'generous' — not a temporary feeling." — (Character trait)
4. "I include stanzas and line breaks." — (Poetry / literary structure)
5. "I answer the question: Where and when does the story take place, and what does it feel like?" — (Setting)
6. "I use dialogue and stage directions instead of a narrator." — (Drama / literary structure)
7. "I answer the question: Why did the character make that choice?" — (Character motivation)
8. "I show how the same event looks different depending on who tells it." — (Comparing point of view / perspectives)

Say: "You know all of these. Today, you get to put them ALL together in one project. You are going to analyze a story, poem, or play using every skill we have learned. Think of this as your chance to show what you know."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model the culminating analysis using a provided text, demonstrating how to apply all four skill areas to one passage.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Literary analysis:** Looking closely at a text to understand how its parts work together — theme, characters, setting, point of view, and structure.
- **Text evidence:** Specific details (words, sentences, events) from the text that support your analysis.
- **Culminating project:** A final project that brings together everything you have learned in the unit.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Before you start your own project, let me show you how to do a full literary analysis. I am going to analyze a short story and address all four skill areas. Watch how I organize my thinking."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Letter"**

> Every afternoon, Mrs. Kim sat on her porch and waited for the mail carrier. She had been waiting for a letter from her daughter, who lived across the ocean. It had been three months since the last one.
>
> One Tuesday, the mail carrier handed her a thick envelope. Mrs. Kim's hands trembled as she opened it. Inside were three pages of handwriting and a photograph of her daughter standing in front of a garden.
>
> Mrs. Kim read the letter slowly, out loud, even though no one was listening. Her daughter wrote about her new job, about planting tomatoes, about the stray cat that slept on her doorstep. Ordinary things. But to Mrs. Kim, every sentence was a gift.
>
> She read the letter three times. Then she placed it in a wooden box on her dresser, next to all the others. She looked at the photograph one more time, touched the edge of it gently, and whispered, "I am so proud of you."
>
> That evening, Mrs. Kim sat down at her kitchen table, picked up a pen, and began to write back.

Say: "Now let me do a full analysis."

Write on the board:

**1. Theme (RL.4.2)**

Say: "What is the topic? Distance, family, connection. What is the theme? Let me trace the events."

Write: Events: Mrs. Kim waits for the letter; she reads it three times, treasuring ordinary details; she saves it with the others; she writes back.

Say: "Theme: Love and connection between family members can be kept alive through small, everyday acts — like writing letters — even when distance separates them."

**2. Character Analysis (RL.4.3)**

Say: "What are Mrs. Kim's traits? Let me use the evidence."

Write:
- **Devoted / Loving:** She waits on the porch every afternoon for the letter; she reads it out loud three times; she saves every letter in a special box.
- **Patient:** She has been waiting three months without giving up.
- **Proud:** She whispers "I am so proud of you" while looking at the photograph.
- **Feeling (not trait):** She trembles when she opens the letter — that is excitement, a temporary feeling.

**3. Point of View (RL.4.6)**

Say: "What point of view is this? Third person — the narrator uses 'she' and 'her' to describe Mrs. Kim. The narrator tells us Mrs. Kim's thoughts and feelings. We do NOT know what the daughter is thinking or feeling — only what she wrote in her letter."

Write: "If this story were told from the daughter's first-person perspective, we would know how the daughter feels about being far from her mother. The story would feel very different."

**4. Literary Structure (RL.4.5)**

Say: "What form is this? Prose — it is written in paragraphs with a narrator, dialogue in quotation marks. If this were a poem, it might focus on one image — maybe just the moment Mrs. Kim opens the letter — and it would be shorter and more emotional. If this were a drama, we would see Mrs. Kim sitting on the porch, opening the letter, and the audience would hear her read parts of it aloud."

Say: "That is a complete literary analysis. Theme, character, point of view, and structure. Now it is your turn."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students prepare for their independent project by practicing the analysis framework with a partner.

Say: "Before you work on your own, let us practice the framework together with a short passage. Work with your partner."

**Passage: "The Map"**

> I found the map in a box in the garage. It was old and yellow, with creases so deep the paper almost tore when I unfolded it. Someone had drawn red circles around three places: the old church, the reservoir, and a spot in the woods marked with a tiny star.
>
> "Mom, whose map is this?" I asked.
>
> She looked at it for a long time. "That was your grandfather's. He loved exploring. He used to say every neighborhood has secrets if you look hard enough."
>
> I studied the map. The red circles seemed to pulse, like they were calling me. I grabbed my backpack, filled my water bottle, and headed for the door.
>
> "Be back by dinner," Mom called.
>
> "I will," I said. But in my head, I was already standing at the spot with the tiny star, wondering what Grandpa had found there.

Say: "With your partner, discuss and write brief answers for each skill area."

1. **Theme:** What is the theme? What events build toward it?
2. **Character:** What are the narrator's traits? Use evidence.
3. **Point of View:** What POV is this? What does the reader know and not know?
4. **Structure:** What form is this? How would it be different as a poem or drama?

Allow 4-5 minutes. Then discuss briefly:

1. **Theme:** Curiosity and the desire to explore and connect with the people who came before us lead us on new adventures. (Events: finding the map, learning it was grandfather's, feeling drawn to the marked places, heading out to explore.)
2. **Character traits:** Curious (studying the map, imagining the star), adventurous (grabbing backpack and heading out immediately), connected to family (asking about the map, thinking about what Grandpa found).
3. **POV:** First person — the narrator uses "I." The reader knows the narrator's thoughts and excitement but NOT what the mom is fully thinking, what Grandpa was like, or what is at the starred location.
4. **Structure:** Prose — paragraphs, dialogue in quotation marks, narrator. As a poem, it might focus on the image of the map and the feeling of discovery. As a drama, it would show the conversation with Mom and the narrator studying the map.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students complete the culminating Literary Analysis Project on a self-selected or provided text.

Say: "Now it is time for your project. If you brought a book or story you are reading, you will analyze a scene or chapter from it. If you did not bring one, you may use any of the passages from today's lesson or from earlier lessons in this unit."

**Literary Analysis Project Template:**

Students should copy or receive this framework and fill it in:

**My Text:**
- Title: _______________
- Author (if known): _______________
- Form: (Prose / Poetry / Drama)

**Part 1: Theme (RL.4.2)**
- What is the topic of this text? (1-2 words)
- What is the theme? (Full sentence)
- List two key events that build toward the theme.

**Part 2: Character Analysis (RL.4.3)**
- Choose one character. Name: _______________
- Trait 1: _______________ Evidence: _______________
- Trait 2: _______________ Evidence: _______________
- What challenge does this character face? How do they respond?

**Part 3: Setting (RL.4.3)**
- Where does the story take place?
- When does it take place?
- What is the atmosphere? (What does the setting feel like?)
- How does the setting influence the character or events?

**Part 4: Point of View (RL.4.6)**
- What point of view is this text written in? (first person / third person)
- What pronoun clues told you?
- What does the reader know because of this POV?
- What does the reader NOT know?

**Part 5: Literary Structure (RL.4.5)**
- What form is this text? (prose / poetry / drama)
- Name two structural elements you see in this text.
- How would this text be different in another form? (Choose one: poem, drama, or prose and explain.)

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete all five parts of the Literary Analysis Project using a provided passage or a self-selected text.
**Stretch:** Complete all five parts and add a "Connections" section: How does the theme of your text connect to the theme of another text you have read this unit? Compare the two.
**Challenge:** Complete all five parts, the Connections section, and write a short adaptation: Rewrite one paragraph or scene from your text in a different literary form (e.g., turn a prose paragraph into a poem or a drama scene). Explain what changed.

### Interleaved Review
Students will naturally review all unit skills through the project. No separate interleaved review items are needed for this lesson.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Say: "On a piece of paper, answer these three quick questions."

1. Which of the four unit skills was easiest for you? (Theme, Character/Setting/Event, Point of View, or Literary Structure) Why?
2. Which skill was hardest? Why?
3. Write one thing you know now about reading literature that you did not know before this unit.

**Expected Answers:** These are reflective questions with no single correct answer. Look for:
- The student can name all four skill areas correctly.
- The student provides a specific reason for what was easy or hard (not just "I liked it" or "it was boring").
- The student names a specific skill or concept in question 3 (e.g., "I learned that theme is not the same as topic" or "I learned that the same story feels different as a poem than as prose").

**Mastery = Student demonstrates awareness of all four skill areas, articulates a specific strength and challenge, and identifies a concrete learning gain.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- This is a reflection exit ticket, so "not meeting mastery" here means the student cannot name or distinguish the four skill areas. If this happens, schedule a brief review session to revisit the anchor charts before the unit assessment.
- Have the student write definitions for each skill area using their own words.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for the unit assessment.
- Celebrate the student's growth and encourage them to continue applying these skills in independent reading.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Presentation:** Students present their Literary Analysis Project to a partner, a small group, or the class. They explain their analysis and answer questions.
2. **Book Recommendation:** Students write a paragraph recommending their analyzed text to a classmate, using their analysis to explain what makes the text interesting (e.g., "The theme is about..., the main character is..., and it is told in first person, which makes you feel like you are really there.").
3. **Unit Reflection Letter:** Students write a letter to their future selves describing what they learned in this unit and what they want to remember about analyzing literature.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Self-selection guidance:** If students are choosing their own texts, help them select passages that are rich enough to analyze (not too simple). A good passage should have a clear character, a setting, and at least one event that reveals something about the character or theme. Picture books, short stories, and chapter book excerpts all work well.
- **Project support:** Some students will need scaffolding to complete the project. Pair struggling students with a partner, or allow them to use a passage from an earlier lesson that they already analyzed in part — they just need to fill in the remaining skill areas.
- **Celebration atmosphere:** This is the last lesson before the assessment. Keep the tone positive and celebratory. If time allows, let students share their analyses aloud. Reading analysis out loud reinforces learning and builds community.
- **Connecting to the assessment:** Let students know that the unit assessment will ask similar questions to the Literary Analysis Project — theme, character, setting, POV, and structure — but with new passages. Today's project is excellent preparation.
- **Time management:** The Independent Practice (the project) may take longer than 12 minutes for some students. If needed, extend this section to 15-18 minutes by shortening the Guided Practice to a brief oral discussion rather than a written exercise.
- **Portfolio opportunity:** Collect the Literary Analysis Projects for student portfolios. They serve as strong evidence of comprehension and analytical thinking.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice — "The Map"
1. **Theme:** Curiosity and the desire to explore connect us to the people who came before us and lead us on new adventures. Key events: (a) The narrator finds the old map with circled places, (b) Mom explains it was Grandpa's and shares his belief that every neighborhood has secrets, (c) The narrator grabs a backpack and heads out to explore.

2. **Character traits:** Curious — studies the map, imagines standing at the starred spot. Adventurous — immediately grabs backpack, fills water bottle, heads out. Connected to family — asks Mom about the map, thinks about what Grandpa found.

3. **POV:** First person. Pronouns: "I found," "I asked," "I studied." The reader knows the narrator's excitement and imagination (picturing standing at the starred spot). The reader does NOT know what Mom is really feeling, what Grandpa actually found at the starred locations, or what is there now.

4. **Structure:** Prose — organized in paragraphs, dialogue in quotation marks, narrator describes actions. As a poem, it might focus on the image of the map itself — the creases, the red circles, the tiny star — and compress the story into a few stanzas about discovery. As a drama, it would show the conversation between the narrator and Mom, with stage directions for studying the map and heading out the door.

### Independent Practice — Literary Analysis Project
Answers will vary by text. Use the rubric below to evaluate:

| Skill Area | Meets Expectations | Needs Improvement |
|------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Theme | States theme as a full sentence; lists 2 events that build toward it | States topic instead of theme, or gives no events |
| Character | Names 2 traits with text evidence; identifies challenge and response | Lists feelings instead of traits, or gives vague evidence |
| Setting | Describes where, when, and atmosphere with specific details; explains influence | Only names location without detail or atmosphere |
| Point of View | Correctly identifies POV with pronoun evidence; explains what reader knows/does not know | Misidentifies POV or cannot explain its effect |
| Structure | Correctly identifies form; names 2 structural elements; explains how another form would differ | Cannot identify form or name structural elements |

### Exit Ticket
See expected answers above (reflective — no single correct answer).
