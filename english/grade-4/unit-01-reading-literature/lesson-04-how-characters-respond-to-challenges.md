# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 4

## Topic
How Characters Respond to Challenges

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the main challenge or conflict a character faces in a story
- Analyze how a character responds to a challenge by examining their actions, decisions, and words
- Explain a character's motivation — why the character makes the choices they make

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify character traits using text evidence (Lesson 3)
- Ability to distinguish traits from feelings (Lesson 3)
- Understanding that characters' actions reveal who they are

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect personal experience with character challenges to build engagement and activate thinking about motivation.

**Activity: What Would You Do?**

Say: "Imagine this situation. You are at the park with your friends, and you see a younger kid sitting alone on a bench, crying. Nobody else seems to notice. What would you do?"

Allow students 30 seconds to think, then ask 2-3 students to share.

Say: "You all had different answers, and that is interesting because it tells me something about who you are. The choices you make when things are hard — when there is a challenge or a problem — reveal your character. The same is true for characters in stories."

Say: "Today we are going to study how characters respond to challenges. We are not just asking WHAT they do — we are asking WHY. Why does a character make the choices they make? That is called motivation."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify a character's challenge, analyze their response, and explain their motivation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Challenge/Conflict:** A problem, difficulty, or obstacle a character faces in the story. This is what makes the story interesting.
- **Response:** How a character reacts to the challenge — what they do, say, and decide.
- **Motivation:** The reason behind a character's choices. Motivation answers the question "Why did they do that?"
- **Internal challenge:** A problem inside the character (fear, doubt, guilt, jealousy).
- **External challenge:** A problem that comes from outside the character (a storm, a bully, a broken object, a rule).

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Every story has a challenge. Without a challenge, there would be no story — it would just be 'everything is fine, the end.' The challenge is what forces the character to make choices, and those choices tell us who they really are."

Write on the board:
**Challenge → Response → What It Reveals**

Say: "Here is the pattern we are going to follow today. First, we find the challenge. Then we look at how the character responds. Then we ask: What does that response tell us about the character? And why did they respond that way?"

Say: "Let me also teach you that challenges come in two types."

Write on the board:
- **External challenge:** Something outside the character (a problem at school, a broken bike, a lost pet)
- **Internal challenge:** Something inside the character (feeling afraid, feeling jealous, feeling unsure)

Say: "Sometimes a character faces both at the same time. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Storm"**

> Lena and her dad were canoeing on the lake when the sky turned dark. A cold wind blew across the water, and the waves started to rock their canoe.
>
> "We need to get to shore," Dad said. He started paddling hard.
>
> Lena gripped the sides of the canoe. Her heart was pounding. She wanted to close her eyes and wait for it to be over. But she saw her dad struggling against the wind, and she knew he could not do it alone.
>
> She picked up her paddle. Her arms were shaking, but she dug the paddle into the water and pulled. Again and again. The canoe crawled toward the shore.
>
> When they finally reached the bank, Dad helped her out. His hands were trembling too.
>
> "You were incredible out there," he said.
>
> Lena's arms ached, but she smiled. "I was really scared."
>
> "Me too," Dad said. "But you paddled anyway."

Say: "Let me use our pattern."

Write on the board:
- **Challenge (external):** A storm hit while Lena and her dad were canoeing on the lake. Waves rocked the canoe.
- **Challenge (internal):** Lena was terrified. She wanted to close her eyes and wait for it to be over.
- **Response:** Even though she was scared, Lena picked up her paddle and helped her dad get the canoe to shore.
- **What it reveals:** Lena is brave and selfless. She pushed past her fear to help.
- **Motivation:** She saw her dad struggling and knew he could not do it alone. Her love for her dad and her sense of responsibility motivated her to act.

Say: "Do you see how both challenges work together? The storm is the external challenge — it is outside Lena. Her fear is the internal challenge — it is inside her. She had to overcome both. And her motivation — why she did it — was because she cared about her dad and knew he needed help."

Say: "Notice something else. Lena said 'I was really scared.' Her dad said 'Me too. But you paddled anyway.' Being brave does not mean you are not scared. It means you act even when you are scared. That is what her response reveals about her character."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying challenges, responses, and motivations with teacher guidance.

Say: "Let us try this together. I will read a new passage. In your notebook, set up the pattern: Challenge, Response, What It Reveals, Motivation."

**Passage: "The New Kid"**

> It was Ezra's first day at a new school. He stood at the edge of the cafeteria, holding his lunch tray, and looked for an empty table. Every table was full of kids talking and laughing. Nobody looked up.
>
> Ezra's throat tightened. At his old school, he had always sat with Marcus and Isaiah. They had been friends since kindergarten. Here, he did not know a single person.
>
> He thought about eating in the hallway. It would be easier than walking up to a table of strangers. But then he remembered something his mom had said that morning: "Nobody is going to come find you. You have to be the one to walk over."
>
> Ezra took a deep breath. He walked to a table where three boys were trading baseball cards.
>
> "Can I sit here?" he asked. His voice came out smaller than he meant it to.
>
> One of the boys looked up. "Sure. Do you collect cards?"
>
> "No," Ezra said. "But I could learn."
>
> The boy grinned. "Pull up a chair."

Say: "Okay, talk to your partner. What is the external challenge Ezra faces?"

Guide toward: He is new at school, does not know anyone, and cannot find a place to sit at lunch.

Say: "What is the internal challenge?"

Guide toward: He is nervous and scared. He misses his old friends. He wants to avoid the situation by eating in the hallway.

Say: "Now, how does Ezra respond? What does he do?"

Guide toward: He walks up to a table of strangers and asks if he can sit there. He admits he does not know about baseball cards but says he could learn.

Say: "What does that response reveal about Ezra?"

Guide toward: He is brave enough to try even when he is uncomfortable. He is open-minded and willing to learn new things.

Say: "And the big question — what is his motivation? Why does he make that choice instead of hiding in the hallway?"

Guide toward: He remembers his mom's advice that nobody will come find him — he has to take the first step. He wants to make friends even though it is scary.

Have students write the complete pattern in their notebooks.

Say: "Now think about theme. What theme might this story have?"

Guide toward: Sometimes you have to be brave and take the first step, even when it is uncomfortable, because good things will not come to you on their own.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze how a character responds to a challenge.

**Passage: "The Promise"**

> Keiko had promised her best friend, Noor, that she would come to Noor's birthday party on Saturday. But on Thursday, Jade — the most popular girl in fourth grade — invited Keiko to go to the water park on the same day.
>
> "Everyone is going," Jade said. "It is going to be the best day ever."
>
> Keiko had always wanted to be invited somewhere by Jade's group. She imagined the water slides, the wave pool, the snack bar. Her stomach fluttered with excitement.
>
> But then she thought about Noor. Noor had been planning her birthday party for weeks. She had told Keiko she was making a special playlist with all their favorite songs. She had asked Keiko to help her set up decorations.
>
> "I wish I could," Keiko told Jade. "But I already made a promise to my friend."
>
> Jade shrugged. "Your loss."
>
> For a second, Keiko felt a sharp pang. Was she making a mistake? But when she called Noor that night and heard how excited Noor was about the party, Keiko knew she had made the right choice.
>
> "I cannot wait," Noor said. "It would not be the same without you."
>
> Keiko smiled into the phone. "I cannot wait either."

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences.

1. What is Keiko's external challenge? What is her internal challenge?
2. How does Keiko respond to the challenge? Describe her decision.
3. What is Keiko's motivation for making that choice? Use evidence from the text.
4. What character trait does Keiko's response reveal? Support your answer with at least two details from the passage.
5. After Keiko turns down Jade's invitation, she feels "a sharp pang." Is this a trait or a feeling? Explain.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-5 as written.
**Stretch:** After answering questions 1-5, write a paragraph explaining what you think would have happened if Keiko had gone to the water park instead. How would Noor have felt? How might Keiko have felt? Would the theme of the story change?
**Challenge:** After completing all questions, write about a time you had to choose between something you wanted to do and something you had promised to do. What did you decide? How did it turn out? Compare your experience to Keiko's.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Lesson 3 review) Create a mini character trait web for Noor using only the evidence available in the passage. Include at least one trait with evidence.
7. (Lesson 1 review) What is the theme of "The Promise"? Write it as a complete sentence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following mini-passage:

> Ben was supposed to turn in his book report on Monday, but he had not started it. On Sunday afternoon, his friend texted and asked him to play basketball. Ben wanted to go, but he sat down at his desk and started writing. "I am not making excuses this time," he said to himself.

1. What is Ben's challenge?
2. How does Ben respond?
3. What does his response reveal about his character? What is his motivation?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Challenge: Ben has a book report due on Monday and has not started it, but his friend is asking him to play basketball (external: deadline and invitation; internal: wanting to play instead of work).
2. Response: He stays home and starts writing the book report instead of going to play.
3. His response reveals that he is responsible and determined. His motivation is that he does not want to make excuses anymore — he wants to follow through on his responsibilities.

**Mastery = Student identifies the challenge, describes the response, and provides a reasonable explanation of what the response reveals about the character (trait + motivation).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Work with the student on the Challenge → Response → Reveal pattern using a simple, familiar story (e.g., Goldilocks, The Three Billy Goats Gruff).
- Provide the sentence frame: "The character chose to ___ because ___. This shows that the character is ___."
- Practice orally with two additional scenarios before moving on.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 5, which examines settings and events in depth.
- Encourage the student to use the Challenge → Response → Reveal pattern when reading independently.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **What If?** The student rewrites the ending of "The Promise" so that Keiko makes the opposite choice (goes to the water park). Then the student explains how the theme changes.
2. **Character Challenge Chart:** The student creates a three-column chart for a book they are currently reading: Challenge | Response | What It Reveals.
3. **Two-Voice Poem:** The student writes a short poem from Keiko's perspective and another from Noor's perspective about the same event (the birthday party decision), showing how each character experienced it differently.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Motivation is the new concept:** Students practiced identifying traits in Lesson 3. Lesson 4 adds the "why" — motivation. This is more abstract and requires inference. Model the thinking process explicitly: "I know the character chose X. Why? What did they care about? What were they afraid of?"
- **Internal vs. external challenges:** Some students will only see the external challenge. Keep prompting: "That is what happened to the character. But what was happening inside the character? What were they feeling or worrying about?"
- **Character vs. self:** Many students will connect personally to these scenarios. That is great — personal connections deepen comprehension. But redirect if a student's sharing becomes too personal or lengthy for the group setting.
- **Connecting challenge to theme:** Help students see that the way a character handles a challenge almost always connects to the theme. In "The Promise," Keiko's choice to keep her promise connects to the theme about loyalty and integrity.
- **Pacing:** The Guided Practice passage is rich and may generate extended discussion. If you are running short on time, have students answer Independent Practice questions 1-4 in class and assign 5-7 as homework.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **External challenge:** Jade invites Keiko to the water park on the same day as Noor's birthday party, and Keiko has to choose between the two. **Internal challenge:** Keiko has always wanted to be invited by Jade's group, so she is tempted to go. She has to struggle between what she wants and what she promised.

2. Keiko tells Jade that she already made a promise to her friend and turns down the water park invitation. She chooses to keep her commitment to Noor.

3. Keiko's motivation is her loyalty to Noor and her belief that keeping a promise matters. Evidence: She thought about how Noor had been planning the party for weeks, made a special playlist, and asked Keiko to help with decorations. When she heard Noor's excitement on the phone ("It would not be the same without you"), she knew she had made the right choice.

4. Keiko's response reveals that she is **loyal** and has **integrity** (she keeps her promises). Evidence: (1) She tells Jade "I already made a promise to my friend" — she does not make up an excuse or lie. (2) Even when Jade says "Your loss" and Keiko feels a pang of regret, she still sticks with her decision. (3) She called Noor that night, showing she cared about the friendship.

5. The "sharp pang" Keiko feels is a feeling, not a trait. It is a temporary emotion — a moment of doubt and regret — that passes once she hears Noor's excitement. Her trait is loyalty, which is lasting and is shown by her decision to keep her promise despite the temptation.

### Interleaved Review
6. **Noor trait web:** Trait: **Thoughtful / Caring** — Evidence: Noor made a special playlist with all their favorite songs for her birthday party, and she asked Keiko to help set up decorations, showing she values their friendship and wanted Keiko to feel included.

7. **Theme:** Keeping your promises to the people who matter most is more important than chasing something exciting and new. (Accept reasonable variations about loyalty, integrity, or true friendship.)

See expected answers above.
