# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1: Reading Literature

## Unit Assessment

### Overview
This assessment evaluates student mastery of the four standards addressed in Unit 1:
- **RL.4.2** — Determine a theme of a story, drama, or poem from details in the text; summarize the text
- **RL.4.3** — Describe in depth a character, setting, or event in a story or drama, drawing on specific details in the text
- **RL.4.6** — Compare and contrast the point of view from which different stories are narrated, including the difference between first- and third-person narrations
- **RL.4.5** — Explain major differences between poems, drama, and prose, and refer to structural elements of each

### Administration Notes
- **Estimated time:** 40-50 minutes
- **Format:** 5 passages with 4-5 questions each (24 questions total)
- **Scoring:** Each question is worth 1 point. Total = 24 points.
- **Mastery threshold:** 70% overall (17/24) AND at least 50% in each standard
- Students may reread passages as needed
- Read directions aloud if needed for students with accommodations

## Passage 1: "The Quilt" (Prose)
*Standards assessed: RL.4.2 (Theme), RL.4.3 (Character/Setting)*

> Abuela spread the old quilt across the bed. It was faded and patched in several places. Some squares were made from flowered fabric, some from blue denim, and one — in the very center — was cut from a soft white shirt with tiny pearl buttons.
>
> "This quilt tells our family's story," Abuela said. She pointed to a yellow square. "This was from your mother's favorite dress when she was your age." She pointed to a piece of rough brown fabric. "This was from your grandfather's work jacket. He wore it every day on the farm."
>
> Iris ran her fingers across the different textures. The flowered fabric was silky. The denim was stiff. The white shirt square was the softest of all.
>
> "Whose shirt was that?" Iris asked, touching the center square.
>
> Abuela smiled but her eyes looked watery. "That was your great-grandmother's. She wore it on the day she arrived in this country. She had nothing else, but she had that shirt and she had courage."
>
> Iris looked at the quilt differently now. It was not just old fabric stitched together. It was a map of her family — every square a person, every stitch a memory.
>
> "Can I sleep under it tonight?" Iris asked.
>
> "Of course," Abuela said. "That is what it is for — to keep you warm and to remind you where you come from."

**Questions:**

**1.** What is the theme of "The Quilt"? Write your answer as a complete sentence. (RL.4.2)

**2.** List two key events from the passage that help build toward the theme. (RL.4.2)

**3.** Describe Abuela using two character traits. For each trait, provide specific evidence from the text that supports it. (RL.4.3)

**4.** Describe the setting's atmosphere — what does the scene feel like? Use at least two details from the text to support your answer. (RL.4.3)

**5.** When Iris looks at the quilt differently and sees it as "a map of her family — every square a person, every stitch a memory," what does this tell the reader about how Iris has changed during the passage? (RL.4.3)

## Passage 2: "The Storm Cellar" (First Person)
*Standards assessed: RL.4.6 (Point of View), RL.4.3 (Character)*

> I heard the sirens go off just as I was feeding the chickens. The sky turned a strange green color, like nothing I had ever seen. My dad came running out of the house.
>
> "Tornado warning! Get to the cellar, NOW!"
>
> I dropped the feed bucket and ran. The wind was already picking up, pulling at my shirt and making the trees bend sideways. I grabbed my little sister, Rosie, who was standing in the yard looking confused. She was only five. She did not understand what was happening.
>
> "Come on, Rosie. We have to go underground," I said, trying to keep my voice calm even though my heart felt like it might explode.
>
> We made it to the storm cellar just as the rain started. Dad pulled the heavy door shut and latched it. Inside, it was dark and damp. The concrete walls were cold. I could hear the wind screaming outside, getting louder and louder.
>
> Rosie buried her face in my shoulder. "Is the sky falling?" she whispered.
>
> "No," I said. "It is just a really bad storm. We are safe down here."
>
> I held her tight and listened. The sound outside was like a train rumbling through our yard. It lasted maybe five minutes, but it felt like an hour.
>
> When Dad finally opened the door, the first thing I saw was sunlight. The storm had passed. I carried Rosie up the steps. The big oak tree in the yard had fallen, and the chicken coop was sideways, but our house was still standing.
>
> "Is everybody okay?" Dad asked.
>
> I looked at Rosie, who was blinking at the sunlight. I looked at Dad, who was breathing hard but smiling.
>
> "Everybody is okay," I said.

**Questions:**

**6.** What point of view is this passage written in? List two pronoun clues from the narration (not dialogue) that helped you decide. (RL.4.6)

**7.** Because of this point of view, whose thoughts and feelings does the reader know? Whose thoughts and feelings does the reader NOT know? Give a specific example. (RL.4.6)

**8.** If this story were rewritten in third person, name one new piece of information the narrator could include that the first-person narrator cannot. (RL.4.6)

**9.** Identify two character traits for the narrator. For each trait, provide specific evidence from the text. (RL.4.3)

**10.** Is "scared" a character trait or a character feeling for the narrator? Explain your answer using evidence from the passage. (RL.4.3)

## Passage 3: Two Versions of the Same Event
*Standards assessed: RL.4.6 (Comparing Points of View), RL.4.2 (Theme)*

**Version A — Wen's Perspective:**

> I spent all morning building the tallest tower I had ever made out of blocks. It was fourteen levels high and almost reached the top of the bookshelf. I stacked each block carefully, holding my breath every time the tower wobbled. When I placed the last block on top, I felt like I had built a skyscraper.
>
> Then Caleb walked by and bumped the table. The whole tower crashed. Blocks flew everywhere. I stared at the pile on the floor and wanted to scream.
>
> "Oops," Caleb said, and kept walking. He did not even stop.
>
> I sat there for a long time, looking at the mess. Then I picked up the first block and started again.

**Version B — Caleb's Perspective:**

> I was walking to the reading corner to get my book when my backpack strap caught on the edge of the table. The table shook, and Wen's block tower came crashing down. I felt terrible.
>
> "Oops," I said, but I was so embarrassed I did not know what else to say. I kept walking because I was afraid Wen would yell at me. I could feel my face turning red.
>
> From the reading corner, I watched Wen sit on the floor, staring at the blocks. I wanted to go help, but I was too nervous. Then Wen picked up a block and started rebuilding. I wished I had said sorry. I wished I had stayed.

**Questions:**

**11.** In Wen's version, Wen believes Caleb "did not even stop" and did not care. According to Caleb's version, is that true? Use evidence from Caleb's passage to explain. (RL.4.6)

**12.** What does the reader understand by reading BOTH versions that neither Wen nor Caleb understands alone? (RL.4.6)

**13.** What is the theme of these two passages taken together? Write your answer as a complete sentence. (RL.4.2)

**14.** Both versions are written in first person. Identify one pronoun clue from EACH version that confirms this. (RL.4.6)

## Passage 4: A Poem
*Standards assessed: RL.4.5 (Literary Structure), RL.4.2 (Theme)*

> **Moving Day**
>
> The boxes are stacked by the door,
> Taped shut, labeled in marker.
> My room is an echo now —
> Every sound bounces off bare walls.
>
> I press my hand against the windowsill
> Where I carved my initials in second grade.
> Still there. My letters in the wood,
> Proof that I lived in this house.
>
> Outside, the moving truck hums.
> My mother calls my name.
> I take one last look —
> The empty closet, the scuffed floor,
> The rectangle on the wall
> Where my poster used to hang.
>
> I close the door softly,
> Like tucking someone into bed,
> And carry my last box
> Into whatever comes next.

**Questions:**

**15.** What literary form is this text? Name two structural elements that helped you identify it. (RL.4.5)

**16.** The poem is organized into four stanzas. What happens in the last stanza? How does separating it into its own stanza affect the reader's experience? (RL.4.5)

**17.** If this poem were rewritten as prose (a paragraph in a story), what details or information could the author ADD that the poem does not include? Name at least one. (RL.4.5)

**18.** What is the theme of this poem? Write your answer as a complete sentence. Use one piece of evidence from the poem to support it. (RL.4.2)

## Passage 5: A Drama Scene
*Standards assessed: RL.4.5 (Literary Structure), RL.4.3 (Character/Setting/Event)*

> SCENE 1: A school hallway. The final bell has rung. Students are leaving. JADA stands at her locker, looking at a crumpled piece of paper. Her friend TYLER walks over.
>
> TYLER: Hey, you coming to the park?
>
> JADA: (not looking up) Maybe later.
>
> TYLER: (noticing the paper) What is that?
>
> JADA: (sighing) I got my math test back. (She holds up the paper.) Fifty-eight percent.
>
> TYLER: (wincing) Ouch. That is rough.
>
> JADA: I studied for two hours. Two hours, Tyler. And I still failed. Maybe I am just bad at math.
>
> TYLER: (leaning against the locker next to her) You are not bad at math. Remember when you helped me figure out fractions? You explained it better than the teacher.
>
> JADA: (quietly) Fractions are different. This was long division and word problems. I do not even understand what the questions are asking.
>
> TYLER: So ask for help. Go talk to Ms. Rivera. She does tutoring on Thursdays.
>
> JADA: (crumpling the paper more) I do not want her to think I am stupid.
>
> TYLER: (firmly) You helped me when I was struggling. That is not stupid — that is brave. Going to get help when you need it is the same thing.
>
> (JADA looks at TYLER for a long moment. She slowly un-crumples the paper and folds it neatly.)
>
> JADA: Thursday tutoring?
>
> TYLER: Thursday tutoring. I will even go with you.
>
> JADA: (smiling for the first time) Okay. Thursday.

**Questions:**

**19.** What literary form is this text? Name three structural elements from the passage that helped you identify it. (RL.4.5)

**20.** In this drama scene, we learn about Jada's feelings through her dialogue and the stage directions. Find one example of a stage direction that reveals how Jada feels. What does it tell you? (RL.4.5)

**21.** Describe Jada using two character traits. For each, provide evidence from the text (dialogue or stage directions). (RL.4.3)

**22.** How does Jada's response to her challenge change during the scene? Describe her response at the beginning and her response at the end. What causes the change? (RL.4.3)

**23.** Describe the setting of this scene. How does the setting (a school hallway after the final bell) influence the conversation between Jada and Tyler? (RL.4.3)

**24.** How is reading this drama scene different from reading a prose story about the same event? What does the drama version do well, and what does it leave out compared to prose? (RL.4.5)

## Answer Key

### Passage 1: "The Quilt"

**1.** (RL.4.2) Theme: Family memories and history connect us to the people who came before us and remind us where we come from. (Accept reasonable variations that capture the idea that family stories/history are carried through objects and memories.)

**2.** (RL.4.2) Any two of the following:
- Abuela explains that each square of the quilt came from a family member's clothing, turning ordinary fabric into meaningful stories.
- Abuela tells Iris about great-grandmother's shirt — that she arrived in a new country with nothing but that shirt and courage.
- Iris begins to see the quilt not as old fabric but as "a map of her family — every square a person, every stitch a memory."
- Iris asks to sleep under the quilt, and Abuela says it is meant "to keep you warm and to remind you where you come from."

**3.** (RL.4.3) Two traits for Abuela:
- **Loving / Nurturing:** She carefully spreads the quilt and shares family stories with Iris, passing down memories from generation to generation. She says the quilt is meant to keep Iris warm and remind her of her roots.
- **Proud of her family / Sentimental:** She remembers whose clothing each square came from and tells the stories with care. Her eyes get watery when talking about great-grandmother, showing deep emotional connection to her family history.

**4.** (RL.4.3) The atmosphere is warm, intimate, and tender. Details: Abuela and Iris are together on a bed, touching the quilt and sharing stories. Iris runs her fingers across different textures. Abuela's eyes are watery when she talks about great-grandmother. The scene feels like a quiet, private moment between grandmother and granddaughter.

**5.** (RL.4.3) At the beginning, Iris seems to see the quilt as just an old blanket — she notices that it is "faded and patched." By the end, after hearing Abuela's stories, she sees it as "a map of her family — every square a person, every stitch a memory." This change shows that Iris has gained a deeper understanding of her family's history and now values the quilt not for what it looks like but for what it represents. She even asks to sleep under it, showing she wants to feel connected to that history.

### Passage 2: "The Storm Cellar"

**6.** (RL.4.6) First-person point of view. Pronoun clues (any two from narration, not dialogue): "I heard the sirens," "I dropped the feed bucket," "I grabbed my little sister," "my heart felt like it might explode," "I held her tight," "I carried Rosie up the steps."

**7.** (RL.4.6) The reader knows the narrator's thoughts and feelings — for example, the narrator's heart "felt like it might explode" and they tried to keep their voice calm even though they were scared. The reader does NOT know what Dad or Rosie are truly thinking or feeling from the inside. For example, when Rosie asks "Is the sky falling?" the reader knows she said it, but we can only guess what she was feeling because the narrator cannot read her mind.

**8.** (RL.4.6) A third-person narrator could reveal what Dad was thinking or feeling during the storm — for example, whether he was as scared as the narrator, or whether he was thinking about the house, the animals, or his children's safety. (Also accept: what Rosie was actually thinking, or what was happening outside while they were in the cellar.)

**9.** (RL.4.3) Two traits for the narrator:
- **Protective / Caring:** The narrator grabbed Rosie immediately and carried her to the cellar, held her tight during the storm, told her they were safe, and carried her up the steps afterward.
- **Brave / Responsible:** Even though the narrator was terrified (heart "might explode"), they kept their voice calm for Rosie's sake and stayed strong throughout the emergency.

**10.** (RL.4.3) "Scared" is a feeling, not a trait. The narrator felt scared during the tornado — their heart was pounding and they were afraid. But their lasting character traits are bravery and protectiveness, shown by how they acted DESPITE being scared. They kept their voice calm, held Rosie, and told her they were safe. The fear was temporary; the courage was lasting.

### Passage 3: Two Versions — Wen and Caleb

**11.** (RL.4.6) No, it is not true that Caleb did not care. According to Caleb's version, he "felt terrible" when the tower crashed, but he was "so embarrassed" he did not know what to say. He kept walking because he was "afraid Wen would yell" at him. He watched Wen from the reading corner and "wished I had said sorry" and "wished I had stayed." Caleb cared very much — he just did not know how to express it.

**12.** (RL.4.6) By reading both versions, the reader understands that this was a misunderstanding. Wen thinks Caleb does not care, but Caleb actually feels terrible and regrets not apologizing. Wen does not know that Caleb was watching and wanting to help. Caleb does not know that Wen interpreted his silence as not caring. The reader can see both sides and understand that the real problem is that neither person communicated what they were feeling.

**13.** (RL.4.2) Theme: Misunderstandings happen when people do not communicate their true feelings, and things are often not what they seem from only one perspective. (Accept reasonable variations: "You cannot always know what someone else is feeling just by watching their actions" or "Before judging someone, consider that there might be a side of the story you do not know.")

**14.** (RL.4.6) Version A (Wen): "I spent all morning building" — the narrator uses "I." Version B (Caleb): "I was walking to the reading corner" — the narrator uses "I." Both narrators are characters in the story telling their own experience.

### Passage 4: "Moving Day" (Poem)

**15.** (RL.4.5) This is a poem (poetry). Structural elements (any two): It is organized into stanzas (four stanzas separated by blank lines). It uses line breaks (each line starts on a new line rather than flowing in paragraphs). It uses imagery and compressed language ("My room is an echo now," "Like tucking someone into bed"). It does not use traditional paragraph structure, a narrator telling the story, or dialogue in quotation marks.

**16.** (RL.4.5) In the last stanza, the speaker closes the door "softly, / Like tucking someone into bed" and carries the last box "Into whatever comes next." Separating this into its own stanza creates a pause before the final moment — it slows the reader down and gives the ending more emotional weight. The reader feels the finality of the goodbye. The stanza break also marks a shift from looking back (remembering the room) to moving forward (leaving), which makes the transition feel significant.

**17.** (RL.4.5) As prose, the author could add: the character's direct thoughts about how they feel about moving (e.g., whether they are excited, sad, or both), a conversation with the mother about why they are moving, background information about where they are going, or a narrator's description of memories made in the house. The poem leaves these out and focuses on images and feelings, while prose would give more context and explanation. (Accept any reasonable answer.)

**18.** (RL.4.2) Theme: Even when we have to leave places we love, we carry the memories and experiences with us into the future. Evidence: The speaker carves initials in the windowsill ("Proof that I lived in this house") and closes the door "softly, / Like tucking someone into bed" — showing they are saying a loving goodbye but also moving forward "Into whatever comes next." (Accept reasonable variations about change, letting go, and memory.)

### Passage 5: Drama Scene — Jada and Tyler

**19.** (RL.4.5) This is drama (a play). Structural elements (any three): Scene heading with setting description ("SCENE 1: A school hallway..."), character names in capitals before their lines (JADA, TYLER), stage directions in parentheses telling actors what to do ("not looking up," "sighing," "leaning against the locker"), no quotation marks around dialogue, the story is told entirely through dialogue and stage directions (no narrator describing events).

**20.** (RL.4.5) Example stage directions that reveal Jada's feelings (any one):
- "(sighing)" — reveals she is frustrated or discouraged.
- "(crumpling the paper more)" — reveals she is upset and possibly wants to hide the test or is expressing anger/disappointment.
- "(quietly)" — reveals she is ashamed or insecure when she says she does not understand the questions.
- "(smiling for the first time)" — reveals she finally feels hopeful or supported.
- "(She slowly un-crumples the paper and folds it neatly)" — reveals she is starting to accept the situation and take a step forward instead of giving up.

**21.** (RL.4.3) Two traits for Jada:
- **Hardworking / Dedicated:** She studied for two hours for the math test, showing she puts effort into her schoolwork even though it is hard for her. She also previously helped Tyler with fractions, showing she engages seriously with learning.
- **Caring / Generous:** Tyler says Jada helped him figure out fractions and "explained it better than the teacher." She was willing to help a friend even when she had her own struggles.
(Also accept: Insecure / Self-doubting — she says "Maybe I am just bad at math" and is afraid Ms. Rivera will think she is "stupid," though she ultimately agrees to seek help, showing growth.)

**22.** (RL.4.3) At the beginning, Jada feels defeated and is ready to give up. She crumples the paper, says she is "just bad at math," and does not want to ask for help because she is afraid of being judged. By the end, she un-crumples the paper, folds it neatly, and agrees to go to Thursday tutoring. The change is caused by Tyler's encouragement — specifically, his reminder that she helped HIM when he was struggling, and his statement that "going to get help when you need it" is brave, not stupid. Tyler's offer to go with her also gives her the confidence to say yes.

**23.** (RL.4.3) The setting is a school hallway after the final bell, with students leaving. This setting influences the conversation because: (1) the hallway is emptying, giving Jada and Tyler a semi-private space to talk honestly — Jada might not share her test score in a crowded cafeteria; (2) standing at her locker, Jada is looking at the crumpled paper, which means the bad test result is fresh and immediate; (3) the fact that it is after school means there is time to have this conversation without being rushed to class.

**24.** (RL.4.5) The drama version is built for performance — the audience would see the actors' facial expressions and hear the tone of their voices, which adds emotional power to moments like Jada's sigh and Tyler's firm encouragement. The stage directions guide the actors on how to deliver the lines. However, the drama version leaves out internal thoughts — we do not know what Jada is thinking when she pauses and un-crumples the paper, only what she does. In a prose version, a narrator could describe her inner thoughts (e.g., "Maybe Tyler was right. Maybe asking for help was not the same as admitting she was stupid."). Prose gives more internal access; drama gives more external action and dialogue.

### Scoring Notes
- Questions requiring two items (e.g., "two traits with evidence," "two key events") should receive full credit if both items are complete and accurate. Award half credit if only one item is provided correctly.
- Accept reasonable variations in theme statements as long as the student has expressed a complete thought (not just a topic word) and it is supported by the text.
- For point-of-view questions, the student must identify the correct POV AND provide the requested evidence or explanation.
- For structure questions, the student must name the correct form AND identify valid structural elements.
