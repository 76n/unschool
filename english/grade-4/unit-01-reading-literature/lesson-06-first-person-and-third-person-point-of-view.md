# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 6

## Topic
First-Person and Third-Person Point of View

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define first-person and third-person point of view and explain the difference between them
- Identify whether a passage is written in first person or third person by looking at pronoun clues
- Explain how the narrator's point of view affects what the reader knows about the characters and events

## Prerequisites
- Ability to describe characters in depth using text evidence (Lesson 3)
- Understanding that different characters have different experiences and feelings (Lesson 4)
- Familiarity with pronouns (I, me, my vs. he, she, they, his, her, their)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Index cards (4-6 per student for sorting activity)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept that the same event looks different depending on who is telling the story.

**Activity: Who Is Telling the Story?**

Say: "Listen carefully. I am going to describe the same event two different ways."

Read aloud:

Version 1: "I walked into the cafeteria and looked for my friends. I could not see them anywhere. My stomach dropped. I did not want to sit alone."

Version 2: "Zara walked into the cafeteria and looked for her friends. She could not see them anywhere. Her stomach dropped. She did not want to sit alone."

Say: "What is different about those two versions? Turn to your partner and discuss."

Allow 30 seconds, then call on students.

Guide toward: In Version 1, the character is saying "I" — she is telling her own story. In Version 2, someone else is describing what Zara did and felt, using "she" and "her."

Say: "Great observation. The difference you just noticed is called point of view. It is about WHO is telling the story. Today we are going to learn the names for these two kinds of narration and figure out why it matters."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach first-person and third-person point of view, their defining features, and how each affects the reader's experience.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Point of view (POV):** Who is telling the story. The point of view determines what the reader can see, hear, know, and feel.
- **First-person point of view:** The story is told by a character IN the story, using the pronouns I, me, my, we, us. The reader sees everything through that one character's eyes.
- **Third-person point of view:** The story is told by a narrator OUTSIDE the story, using the pronouns he, she, they, his, her, their. The narrator can describe what different characters do and sometimes what they think.
- **Narrator:** The voice telling the story. The narrator may or may not be a character in the story.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "There are two main points of view we are going to study. Let me put them side by side."

Write on the board:

| First Person | Third Person |
|--------------|-------------|
| The narrator IS a character in the story | The narrator is OUTSIDE the story |
| Uses: I, me, my, we, us | Uses: he, she, they, his, her, their |
| The reader only knows what THIS character sees, thinks, and feels | The reader can learn about multiple characters |
| Example: "I ran to the door." | Example: "She ran to the door." |

Say: "The easiest way to tell the difference is to look at the pronouns. If you see 'I' telling the story — not in dialogue, but in the narration — that is first person. If you see 'he,' 'she,' or 'they' in the narration, that is third person."

Say: "But here is the tricky part. Characters can say 'I' in their dialogue even in a third-person story. So you need to check the narration — the parts that are NOT inside quotation marks."

Write an example:
> She sat down and opened her book. "I love this chapter," she said.

Say: "What point of view is this? The narrator says 'She sat down' and 'her book.' The character says 'I love this chapter' inside quotation marks, but that is dialogue, not narration. So this is third person."

Say: "Now here is why this matters. In first person, you are stuck inside one character's head. You only know what THEY know. You see the world through THEIR eyes. In third person, the narrator can show you what different characters are doing, even when they are apart."

### Worked Examples

Say: "Let me read two versions of the same story. Listen for the differences."

**Version A — First Person:**

> I watched my little brother climb the oak tree in our backyard. He grabbed the lowest branch and pulled himself up. My hands started to sweat. I wanted to tell him to come down, but he looked so happy up there.
>
> "Be careful!" I called.
>
> "I am fine!" he yelled back, climbing higher.
>
> I could not see his face anymore. All I could see were his sneakers dangling between the leaves. I did not know if he was scared or having the time of his life. I just stood there, holding my breath.

**Version B — Third Person:**

> Dani watched her little brother, Leo, climb the oak tree in their backyard. He grabbed the lowest branch and pulled himself up. Dani's hands started to sweat. She wanted to tell him to come down, but he looked so happy up there.
>
> "Be careful!" Dani called.
>
> "I am fine!" Leo yelled back, climbing higher. His heart was racing, but not from fear — from excitement. He could see the whole neighborhood from up here. The rooftops, the park, even the school. He grinned and reached for the next branch.
>
> Down below, Dani could not see his face. She did not know he was grinning.

Say: "What is different? In Version A — first person — we are inside Dani's head. We see what she sees and feel what she feels. She says 'I did not know if he was scared or having the time of his life.' We do not know either, because we are stuck with her."

Say: "In Version B — third person — the narrator can show us BOTH characters. We see Dani's worry AND Leo's excitement. We know Leo is grinning, even though Dani does not. Third person lets the reader know more than any single character knows."

Write on the board:
- **First person** = Reader knows only what one character knows
- **Third person** = Reader can know what multiple characters know

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying point of view and analyzing how it affects what the reader knows.

**Activity: POV Detective**

Say: "I am going to read three short passages. For each one, decide: Is this first person or third person? How do you know? And what does the reader know because of this point of view?"

**Passage 1:**

> My best friend, Ravi, told me a secret at recess. I promised I would not tell anyone. But when I got home, my sister asked why I was smiling. I almost told her. I had to bite my lip to stop the words from coming out.

Say: "First person or third person?"

Students: First person.

Say: "How do you know?"

Students: The narrator uses "I," "my," and "me."

Say: "What does the reader know because of this POV?"

Guide toward: We know the narrator was told a secret, that they promised to keep it, and that they almost told their sister. But we do NOT know what the secret is (if the narrator does not tell us) and we do NOT know how Ravi feels.

**Passage 2:**

> Priya sat alone on the bus, her backpack on the seat beside her. She stared out the window and pressed her forehead against the cool glass. Two rows behind her, Marcus watched and wondered if she was okay. He thought about saying something but was not sure what to say.

Say: "First person or third person?"

Students: Third person.

Say: "How do you know?"

Students: The narrator uses "she," "her," "he," and "his."

Say: "What does the reader know?"

Guide toward: We know what BOTH characters are doing and thinking. We know Priya seems sad and that Marcus is worried about her. Neither character knows what the other is thinking, but the reader knows both.

**Passage 3:**

> I had no idea that my dog, Chester, had dug under the fence until I saw the hole. My heart sank. I grabbed his leash and ran out the front door, calling his name. Every minute he was gone felt like an hour.

Say: "First person or third person?"

Students: First person.

Say: "What do we know — and what do we NOT know?"

Guide toward: We know the narrator is panicked and searching for Chester. We do NOT know where Chester is, what Chester is doing, or whether Chester is safe. We are limited to the narrator's experience.

Say: "Great work. Now let me ask a deeper question. If Passage 3 were rewritten in third person, what new information could the author include?"

Guide toward: The author could show us where Chester is — maybe he is happily playing in a neighbor's yard. The reader could know Chester is safe even though the character does not.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students identify point of view in a longer passage and analyze its effect on the reader's understanding.

**Passage: "The Bike Race" (First Person)**

> I gripped the handlebars and stared at the starting line. There were at least twenty kids lined up beside me, their bikes gleaming in the Saturday morning sun. My bike was old. The paint was chipped, and one of the reflectors was missing. I tried not to think about it.
>
> The whistle blew. I pushed off hard, my legs pumping as fast as they could. Wind roared in my ears. I passed one rider, then another. My lungs burned, but I kept going.
>
> Around the bend, I could see the finish line — a red ribbon stretched between two cones. There was one rider ahead of me, a tall kid in a blue jersey. I pushed harder. My legs screamed. The ribbon got closer.
>
> I crossed the line and looked up at the judges. Did I win? I could not tell. I bent over, hands on my knees, gasping for air. My dad ran over and wrapped me in a hug.
>
> "Second place!" he shouted. "You got second place!"
>
> I wanted to be disappointed. But my dad's face was so full of pride that I just smiled. Second place on a bike with chipped paint. That was not bad at all.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences.

1. What point of view is this passage written in? What pronoun clues told you?
2. Whose thoughts and feelings does the reader know? Whose thoughts and feelings does the reader NOT know?
3. The narrator says, "There were at least twenty kids lined up beside me." Does the reader know how any of those other kids felt at the starting line? Why or why not?
4. If this story were rewritten in third person, what new information could the author include? Give at least two specific examples.
5. Why do you think the author chose first-person point of view for this story? How does it make the story feel different than if it were in third person?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-5 as written.
**Stretch:** After completing questions 1-5, rewrite the last paragraph of the passage in third person. Change the pronouns and add one new detail that the third-person narrator could reveal (e.g., what the tall kid in the blue jersey was thinking or doing).
**Challenge:** After completing all questions, rewrite the entire first paragraph of the passage in third person. Then write two to three sentences comparing how the two versions feel different to you as a reader.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Lesson 3-4 review) Identify one character trait for the narrator of "The Bike Race." What evidence from the text supports it? What challenge did the narrator face, and how did they respond?
7. (Lesson 5 review) Describe the setting of "The Bike Race" using the three dimensions (Where, When, Atmosphere). How does the setting influence the narrator?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following two mini-passages:

**Passage A:** "She opened the gift and gasped. A tiny gold locket lay inside the box. She looked at her grandmother with tears in her eyes."

**Passage B:** "I opened the gift and gasped. A tiny gold locket lay inside the box. I looked at my grandmother with tears in my eyes."

1. Which passage is first person? Which is third person?
2. What pronoun clues helped you decide?
3. In Passage A (third person), could the narrator also tell us what the grandmother was thinking? In Passage B (first person), could the narrator tell us what the grandmother was thinking? Explain.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Passage B is first person. Passage A is third person.
2. Passage B uses "I" and "my" — the narrator is the character. Passage A uses "She" and "her" — the narrator is outside the story.
3. In Passage A (third person), yes — the narrator could also tell us what the grandmother was thinking because the third-person narrator can access multiple characters' thoughts. In Passage B (first person), no — the narrator can only tell us their own thoughts and feelings. They would have to guess how the grandmother felt based on what they observed.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies both POVs, provides pronoun evidence, and explains that first person limits knowledge to one character while third person can include multiple characters.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Create a two-column sort with index cards. On each card, write a sentence (either first person or third person). Have the student sort them into two columns and explain each choice.
- Emphasize the pronoun check: "Look at the sentences that are NOT dialogue. Do they say 'I' or 'he/she'?"
- Practice with two more short passages before Lesson 7.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7, which compares how the same events look different from different characters' perspectives.
- Encourage the student to identify the point of view in their independent reading and think about how it affects what they know.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **POV Flip:** The student takes "The Bike Race" and rewrites the first two paragraphs from the perspective of the tall kid in the blue jersey (first person) OR from a third-person narrator who can see both racers.
2. **POV Scavenger Hunt:** The student looks through their classroom library or book collection and sorts 5-6 books into "First Person" and "Third Person" based on reading the first page.
3. **POV Interview:** The student writes a short first-person diary entry from the perspective of a character from an earlier lesson (e.g., Nadia from Lesson 2, Diego from Lesson 3, Keiko from Lesson 4).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Dialogue trap:** The biggest mistake students make is identifying a story as first person because a character says "I" in their dialogue. Drill the rule: "Check the narration, NOT the dialogue." Have students physically underline narration and circle dialogue to practice the difference.
- **Second person:** A student may ask about "you." Briefly explain that second person exists ("You walk into the room...") but it is rare in literature and not part of this lesson. If asked, say: "Good question — that is called second person. It is not common in stories, but you might see it in instructions or choose-your-own-adventure books."
- **Third-person limited vs. omniscient:** Fourth graders do not need to distinguish between these sub-types. If it comes up, briefly say: "Sometimes a third-person narrator knows everyone's thoughts, and sometimes they only share one character's. Both are third person."
- **Anchor chart:** Create a "Point of View" anchor chart with the two columns from Direct Instruction. Keep it visible for Lesson 7.
- **Building toward Lesson 7:** This lesson identifies the two POVs. Lesson 7 builds on this by having students compare how the same event looks different when told from different characters' perspectives.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The passage is written in first-person point of view. Pronoun clues: "I gripped," "my bike," "I pushed off," "my legs," "I crossed," "my dad." The narrator is a character in the story telling their own experience.

2. The reader knows the narrator's thoughts and feelings — their nervousness about the old bike, the burning in their lungs, the uncertainty about whether they won, and the decision to feel proud of second place. The reader does NOT know the thoughts or feelings of the other riders, the kid in the blue jersey, the judges, or even the dad (beyond what the narrator observes about his face).

3. No, the reader does not know how any of the other twenty kids felt. Because the story is in first person, we only know the narrator's experience. The narrator says there were "at least twenty kids," but we cannot know if they were nervous, excited, or confident because the first-person narrator cannot enter their minds.

4. If rewritten in third person, the author could include: (1) What the tall kid in the blue jersey was thinking during the race — maybe he was also pushing as hard as he could and heard the narrator catching up. (2) What the dad was feeling as he watched from the sideline — maybe he was pacing nervously the entire time. (3) How the judges reacted as the two riders crossed the finish line — maybe they had to look at a photo finish.

5. The author likely chose first person to make the reader feel like they ARE the racer — experiencing the wind, the burning lungs, and the uncertainty of the outcome in real time. In third person, the reader would watch the race from the outside. In first person, the reader feels like they are riding the bike. It creates more intensity and makes the emotional payoff at the end (feeling proud of second place) more personal.

### Interleaved Review
6. **Trait:** Determined / resilient. **Evidence:** The narrator pushed hard even though their bike was old and their lungs burned. They kept racing even when there was a rider ahead. When they got second place instead of first, they chose to feel proud instead of disappointed. **Challenge:** Racing against twenty kids with a chipped, old bike. **Response:** They gave everything they had and accepted the outcome with pride.

7. **Setting:** Where: An outdoor bike race course with a starting line, a bend, and a finish line with a red ribbon. When: Saturday morning, in the sunshine. Atmosphere: Competitive, exciting, energetic — gleaming bikes, wind roaring, the narrator pushing hard. The setting creates a high-energy atmosphere that fuels the narrator's determination and makes the race feel intense and exciting.
