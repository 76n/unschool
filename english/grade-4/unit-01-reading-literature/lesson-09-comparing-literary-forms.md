# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Comparing Literary Forms

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read the same story told as prose, a poem, and a drama scene and compare the three versions
- Explain how the structure of each literary form affects the meaning and the reader's experience
- Analyze what each form does best — what is gained and what is lost when a story changes forms

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify poems, drama, and prose and name their structural elements (Lesson 8)
- Understanding of how structure (stanzas, stage directions, paragraphs) shapes a text (Lesson 8)
- Ability to describe setting, character, and theme using text evidence (Lessons 1-5)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the structural elements of the three literary forms and build curiosity about how form shapes meaning.

**Activity: Quick Recall**

Say: "Before we start today, let us do a quick review. I am going to name a structural element, and you tell me which form it belongs to — prose, poetry, or drama. Ready?"

1. "Stanzas" — (Poetry)
2. "Stage directions" — (Drama)
3. "Paragraphs and chapters" — (Prose)
4. "Line breaks" — (Poetry)
5. "Character names written before their lines, no quotation marks" — (Drama)
6. "Dialogue inside quotation marks mixed with description" — (Prose)

Say: "Excellent. You know the building blocks. Today we are going to do something really interesting. We are going to read the SAME story in all three forms and ask: How does the form change the experience? What does each form do better than the others? Let us find out."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Read a story in three forms and model a detailed comparison of how form affects meaning and experience.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Form:** The type or shape of a text — prose, poetry, or drama. Form determines how the text looks on the page and how the reader experiences it.
- **Pacing:** How fast or slow a story feels. Different forms create different pacing.
- **Emphasis:** What the text draws your attention to. Each form emphasizes different things.
- **Adaptation:** When a story originally written in one form is rewritten in another form.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Today we are going to read one story told three ways. The story is about a girl named Opal who finds a bird with a broken wing. Same characters, same events, same ending — but the form changes each time. Pay attention to how each version makes you feel."

### Worked Examples

**Prose Version: "The Broken Wing"**

> Opal was walking through the tall grass behind her grandparents' farmhouse when she heard a faint chirping sound. She followed the sound to a spot near the fence post where the grass was flattened in a small circle. Sitting in the center was a robin. Its right wing stuck out at an odd angle, and it tilted to one side when it tried to hop.
>
> "Oh no," Opal whispered. She knelt down slowly, careful not to scare the bird. The robin looked up at her with one dark eye. It chirped again, softer this time, as if it knew she was trying to help.
>
> Opal gently cupped the bird in her hands. She could feel its heart beating fast against her palms, quick as a tiny drum. She carried it inside and set it in a shoebox lined with an old towel.
>
> Every morning for the next two weeks, Opal brought the robin water and small pieces of fruit. She talked to it in a quiet voice. Slowly, the wing healed. The bird started to flutter, then hop, then stretch its wings wide.
>
> On a Saturday morning, Opal carried the shoebox outside and set it on the porch railing. The robin sat still for a moment, turning its head to look at her. Then it spread its wings and flew — up past the fence post, past the roof, into the wide blue sky.
>
> Opal watched until the robin was just a dark speck. She did not feel sad. She felt proud.

Say: "This is the prose version. Notice how the narrator tells us everything — what Opal sees, hears, feels, and thinks. We get a full picture with lots of detail and the story unfolds over two weeks."

**Poetry Version: "The Broken Wing"**

> A chirp in the tall grass,
> A circle pressed flat by pain.
> A robin tilts, one wing bent wrong,
> And waits.
>
> I kneel. The bird watches me
> With one dark, trusting eye.
> Its heart beats quick against my palms —
> A tiny drum, a tiny life.
>
> Two weeks of water,
> Soft words,
> Patience.
> The wing remembers how to open.
>
> Saturday morning.
> I lift the lid.
> The robin sits. Looks at me.
> Flies.
>
> Up past the fence,
> Past the roof,
> Into the wide blue nothing.
>
> I am not sad.
> I am full.

Say: "This is the poetry version. What is different? It is much shorter. The details are compressed — 'Two weeks of water, / Soft words, / Patience' captures the entire healing period in three words. The poem focuses on feelings and images. And that last line — 'I am full' — hits harder in a poem because there is nothing around it. The white space on the page gives it weight."

**Drama Version: "The Broken Wing"**

> SCENE 1: The backyard of a farmhouse. Tall grass and a wooden fence. OPAL walks through the grass, stops, and listens.
>
> OPAL: (tilting her head) Did you hear that? (She follows the sound to the fence post and kneels.) Oh, a robin! Its wing — it is broken.
>
> (The ROBIN chirps softly.)
>
> OPAL: (gently picking up the bird) It is okay, little one. I have got you. (She cradles it in her hands.) Your heart is beating so fast.
>
> SCENE 2: The farmhouse porch, two weeks later. A shoebox sits on the railing. OPAL opens the lid. The ROBIN hops to the edge of the box and looks around.
>
> OPAL: (whispering) You are ready, are you not?
>
> (The ROBIN spreads its wings and flies upward. OPAL watches, shading her eyes against the sun.)
>
> OPAL: (smiling, wiping her eyes) Fly high, little one.

Say: "This is the drama version. What is different here? We jump from Scene 1 to Scene 2 — two weeks pass between scenes with no description of the healing process. We do not hear Opal's thoughts unless she says them aloud. The drama version is built for action and dialogue. It is meant to be watched, not read quietly."

Now draw a comparison chart on the board:

| Feature | Prose | Poetry | Drama |
|---------|-------|--------|-------|
| How it tells the story | Narrator describes everything | Images and compressed language | Dialogue and stage directions |
| Pacing | Steady — covers two weeks in detail | Compressed — jumps through time quickly | Skips ahead — scenes jump in time |
| What we know about Opal | Her thoughts, feelings, actions (full picture) | Her feelings, expressed through images | Only what she says and does |
| Strongest moment | The two weeks of care (detail) | "I am full" (emotional impact) | Opal saying goodbye (performance) |
| Best for... | Reading alone, deep immersion | Feeling emotion, using imagination | Watching on stage, hearing dialogue |

Say: "No form is better than the others. Each one does something the others cannot. Prose gives the most detail. Poetry gives the most emotional punch. Drama gives the most action and is designed to be performed."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students compare a new set of three versions with teacher guidance.

Say: "Now we are going to read another story in three forms. This time, you are going to help me build the comparison chart."

**Prose Version: "The Last Leaf"**

> Autumn had stripped the maple tree of almost every leaf. Only one remained — a small, bright orange leaf clinging to the highest branch. Every afternoon, Mateo sat on the porch steps and watched it. The wind would shake the branches, and the leaf would tremble, but it held on.
>
> "Why does that leaf not fall?" Mateo asked his grandmother.
>
> She sat down beside him. "Maybe it is not ready yet."
>
> Mateo thought about that. He was not ready for a lot of things either. He was not ready for his family to move to a new city next month. He was not ready to leave his friends, his school, or this porch.
>
> One morning, Mateo looked up and the leaf was gone. The branch was bare. But on the ground below the tree, the orange leaf lay in the grass, still bright, still whole.
>
> Mateo picked it up and pressed it between the pages of his favorite book. Change was coming. But he could carry a piece of this place with him.

**Poetry Version: "The Last Leaf"**

> One leaf — bright orange —
> Clings to the highest branch
> While the rest have let go.
>
> The wind shakes. The leaf holds.
> I hold too,
> Not ready to fall,
> Not ready to leave.
>
> Then one morning,
> The branch is bare.
> The leaf rests in the grass,
> Still bright, still whole.
>
> I press it between pages,
> A piece of here
> I will carry with me
> Into whatever comes next.

**Drama Version: "The Last Leaf"**

> SCENE 1: A front porch. A maple tree with one remaining leaf visible upstage. MATEO sits on the steps, looking up at the tree. GRANDMOTHER comes outside.
>
> MATEO: Grandma, why does that leaf not fall?
>
> GRANDMOTHER: (sitting beside him) Maybe it is not ready yet.
>
> MATEO: (quietly) I know how it feels.
>
> GRANDMOTHER: (putting her arm around him) I know you do.
>
> SCENE 2: The same porch, a few weeks later. The tree is bare. MATEO stands under it, looking at the ground. He bends down and picks up the orange leaf.
>
> MATEO: (turning the leaf over in his hands) Still bright. Still whole. (He opens a book and presses the leaf inside.) Okay. I am ready now.

Say: "With your partner, discuss these three questions. Then we will share."

1. What does the prose version tell you about Mateo that the other versions do not?
2. What makes the poem version feel different from the prose?
3. What does the drama version emphasize that the other two do not?

Allow 3 minutes for discussion, then guide:

1. **Prose:** We learn Mateo's inner thoughts in detail — he is not ready for his family to move, not ready to leave friends, school, or the porch. The prose version gives us the full explanation.
2. **Poetry:** The poem is more personal — it uses "I" and compresses Mateo's complex feelings into short, powerful lines. "Not ready to fall, / Not ready to leave" makes a connection between the leaf and Mateo that hits emotionally because of the line break.
3. **Drama:** The drama version emphasizes the relationship between Mateo and his grandmother through their dialogue. The line "I know how it feels" and Grandmother's "I know you do" create a touching moment that would be powerful on stage. The drama also ends with "Okay. I am ready now," which is a line the actor would deliver directly to the audience.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently compare three versions of a story and analyze how form affects meaning.

**Read all three versions of the same story, then answer the questions.**

**Prose Version: "The Bridge"**

> The old wooden bridge over Miller Creek was the only way to get to school from Rani's side of town. Every morning, she walked across it, listening to the boards creak under her feet. The bridge swayed a little when the wind blew, and Rani always held the railing tight.
>
> One morning after a heavy rainstorm, Rani found the bridge damaged. Two boards in the middle were missing, leaving a gap too wide to step across. The creek below was fast and muddy. Rani stared at the gap. She could walk the long way around — an extra thirty minutes — or she could try to jump across.
>
> She thought about the spelling test first period. She had studied hard. She could not miss it. Rani backed up three steps, ran forward, and leaped. Her feet hit the other side of the gap and she stumbled but caught the railing. Her heart hammered.
>
> She stood up, breathed, and kept walking. She made it to school with five minutes to spare.

**Poetry Version: "The Bridge"**

> After the storm,
> Two boards gone —
> A gap in the middle
> Of the only way across.
>
> Muddy water rushing underneath.
> My hands grip the railing.
> I could turn around,
> Walk the long way.
>
> But the spelling test waits.
> I studied every word.
> I back up. Three steps.
> Run. Leap.
>
> My feet find the other side.
> My heart hammers.
> I stand. I breathe.
> I keep walking.

**Drama Version: "The Bridge"**

> SCENE 1: An old wooden bridge over a creek. Two boards are missing in the middle. RANI stands at the edge of the gap, looking down at the rushing water.
>
> RANI: (looking at the gap) Two boards. Gone. (She looks at the water below, then back at the gap.) I could walk around. It would take thirty minutes. (She checks her watch.) The spelling test is first period.
>
> (She grips the railing and stares at the gap.)
>
> RANI: (taking a deep breath) I studied too hard to miss this. (She backs up three steps, runs, and leaps across the gap. She stumbles but catches the railing.)
>
> RANI: (breathing hard, looking back at the gap) I made it. (She stands tall and walks forward.)

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences.

1. What is the same across all three versions? List at least three facts that appear in every version.
2. The prose version describes the bridge in detail ("boards creak," "bridge swayed," "held the railing tight"). Why is prose good at this kind of description? Why is it harder for drama to include these details?
3. In the poem version, the line "Run. Leap." is only two words. How does this short line affect the reader's experience? What does it feel like to read it?
4. In the drama version, Rani speaks her thoughts aloud ("I studied too hard to miss this"). In real life, would she say this out loud? Why does drama have characters say things they might only think?
5. Which version do you think is the most exciting to experience? Explain your answer by referring to specific structural elements of that form.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-5 as written.
**Stretch:** After completing questions 1-5, imagine a fourth-grade student who is very nervous about taking a test. Which version of "The Bridge" would you recommend they read, and why? How would the form affect how the story helps them feel braver?
**Challenge:** After completing all questions, write a new version of "The Bridge" in one of the three forms — but change the setting to a different obstacle (e.g., a steep hill, a dark tunnel, a crowded hallway). Keep the same theme and character, but show how the new setting changes the events.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Lesson 1-2 review) What is the theme of "The Bridge"? Write it as a complete sentence. List two key events that build toward the theme.
7. (Lesson 4 review) What is Rani's challenge, and how does she respond? What does her response reveal about her character? What is her motivation?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Think about the three versions of "The Bridge" you just read. Answer:

1. Name one thing prose can do that drama cannot.
2. Name one thing poetry does better than prose.
3. If you were going to perform "The Bridge" on stage, which version would you choose? Why?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Prose can describe a character's inner thoughts without the character saying them aloud. Prose can also include detailed descriptions of settings and actions that drama has to show through stage directions or dialogue. (Accept any valid answer about prose's advantages.)
2. Poetry creates stronger emotional impact in fewer words. The short lines and line breaks create emphasis and pacing that prose cannot match. (Accept any valid answer about poetry's advantages — imagery, compression, rhythm, white space.)
3. The drama version, because it has stage directions and dialogue that an actor can follow. The character names are labeled, and the actions are described in parentheses. Prose and poetry are not designed for stage performance.

**Mastery = Student identifies at least one unique strength of each form and correctly explains why drama is best for performance.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the comparison chart from Direct Instruction and review what each form does best.
- Have the student re-read one pair of passages from today (e.g., the prose and drama versions of "The Bridge") and circle specific differences.
- Use a sentence frame: "Prose is good at ___ because ___. Poetry is good at ___ because ___. Drama is good at ___ because ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10, the culminating celebration lesson.
- Encourage the student to think about form choices when reading — "Why did the author write this as a poem instead of a story?"

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Adaptation Project:** The student chooses a short story from their independent reading and adapts one scene as either a poem or a drama. They write a brief reflection explaining what changed and why.
2. **Form Debate:** Two students take opposing sides on the question "Which form tells a story best?" One argues for prose; the other argues for poetry or drama. Each must use specific structural reasons.
3. **Triple Retelling:** The student writes the same short event (e.g., a character finding a hidden door) in all three forms: a prose paragraph, a four-line poem, and a brief drama scene. They label the structural elements in each.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Equal respect for all forms:** Students often rank prose as "normal" and poetry/drama as "weird." Actively counter this by showing that each form has unique strengths. Ask: "Would this poem be better as a story? What would it lose?" Help students see that poets and playwrights make deliberate choices.
- **Reading aloud matters:** This lesson benefits enormously from reading the passages aloud. Prose should be read in a steady narrative voice. Poetry should be read slowly with pauses at line breaks. Drama should be read with different voices for each character. If possible, have students read the drama scenes with partners.
- **The comparison chart is the anchor strategy:** Students should be able to use the comparison chart (from Direct Instruction) as a reference throughout Independent Practice. Leave it visible on the board.
- **Building toward Lesson 10:** In the celebration lesson, students will analyze a self-selected text using ALL the skills from this unit. Remind students that one of those skills is identifying the literary form and explaining how structure affects meaning.
- **Pacing note:** This lesson has a lot of reading. If time is tight, you can skip the Guided Practice and spend more time on Independent Practice, or have students read the three versions of "The Bridge" in pairs rather than independently.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Same across all three versions:** (Any three of the following) Rani walks to school across an old wooden bridge; after a rainstorm, two boards are missing from the bridge; the creek below is muddy and rushing; Rani has a spelling test she does not want to miss; she decides to jump across the gap; she makes the jump successfully and catches the railing; she makes it to school in time.

2. Prose is good at description because the narrator can paint a full picture using sentences and paragraphs — the creaking boards, the swaying bridge, the way Rani holds the railing. In drama, you cannot have a narrator describing these details. The audience has to SEE the bridge on stage, and the actor has to SHOW the nervousness through their actions. Stage directions can give some guidance, but they are not as detailed as prose narration.

3. The line "Run. Leap." is only two words, each its own sentence. This makes the reader experience the speed and urgency of the moment. It feels fast and breathless — you read it quickly, just like Rani moved quickly. The short line and the period after each word create a punching rhythm that matches the action. In prose, the sentence "She ran forward and leaped" has the same information but does not feel as immediate.

4. In real life, Rani probably would not say "I studied too hard to miss this" out loud. Drama has characters speak their thoughts because there is no narrator to reveal what they are thinking. The audience needs to hear the character's reasoning to understand why they make their choices. This is a convention of drama — characters sometimes speak thoughts aloud so the audience can follow the story.

5. Answers will vary. Accept any answer supported by structural reasoning. Example: "The poem version is the most exciting because the short lines and line breaks create a fast pace that makes the reader feel the adrenaline. The line 'Run. Leap.' is two words that feel like your heart is pounding." OR "The drama version is most exciting because you can picture an actor actually running and jumping across the gap on stage, and the stage directions make it feel real."

### Interleaved Review
6. **Theme:** When you have prepared and worked hard, you can find the courage to face obstacles instead of taking the easy way out. (Accept reasonable variations about courage, preparation, and determination.) **Key events:** (1) Rani found the bridge damaged with missing boards. (2) She chose to jump across the gap instead of walking the long way because she had studied hard and did not want to miss the spelling test.

7. **Challenge:** External — two boards are missing from the bridge, making it dangerous to cross. Internal — Rani is scared (the creek is fast and muddy) and has to decide whether to jump or take the long way. **Response:** She decides to jump because she prepared for the spelling test and does not want to miss it. She backs up, runs, and leaps across. **What it reveals:** Rani is determined and brave — she faces her fear because she values her hard work. **Motivation:** She studied hard for the spelling test and did not want that effort to go to waste.
