# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 8

## Topic
Poems, Drama, and Prose

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the structural elements of poems (stanzas, line breaks, rhythm), drama (dialogue, stage directions, scenes), and prose (chapters, paragraphs, sentences)
- Classify a text as a poem, a drama, or prose based on its structure
- Explain why structure matters — how the form of a text affects the reading experience

## Prerequisites
- Ability to read and comprehend grade-level fiction (RL.3.1)
- Familiarity with stories told in paragraph form (prose)
- Understanding of dialogue and how characters speak in stories (Lessons 3-4)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Index cards (6 per student for sorting activity)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate awareness that texts can look very different on the page and introduce the idea that structure tells us what type of text we are reading.

**Activity: What Does It Look Like?**

Say: "I am going to show you three pieces of text on the board. I am not going to tell you anything about them. Just look at HOW they are arranged on the page. Tell me what you notice."

Write on the board:

**Text 1:**
> The wind blew through the trees. Maya pulled her jacket tighter and kept walking. The path ahead was dark, but she could see a faint light in the distance.

**Text 2:**
> The wind blows cold tonight,
> Rattling the bare branches.
> I pull my jacket close
> And follow the distant light —
> A small, stubborn star.

**Text 3:**
> SCENE 1: A dark forest path. MAYA walks slowly, hugging her jacket.
>
> MAYA: (shivering) It is so cold out here.
>
> VOICE (offstage): Keep going! You are almost there!
>
> MAYA: (squinting ahead) I see a light. Just a little farther.

Say: "Turn to your partner. What looks different about these three texts? Do not worry about what they say yet — just look at how they look on the page."

Allow 60 seconds, then collect observations. Guide toward: Text 1 is regular paragraphs. Text 2 has short lines and no regular sentences. Text 3 has character names in capitals and directions in parentheses.

Say: "You just identified three different literary forms — prose, poetry, and drama. Today we are going to learn the names for the pieces that make each one unique."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the structural elements of each literary form and provide clear examples.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Prose:** Writing in regular sentences and paragraphs. Stories, novels, and most fiction and nonfiction are written in prose.
- **Poetry/Poem:** Writing arranged in lines and stanzas, often with rhythm, imagery, and compressed language. Poems may or may not rhyme.
- **Drama/Play:** Writing meant to be performed by actors. Drama uses dialogue (lines spoken by characters), stage directions (instructions for actors), and is organized into scenes or acts.
- **Stanza:** A group of lines in a poem, separated by a blank line — like a paragraph in prose.
- **Line break:** The place where a line of poetry ends and a new line begins. Poets choose where to break lines on purpose.
- **Stage directions:** Instructions in a play that tell actors how to move, speak, or behave. Stage directions are usually in parentheses or italics.
- **Scene:** A section of a play that takes place in one time and place. When the setting or time changes, a new scene begins.
- **Dialogue:** The words characters speak. In prose, dialogue is inside quotation marks. In drama, dialogue follows the character's name with no quotation marks.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "There are three main literary forms: prose, poetry, and drama. Each one has its own structure — its own set of building blocks. Let me show you the building blocks for each."

Write on the board:

**PROSE (Stories and Novels)**
- Organized into chapters, paragraphs, and sentences
- Dialogue is in quotation marks
- Description, action, and dialogue are mixed together
- The narrator tells the story

**POETRY (Poems)**
- Organized into stanzas (groups of lines)
- Uses line breaks — each line starts on a new line
- Often uses rhythm, imagery, and compressed language
- May or may not rhyme
- No narrator "telling" the story — the poem shows or speaks directly

**DRAMA (Plays)**
- Organized into scenes (and sometimes acts)
- Character names are written before their lines
- Dialogue does NOT use quotation marks
- Stage directions in parentheses tell actors what to do
- No narrator — the story is told entirely through dialogue and action

Say: "Think of it this way. In prose, the author TELLS you the story. In poetry, the author PAINTS the story with words. In drama, the author SHOWS you the story through characters talking and acting."

### Worked Examples

Say: "Let me show you each form in action. All three passages tell the same story — a girl named Zara finding a lost kitten. Watch how the structure changes."

**Prose Version:**

> Zara heard a tiny sound coming from under the porch. She knelt down and peered into the dark space beneath the wooden boards. Two green eyes blinked back at her. It was a kitten — small, gray, and shivering.
>
> "Hey there, little one," Zara whispered. She stretched her hand out slowly. The kitten sniffed her fingers, then rubbed its head against her palm.
>
> Zara smiled. "I think you need a home," she said softly.

Say: "This is prose. I see paragraphs, sentences, and dialogue in quotation marks. The narrator describes what Zara does and tells us what the kitten looks like. This is the form you are most used to reading."

**Poetry Version:**

> Under the porch,
> Two green eyes blink
> In the dusty dark.
>
> A kitten — small, gray,
> Shivering like a leaf
> In an autumn wind.
>
> I stretch out my hand.
> A cold nose.
> A tiny, rough tongue.
>
> And just like that,
> I am no longer alone.

Say: "This is a poem. I see lines and stanzas — four stanzas separated by blank lines. Notice the line breaks. The poet chose where each line ends. There are no quotation marks and no narrator describing the scene in sentences. The poem uses images — 'shivering like a leaf' — to paint a picture. It is shorter and more compressed than the prose version."

**Drama Version:**

> SCENE 1: The front porch of a house. ZARA kneels on the ground, peering under the porch.
>
> ZARA: (whispering) Do you hear that? Something is under there.
>
> (A tiny meow comes from under the porch.)
>
> ZARA: (lying flat on the ground, reaching under the porch) Come here, little one. It is okay.
>
> (A small gray KITTEN crawls out, shivering. ZARA gently picks it up.)
>
> ZARA: (smiling, holding the kitten close) I think you need a home. And I think I just found a new friend.

Say: "This is drama. I see a scene heading that tells us where we are. Character names are in capitals before their lines. Stage directions are in parentheses — they tell the actor what to do. There are no quotation marks. The whole story is told through what the character says and does."

Say: "Same story. Same kitten. Same girl. But the reading experience is different because of the structure."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying the structural elements of each form.

**Activity: Structure Detectives**

Say: "I am going to show you three new texts. For each one, I want you to tell me: (1) Is it prose, poetry, or drama? (2) What structural clues told you?"

**Text A:**

> The old oak stood alone in the meadow,
> Its branches wide as open arms,
> Catching every bird, every breeze,
> Every secret whispered by the wind.
>
> If I could be a tree,
> I would stand just like that —
> Patient, steady, listening
> To everything the world has to say.

Say: "What is this? Talk to your partner."

Guide toward: This is a **poem**. Clues: It is organized into stanzas (two stanzas), uses line breaks, has imagery ("branches wide as open arms"), and uses compressed language rather than full sentences and paragraphs.

**Text B:**

> ACT 1, SCENE 2: A classroom. TEACHER stands at the front. ANNA sits at her desk, looking worried.
>
> TEACHER: Today, we are going to share our stories aloud. Who wants to go first?
>
> (Silence. Nobody raises a hand.)
>
> ANNA: (to herself, quietly) Please do not pick me. Please do not pick me.
>
> TEACHER: (smiling) Anna, how about you?
>
> ANNA: (standing slowly, clutching her paper) Okay. (takes a deep breath) My story is called "The Day I Found Courage."

Say: "What is this? What are the structural clues?"

Guide toward: This is **drama**. Clues: It has a scene heading (ACT 1, SCENE 2 with a setting description), character names in capitals before their lines, stage directions in parentheses, and no quotation marks around dialogue.

**Text C:**

> Anna stared at the blank page in front of her. The classroom was quiet except for the scratching of pencils. Everyone else seemed to know exactly what to write. Anna picked up her pen, put it down, and picked it up again.
>
> "Just start with one sentence," she told herself. She wrote: "The day I found courage was the day I almost gave up." She stared at the words. Actually, that was not bad. She kept writing.

Say: "And this one?"

Guide toward: This is **prose**. Clues: It is organized into paragraphs and sentences, dialogue is in quotation marks, there is a narrator describing Anna's actions and thoughts, and there are no stanzas, line breaks, stage directions, or scene headings.

Say: "Nice work. Now let us talk about how these forms feel different to a reader."

Ask: "If you wanted to know exactly what Anna was thinking and feeling, which form gives you the most detail?"

Guide toward: Prose — because the narrator can describe Anna's inner thoughts.

Ask: "If you wanted to feel the emotion in the fewest possible words, which form does that best?"

Guide toward: Poetry — because poems use compressed language and imagery to create strong feelings quickly.

Ask: "If you wanted to watch this story come alive on a stage, which form is designed for that?"

Guide toward: Drama — because it has dialogue and stage directions that actors can follow.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify literary forms and their structural elements.

**Read the following three texts and answer the questions below.**

**Text 1:**

> The attic was dusty and dark. Jasmine pushed open the old trunk and found a stack of letters tied with a blue ribbon. The handwriting on the envelopes was faded but careful. She picked up the first letter and began to read. "Dear Eleanor," it started. "I am writing to tell you that the garden is in full bloom."
>
> Jasmine smiled. She did not know who Eleanor was, but she wanted to find out.

**Text 2:**

> Dust and darkness,
> An old trunk opens with a groan.
> Letters tied in blue,
> Faded ink on yellowed paper.
>
> Who was Eleanor?
> What garden bloomed for her?
> The questions float like dust
> In a beam of attic light.

**Text 3:**

> SCENE 1: An attic. Dusty boxes and old furniture. JASMINE opens a trunk and pulls out a stack of letters.
>
> JASMINE: (reading the envelope) "Dear Eleanor." (looks up) Who is Eleanor?
>
> (She unties the blue ribbon and opens the first letter.)
>
> JASMINE: (reading aloud) "I am writing to tell you that the garden is in full bloom." (smiling) I have to find out who wrote these.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences.

1. Label each text as prose, poetry, or drama. For each, list two structural clues that helped you decide.
2. All three texts tell a similar story. What is the same across all three versions?
3. What information does the prose version include that the drama version does not? Why?
4. The poem asks, "Who was Eleanor? What garden bloomed for her?" How does asking questions in a poem create a different feeling than the prose version, which says Jasmine "wanted to find out"?
5. If you were an actor, which version would you use to perform this story? Why?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-5 as written.
**Stretch:** After completing questions 1-5, choose one of the three versions and rewrite it in a DIFFERENT form. For example, turn the poem into prose, or turn the prose into a drama scene.
**Challenge:** After completing all questions, write your own original short text in ALL THREE forms — prose, poetry, and drama — about the same event (e.g., finding a treasure, seeing a rainbow, meeting a friend). Each version should be at least 4 lines/sentences.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Lesson 6 review) What point of view is Text 1 (the prose version) written in? How do you know?
7. (Lesson 5 review) Describe the setting of the attic using the three dimensions (Where, When, Atmosphere). Use details from any of the three versions.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following text:

> Morning light,
> A dew-covered web
> Stretched between fence posts,
> Sparkling like a tiny constellation.
>
> The spider waits,
> Still as a secret,
> In the center of her universe.

1. Is this text prose, poetry, or drama?
2. Name two structural clues that helped you decide.
3. What is one element this text does NOT have that a drama would have?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Poetry.
2. Any two of: It has stanzas (two stanzas separated by a blank line), line breaks (each line starts on a new line), imagery ("sparkling like a tiny constellation," "still as a secret"), compressed language (not full sentences in paragraph form).
3. A drama would have character names before dialogue, stage directions in parentheses, and scene headings. This poem has none of those.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the form as poetry, provides two valid structural clues, and names one structural element of drama that is absent.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Create a simple reference card with three columns (Prose, Poetry, Drama) listing the key structural elements of each. Have the student use the card while re-examining today's passages.
- Practice with the sorting activity: Write six text excerpts on index cards and have the student sort them into three categories.
- Review before Lesson 9.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9, which compares how the same story feels different in each literary form.
- Encourage the student to find examples of poems and plays in their classroom library.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Form Museum:** The student creates a three-panel poster with one example of each literary form, labeling all structural elements (stanzas, stage directions, paragraphs, etc.).
2. **Drama Performance:** The student takes one of the drama excerpts from today's lesson and performs it aloud (or with a partner), following the stage directions.
3. **Poetry Hunt:** The student finds three poems in books, magazines, or online and identifies the structural elements in each (stanzas, line breaks, rhyme or no rhyme, imagery).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Poems do not have to rhyme:** This is one of the most persistent misconceptions among fourth graders. All of today's poem examples use free verse intentionally. If a student says "That is not a poem because it does not rhyme," address it directly: "Many poems do not rhyme. What makes it a poem is the use of lines, stanzas, and compressed, image-rich language."
- **Drama formatting:** Students may not have seen play scripts before. Take extra time pointing out that the character name before each line is part of the structure, not part of what the character says. Also clarify that stage directions are instructions, not dialogue.
- **Visual layout matters:** For this lesson, how the text looks on the page is as important as what it says. If projecting or writing on the board, preserve the formatting carefully — especially line breaks in poetry and indented stage directions in drama.
- **Connection to Lesson 9:** Today's lesson identifies the structural elements. Lesson 9 goes deeper by comparing how the same story feels and means different things in each form. Today is identification; tomorrow is analysis.
- **Anchor chart:** Add a "Literary Forms" anchor chart with three columns listing the structural elements of each form. Students will reference this in Lesson 9 and the unit assessment.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Text 1: Prose.** Clues: organized into paragraphs and sentences; dialogue is in quotation marks; a narrator describes Jasmine's actions and thoughts.
   **Text 2: Poetry.** Clues: organized into stanzas (two stanzas); uses line breaks; uses imagery ("questions float like dust in a beam of attic light"); compressed language rather than full sentences.
   **Text 3: Drama.** Clues: has a scene heading (SCENE 1 with setting description); character name (JASMINE) in capitals before dialogue; stage directions in parentheses; no quotation marks for spoken lines.

2. All three versions describe someone finding old letters in an attic trunk. The letters are tied with a blue ribbon, the handwriting is faded, the first letter is addressed to "Eleanor" and mentions a garden in bloom, and the person who finds the letters wants to know more about Eleanor.

3. The prose version includes Jasmine's internal thoughts — "She did not know who Eleanor was, but she wanted to find out." The drama version cannot show internal thoughts directly because drama is told through dialogue and stage directions only. The drama version compensates by having Jasmine speak her thoughts aloud: "Who is Eleanor?" and "I have to find out who wrote these."

4. The poem's questions ("Who was Eleanor? What garden bloomed for her?") feel open and wondering — they hang in the air without answers, which creates a mood of mystery and curiosity. The prose version tells us directly that Jasmine "wanted to find out," which is more matter-of-fact. The poem makes the reader feel the curiosity themselves, while the prose describes the curiosity.

5. The drama version (Text 3) would be best for performance because it is designed to be acted out. It includes stage directions that tell the actor what to do (opening the trunk, reading the letter, smiling), and the dialogue tells the actor exactly what to say. Prose and poetry are written to be read, not performed on stage.

### Interleaved Review
6. Text 1 is written in third-person point of view. Clues: the narrator uses "she" and "her" to describe Jasmine ("She pushed open the old trunk," "She picked up the first letter"). The narrator is outside the story.

7. **Setting of the attic:** Where: An attic, possibly in an old house, with boxes, old furniture, and a trunk. When: Not specified exactly, but the dusty atmosphere suggests it has not been visited in a long time. Atmosphere: Mysterious, quiet, a little forgotten — the dust, darkness, faded ink, and yellowed paper create a sense of age and hidden secrets waiting to be discovered.
