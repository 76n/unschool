# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 2

## Topic
How Events Reveal Theme

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Trace how key events in a story build toward the theme
- Explain how a character's actions and decisions at different points in a story reveal what the author wants the reader to learn
- Summarize a story in a way that includes the theme

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of theme as a full-sentence message or lesson (Lesson 1)
- Ability to distinguish topic from theme (Lesson 1)
- Ability to identify the beginning, middle, and end of a story

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review topic vs. theme from Lesson 1 and introduce the idea that theme does not appear all at once.

**Activity: Theme Throwback**

Say: "Yesterday we learned the difference between topic and theme. Quick check — I am going to say something, and you tell me if it is a topic or a theme. Ready?"

Read aloud:
1. "Kindness" — (Students should say: topic)
2. "Small acts of kindness can make a big difference in someone's day." — (Students should say: theme)
3. "Courage" — (Students should say: topic)
4. "You do not have to be fearless to be brave — you just have to try." — (Students should say: theme)

Say: "Great. You remember the difference. Now here is today's big question: How does a story SHOW you its theme? Does the author just write 'The theme is...' at the end?"

Allow students to respond. Guide them toward: No, the theme is shown through what happens — through the events and what the characters do and learn.

Say: "Exactly. Today we are going to trace how the events in a story build the theme, step by step, like climbing a staircase. Each event is a step, and the theme is what you see when you reach the top."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to trace theme development through a sequence of events using an event-to-theme chart.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Key events:** The most important things that happen in the story — the moments where something changes or the character makes a decision.
- **Theme development:** The way the author builds the theme gradually through events, character actions, and character changes.
- **Summarize:** Retell the most important parts of a story in your own words, in order, including the theme.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When we read a story, the theme does not just pop up at the end. The author drops clues all the way through. The key events — the big, important moments — are where the theme lives. Let me show you what I mean."

Draw an "Event Staircase" on the board — four steps going upward, with "THEME" written at the top.

Say: "We are going to read a story together. As we read, I want us to find the key events that build toward the theme. I will fill in the staircase as we go."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Tryouts"**

> Nadia had wanted to be on the school soccer team since second grade. Now she was finally old enough to try out. She practiced every afternoon in the backyard, kicking the ball against the fence until the sun went down.
>
> On the day of tryouts, the field was packed with students. Nadia's hands were sweating. During the dribbling drill, she tripped over the ball and fell. A few kids laughed. She wanted to walk off the field right then.
>
> But she got back up. During the passing drill, she sent three perfect passes to her partner. In the scrimmage, she stole the ball twice and set up a goal. When the coach posted the list the next day, Nadia's name was on it.
>
> "I almost quit after I fell," she told her mom.
>
> "But you did not," her mom said. "And that made all the difference."

Say: "Let me fill in the staircase. What was the first key event?"

Write on Step 1: **Nadia practiced every afternoon to prepare for tryouts.**

Say: "What does this tell us? She really wanted this and was willing to work hard. That is a clue about the theme."

Write on Step 2: **She tripped during the dribbling drill and wanted to quit.**

Say: "This is the challenge — the moment things got hard. Watch what she does next."

Write on Step 3: **She got back up and performed well in the rest of tryouts.**

Say: "She did not give up even though she was embarrassed. That is a huge clue."

Write on Step 4: **She made the team. She told her mom she almost quit, and her mom said 'that made all the difference.'**

Say: "Now I look at the whole staircase. Nadia worked hard, faced a setback, chose to keep going, and succeeded. What is the theme?"

Write at the top: **Theme: When you keep going after a setback instead of giving up, you give yourself the chance to succeed.**

Say: "Now let me summarize this story in a way that includes the theme. Watch how I do it."

Write on the board: "Nadia practiced hard for soccer tryouts but tripped and fell during the first drill. Instead of quitting, she kept going and played well enough to make the team. The story shows that when you keep going after a setback, you give yourself the chance to succeed."

Say: "See how my summary hits the big events AND states the theme at the end? That is what a strong summary looks like."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students work with the teacher to trace events and identify theme in a new passage, then practice summarizing.

Say: "Now we are going to do this together with a new story. I will read it aloud, and you are going to help me fill in the Event Staircase. Draw four steps in your notebook with 'THEME' at the top."

**Passage: "The Group Project"**

> Mr. Reeves assigned a group project about animals of the rainforest. Owen was grouped with Ava and Daniel. Owen loved research, but he did not like working with other people. He liked doing things his own way.
>
> At their first group meeting, Ava suggested they divide the work. "I will do the poster, Daniel can write the report, and Owen can do the research," she said.
>
> "I would rather just do the whole thing myself," Owen said. Ava looked hurt. Daniel shrugged and opened his notebook.
>
> Owen stayed up until ten o'clock doing research, writing notes, and sketching a poster design. The next morning he was so tired he could barely keep his eyes open in class.
>
> At their second meeting, Ava showed Owen a poster she had started anyway. It was really good — she had drawn detailed pictures of toucans and tree frogs. Daniel had written two paragraphs about the rainforest canopy that Owen had not even thought about.
>
> Owen looked at his own messy notes. Then he looked at what Ava and Daniel had done. "I am sorry," he said quietly. "I should have let us work together from the start. Your stuff is amazing."
>
> "That is what teams are for," Ava said with a smile.
>
> They put it all together, and their project was the best one in the class.

Say: "Okay, let us build the staircase. What is the first key event? Talk to your partner and agree on one."

Guide students to fill in:
- **Step 1:** Owen was put in a group but wanted to do everything himself.
- **Step 2:** Owen tried to do the whole project alone and ended up exhausted with messy work.
- **Step 3:** He saw that Ava and Daniel had done excellent work on their parts.
- **Step 4:** Owen apologized and they combined their work to create the best project.

Say: "Now look at the staircase. What is the theme? Write a full sentence on your paper."

Allow 60 seconds, then discuss. Guide students toward: **Working with others and sharing responsibility leads to better results than trying to do everything alone.**

Say: "Now write a two-to-three-sentence summary that includes the theme. Remember: hit the big events, then state the theme."

Allow 2-3 minutes for writing. Call on one or two students to share. A strong response might be: "Owen did not want to work with his group on a school project and tried to do everything by himself. When he saw that his teammates had done great work without him, he realized he should have shared the work. The story teaches that working with others leads to better results than trying to do everything alone."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently trace events and identify theme in a new story.

**Passage: "The Garden Lesson"**

> Rosa and her grandmother planted a vegetable garden every spring. This year, Rosa wanted to grow pumpkins. Her grandmother said they should also plant tomatoes, beans, and peppers — the same vegetables they grew every year.
>
> "Pumpkins take a lot of space and a lot of patience," Grandma said. "They grow slowly."
>
> "I do not care," Rosa said. "I want to try."
>
> So they planted everything, including a small patch of pumpkins in the corner. Every day after school, Rosa watered the garden. The tomatoes grew quickly. Within three weeks, tiny green tomatoes hung on the vines. The beans climbed their poles. The peppers turned bright red.
>
> But the pumpkin patch just sat there. After a month, the plants had big green leaves but no pumpkins. Rosa checked every morning. Nothing.
>
> "Maybe I should pull them out," Rosa said one evening. "They are not working."
>
> "Give them time," Grandma said. "Good things do not always happen on your schedule."
>
> Rosa kept watering. Two weeks later, she spotted something small and round tucked under a leaf. Then another one, and another. By October, Rosa had five fat orange pumpkins — more than she had dreamed of.
>
> "You were right, Grandma," Rosa said, lifting the biggest one. "Some things just need more time."

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences.

1. Draw an Event Staircase with four steps and fill in the four key events that build toward the theme.
2. What is the theme of this story? Write it as a complete sentence.
3. Find two pieces of evidence from the text that support the theme.
4. Write a two-to-three-sentence summary of the story that includes the theme.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete questions 1-4 as written.
**Stretch:** After completing questions 1-4, explain what might have happened if Rosa had pulled out the pumpkin plants when she wanted to. How would that have changed the theme?
**Challenge:** After completing all questions, write about a different story you have read (or a movie or show) where the same theme — patience pays off — appears. Describe the key events and explain how they build the same theme.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Lesson 1 review) What is the topic of "The Garden Lesson"? How is the topic different from the theme?
6. (Grade 3 review — RL.3.3) How do Rosa's actions at the beginning of the story (wanting pumpkins, insisting on planting them) contribute to what happens later? Use details from the text.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following mini-passage:

> Kevin wanted to win the spelling bee. He studied every night for a month. On the day of the bee, he misspelled a word in the third round and was eliminated. He was upset, but his teacher said, "You learned two hundred new words this month. That knowledge is yours to keep." Kevin realized the real prize was not the trophy — it was everything he had learned along the way.

1. Name two key events that build toward the theme.
2. What is the theme of this passage?
3. Write a one-sentence summary that includes the theme.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Key events: Kevin studied every night for a month; he misspelled a word and was eliminated; his teacher pointed out that he had learned two hundred new words; Kevin realized the real prize was what he had learned. (Any two of these.)
2. Theme: The effort you put into something and what you learn along the way are more valuable than winning. (Accept reasonable variations.)
3. Summary example: Kevin studied hard for the spelling bee and was upset when he lost, but he realized that the knowledge he gained was more valuable than a trophy.

**Mastery = Student identifies at least two key events AND writes a theme as a complete sentence that reflects the passage's message.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Re-teach the Event Staircase strategy using a familiar fairy tale (e.g., "The Three Little Pigs") so the student can focus on the skill rather than comprehension.
- Provide the sentence frame: "The author wants the reader to learn that _______________."
- Practice with one additional short passage before Lesson 3.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3, which shifts focus to describing characters in depth.
- Encourage the student to try the Event Staircase strategy with their independent reading book.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Event Staircase Poster:** The student creates a large, illustrated Event Staircase for a favorite book, showing how the key events lead to the theme.
2. **Rewrite the Ending:** The student rewrites the ending of "The Garden Lesson" so that Rosa pulls out the pumpkins early. Then the student explains how the theme changes with the new ending.
3. **Theme Song:** The student writes a short poem or song (4-8 lines) that captures the theme of one of today's passages.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Building on Lesson 1:** Students now move from identifying theme to tracing how it develops. The Event Staircase is a concrete graphic organizer that makes this abstract process visible. Keep the staircase format consistent so students internalize the strategy.
- **Summarizing with theme:** Many students will summarize by retelling every event. Coach them to select only the key events (3-4) and to end with a theme statement. A summary that includes the theme is much stronger than one that does not.
- **Common error:** Students may list events that are interesting but not key to the theme (e.g., "Rosa watered the garden every day" is a detail, not a key event). Help them distinguish: "Does this event help build the message? Does it show a turning point or a decision?"
- **Pacing adjustment:** If the Guided Practice passage takes longer than expected, shorten the Independent Practice by having students answer questions 1 and 2 only, and assigning the rest as take-home practice.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Event Staircase:**
   - Step 1: Rosa wanted to plant pumpkins even though Grandma said they take a lot of patience.
   - Step 2: The other vegetables grew quickly, but the pumpkins did not show any fruit for over a month.
   - Step 3: Rosa wanted to pull out the pumpkin plants, but Grandma told her to give them time.
   - Step 4: Rosa kept watering, and by October she had five fat pumpkins — more than she had dreamed of.

2. **Theme:** Good things sometimes take longer than you expect, and patience is rewarded if you do not give up. (Accept reasonable variations about patience and perseverance.)

3. **Evidence (any two):**
   - Grandma said "Good things do not always happen on your schedule," hinting at the lesson about patience.
   - Rosa wanted to pull out the pumpkins because they were not growing fast enough, but she kept watering — and eventually they grew.
   - The pumpkins ended up being "more than she had dreamed of," showing that her patience was rewarded.
   - Rosa said "Some things just need more time," showing she learned the lesson.

4. **Summary example:** Rosa planted pumpkins even though her grandmother warned they would grow slowly. After weeks of waiting with no pumpkins, she wanted to give up, but her grandmother encouraged her to be patient. Eventually the pumpkins grew beautifully, teaching Rosa that patience and persistence pay off.

### Interleaved Review
5. The topic is patience (or growing things / gardening). The topic is just one or two words that tell what the story is about. The theme is a full sentence that tells the lesson or message: patience is rewarded if you do not give up.
6. Rosa's actions at the beginning — insisting on planting pumpkins even though Grandma warned they took patience — set up the whole story. Because Rosa chose to plant them, she had to learn to wait. Her determination at the start made the lesson at the end possible. If she had just planted what Grandma suggested, there would be no challenge and no lesson to learn.
