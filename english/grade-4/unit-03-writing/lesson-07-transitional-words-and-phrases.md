# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 7

## Topic
Transitional Words and Phrases

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify and use transitional words and phrases organized by purpose (sequence, addition, contrast, cause/effect, example, conclusion)
- Apply appropriate transitions across all three writing types (opinion, informative, narrative)
- Understand how transitions connect ideas, signal shifts, and guide the reader through a piece of writing

## Prerequisites
- Ability to use basic transition words (First, Also, Finally, Next, Then) from Grade 3
- Experience writing opinion, informative, and narrative pieces (Lessons 1-6 of this unit)
- Understanding of paragraph structure and how ideas connect across sentences

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils or highlighters (6 colors — one for each transition category)
- Index cards (for transition word bank — the student will create a personal reference set)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for displaying the transition chart
- Writing samples from Lessons 1-6 (the student's own work, if available)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate what happens when transitions are missing and activate awareness of how transitions guide the reader.

**Activity: With and Without**

Say to the student: "I am going to read you two versions of the same paragraph. Listen carefully and tell me which one is easier to follow and WHY."

**Version A (no transitions):**
> Dogs need exercise. They need to be walked every day. Dogs can become bored and destructive. They should be fed twice a day. Puppies need more food than adult dogs. You should take your dog to the veterinarian once a year.

**Version B (with transitions):**
> Dogs need regular exercise. For example, most dogs should be walked at least once a day to stay healthy and happy. Without enough exercise, dogs can become bored and destructive. In addition to exercise, dogs need proper nutrition. Adult dogs should be fed twice a day, while puppies need smaller, more frequent meals because they are still growing. Finally, it is important to take your dog to the veterinarian at least once a year for a checkup.

Ask: "Which version is easier to follow?" (Version B.)
Ask: "What makes it easier?" (The transition words connect ideas, show relationships, and guide the reader.)

Say: "Transitions are like road signs on a highway. They tell the reader: 'Here comes an example,' 'Here is another idea,' 'Now I am going to contrast two things,' or 'Here is my conclusion.' Without them, the reader has to guess how ideas connect. With them, the writing flows smoothly. Today, we are going to build a powerful toolkit of transition words and phrases that you can use in ALL types of writing."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach six categories of transitions and how they function across opinion, informative, and narrative writing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Transition:** A word or phrase that connects ideas and shows the relationship between them — it helps the reader move smoothly from one thought to the next
- **Signal word:** Another term for a transition — it signals to the reader what kind of idea is coming next
- **Cohesion:** When all the parts of a piece of writing fit together smoothly — transitions create cohesion by linking sentences and paragraphs

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: The Six Categories of Transitions**

Say: "In Grade 3, you learned a few transitions: First, Also, Finally, Next, Then. Those are great — and you will keep using them. But in Grade 4, you need a much BIGGER toolkit. Transitions come in six categories, and each category has a different JOB."

Write the Transition Reference Chart (this will become the student's personal reference):

**1. SEQUENCE (Show order)**
- First, Second, Third
- Next, Then, After that
- Before, During, After
- Meanwhile, At the same time
- Finally, Last, In the end

**2. ADDITION (Add more information)**
- In addition, Additionally, Furthermore
- Also, Moreover, Another reason
- As well as, Along with
- Not only... but also

**3. CONTRAST (Show differences or counterarguments)**
- However, On the other hand
- Although, Even though
- But, Yet, While
- In contrast, Unlike
- Nevertheless, Despite this

**4. CAUSE and EFFECT (Show why something happened)**
- Because, Since, Due to
- As a result, Therefore, Consequently
- This means that, For this reason
- So, Thus, Leading to

**5. EXAMPLE (Introduce evidence or illustrations)**
- For example, For instance
- Such as, Specifically
- In particular, To illustrate
- One example is

**6. CONCLUSION (Wrap up ideas)**
- In conclusion, To summarize
- For these reasons, Therefore
- All in all, Overall
- Clearly, As a result
- In the end

Say: "You do not need to memorize all of these. But you DO need to know which CATEGORY to use when. If you want to add another reason, reach for an ADDITION transition. If you want to show why something happened, reach for a CAUSE/EFFECT transition."

**Step 2: Transitions in Each Writing Type**

Say: "Different types of writing use transitions differently. Let me show you."

**In OPINION writing:**
> Schools should offer free breakfast to all students. **First,** many students come to school hungry because their families cannot afford breakfast. **As a result,** these students struggle to focus in class. **Furthermore,** studies show that students who eat breakfast score higher on tests. **For example,** a district in Texas saw test scores rise by 10% after starting a free breakfast program. **However,** some people argue that free breakfast is too expensive. **Nevertheless,** the benefits to student learning far outweigh the cost. **For these reasons,** every school should provide a free morning meal.

Say: "I used transitions from FIVE categories: sequence (First), cause/effect (As a result), addition (Furthermore), example (For example), contrast (However, Nevertheless), and conclusion (For these reasons). Opinion writing needs ALL these types because you are building an argument."

**In INFORMATIVE writing:**
> Tornadoes form when warm, moist air meets cold, dry air. **During** this collision, the warm air rises rapidly. **As a result,** the air begins to spin. **Additionally,** if wind speeds increase at higher altitudes, the spinning column tightens and becomes stronger. **For instance,** an EF5 tornado — the most powerful category — can produce winds exceeding 200 miles per hour. **In contrast,** an EF1 tornado has winds between 86 and 110 miles per hour and typically causes minor damage.

Say: "Informative writing relies heavily on SEQUENCE, ADDITION, CAUSE/EFFECT, EXAMPLE, and CONTRAST transitions because you are explaining how things work and comparing information."

**In NARRATIVE writing:**
> **At first,** Mia did not notice anything unusual about the house. **But then** she heard a creak from upstairs — slow and deliberate, like a footstep on an old wooden floor. She froze. **Meanwhile,** outside, the wind picked up and rattled the shutters. **After a moment,** she took a deep breath and started climbing the stairs. **Although** every part of her wanted to turn around, her curiosity was stronger than her fear. **Finally,** she reached the top and pushed open the door.

Say: "Narrative writing uses SEQUENCE transitions most often (to manage time), but also uses CONTRAST (to show internal conflict — 'Although every part of her wanted to turn around...') and CAUSE/EFFECT (to show why characters act the way they do)."

### Worked Examples

**Model Text: Revising a Paragraph by Adding Transitions**

Say: "Let me show you how transitions can transform a paragraph. Here is a paragraph WITHOUT transitions."

**Before:**
> Elephants are the largest land animals on Earth. An adult African elephant can weigh up to 14,000 pounds. They are gentle and intelligent. They have been known to mourn their dead and show empathy toward other elephants. Elephants face serious threats from poaching and habitat loss. Conservation efforts are helping some elephant populations recover.

Say: "The facts are good, but the paragraph feels choppy. The ideas are not connected. Watch what happens when I add transitions."

**After:**
> Elephants are the largest land animals on Earth. **For example,** an adult African elephant can weigh up to 14,000 pounds. **Despite** their enormous size, they are remarkably gentle and intelligent. **In fact,** they have been known to mourn their dead and show empathy toward other elephants. **Unfortunately,** elephants face serious threats from poaching and habitat loss. **However,** conservation efforts are helping some elephant populations recover.

Say: "Same facts, but now the writing FLOWS. Each transition tells the reader what kind of connection to expect: 'For example' introduces supporting evidence, 'Despite' shows a contrast, 'In fact' adds surprising detail, 'Unfortunately' signals bad news, and 'However' signals a shift to good news."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice selecting and inserting appropriate transitions into existing writing.

**Activity Part 1: Fill in the Transition (4 minutes)**

Say: "I am going to give you sentences with blanks where transitions should go. Choose the BEST transition from the options provided."

1. Pandas eat mostly bamboo. _____, they occasionally eat small animals or fish.
   Options: However / For example / Because
   Answer: **However** (contrast — the occasional eating of other food contrasts with "mostly bamboo")

2. The students raised money by selling lemonade. _____, they were able to donate $200 to the animal shelter.
   Options: In contrast / As a result / For instance
   Answer: **As a result** (cause/effect — the fundraising led to the donation)

3. There are many ways to stay active. _____, you can ride a bike, swim, play basketball, or go for a hike.
   Options: Nevertheless / For example / Therefore
   Answer: **For example** (example — the list illustrates the "many ways")

4. _____ the rain was pouring down, the soccer game continued.
   Options: Because / Although / In addition
   Answer: **Although** (contrast — the rain would be expected to stop the game, but it did not)

5. The team practiced every day after school. _____, they held extra sessions on Saturday mornings.
   Options: However / In addition / As a result
   Answer: **In addition** (addition — the Saturday sessions are added to the weekday practice)

**Activity Part 2: Revise Your Own Writing (6-8 minutes)**

Say: "Now here is the really powerful part. Pull out a piece of writing you have done in this unit — your opinion piece from Lessons 1-2 OR your informative piece from Lessons 3-4 OR your narrative from Lessons 5-6."

If the student's previous work is not available, provide this paragraph to revise:

> Video games are not just for fun. They can improve hand-eye coordination. Some video games teach problem-solving and strategy. Too much screen time can be unhealthy. It is important to balance gaming with outdoor activities and reading. Video games can be a valuable part of a kid's life when used in moderation.

Say: "Read through your piece. Find at least THREE places where you could add a transition to make the writing flow better. Circle those spots. Then choose the best transition for each one from our chart."

Guide the student through the revision process. For the sample paragraph above, a revised version might look like:

> Video games are not just for fun. **For instance,** they can improve hand-eye coordination. **Additionally,** some video games teach problem-solving and strategy. **However,** too much screen time can be unhealthy. **Therefore,** it is important to balance gaming with outdoor activities and reading. **Overall,** video games can be a valuable part of a kid's life when used in moderation.

Say: "See how three or four transitions can transform a choppy paragraph into a smooth, connected piece of writing?"

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently uses transitions across different writing types.

**Directions:** "Complete TWO of the three tasks below. Each task requires you to use transitions from at least three different categories."

**Task 1 — Opinion Paragraph:**
Write a short opinion paragraph (5-7 sentences) about this claim: "Fourth graders should be allowed to choose their own books for reading assignments instead of having the teacher choose for them." Use at least four transitions from at least three different categories.

**Task 2 — Informative Paragraph:**
Write a short informative paragraph (5-7 sentences) about a natural disaster (earthquake, tornado, hurricane, flood, or wildfire). Use at least four transitions from at least three different categories. Remember: informative, not opinion.

**Task 3 — Narrative Paragraph:**
Write a short narrative scene (5-7 sentences) about a character who is running late for something important. Use at least three transitions to manage the sequence of events and show time passing.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete two of the three tasks. Each piece should use at least four transitions from at least three categories. Underline or highlight each transition you use.

**Stretch:** Complete all three tasks. Each piece should use at least five transitions from at least four categories. After writing, label each transition with its category (sequence, addition, contrast, cause/effect, example, conclusion). No transition word should be repeated across the three pieces.

**Challenge:** Complete the stretch tier. Then write a one-paragraph explanation of which category of transitions you find MOST useful and why. Include examples from your own writing to support your point. (This is metacognitive reflection — thinking about your own thinking.)

### Interleaved Review

After completing the transition tasks, answer these review questions:

1. **Narrative Review (Lessons 5-6):** "What are the two jobs of dialogue in a narrative?" (Reveal character personality and advance the plot.)

2. **Opinion Writing Review (Lessons 1-2):** "What three parts make up a strong opinion introduction?" (A hook, background information, and a claim/thesis statement.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this passage and answer the questions:

> Recycling is important for protecting the environment. **First,** recycling reduces the amount of waste sent to landfills. **For example,** recycling one ton of paper saves 17 trees and 7,000 gallons of water. **In addition,** recycling conserves natural resources by reusing materials instead of extracting new ones. **However,** not everyone recycles regularly. **As a result,** millions of tons of recyclable materials end up in landfills each year. **Therefore,** communities should make recycling easier by providing curbside pickup and clear guidelines.

1. Identify the transition that shows CONTRAST. ("However")
2. Identify the transition that introduces an EXAMPLE. ("For example")
3. Identify the transition that shows CAUSE and EFFECT. ("As a result" or "Therefore")
4. Why are transitions important in writing? (They connect ideas, show relationships between sentences, and help the reader follow the writer's thinking smoothly.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies at least three transition categories and explains why transitions matter.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Create a personal transition word bank on index cards — one card per category with 4-5 transitions listed
- Practice daily: give the student two sentences and have them choose the best transition to connect them
- Return to the student's previous writing and add transitions together — physically revising existing work makes the concept concrete
- Post the transition chart where the student can see it during all writing activities

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 8 (Revising and Editing) next session
- Preview: "You have now learned to write opinion, informative, and narrative pieces with strong transitions. Next, we are going to learn how to REVISE and EDIT your writing — how to take a good draft and make it great."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Transition Scavenger Hunt:** The student reads a page from a textbook, novel, or article and highlights every transition word/phrase they find. Then they sort the transitions into the six categories. Which category appears most often? Why?
- **Transition Challenge Game:** The facilitator gives the student a transition word (e.g., "Nevertheless"). The student must use it correctly in a sentence within 30 seconds. Take turns. Increase difficulty by requiring specific categories.
- **Create a Transition Reference Card:** The student designs a personal reference card (index card or bookmark-sized) with their favorite transitions organized by category. They keep this card in their writing folder and reference it during every writing activity.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can be completed in one session for students who already use basic transitions confidently. If the student relies on "First, Next, Then, Finally" for everything, allow two sessions — one to introduce the six categories with practice, one for independent writing application.

**Connection to Grade 3:** In Grade 3, students used basic sequence transitions (First, Next, Then, Finally) and simple addition transitions (Also, Another reason). The new skills in Grade 4 are: (1) expanding the transition toolkit to include contrast, cause/effect, example, and conclusion categories, (2) choosing transitions based on the RELATIONSHIP between ideas (not just the order), and (3) applying transitions across all three writing types. Celebrate what the student already knows before expanding.

**The most important takeaway:** Students should understand that transitions are not decoration — they are MEANING. Each transition tells the reader what kind of relationship to expect between ideas. "However" tells the reader a contrast is coming. "As a result" tells the reader a consequence is coming. "For example" tells the reader evidence is coming. When students choose transitions intentionally, their writing becomes not just smoother but clearer and more logical.

**Overuse warning:** Some students, once they learn about transitions, start EVERY sentence with one. This is understandable enthusiasm but creates awkward, mechanical writing. If you see this, say: "Transitions are like seasoning in cooking. A little makes everything better. Too much overwhelms the flavor. Not every sentence needs a transition — just the ones where the reader needs a signal."

**The personal transition reference card is valuable:** If the student creates a physical reference card during the Extension activity, encourage them to keep it in their writing folder and use it for EVERY writing assignment going forward. Over time, the transitions will become automatic, but having the card as a reference during the learning process is extremely helpful.

## Answer Key / Rubric

**Warm-Up:** Version B is easier to follow because transitions connect ideas and show relationships. The reader knows when an example is coming, when a new idea is being added, and when the conclusion is approaching.

**Guided Practice — Fill in the Transition:**
1. However (contrast)
2. As a result (cause/effect)
3. For example (example)
4. Although (contrast)
5. In addition (addition)

**Independent Practice:** Answers will vary by task and tier. Evaluate using these criteria:

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Number of Transitions** | At least 4 per piece (on-level) or 5 (stretch) | 2-3 transitions per piece | Fewer than 2 transitions |
| **Variety of Categories** | Transitions from at least 3 categories (on-level) or 4 (stretch) | Transitions from 2 categories | All transitions from one category |
| **Correct Usage** | All transitions used correctly — they match the relationship between ideas | Most transitions are correct; 1-2 are slightly off | Transitions are used randomly without matching the relationship |
| **Writing Quality** | Writing flows smoothly; transitions enhance readability | Writing is somewhat choppy despite transitions | Writing is disjointed; transitions feel forced |
| **Writing Type Match** | Tone matches the writing type (opinion argues, informative teaches, narrative tells a story) | Mostly matches but slips in places | Writing type is unclear |

**Interleaved Review:**
1. The two jobs of dialogue: reveal character personality and advance the plot.
2. A strong opinion introduction has three parts: a hook, background information, and a claim/thesis statement.

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Contrast: "However"
2. Example: "For example"
3. Cause/effect: "As a result" or "Therefore" (either is correct)
4. Transitions connect ideas, show relationships between sentences, and help the reader follow the writer's thinking smoothly.
