# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 8

## Topic
Revising and Editing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Revise writing for clarity, organization, word choice, and sentence variety
- Edit writing for grammar, spelling, capitalization, and punctuation errors
- Use a self-editing and peer-editing checklist to systematically improve a draft

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write opinion, informative, and narrative pieces (Lessons 1-7 of this unit)
- Understanding of basic grammar, spelling, capitalization, and punctuation conventions
- Familiarity with the revision and editing process from Grade 3 (W.3.5)

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils or pens (at least 3 colors: one for content revisions, one for word choice, one for editing corrections)
- A draft of writing from this unit (the student's best opinion, informative, or narrative piece from Lessons 1-7)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Printed or hand-drawn editing checklist (template provided below)
- A dictionary (print or online)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Establish the difference between revising and editing and build a positive attitude toward the revision process.

**Activity: Revise or Edit?**

Say to the student: "Today we are going to learn two different skills that make good writing great: REVISING and EDITING. These are NOT the same thing. Let me explain."

Write on the board:

**REVISING = Making your writing BETTER**
- Focus: ideas, organization, word choice, sentence variety, clarity
- Question: "Does this say what I mean? Is it organized? Are my words precise?"

**EDITING = Making your writing CORRECT**
- Focus: spelling, grammar, capitalization, punctuation
- Question: "Are there any mistakes that would distract or confuse the reader?"

Say: "Here is an easy way to remember: REVISE first, EDIT second. It does not make sense to fix the spelling of a sentence you might delete during revision."

Now read these changes. The student identifies each as REVISING or EDITING.

1. Changing "went" to "sprinted" in a narrative. (Revising — improving word choice.)
2. Fixing "their" to "there" in a sentence about a location. (Editing — correcting a spelling/homophone error.)
3. Moving a paragraph from the end to the middle because it fits better there. (Revising — improving organization.)
4. Adding a capital letter to the start of a sentence. (Editing — correcting capitalization.)
5. Adding a transition word between two paragraphs. (Revising — improving flow and cohesion.)
6. Putting quotation marks around dialogue. (Editing — correcting punctuation.)

Say: "Great! Professional authors revise and edit EVERY piece they write. This is not a sign that the first draft was bad — it is a sign that the writer cares enough to make it the best it can be."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach specific revision strategies and editing skills, and introduce the self-editing checklist.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Revise:** To improve the content and craft of writing — changing ideas, reorganizing, improving word choice, and adding or removing details
- **Edit:** To correct errors in grammar, spelling, capitalization, and punctuation
- **Draft:** A version of a piece of writing that is not yet final — most writing goes through multiple drafts
- **Word choice:** The specific words a writer selects — precise, vivid words are more effective than vague, generic words
- **Sentence variety:** Using a mix of short and long sentences, and starting sentences in different ways, to keep writing interesting
- **Peer editing:** When another person reads your writing and provides feedback on content and corrections

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Five Revision Strategies**

Say: "When you revise, you are looking at the BIG PICTURE of your writing. Here are five strategies."

Write the Five Revision Strategies:

**Strategy 1: Read it aloud.**
Say: "Read your writing OUT LOUD. Your ears will catch problems your eyes miss. If a sentence sounds awkward when you say it, it will sound awkward to a reader."

**Strategy 2: Check your organization.**
Say: "Does your writing follow a logical order? In opinion writing, do your reasons build from least to most important? In informative writing, is each paragraph focused on one subtopic? In narrative writing, do events happen in a clear sequence?"

**Strategy 3: Upgrade your word choice.**
Say: "Look for vague or overused words and replace them with precise, vivid ones."

Write the upgrade chart:

| Vague Word | Precise Upgrades |
|---|---|
| good | excellent, impressive, valuable, outstanding |
| bad | terrible, harmful, disappointing, destructive |
| nice | generous, thoughtful, pleasant, welcoming |
| said | whispered, exclaimed, muttered, announced, replied |
| went | sprinted, shuffled, marched, tiptoed, raced |
| big | enormous, towering, massive, vast |
| small | tiny, miniature, compact, microscopic |
| very | (remove it and choose a stronger word: "very tired" becomes "exhausted") |

**Strategy 4: Vary your sentences.**
Say: "If every sentence starts the same way or is the same length, the writing sounds monotonous. Mix it up."

Write the before and after:

**Before (monotonous):**
> The dog ran across the yard. The dog jumped over the fence. The dog chased the squirrel. The dog was fast.

**After (varied):**
> The dog shot across the yard like a furry cannonball. In one smooth leap, it cleared the fence. The squirrel did not stand a chance — within seconds, the chase was on, and the dog was gaining fast.

Say: "I changed the sentence starters, varied the lengths, added a simile, and combined ideas. Same story, but much more engaging."

**Strategy 5: Add what is missing, cut what is extra.**
Say: "Sometimes a draft needs MORE — a missing detail, a transition, a stronger ending. Sometimes it needs LESS — a sentence that repeats what was already said, a detail that does not fit, or a word that adds nothing. Good writers add AND cut."

**Step 2: The Editing Checklist**

Say: "After revising, it is time to EDIT. Here is a checklist you will use for every piece of writing from now on."

Write the Self-Editing Checklist:

```
SELF-EDITING CHECKLIST

SPELLING
[ ] I checked words I was unsure about using a dictionary
[ ] I checked common homophones: their/there/they're, your/you're, its/it's, to/too/two

CAPITALIZATION
[ ] Every sentence starts with a capital letter
[ ] Proper nouns (names, places, days, months) are capitalized
[ ] Titles and headings are capitalized correctly

PUNCTUATION
[ ] Every sentence ends with a period, question mark, or exclamation point
[ ] Commas are used in lists, after introductory words, and before conjunctions in compound sentences
[ ] Dialogue has quotation marks, commas, and correct end punctuation

GRAMMAR
[ ] Subjects and verbs agree (she runs, not she run)
[ ] Verb tenses are consistent (do not switch between past and present randomly)
[ ] Pronouns are clear (the reader knows who "he" or "she" refers to)

PARAGRAPHING
[ ] Each paragraph has ONE main idea
[ ] A new paragraph starts when the topic changes
[ ] A new paragraph starts when a different character speaks in dialogue
```

### Worked Examples

**Model Text: Revising and Editing a Draft**

Say: "Watch me revise and edit a draft. I will use a think-aloud so you can hear my process."

**Original Draft:**

> I think that schools should have longer recess. Kids need to run around. Recess is fun. Also, recess helps kids focus. studies show that kids who have more recess do better in class. Finally, recess teaches kids to get along with each other. kids learn to share and take turns. For these reasons schools should have more recess.

Say: "First, let me REVISE for content and craft."

**Revision 1 — Upgrade the claim:** "'I think that schools should have longer recess' is a casual opinion, not a claim. Let me upgrade it."
Revised: "Schools should extend daily recess to at least 45 minutes because physical activity improves focus, health, and social skills."

**Revision 2 — Add evidence:** "'Kids need to run around' does not have evidence. Let me add some."
Revised: "Students need regular physical activity to maintain their health. According to the Centers for Disease Control, children should get at least 60 minutes of physical activity each day, and recess is one of the few guaranteed times for movement during the school day."

**Revision 3 — Cut the filler:** "'Recess is fun' does not support the claim. It is filler. Cut it."

**Revision 4 — Upgrade word choice:** "'do better in class' is vague. Let me be more precise."
Revised: "demonstrate improved concentration and higher test scores."

**Revision 5 — Add transitions:** "The paragraph needs smoother connections."
Add: "Furthermore" before the social skills paragraph. "For example" before the evidence about sharing.

Say: "Now let me EDIT for correctness."

**Edit 1:** "studies" should be capitalized — it starts a sentence. Fix: "Studies"
**Edit 2:** "kids" should be capitalized — it starts a sentence. Fix: "Kids"
**Edit 3:** "For these reasons schools" needs a comma. Fix: "For these reasons, schools"

**Final Revised and Edited Version:**

> Schools should extend daily recess to at least 45 minutes because physical activity improves focus, health, and social skills. Students need regular physical activity to maintain their health. According to the Centers for Disease Control, children should get at least 60 minutes of physical activity each day, and recess is one of the few guaranteed times for movement during the school day. Additionally, studies show that students who have longer recess periods demonstrate improved concentration and higher test scores. Furthermore, recess teaches students essential social skills. For example, on the playground, children learn to share equipment, take turns, resolve disagreements, and cooperate in group games. For these reasons, schools should prioritize extending recess time.

Say: "Compare the original and the final version. Same basic argument, but the final version is more organized, more developed, more precise, and error-free. That is the power of revising and editing."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice revising and editing a piece of writing together using the checklist.

**Activity: Revise and Edit Together**

Say: "Now let's revise and edit a piece of writing together. Here is a draft that needs work."

**Draft to revise and edit:**

> last summer me and my family went to yellowstone national park. it was really cool. we saw geysers and hot springs and animals. the geysers were amazing. old faithful is a geyser that goes off every like 90 minutes or something. We watched it and it shooted water super high into the air. it was so cool. we also saw bison which are really big and kind of scary looking. my sister said look at that one its huge. we also saw a bear from far away which was exciting. yellowstone is the best place ever everyone should go there.

**Step 1: Revise together.**

Say: "Let's go through the five revision strategies."

1. **Read aloud.** Read the draft aloud together. Ask: "What sounds awkward or unclear?"
2. **Check organization.** Ask: "Is this organized by subtopic, or is everything jumbled?" (Jumbled — geysers, animals, and opinions are mixed.)
3. **Upgrade word choice.** Find vague words: "cool" (appears twice), "amazing," "super high," "really big," "kind of scary looking," "exciting." Replace each with more precise language.
4. **Vary sentences.** Many sentences start with "We" or "It." Vary the starters.
5. **Add/cut.** Add sensory details. Cut "it was really cool" and "it was so cool" — they are filler.

**Step 2: Edit together.**

Go through the editing checklist:
- **Spelling:** "shooted" should be "shot"; "its" should be "it's" (in dialogue)
- **Capitalization:** "last" should be "Last"; "me" should come after "my family" ("My family and I"); "yellowstone national park" should be "Yellowstone National Park"; "old faithful" should be "Old Faithful"; several sentences missing capital letters
- **Punctuation:** Missing comma after introductory phrases; dialogue needs quotation marks and proper punctuation; missing period at the end
- **Grammar:** "me and my family" should be "My family and I"; "goes off every like 90 minutes or something" — remove "like" and "or something"

**Step 3: Write the revised version together.**

Guide the student to produce something like:

> Last summer, my family and I traveled to Yellowstone National Park in Wyoming. We spent three days exploring geysers, hot springs, and wildlife — and every moment was unforgettable.
>
> The geysers were the highlight of the trip. Old Faithful, the park's most famous geyser, erupts approximately every 90 minutes, shooting boiling water over 100 feet into the air. We sat on the wooden benches and waited, and when the eruption finally came, a massive column of steam and water roared into the sky. The ground trembled under our feet.
>
> We also encountered incredible wildlife. A herd of bison grazed in a meadow beside the road, their shaggy brown coats dusty from rolling in the dirt. "Look at that one — it's huge!" my sister gasped, pressing her face against the car window. Later, we spotted a black bear lumbering through the trees about 200 yards away, which our guide said was a safe distance.
>
> Yellowstone is a place where the Earth feels alive. For anyone who loves nature, it is a trip worth taking.

Say: "Look at the difference! Same story, but now it is organized, vivid, precise, and correctly written."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently revises and edits one of their own drafts from this unit.

**Directions:** "Select your BEST draft from this unit — your opinion piece, your informative piece, or your narrative. Using the five revision strategies and the self-editing checklist, revise and edit your draft. Use colored pencils: one color for content revisions (ideas, organization, word choice), one color for editing corrections (spelling, capitalization, punctuation, grammar). Then write a clean final copy."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Revise the draft using at least three of the five revision strategies. Edit using the checklist. Make at least five improvements total (revisions + edits). Write a clean final copy.

**Stretch:** Revise the draft using all five strategies. Edit using the checklist. Upgrade at least three vague words to precise alternatives. Vary at least two sentence starters. Add at least one transition that was missing. Write a clean final copy that is noticeably stronger than the original.

**Challenge:** Complete the stretch tier. Then write a brief "Revision Reflection" (3-5 sentences) explaining what you changed and WHY. Example: "I replaced 'went' with 'sprinted' because it shows how fast the character was moving. I moved my strongest reason to the end because I learned in Lesson 2 that the strongest argument should come last."

### Interleaved Review

After completing the revision, answer these review questions:

1. **Transition Review (Lesson 7):** "Name one transition from each of these categories: sequence, contrast, and cause/effect." (Answers will vary. Examples: Sequence — "First" or "Next." Contrast — "However" or "Although." Cause/effect — "As a result" or "Because.")

2. **Narrative Review (Lessons 5-6):** "What is the difference between an action beat and a dialogue tag?" (A dialogue tag tells who is speaking using "said," "asked," etc. An action beat shows who is speaking through an action: 'Mia crossed her arms. "I don't believe you."')

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this sentence and make all necessary corrections (both revisions and edits):

> me and marcos went to the store and we buyed some really good chips and stuff and then we went home and eated them and it was good.

The student should produce something like:

> Marcos and I walked to the corner store after school. We bought two bags of spicy jalapeño chips — our favorite — and carried them home. We sat on the porch steps, crunching through the whole bag in ten minutes, our fingers stained orange from the seasoning.

Questions:
1. Name two REVISIONS you made. (Answers will vary. Possible: upgraded "went" to "walked," added specific details like "jalapeño" and "porch steps," removed vague words like "stuff" and "good," varied sentence structure.)
2. Name two EDITS you made. (Answers will vary. Possible: changed "me and marcos" to "Marcos and I," changed "buyed" to "bought," changed "eated" to "ate," capitalized "Marcos.")

**Mastery = Student makes at least two meaningful revisions (improved word choice, added details, improved organization) AND at least two correct edits (fixed spelling, grammar, capitalization, or punctuation).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice revising and editing with very short passages (2-3 sentences) before attempting full paragraphs
- Focus on ONE revision strategy at a time (today: word choice only; tomorrow: sentence variety only)
- Focus on ONE editing category at a time (today: capitalization only; tomorrow: punctuation only)
- Use the editing checklist as a step-by-step guide, checking one category at a time rather than trying to catch everything at once

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 9 (Typing and Publishing) next session
- Preview: "You now know how to revise and edit your writing. Next, we are going to take your best, polished piece and TYPE it on a computer. You will learn keyboarding skills and produce a published piece of writing."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Peer Editing Practice:** If another student or family member is available, practice peer editing. The student reads the partner's writing and uses the checklist to provide feedback. The partner does the same in return. Discuss: What did someone else catch that you missed?
- **Before and After Gallery:** The student displays their original draft and their revised final copy side by side. They annotate the changes with sticky notes explaining WHY each change was made. This makes the revision process visible and celebrates the improvement.
- **Word Choice Challenge:** Take any paragraph the student has written and find five words that could be upgraded. For each word, brainstorm three alternatives. Choose the best one and explain why.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson works best as a single session focused on learning the revision and editing strategies, followed by a second session where the student applies those strategies to their own writing at length. If time is limited, the guided practice (revising the Yellowstone paragraph together) is the most impactful activity.

**Revise FIRST, edit SECOND:** This order matters. Students (and adults) naturally gravitate toward editing first because catching typos feels productive and concrete. But if a student spends time perfecting the spelling of a sentence they later delete during revision, that time is wasted. Train the habit: big-picture changes first, then fine-tuning.

**Attitude toward revision:** Many students see revision as punishment — "I have to do it over because it was not good enough." Reframe: professional authors revise dozens of times. J.K. Rowling revised the first Harry Potter chapter multiple times. Revision is not failure — it is CRAFT. Start every revision session by identifying two things that are already STRONG in the draft before suggesting changes.

**The editing checklist as a living document:** The checklist provided in this lesson is a starting point. As the student encounters new grammar and punctuation rules (especially in Unit 4), add those to the checklist. Over time, the checklist becomes a personalized tool that grows with the student's skills.

**Common issues at this level:**
- Homophones (their/there/they're, your/you're, its/it's, to/too/two) — these are the most frequent spelling errors in Grade 4 writing
- Run-on sentences — "We went to the park and we played and we had fun and then we went home" needs to be broken into shorter sentences
- Inconsistent verb tense — students often shift between past and present within the same paragraph
- Starting every sentence with the subject — "I went... I saw... I ate..." needs sentence variety
