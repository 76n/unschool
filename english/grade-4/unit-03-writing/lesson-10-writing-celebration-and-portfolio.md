# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 10

## Topic
Writing Celebration and Portfolio

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Review and reflect on all three writing types learned in this unit (opinion, informative, narrative)
- Select their best work for a writing portfolio based on specific criteria
- Share writing with an audience and articulate what makes a piece effective

## Prerequisites
- Completion of Lessons 1-9 of this unit (opinion, informative, and narrative writing; transitions; revising and editing; typing and publishing)
- A collection of writing drafts and finished pieces from across the unit
- The published piece from Lesson 9

## Materials Needed
- All writing drafts and finished pieces from Lessons 1-9 (the student's writing folder)
- The published piece from Lesson 9
- A folder, binder, or envelope for the writing portfolio
- Lined paper and pencil (for reflection writing)
- Colored pencils (optional, for decorating the portfolio cover)
- An audience for sharing (a family member, friend, or the facilitator — someone who will listen and respond)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Reflect on the writing journey across this unit and build excitement for celebration.

**Activity: Writing Timeline**

Say to the student: "You have been on an incredible writing journey over the last several weeks. Let's look back at everything you have learned."

Draw a simple timeline on the board (or have the student draw it on paper) and fill it in together:

```
Lesson 1: Opinion — Stating a Claim
Lesson 2: Opinion — Supporting with Evidence
Lesson 3: Informative — Introducing a Topic
Lesson 4: Informative — Developing with Facts and Details
Lesson 5: Narrative — Setting and Characters
Lesson 6: Narrative — Dialogue and Pacing
Lesson 7: Transitional Words and Phrases
Lesson 8: Revising and Editing
Lesson 9: Typing and Publishing
Lesson 10: TODAY — Celebration!
```

Say: "Look at all that! You learned to write claims, support opinions with evidence, organize informative writing with headings and precise vocabulary, create vivid settings and characters, write purposeful dialogue, use six categories of transitions, revise and edit like a professional, and type and publish your work. That is an ENORMOUS amount of growth."

Ask: "Which lesson was your favorite? Which skill was the hardest to learn? Which skill are you most proud of?" (Let the student reflect and share.)

Say: "Today is about CELEBRATING your growth as a writer. You are going to look back at all your work, choose your best pieces for a portfolio, and share your writing with someone who will appreciate it."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to evaluate your own writing, select portfolio pieces, and write a reflection.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Portfolio:** A collection of your best work that shows your skills and growth as a writer
- **Reflection:** Thinking carefully about your own work — what you did well, what was hard, and what you learned
- **Audience:** The person or people who will read or listen to your writing
- **Growth:** The improvement you can see when you compare earlier work to later work — growth happens through practice, feedback, and effort

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: What Goes in a Writing Portfolio?**

Say: "A writing portfolio is not a folder of EVERYTHING you wrote. It is a carefully chosen collection of your BEST work. Professional writers, artists, and photographers all keep portfolios. When you apply for a job or a school program someday, you may be asked to share a portfolio. Today, you are building your first one."

Write the Portfolio Contents:

**Your portfolio should include:**
1. **One opinion piece** — your strongest opinion writing from Lessons 1-2 (or the unit assessment)
2. **One informative piece** — your strongest informative writing from Lessons 3-4 (or the unit assessment)
3. **One narrative piece** — your strongest narrative writing from Lessons 5-6 (or the unit assessment)
4. **Your published piece** from Lesson 9 (this could be one of the above three, polished and typed)
5. **A reflection** — a short written piece explaining what you learned and how you grew (you will write this today)

**Step 2: How to Choose Your Best Work**

Say: "How do you decide which pieces are your BEST? Here are the questions to ask yourself."

Write the Selection Criteria:

**For your opinion piece, ask:**
- Does it have a clear, specific claim?
- Are the reasons supported with evidence?
- Does it use linking words to connect ideas?
- Does the conclusion leave the reader thinking?

**For your informative piece, ask:**
- Does it introduce the topic clearly?
- Is the information grouped into focused paragraphs?
- Does it use facts, definitions, details, and precise vocabulary?
- Does it have a strong conclusion?

**For your narrative piece, ask:**
- Does it have a vivid setting with sensory details?
- Are the characters introduced with depth?
- Does the dialogue serve a purpose?
- Is the pacing varied and effective?
- Does the ending feel satisfying?

**For ALL pieces, ask:**
- Is it well organized?
- Are transitions used to connect ideas?
- Is the writing revised and edited (few or no errors)?
- Am I proud of this piece?

Say: "That last question is the most important. Your portfolio should contain writing that you are PROUD of — writing that represents your best effort and your best thinking."

**Step 3: Writing a Reflection**

Say: "The final piece in your portfolio is a REFLECTION. This is where you think about your growth as a writer. It is not a summary of what you wrote — it is an explanation of what you LEARNED."

Write the Reflection Prompts:

Your reflection should answer these questions (in 1-2 paragraphs):
1. Which type of writing (opinion, informative, or narrative) was the most challenging for you? Why?
2. Which type of writing was the most enjoyable? Why?
3. What is one specific skill you improved during this unit?
4. What is one goal you have for your writing going forward?

### Worked Examples

**Model Reflection:**

Say: "Let me show you what a reflection looks like."

> Looking back on this writing unit, I am surprised by how much I have grown as a writer. The most challenging type of writing for me was informative writing, especially grouping information into separate paragraphs with headings. At first, I kept mixing facts from different subtopics in the same paragraph. But after using the index card sorting activity in Lesson 3, I learned to organize my thoughts before I started writing, and my informative piece about volcanoes turned out to be one of my strongest works.
>
> My favorite type of writing was narrative. I loved creating characters and writing dialogue that showed their personalities. The dialogue lesson was hard at first — I kept forgetting where to put the commas — but by the end, I was writing conversations that felt real. One specific skill I improved is using sensory details. My early stories just said things like "it was a nice day," but now I describe what I see, hear, smell, and feel. My goal going forward is to write longer narratives with more developed plots and to get faster at typing so I can publish my stories more easily.

Say: "This reflection is honest. It talks about what was hard (informative writing, dialogue punctuation), what was enjoyable (narrative writing), a specific skill that improved (sensory details), and a goal (longer narratives, faster typing). Your reflection should be just as specific and honest."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Guide the student through selecting portfolio pieces and beginning the reflection.

**Activity: Portfolio Assembly**

**Step 1: Spread out all writing (3 minutes).**
Say: "Let's pull out every piece of writing you have done during this unit. Lay them all out so you can see them."

Help the student organize their work by type:
- Opinion pieces (Lessons 1-2)
- Informative pieces (Lessons 3-4)
- Narrative pieces (Lessons 5-6)
- Transition practice (Lesson 7)
- Revised drafts (Lesson 8)
- Published piece (Lesson 9)

**Step 2: Select the best pieces (4 minutes).**
Say: "Now, using the selection criteria we discussed, choose ONE opinion piece, ONE informative piece, and ONE narrative piece. These do not have to be the longest — they should be the STRONGEST."

For each piece the student selects, ask: "Why did you choose this one? What makes it your best?" This builds the skill of evaluating one's own writing.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student is indecisive, help by reading two options side by side and comparing them against the criteria. "This one has a stronger claim, but that one has better evidence. Which element matters more to you?"*

**Step 3: Begin the reflection (5 minutes).**
Say: "Now let's start your reflection. Let's talk through the questions first, and then you will write."

Have a conversation about:
- Which type was hardest? (Prompt: "Think about the lesson where you struggled most or needed the most help.")
- Which type was most enjoyable? (Prompt: "Think about which writing you would choose to do again, just for fun.")
- What skill improved? (Prompt: "Look at your earliest writing from Lesson 1 and your most recent writing. What is different?")
- What is your goal? (Prompt: "What do you want to work on next? What do you wish you were better at?")

Say: "Now write your reflection — 1-2 paragraphs. Be specific and honest."

---

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student completes the portfolio assembly, finishes the reflection, and prepares to share.

**Directions:** "Complete these tasks to finish your writing portfolio."

**Task 1: Finish your reflection (5-7 minutes).**
Write 1-2 paragraphs answering the four reflection questions. Use specific examples from your own writing.

**Task 2: Assemble the portfolio (3 minutes).**
Arrange your selected pieces in this order:
1. Portfolio cover (your name, "Grade 4 Writing Portfolio," and the date)
2. Table of contents listing each piece and its type
3. Opinion piece
4. Informative piece
5. Narrative piece
6. Published piece (if different from the above)
7. Reflection (last)

**Task 3: Prepare to share (2-3 minutes).**
Choose ONE piece from your portfolio to read aloud to your audience. Practice reading it with expression. Think about: What do you want your audience to notice? What are you most proud of in this piece?

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete all three tasks. The reflection should be 5-8 sentences (1 paragraph). The portfolio should contain at least three pieces plus the reflection. Prepare to read one piece aloud.

**Stretch:** Complete all three tasks. The reflection should be 8-12 sentences (2 paragraphs). The portfolio should contain at least four pieces plus the reflection. Include a brief "About the Author" page with a short biography (3-4 sentences in third person: "[Student name] is a fourth-grade writer who enjoys..."). Prepare to read one piece aloud and explain to the audience why you chose it.

**Challenge:** Complete the stretch tier. Additionally, write a one-paragraph "Letter to Next Year's Writer" — a letter to a future fourth grader who will take this unit. What advice would you give them? What should they expect? What strategies helped you the most?

### Interleaved Review

After completing the portfolio, answer these review questions as a final unit review:

1. **Opinion Writing:** "What is the difference between a claim and a casual opinion?" (A claim is specific, debatable, and previews reasons. A casual opinion is vague and personal.)

2. **Informative Writing:** "Name three ways to develop informative writing." (Facts, definitions, concrete details, and quotations — any three.)

3. **Narrative Writing:** "What are the two jobs of dialogue?" (Reveal character personality and advance the plot.)

4. **Transitions:** "Give one transition from each category: sequence, contrast, and example." (Answers will vary. Example: First, However, For instance.)

5. **Process:** "What is the difference between revising and editing?" (Revising improves content — ideas, organization, word choice. Editing corrects errors — spelling, grammar, punctuation.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

This lesson's exit ticket is the sharing itself. The student reads their selected piece aloud to the facilitator (or another audience member).

After the reading, ask:

1. "What type of writing is this piece?" (The student should correctly identify it as opinion, informative, or narrative.)
2. "What is one thing you are especially proud of in this piece?" (The student should name a specific craft element: a strong claim, vivid sensory details, a purposeful dialogue exchange, a smooth transition, a precise vocabulary word, etc.)
3. "What is one thing you would do differently if you wrote this piece again?" (The student should demonstrate reflective thinking — e.g., "I would add more evidence to my second reason" or "I would develop the ending more.")

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the writing type, names a specific strength, and identifies a specific area for growth.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Help the student identify one strength by pointing to a specific sentence or paragraph: "Look at this line — what did you do well here?"
- If the student cannot identify areas for growth, ask guiding questions: "Is there a paragraph that feels shorter or weaker than the others? Is there a place where you wish you had more details?"
- Reassure the student that identifying areas for growth is a sign of STRENGTH, not weakness — it shows they are thinking critically about their own work

### If Mastery Is Met
- Celebrate! The student has completed the Grade 4 Writing unit.
- Encourage the student to keep writing between units — journal entries, letters, stories, opinion pieces about topics they care about
- Preview: "You are now ready for the Unit Assessment, which will give you a chance to show everything you have learned about opinion, informative, and narrative writing."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Author Reading:** The student hosts a formal "author reading" — sitting in a special chair, introducing their piece, reading it aloud with expression, and answering questions from the audience. Model how real authors do readings at bookstores and literary festivals.
- **Writing Awards:** Create certificates or awards for the student's writing: "Best Claim," "Most Vivid Setting," "Best Use of Dialogue," "Most Improved Writer," etc. Celebrate specific achievements.
- **Letter to a Favorite Author:** The student writes a letter to a favorite book author, sharing what they learned about writing this unit and asking questions about the author's writing process. If possible, mail the letter — many authors respond to student mail.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can be completed in one session if the student has kept their writing organized throughout the unit. If drafts need to be located, reread, or organized, allow two sessions: one for portfolio assembly and reflection writing, one for sharing and celebration.

**The celebration matters:** It may be tempting to skip the celebration and move straight to the assessment. Do not skip it. The celebration serves a critical purpose: it teaches students that writing has an audience, that their work has value, and that the effort of the writing process leads to a product worth sharing. When students share their writing and receive genuine, positive responses, they develop confidence and motivation that carry forward into future writing tasks.

**Make the audience real:** The best celebrations involve a real audience. Invite a family member, a neighbor, a grandparent via video call, or a friend. If no one else is available, the facilitator serves as the audience — and a genuinely engaged, responsive, attentive audience. Ask real questions about the writing. Comment on specific things you noticed and appreciated. React authentically.

**The reflection is a metacognitive skill:** Writing about one's own thinking and learning (metacognition) is a high-level skill that develops throughout the upper elementary grades. If the student struggles to write the reflection, have the conversation FIRST (orally), then help them turn their spoken thoughts into written sentences. The oral-to-written bridge makes reflection more accessible.

**Portfolio as a growth tool:** If the student continues through the rest of Grade 4 ELA, this portfolio becomes a baseline. At the end of the year, the student can compare their Unit 3 writing to their later writing and see concrete evidence of growth. Save the portfolio.

**Praise specifics, not generalities:** When the student shares their work, resist the urge to say only "Great job!" Instead, comment on SPECIFIC craft elements: "I love how your claim previews all three reasons." "The sensory details in your setting made me feel like I was standing in that attic." "Your dialogue between the two friends sounds like a real conversation." Specific praise teaches students what they did well and encourages them to do it again.

## Answer Key / Rubric

**Portfolio Evaluation Criteria:**

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opinion Piece Selected** | A strong opinion piece with a clear claim, supported reasons with evidence, and transitions | An opinion piece is present but incomplete or underdeveloped | No opinion piece in portfolio |
| **Informative Piece Selected** | A strong informative piece with a clear introduction, grouped paragraphs, and developed content | An informative piece is present but incomplete or underdeveloped | No informative piece in portfolio |
| **Narrative Piece Selected** | A strong narrative with vivid setting, developed characters, purposeful dialogue, and satisfying ending | A narrative piece is present but incomplete or underdeveloped | No narrative piece in portfolio |
| **Published Piece** | A typed or neatly handwritten final copy with correct formatting | A final copy is present but formatting is incomplete | No published piece |
| **Reflection** | 1-2 paragraphs with specific answers to all four reflection questions; demonstrates self-awareness | Reflection is present but vague or answers fewer than three questions | No reflection |
| **Sharing** | Student reads a piece aloud with expression, identifies a strength and an area for growth | Student reads aloud but cannot articulate strengths or growth areas | Student does not share or cannot discuss their work |

**Interleaved Review (Final Unit Review):**
1. A claim is specific, debatable, and previews reasons. A casual opinion is vague and personal.
2. Any three: facts, definitions, concrete details, quotations.
3. Reveal character personality and advance the plot.
4. Answers will vary. One transition per category is correct.
5. Revising improves content (ideas, organization, word choice). Editing corrects errors (spelling, grammar, punctuation).

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Student correctly identifies the writing type (opinion, informative, or narrative).
2. Student names a specific strength (e.g., "My claim previews all three reasons" or "I used sensory details in the setting" — not just "it was good").
3. Student names a specific area for growth (e.g., "I would add more evidence" or "I would develop the ending" — not just "I would make it better").
