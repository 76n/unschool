# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 3

## Topic
Informative Writing: Introducing a Topic

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write an informative introduction that clearly introduces a topic without stating an opinion
- Group related information into separate paragraphs, each with a clear focus
- Use headings and text features to organize informative writing

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write an informative paragraph with a topic sentence, grouped facts and details, at least one definition, and a concluding sentence (Grade 3, W.3.2)
- Ability to distinguish between informative writing and opinion writing
- Ability to write complete paragraphs with topic sentences, supporting details, and concluding sentences

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils or highlighters (3-4 colors for color-coding paragraphs)
- Index cards or sticky notes (for sorting and grouping information)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Graphic organizer template (described below; can be drawn on lined paper)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish informative writing from opinion writing and activate prior knowledge about informative text structure.

**Activity: Informative or Opinion?**

Say to the student: "For the last two lessons, you have been writing OPINION pieces — trying to convince a reader. Today, we are switching gears. We are going to write INFORMATIVE pieces — trying to TEACH a reader. First, let's make sure you can tell the difference."

Read these opening sentences one at a time. The student identifies each as INFORMATIVE or OPINION.

1. "Hurricanes are the most dangerous type of weather, and the government should spend more money protecting coastal cities." (Opinion — it says what the writer THINKS should happen.)
2. "Hurricanes are powerful storms that form over warm ocean water and can produce winds of over 150 miles per hour." (Informative — it teaches facts about hurricanes without stating a preference.)
3. "Everyone should learn to swim because it is an essential life skill." (Opinion — it argues that everyone SHOULD do something.)
4. "The human heart beats approximately 100,000 times per day, pumping about 2,000 gallons of blood through the body." (Informative — it presents facts.)
5. "Recycling is the most important thing kids can do to help the planet." (Opinion — "most important" is a judgment.)

Say: "Great! The key difference: informative writing TEACHES. Opinion writing ARGUES. Today and in the next lesson, we are going to become expert informative writers. In Grade 3, you wrote informative PARAGRAPHS. In Grade 4, you are going to write informative PIECES with multiple paragraphs, headings, and organized sections."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to write a clear informative introduction, group information into focused paragraphs, and use headings.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Informative/explanatory writing:** Writing that teaches the reader about a topic using facts, definitions, details, and quotations — it does NOT try to convince the reader of anything
- **Topic sentence:** The sentence at the beginning of a paragraph that tells the reader what that paragraph will be about
- **Heading:** A title for a section of informative writing that tells the reader what that section covers — like a label on a file folder
- **Grouping:** Organizing related information together in the same paragraph or section instead of scattering it randomly throughout the piece
- **Text features:** Elements like headings, bold words, lists, or diagrams that help organize and highlight information in a text

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: The Informative Introduction**

Say: "In opinion writing, your introduction states a CLAIM. In informative writing, your introduction does something different — it introduces the TOPIC and tells the reader what they will learn. It should NOT include your personal opinion."

Write the formula where the student can see it:

**Informative Introduction Formula:**
1. **Hook** — An interesting fact, question, or description that draws the reader into the topic
2. **Topic statement** — A clear sentence that tells the reader what the piece is about
3. **Preview** — A sentence that hints at the subtopics or categories the reader will learn about

Say: "Notice the difference from opinion writing. In opinion writing, you end the introduction with a CLAIM. In informative writing, you end with a PREVIEW of what you will teach."

**Step 2: Grouping Information into Paragraphs**

Say: "In Grade 3, you wrote one paragraph of informative text. In Grade 4, you will write MULTIPLE paragraphs, and each paragraph will focus on ONE subtopic. This is called GROUPING."

Write this example on the board:

**Topic: Sea Turtles**

Bad grouping (all mixed together):
> Sea turtles live in the ocean. They eat jellyfish. Baby sea turtles are called hatchlings. Sea turtles can hold their breath for a long time. Some sea turtles are endangered. Hatchlings crawl from the nest to the ocean. Sea turtles eat seaweed too.

Good grouping (organized by subtopic):
> **Paragraph 1 — What Sea Turtles Look Like:** appearance, size, shell
> **Paragraph 2 — Where Sea Turtles Live:** ocean habitats, migration
> **Paragraph 3 — What Sea Turtles Eat:** diet, hunting methods
> **Paragraph 4 — Baby Sea Turtles:** nesting, hatching, journey to the sea

Say: "See the difference? When you GROUP related information, the reader can follow your thinking. Each paragraph is like a chapter in a mini-book — it has ONE focus."

**Step 3: Using Headings**

Say: "Headings are like signs on the highway. They tell the reader, 'This section is about THIS.' In informative writing, headings help the reader find information quickly and understand how the piece is organized."

Write heading examples:
- **Bold and on its own line:** "The Life Cycle of a Sea Turtle" (this is a heading)
- **Short and descriptive:** Good headings: "What Sea Turtles Eat," "Where Sea Turtles Live." Bad headings: "Stuff About Turtles," "More Information."

Say: "A good heading is specific. It tells the reader exactly what that section covers. 'Stuff About Turtles' tells the reader nothing. 'What Sea Turtles Eat' tells the reader exactly what to expect."

### Worked Examples

**Model Text: Informative Piece with Introduction, Grouped Paragraphs, and Headings**

Say: "Watch me write an informative piece about honeybees. I am going to show you how I plan the grouping, write the introduction, and use headings."

**Planning (think-aloud):**
Say: "First, I need to decide how to group my information. What are the main subtopics about honeybees? I know about their bodies, their jobs in the hive, how they make honey, and why they are important. That gives me four subtopics — four paragraphs."

Write the graphic organizer:

```
Topic: Honeybees

Subtopic 1: What Honeybees Look Like
  - small, fuzzy bodies
  - yellow and black stripes
  - six legs, two wings, two antennae

Subtopic 2: Jobs in the Hive
  - queen lays eggs
  - worker bees collect nectar
  - guard bees protect the hive

Subtopic 3: How Honey Is Made
  - bees collect nectar from flowers
  - nectar is stored in honeycomb
  - bees fan it with their wings to thicken it

Subtopic 4: Why Honeybees Matter
  - pollinate flowers and crops
  - one-third of food depends on pollination
  - bee populations are declining
```

Say: "Now I have a plan. Each subtopic becomes one paragraph with a heading."

Write the complete piece:

> # All About Honeybees
>
> Have you ever watched a tiny honeybee buzzing from flower to flower on a summer day? That small insect is doing something far more important than it looks. Honeybees are some of the most fascinating and hardworking creatures on Earth. In this piece, you will learn about what honeybees look like, how they live and work together, how they make honey, and why they are so important to our world.
>
> ## What Honeybees Look Like
>
> Honeybees are small insects, usually about half an inch long. They have fuzzy bodies covered in tiny hairs, which help them collect pollen from flowers. Their bodies are striped with bands of golden yellow and dark brown or black. Like all insects, honeybees have six legs, two wings, and two antennae. Their antennae help them smell flowers and communicate with other bees.
>
> ## Jobs in the Hive
>
> A honeybee hive is like a tiny city, and every bee has a job. The queen bee is the leader of the hive. Her main job is to lay eggs — she can lay up to 2,000 eggs in a single day. Worker bees, which are all female, fly out to collect nectar and pollen from flowers. They also build the honeycomb, feed the baby bees, and keep the hive clean. Guard bees stand at the entrance of the hive and protect it from intruders. Each bee knows its role and works hard every day.
>
> ## How Honey Is Made
>
> The process of making honey begins when worker bees visit flowers and suck up nectar with their long tongues. They store the nectar in a special stomach called a honey stomach. Back at the hive, they pass the nectar to other bees, who chew it and break it down. Then the bees spread the nectar into the honeycomb — a structure made of tiny wax cells. Finally, the bees fan the nectar with their wings to remove water and thicken it. When the honey is thick enough, the bees seal the cell with a wax cap. One pound of honey requires the nectar from about two million flowers.
>
> ## Why Honeybees Matter
>
> Honeybees are essential to life on Earth. When bees visit flowers to collect nectar, they carry pollen from one flower to another. This process, called pollination, is how many plants reproduce and grow fruit. In fact, about one-third of the food we eat depends on bee pollination, including apples, almonds, blueberries, and cucumbers. Sadly, honeybee populations around the world are declining due to pesticides, habitat loss, and disease. Scientists and farmers are working together to protect these incredible insects because without honeybees, our world would look very different.

Say: "Let's check my work together."

Guide the student through:
- **Introduction:** Does it have a hook (question about watching a bee), a topic statement (honeybees are fascinating), and a preview (what you will learn about)? Yes.
- **Grouping:** Is each paragraph focused on ONE subtopic? Yes — appearance, jobs, honey-making, importance.
- **Headings:** Does each heading clearly describe what the section is about? Yes.
- **Tone:** Does it TEACH without giving my opinion? Yes — I never said "I think bees are cool" or "everyone should help bees."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice planning an informative piece by grouping information into subtopics and writing an introduction.

**Activity: Plan and Introduce**

Say: "Let's plan an informative piece together. I will give you a topic, and we will figure out how to group the information and write the introduction."

**Topic: Volcanoes**

**Step 1: Brainstorm facts.**
Say: "Tell me everything you know about volcanoes. Do not worry about order — just get the facts out." Write all facts on index cards or sticky notes. If the student has limited knowledge, provide these facts to supplement:

- Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's surface
- Hot melted rock called magma rises from deep underground
- When magma reaches the surface, it is called lava
- Lava can be over 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit
- Some volcanoes are active, some are dormant, and some are extinct
- The Ring of Fire is an area around the Pacific Ocean with many volcanoes
- Volcanic eruptions can cause ash clouds, lava flows, and mudslides
- Volcanoes can create new land (like the Hawaiian Islands)
- Mount St. Helens erupted in 1980 in Washington State
- Volcanoes exist on other planets too (Olympus Mons on Mars)

**Step 2: Sort into subtopics.**
Say: "Now let's group these facts. Which facts go together?" Physically sort the cards into piles. Guide the student toward groups like:

- **What Volcanoes Are** (openings in Earth, magma/lava)
- **Types of Volcanoes** (active, dormant, extinct)
- **What Happens During an Eruption** (lava flows, ash clouds, temperature)
- **Famous Volcanoes** (Ring of Fire, Mt. St. Helens, Olympus Mons)

**Step 3: Write headings.**
Say: "Now give each group a heading — a short, specific title." Help the student write clear headings for each group.

**Step 4: Write the introduction.**
Say: "Now let's write our introduction. Remember the formula: hook, topic statement, preview."

Guide the student to write something like:

> Deep beneath the surface of the Earth, rock is so hot that it melts into a glowing liquid. When that liquid finds a way to the surface, the result is one of nature's most powerful events: a volcanic eruption. Volcanoes have shaped our planet for millions of years, creating mountains, islands, and fertile soil. This piece will explain what volcanoes are, the different types of volcanoes, what happens during an eruption, and some of the most famous volcanoes in history and beyond.

**Step 5: Check together.**
- Hook? (Yes — vivid description of melting rock.)
- Topic statement? (Yes — volcanoes have shaped our planet.)
- Preview of subtopics? (Yes — what they are, types, eruptions, famous volcanoes.)
- Any opinion? (No — purely informative.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently plans and writes an informative introduction with a grouping plan.

**Directions:** "Choose ONE of the topics below. First, brainstorm at least 8-10 facts about the topic. Then sort your facts into 3-4 subtopics and write a heading for each. Finally, write a complete informative introduction with a hook, topic statement, and preview."

**Topic Choices:**
- An animal you find fascinating (not honeybees, since that was the model)
- A sport or game and how it works
- Weather and how different types form
- A country or culture you have learned about
- A topic of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Brainstorm at least 8 facts. Sort into 3 subtopics with headings. Write an introduction with a hook, topic statement, and preview of subtopics. Introduction should be 3-5 sentences.

**Stretch:** Brainstorm at least 10 facts. Sort into 4 subtopics with headings. Write an introduction with a creative hook (surprising fact, vivid description, or question), a clear topic statement, and a preview. Write the FIRST body paragraph (one subtopic fully developed with a topic sentence and 3-4 supporting sentences). Introduction and first paragraph should total 8-12 sentences.

**Challenge:** Complete the stretch tier AND write a paragraph explaining WHY the topic is important or interesting — but do this WITHOUT stating an opinion. (For example, instead of "I think dolphins are amazing," write "Dolphins play a vital role in maintaining healthy ocean ecosystems." The student must inform, not persuade.)

### Interleaved Review

After completing the informative planning and introduction, answer these review questions:

1. **Opinion Writing Review (Lessons 1-2):** "What is the difference between a reason and evidence in opinion writing?" (A reason explains WHY you hold your claim. Evidence is the fact, example, or detail that proves your reason is true.)

2. **Reading Review (Unit 1 connection):** "What is a theme in a story, and how is it different from a topic?" (A topic is a single word or phrase like "courage." A theme is a complete message about that topic, like "Being brave does not mean you are not afraid — it means you act even when you are scared.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. What are the three parts of a good informative introduction? (Hook, topic statement, preview of subtopics.)
2. What does it mean to "group" related information? (It means putting facts about the same subtopic together in the same paragraph instead of scattering them throughout the piece.)
3. Read this sentence: "Dolphins are the best animals in the ocean, and everyone should care about protecting them." Is this informative or opinion? How could you rewrite it to be informative? (Opinion — "best" and "everyone should" are opinion language. Informative version: "Dolphins are marine mammals that play an important role in ocean ecosystems.")

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies all three parts of an informative introduction AND explains grouping AND successfully distinguishes informative from opinion writing.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the informative introduction formula (hook, topic statement, preview)
- Practice sorting facts into groups using a concrete topic (e.g., sort facts about dogs into: appearance, behavior, care, history)
- Give the student 5 sentences and have them identify which are informative and which are opinion
- Before the next lesson, have the student write one more informative introduction on a different topic

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 4 (Developing Informative Writing) next session
- Preview: "Today you learned to introduce a topic and plan how to group your information. Next time, we are going to fill in those paragraphs with facts, definitions, details, and even quotations from sources. We are going to make your informative writing RICH and DETAILED."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Text Feature Detective:** Find an informative article in a magazine, textbook, or online source. Identify the headings, bold words, and any other text features. How do these features help the reader navigate the text?
- **Topic Web:** Choose a topic the student is passionate about and create a detailed topic web (graphic organizer) with at least 5 subtopics, each with 3-4 facts. This web can be used as the foundation for a longer informative piece.
- **Heading Challenge:** The facilitator writes 3-4 short informative paragraphs (without headings). The student reads each paragraph and writes an appropriate heading. Then compare: did the heading capture the main focus of the paragraph?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can be completed in one session if the student is comfortable with multi-paragraph structure. If the student has primarily written single paragraphs, allow two sessions — one for understanding grouping and headings, one for writing the introduction and planning.

**Connection to Grade 3:** In Grade 3, students wrote single informative paragraphs with a topic sentence, grouped facts, a definition, and a concluding sentence. The new skills in Grade 4 are: (1) writing multi-paragraph informative pieces, (2) using headings to organize sections, and (3) writing an introduction that previews the structure of the whole piece. Celebrate what the student already knows before introducing what is new.

**The biggest leap — multi-paragraph organization:** Many fourth graders have never written a piece with more than one or two paragraphs. The concept of "each paragraph has ONE focus" is the foundation of all organized writing. Use the index card sorting activity to make this concrete — physically handling cards and sorting them into piles helps students internalize the concept before they try to do it mentally while writing.

**Informative vs. opinion confusion:** Students sometimes slip into opinion language in informative writing ("Honeybees are awesome!" or "Everyone should protect bees!"). When this happens, gently redirect: "That sounds like an opinion. In informative writing, we let the FACTS speak for themselves. Instead of saying bees are awesome, what FACT could you share that would make the reader think bees are awesome?" This trains students to show, not tell.

**Graphic organizer as scaffolding:** The topic web or outline used in Step 2 of Guided Practice is not busywork — it is an essential thinking tool. Students who plan with a graphic organizer before writing consistently produce better-organized informative pieces than those who skip planning. Over time, students will internalize the planning process and may not need a physical organizer, but for now, make it a required step.

**Independent Practice:** Answers will vary by topic and tier. Evaluate using these criteria:

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |

| **Brainstorming** | At least 8 facts listed (10 for stretch) | 5-7 facts listed | Fewer than 5 facts |
| **Grouping** | Facts sorted into 3-4 clear subtopics; related facts stay together | Some grouping visible but 2+ facts are in the wrong group | No grouping; facts remain random |
| **Headings** | Each group has a specific, descriptive heading | Headings are present but vague ("More stuff") | No headings |
| **Introduction — Hook** | Hook grabs reader attention (question, surprising fact, or vivid description) | Opening sentence is present but bland | No hook; jumps straight to facts |
| **Introduction — Topic Statement** | Clearly states what the piece is about without opinion | Topic is mentioned but vaguely | No topic statement |
| **Introduction — Preview** | Previews the subtopics that will follow | Partial preview (mentions some subtopics) | No preview |
| **Tone** | Purely informative; no opinion language | Mostly informative with one slip into opinion | Reads more like an opinion piece than informative |
