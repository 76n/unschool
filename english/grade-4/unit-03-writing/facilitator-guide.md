# Facilitator Guide — Grade 4 ELA, Unit 3: Writing

**Grade:** 4
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 3 — Writing
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 5 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Partially facilitated (adult introduces the lesson, models writing, and supports guided practice; student works more independently during warm-up and independent practice)
**Standards:** W.4.1, W.4.2, W.4.3, W.4.5, W.4.6

---

## Unit Overview

This unit marks a significant step forward in your student's development as a writer. In Grade 3, your student learned to write structured opinion pieces with organized reasons, informative texts with grouped facts and definitions, and narratives with dialogue and temporal words. Those were essential building blocks. Now, in Grade 4, each of those skills deepens and becomes more sophisticated.

Grade 4 opinion writers move from "I can give three reasons for my opinion" to "I can build an argument with a clear claim supported by facts, details, and evidence." They learn that an opinion piece is not just about stating what you believe — it is about convincing a reader through logical reasoning and carefully chosen support. The shift from "reasons" to "reasons supported by evidence" is the fundamental leap of fourth-grade opinion writing.

Grade 4 informative writers move from "I can write facts about a topic" to "I can organize a multi-paragraph piece with headings, precise vocabulary, and quotations from sources." They learn to structure information across paragraphs, use domain-specific language, and develop ideas with concrete details that inform rather than merely list.

Grade 4 narrative writers move from "I can write a story with dialogue" to "I can craft a narrative with vivid settings, developed characters, purposeful dialogue, controlled pacing, and natural transitions." They learn that good storytelling is not just about what happens — it is about how the writer chooses to tell it.

This unit also introduces a new skill: using technology to produce and publish writing. Students practice keyboarding and learn to type at least one page in a single sitting, preparing them for the digital writing expectations they will encounter throughout their academic careers.

**The unit is organized into four strands:**

- **Lessons 1-2: Opinion Writing** — State a clear claim, support it with facts and evidence, use linking words and phrases, write a concluding statement (W.4.1)
- **Lessons 3-4: Informative/Explanatory Writing** — Introduce a topic clearly, group information in paragraphs with headings, develop with facts, definitions, details, and quotations, use precise and domain-specific vocabulary (W.4.2)
- **Lessons 5-6: Narrative Writing** — Establish a situation and introduce characters, describe settings with sensory details, use dialogue and description to develop experiences and events, control pacing with transitional words and phrases (W.4.3)
- **Lessons 7-10: Craft and Process** — Apply transitional words across all three writing types (W.4.1, W.4.2, W.4.3), revise and edit with peer and self-editing strategies (W.4.5), use technology to type and publish writing (W.4.6), celebrate growth and build a writing portfolio (W.4.1, W.4.2, W.4.3)

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Write an opinion piece that introduces a topic, states a clear claim, provides reasons supported by facts and details, uses linking words and phrases to connect opinion and reasons, and ends with a concluding statement related to the opinion
- Write an informative/explanatory piece that introduces a topic clearly, groups related information in paragraphs and sections, develops the topic with facts, definitions, concrete details, and quotations, uses precise and domain-specific vocabulary, and provides a concluding statement or section
- Write a narrative that establishes a situation and introduces a narrator and/or characters, uses dialogue and description to develop experiences and events, uses a variety of transitional words and phrases to manage the sequence of events, and provides a conclusion that follows from the narrated experiences or events
- Apply transitional words and phrases effectively across all three writing types to connect ideas, signal shifts, and guide the reader
- Revise writing for clarity, organization, and word choice; edit for grammar, spelling, capitalization, and punctuation using peer and self-editing checklists
- Use technology, including keyboarding skills, to produce and publish a piece of writing, typing a minimum of one page in a single sitting
- Reflect on growth as a writer, select best work for a portfolio, and share writing with an audience

**Why this matters:** Writing is the visible form of thinking. A student who can construct a logical argument with evidence is learning to reason. A student who can organize information clearly with precise vocabulary is learning to teach others. A student who can craft a narrative with vivid details, purposeful dialogue, and controlled pacing is learning to move readers. And a student who can type, revise, and publish their work is learning the discipline of the writing process — the understanding that good writing is not produced in a single draft but is shaped through revision. This unit teaches all of these skills together, building a fourth grader who does not just write — but writes with purpose, structure, and craft.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Write an opinion piece with a clear opinion, at least three organized reasons with transition words (First, Also, Finally), and a conclusion (Grade 3 Unit 3 skill, W.3.1)
- [ ] Write an informative paragraph with a topic sentence, grouped facts and details, at least one definition, and a concluding sentence (Grade 3 Unit 3 skill, W.3.2)
- [ ] Write a narrative with characters, setting, dialogue with correct punctuation, events sequenced with temporal words, and a satisfying ending (Grade 3 Unit 3 skill, W.3.3)
- [ ] Conduct a short research project by identifying sources, taking notes, and paraphrasing (Grade 3 Unit 3 skill, W.3.7)
- [ ] Spell grade-appropriate words correctly and use strategies for unfamiliar words
- [ ] Write in complete paragraphs with topic sentences, supporting details, and concluding sentences
- [ ] Read grade-level text with accuracy, fluency, and comprehension

**If the student struggles with basic paragraph structure,** spend 2-3 sessions reviewing the five-part opinion structure (introduction, three reasons with transitions, conclusion) and basic informative paragraph structure (topic sentence, facts grouped logically, concluding sentence) before beginning Lesson 1. The lessons in this unit build on Grade 3 foundations.

**If the student has completed Grade 4 Units 1-2 (Reading Literature and Reading Informational Text),** they will have a strong foundation for this writing unit. Understanding theme, character analysis, text structure, and main idea directly strengthens writing. If those units have not been completed, this writing unit can still be taught, but you may need to provide additional modeling of text structure.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Lined paper and pencils** (the student will write extensively throughout this unit)
- **Colored pencils or highlighters** (for color-coding writing elements, peer editing, and revision)
- **Index cards or sticky notes** (for organizing ideas, planning paragraphs, and note-taking)
- **A writing folder or notebook** to collect all drafts and finished pieces across the unit
- **Chart paper or a large whiteboard** (optional but very helpful for modeling writing)
- **A dictionary** (print or online, for spelling during editing and vocabulary work)
- **A computer, tablet, or device with a word processor** (for Lesson 9 — typing and publishing; if no technology is available, the student can produce a polished handwritten final copy)
- **A timer** (for timed keyboarding practice in Lesson 9)
- **Printed or hand-drawn graphic organizers** (templates described in lessons; can be drawn on lined paper)

All model texts, writing samples, graphic organizers, rubrics, and editing checklists are included in the lessons. No additional published books are required, though reading published examples of opinion, informative, and narrative writing is always beneficial.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Opinion Writing: Stating a Claim | 1-2 sessions |
| 2 | Supporting Opinions with Evidence | 1-2 sessions |
| 3 | Informative Writing: Introducing a Topic | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Developing Informative Writing | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Narrative Writing: Setting and Characters | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Narrative Writing: Dialogue and Pacing | 2 sessions |
| 7 | Transitional Words and Phrases | 1-2 sessions |
| 8 | Revising and Editing | 1-2 sessions |
| 9 | Typing and Publishing | 2 sessions |
| 10 | Writing Celebration and Portfolio | 1-2 sessions |
| -- | Unit Assessment | Separate session (45-60 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**

- **Lessons 1-2 (opinion writing)** build on Grade 3 opinion writing. If the student wrote opinion pieces with confidence in Grade 3, these lessons may move briskly. The new skills are claim/thesis language and supporting reasons with specific evidence (facts, examples, details). If the student needs extra time to distinguish between a reason and supporting evidence, take two sessions per lesson.
- **Lessons 3-4 (informative writing)** introduce multi-paragraph structure and domain-specific vocabulary. If the student has not written multi-paragraph pieces before, allow extra time for planning and organizing. The shift from single-paragraph to multi-paragraph is significant.
- **Lesson 6 (dialogue and pacing)** is the most technically demanding lesson in the unit. In Grade 3, students learned basic dialogue punctuation. In Grade 4, they learn to use dialogue purposefully to develop characters and advance the plot, and they learn to control pacing. Plan for two full sessions.
- **Lesson 9 (typing and publishing)** requires access to technology. If the student has limited keyboarding experience, plan for two sessions: one focused on keyboarding practice and one on producing the final published piece. If no technology is available, substitute a polished handwritten final copy with attention to formatting, margins, and legibility.
- **Lessons 9-10 (publishing and celebration)** work best as a two-session sequence: one session for typing/producing the final piece, one session for portfolio selection and celebration.
- A typical fourth grader can sustain focused writing for 20-25 minutes before needing a break. Build in short movement breaks during longer writing sessions.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student writes "I think dogs are cool" as a claim | The student has not distinguished between a casual opinion and a formal claim/thesis statement | Teach the difference: a casual opinion is how you feel; a claim is a debatable statement you will defend with evidence. Model upgrading: "Dogs are cool" becomes "Dogs make the best family pets because they provide companionship, encourage exercise, and teach responsibility." |
| Student provides reasons but no supporting evidence ("First, dogs are loyal.") | The student states reasons without developing them with facts, examples, or details | Ask: "That is your reason. Now prove it. What fact, example, or detail shows that dogs are loyal?" Model adding evidence: "First, dogs are loyal. For example, studies show that dogs can recognize their owner's face and will wait by the door for them to come home." |
| Student writes informative text that sounds like an opinion piece ("Dolphins are amazing animals") | The student has not separated informative from opinion writing | Remind: "An informative topic sentence introduces WHAT you will teach, not what you THINK about it." Model: "Dolphins are amazing" (opinion) vs. "Dolphins are marine mammals known for their intelligence and social behavior" (informative). |
| Student writes flat, undeveloped characters ("There was a boy named Jake. He went to the store.") | The student has not learned to develop characters through description, action, thought, and dialogue | Model character introduction with layers: appearance, personality, action, or thought. "Jake tugged his baseball cap lower over his eyes and shuffled into the store, hoping nobody would notice the mud on his sneakers." |
| Student uses dialogue that does not advance the story ("Hi," said Jake. "Hi," said Mom. "How are you?" "Fine.") | The student includes dialogue to meet the requirement but does not use it purposefully | Teach the two jobs of dialogue: reveal character personality and move the story forward. Compare purposeless dialogue with purposeful: "Mom, I found something incredible in the backyard!" Jake burst through the door, his eyes wide. |
| Student uses the same transition words repeatedly ("First... Then... Then... Then...") | The student has a limited transition vocabulary | Provide a transition word bank organized by purpose (sequence, comparison, contrast, cause/effect, addition). Practice substituting: "Then" could become "After that," "Next," "A moment later," "Meanwhile," or "Before long." |
| Student resists revision ("It is already good") | The student sees revision as punishment rather than craft | Frame revision as what professional authors do. Read a quote from a favorite author about their revision process. Start with strengths: "Here are two things that are excellent. Now let's find one place where we can make it even stronger." |
| Student types very slowly and becomes frustrated | The student lacks keyboarding fluency | Reassure the student that typing speed develops with practice. Focus on accuracy first, speed second. Use the home row positioning taught in Lesson 9. Set realistic goals: even half a page typed in a sitting is a strong start. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to reinforce writing thinking:

- "What is something you have a strong opinion about? Can you state it as a claim — a statement someone might argue with?"
- "If you had to teach someone about ___ using only two paragraphs, what would you put in each paragraph?"
- "Imagine a character who is walking into a room for the first time. What do they see? What do they hear? What do they smell?"
- "What would that character SAY in this situation? Try to make the dialogue sound like something a real person would actually say."
- "You just used the word 'then' three times in a row. What are some other words that could show what happened next?"
- "Read that sentence back to me. Does it say exactly what you mean? How could you make it more precise?"
- "If a reader who knew nothing about this topic read your informative piece, would they learn what they need to know?"
- "What is the strongest piece of writing you have done this year? What makes it strong?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- After multiple sessions, the student cannot write a complete paragraph with a topic sentence, supporting details, and a concluding sentence, even with scaffolding — they may need foundational paragraph instruction before tackling the writing types in this unit
- The student can express ideas fluently in speech but produces very little on paper — this gap between oral and written expression may indicate a fine motor, processing, or attention issue; consider allowing dictation, speech-to-text, or additional time as accommodations
- The student consistently cannot distinguish between an opinion/claim and a fact, even after explicit instruction and multiple practice opportunities
- The student cannot organize ideas into logical categories or paragraphs, even when using graphic organizers — they may need more concrete sorting and categorizing activities before multi-paragraph writing is accessible
- The student shows significant frustration, avoidance, or distress around writing tasks even with scaffolding, topic choice, and encouragement
- Despite repeated instruction, the student cannot produce dialogue with any correct punctuation — they may need a return to the Grade 3 dialogue foundations

If you see these signs, consider:

- Breaking writing tasks into very small steps: plan orally or with pictures, write one sentence at a time, then combine
- Using speech-to-text tools so the student can focus on content and organization rather than the physical act of writing or typing
- Reducing length expectations and increasing structure (provide sentence starters, graphic organizers, word banks, and paragraph frames)
- Allowing the student to choose their own topics for all writing tasks — motivation and engagement increase dramatically when students write about what they care about
- Consulting with a literacy specialist if writing difficulties persist despite scaffolding and encouragement

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 4: Grammar & Vocabulary**, which teaches the language conventions that strengthen all types of writing: relative pronouns and adverbs, progressive verb tenses, modal auxiliaries, frequently confused words, and using context clues and Greek/Latin affixes to determine word meanings.

The writing skills from this unit carry forward throughout the rest of Grade 4 and beyond. Students will continue to write opinions with increasingly sophisticated evidence, informative texts with more complex organizational structures, and narratives with deeper character development and more nuanced craft. The typing skills from Lesson 9 become essential as students are increasingly expected to produce written work digitally. The revision and editing habits from Lesson 8 become part of every writing assignment.

Between units, encourage daily writing practice. Even 10-15 minutes of journal writing, a persuasive letter about something the student cares about, an informative paragraph about something they learned, or a short story keeps writing skills sharp. Read published writing together and discuss craft: "What did this author do to convince the reader?" "How did this author organize the information?" "Why did this character's dialogue sound so real?" The more students read as writers, the better they write.
