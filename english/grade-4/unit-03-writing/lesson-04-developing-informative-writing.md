# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 4

## Topic
Developing Informative Writing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Develop informative paragraphs with facts, definitions, concrete details, and quotations
- Use precise and domain-specific vocabulary to make informative writing clear and authoritative
- Write a concluding section that summarizes the topic and leaves the reader with a final thought

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write an informative introduction that introduces a topic, groups related information, and previews subtopics (Lesson 3)
- Understanding of how to use headings and organize paragraphs by subtopic
- Ability to distinguish informative writing from opinion writing

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils or highlighters (4 colors — one for each type of development: facts, definitions, details, quotations)
- The informative plan/introduction from Lesson 3 (if continuing the same topic)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Index cards (optional, for vocabulary work)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the four types of informative development and activate prior knowledge.

**Activity: What Type of Support Is This?**

Say to the student: "In Lesson 3, you learned to organize informative writing with grouped paragraphs and headings. Today, we are going to learn how to make each paragraph RICH and DETAILED. There are four main ways to develop informative writing. Let me introduce them, and then let's practice."

Write the four types:
1. **Fact** — A statement that can be proven true
2. **Definition** — Explaining what a key term means
3. **Concrete detail** — A specific piece of information that helps the reader picture or understand the topic
4. **Quotation** — The exact words from a source or expert

Read these sentences. The student identifies which type each one is.

1. "The Statue of Liberty stands 305 feet tall from the ground to the tip of the torch." (Fact — this can be measured.)
2. "An ecosystem is a community of living things that interact with each other and their environment." (Definition — it explains what the term means.)
3. "The statue's copper skin has turned green over time because of a chemical reaction between the copper and the air, called oxidation." (Concrete detail — it describes something specific and explains why.)
4. "As President Grover Cleveland said at the dedication in 1886, 'We will not forget that liberty has here made her home.'" (Quotation — the exact words of a real person.)

Say: "Great! Today we are going to use ALL FOUR of these types to develop our informative writing. We are also going to learn how to use PRECISE vocabulary — the exact right words — to make our writing sound knowledgeable and clear."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to develop informative paragraphs with multiple types of support, use domain-specific vocabulary, and write a strong conclusion.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Develop:** To build on an idea by adding supporting information — facts, definitions, details, and quotations that make the idea clear and complete
- **Domain-specific vocabulary:** Words that belong to a particular subject area — words an expert in that field would use (e.g., "photosynthesis" in science, "peninsula" in geography, "tempo" in music)
- **Precise language:** Choosing the exact right word instead of a vague one — "sprinted" instead of "went fast," "crimson" instead of "red"
- **Quotation:** The exact words spoken or written by someone else, placed inside quotation marks and attributed to the speaker or source
- **Concluding section:** The final paragraph of an informative piece that summarizes the main points and leaves the reader with a lasting thought

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Developing a Paragraph with Multiple Types of Support**

Say: "In Grade 3, you developed informative paragraphs with facts and definitions. In Grade 4, we are adding concrete details and quotations, and we are going to use MULTIPLE types in every paragraph. Watch how this works."

Write a weak paragraph and a developed paragraph side by side:

**Weak paragraph (facts only, no development):**
> Octopuses live in the ocean. They have eight arms. They can change color. They are smart.

**Developed paragraph (facts + definitions + details + precise vocabulary):**
> Octopuses are marine invertebrates, which means they are ocean animals without a backbone. They have eight flexible arms lined with powerful suction cups that can grip prey, open jars, and even unscrew lids. One of their most remarkable abilities is camouflage — the ability to change the color and texture of their skin in less than one second. An octopus can transform from bright red to sandy brown to match the ocean floor, helping it hide from predators and sneak up on prey. Marine biologist Dr. Jennifer Mather, who has studied octopuses for over 30 years, has observed them using tools, solving puzzles, and escaping from sealed containers. "They are one of the most intelligent animals in the sea," she explains.

Say: "What is different? Both paragraphs are about octopuses. But the second one DEVELOPS the ideas. Let me show you what I used."

Use four colors to highlight:
- **Facts** (color 1): "eight flexible arms," "change color and texture in less than one second"
- **Definition** (color 2): "marine invertebrates, which means ocean animals without a backbone," "camouflage — the ability to change the color and texture of their skin"
- **Concrete details** (color 3): "suction cups that can grip prey, open jars, and even unscrew lids," "transform from bright red to sandy brown"
- **Quotation** (color 4): Dr. Jennifer Mather's quote

**Step 2: Domain-Specific Vocabulary**

Say: "Notice the words I used in that paragraph: 'marine invertebrates,' 'camouflage,' 'suction cups,' 'predators,' 'prey.' These are DOMAIN-SPECIFIC words — words that belong to the subject of marine biology. Using these words tells the reader, 'This writer really knows the topic.'"

Write a chart:

**Vague Language vs. Precise/Domain-Specific Language:**

| Vague | Precise/Domain-Specific |
|---|---|
| Octopuses change color | Octopuses use camouflage to alter their skin pigment |
| They have things on their arms | Their arms are lined with powerful suction cups |
| They are ocean animals without bones | They are marine invertebrates |
| They eat other sea creatures | They prey on crabs, shrimp, and small fish |

Say: "You do not need to know every scientific word. But when you write about a topic, try to learn and use 3-5 domain-specific words. This makes your writing sound knowledgeable and teaches your reader new vocabulary."

**Step 3: Writing a Concluding Section**

Say: "Every informative piece needs a strong ending. The concluding section does two things: it summarizes what the reader learned, and it leaves them with a lasting thought or connection."

Write the formula:

**Informative Conclusion Formula:**
1. **Summary sentence** — Briefly restate the main topic and key points (do NOT repeat the introduction word-for-word)
2. **Lasting thought** — A sentence that connects the topic to the reader's world, poses a question for further thought, or explains why the topic matters

### Worked Examples

**Model Text: Complete Informative Piece (continuation of octopus topic)**

Say: "Let me show you a complete informative piece. Watch how every paragraph is developed with multiple types of support, and notice the conclusion."

> # The Remarkable Octopus
>
> Imagine an animal that can squeeze through a hole the size of a quarter, change its appearance in the blink of an eye, and solve puzzles that stump other animals. This creature is not from a science fiction movie — it is the octopus. Octopuses are among the most fascinating animals in the ocean, and scientists are still discovering new things about their bodies, their brains, and their behavior.
>
> ## A Body Built for Survival
>
> Octopuses are marine invertebrates, which means they are ocean-dwelling animals without a backbone or skeleton. Because they have no bones, octopuses can squeeze their bodies through incredibly small openings — any gap large enough for their hard beak (the only rigid part of their body) is large enough for the whole animal to pass through. They have eight flexible arms, each lined with hundreds of suction cups that can taste, grip, and manipulate objects. A large Pacific octopus, for instance, has over 2,000 suction cups, and each one can lift about 30 pounds.
>
> ## Masters of Disguise
>
> Perhaps the octopus's most famous ability is camouflage. Camouflage is the ability to change appearance to blend in with the surroundings. Octopuses can change both the color and the texture of their skin in less than one second. Tiny cells in their skin called chromatophores contain pigments that can expand or contract to create different colors. An octopus resting on a sandy ocean floor might turn pale and smooth, while one hiding among coral might become bumpy and bright red. "It is like watching a living, breathing special effect," says marine biologist Dr. Roger Hanlon of the Marine Biological Laboratory.
>
> ## Surprising Intelligence
>
> Scientists have discovered that octopuses are among the most intelligent invertebrates on Earth. In laboratory experiments, octopuses have learned to open screw-top jars to reach food inside, navigate complex mazes, and even recognize individual human faces. Their brains contain about 500 million neurons — more than many mammals. Remarkably, about two-thirds of those neurons are located in their arms, which means each arm can taste, touch, and make decisions somewhat independently from the central brain.
>
> ## Conclusion
>
> From their boneless, squeezable bodies to their color-changing skin and problem-solving brains, octopuses are truly one of nature's most extraordinary creations. Scientists estimate that there are about 300 known species of octopus, and new species are still being discovered in the deep ocean. The next time you see an octopus in an aquarium or a nature documentary, take a closer look — you might just be watching one of the smartest animals on the planet figuring out its next move.

Say: "Let's look at the conclusion. The summary sentence restates the main points — bodies, color-changing, brains. The lasting thought connects to the reader: 'The next time you see an octopus, take a closer look.' It makes the reader want to keep learning."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice developing an informative paragraph with multiple types of support and precise vocabulary.

**Activity: Develop a Paragraph Together**

Say: "Let's develop a single informative paragraph together. I will give you a topic sentence and some raw information. Your job is to help me turn it into a fully developed paragraph."

**Topic sentence:** "The Amazon Rainforest is home to an incredible variety of animal life."

**Raw information (not yet organized or developed):**
- Over 400 species of mammals
- Over 1,300 species of birds
- About 10% of all species on Earth live there
- Jaguars, sloths, pink river dolphins, poison dart frogs
- "Biodiversity" means the variety of living things in an area
- Many species have not been discovered yet
- Dr. Thomas Lovejoy called the Amazon "the greatest expression of life on Earth"

**Step 1: Identify what we have.**
Say: "Let's sort this information. Which are facts? Definitions? Details? Quotations?"

- Facts: 400+ mammals, 1,300+ birds, 10% of species
- Definition: biodiversity
- Details: jaguars, sloths, dolphins, frogs; many undiscovered species
- Quotation: Dr. Lovejoy's quote

**Step 2: Write the paragraph together.**
Guide the student to compose something like:

> The Amazon Rainforest is home to an incredible variety of animal life. Scientists call this variety biodiversity, which refers to the number of different species living in one area. The Amazon's biodiversity is staggering: it is home to over 400 species of mammals, more than 1,300 species of birds, and about 10% of all known species on Earth. Visitors to the rainforest might spot a jaguar prowling through the undergrowth, a three-toed sloth hanging motionless from a branch, a pink river dolphin gliding through muddy waters, or a tiny poison dart frog glowing bright blue on the forest floor. Renowned biologist Dr. Thomas Lovejoy, who spent decades studying the Amazon, called it "the greatest expression of life on Earth." Even today, scientists believe that thousands of species in the Amazon have not yet been discovered or named.

**Step 3: Check and label.**
Use four colors to identify the fact, definition, concrete details, and quotation.

Say: "See how one paragraph can contain all four types of development? That is what makes informative writing RICH."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently develops informative body paragraphs and a conclusion.

**Directions:** "Using the informative plan and introduction you created in Lesson 3 (or choosing a new topic), write TWO fully developed body paragraphs and a concluding section. Each body paragraph must include at least THREE of the four types of development (fact, definition, concrete detail, quotation). Use at least three domain-specific vocabulary words across your paragraphs."

**If starting a new topic, choose ONE:**
- The water cycle and how it works
- How the human digestive system processes food
- The history and rules of a sport you enjoy
- Life in ancient Egypt, ancient Greece, or another civilization

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write two body paragraphs with headings. Each paragraph should include a topic sentence and at least three types of development (out of four: fact, definition, detail, quotation). Write a concluding section with a summary and a lasting thought. Use at least two domain-specific vocabulary words. Total: 15-25 sentences.

**Stretch:** Write three body paragraphs with headings plus a concluding section. Each paragraph should include all four types of development. Use at least four domain-specific vocabulary words. Include at least one sentence that uses a definition embedded with "which means," "which is," or a dash. Total: 20-35 sentences.

**Challenge:** Complete the stretch tier. Then write a one-paragraph reflection comparing informative writing to opinion writing. What is similar? What is different? Which type of writing do you find easier, and why? (This reflection CAN include opinions, since it is a reflection, not an informative piece.)

### Interleaved Review

After completing the informative writing, answer these review questions:

1. **Opinion Writing Review (Lessons 1-2):** "Write a claim about a topic of your choice. Make sure it is specific, debatable, and previews at least two reasons." (Answers will vary. Example: "Schools should offer cooking classes because learning to cook promotes healthy eating and teaches life skills.")

2. **Reading Review (Unit 2 connection):** "What are text features, and how do they help readers?" (Text features are elements like headings, bold words, captions, and diagrams that help readers navigate the text, find information quickly, and understand key concepts.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to read this informative paragraph and identify one fact, one definition, one concrete detail, and the domain-specific vocabulary:

> The Great Wall of China is one of the most impressive structures ever built by humans. Construction began over 2,000 years ago during the Qin Dynasty to protect China from invasions. The wall stretches approximately 13,000 miles across mountains, deserts, and plains. A dynasty is a series of rulers from the same family who govern a country for a long period of time. The wall is so wide at the top that five horses could ride side by side along it. Much of the wall was built with rammed earth, which means workers packed layers of soil and gravel into wooden frames to create solid walls.

1. Name one fact. ("Construction began over 2,000 years ago" or "the wall stretches approximately 13,000 miles.")
2. Name one definition. ("A dynasty is a series of rulers from the same family" or "rammed earth, which means workers packed layers of soil and gravel into wooden frames.")
3. Name one concrete detail. ("The wall is so wide at the top that five horses could ride side by side.")
4. Name two domain-specific vocabulary words. (Dynasty, Qin Dynasty, rammed earth, invasions.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies at least one fact, one definition, one concrete detail, and one domain-specific word.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the four types of development chart and review each type with fresh examples
- Give the student a simple topic sentence and four supporting sentences — have them label which type of development each one is
- Practice writing one definition sentence using the "which means" or "which is" frame
- Before the next lesson, have the student read one page of a nonfiction book and highlight facts, definitions, and details in different colors

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 5 (Narrative Writing: Setting and Characters) next session
- Preview: "You have now written opinion pieces and informative pieces. Next, we are entering the world of NARRATIVE writing — stories! You will learn to create vivid settings and real, interesting characters that readers care about."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Vocabulary Collector:** The student reads a short nonfiction article and collects 5 domain-specific vocabulary words. For each word, they write the word, a definition, and a sentence using it correctly. This builds the habit of learning subject-specific language.
- **Quotation Hunt:** Find a quotation from an expert, author, or historical figure that relates to a topic the student is interested in. Write a short informative paragraph that incorporates the quotation naturally.
- **Before and After:** The student takes a weak, undeveloped paragraph (just a list of facts) and rewrites it as a fully developed paragraph with definitions, concrete details, and precise vocabulary. Compare the two versions side by side.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson is content-heavy. If the student needs extra time to master the four types of development, split into two sessions: Direct Instruction and Guided Practice in session one, Independent Practice in session two. The independent practice can also be extended into a longer writing project.

**Connection to Grade 3:** In Grade 3, students developed informative writing with facts and definitions. The new skills in Grade 4 are: (1) adding concrete details that go beyond basic facts, (2) incorporating quotations from sources or experts, (3) using precise and domain-specific vocabulary, and (4) writing multi-paragraph pieces with a concluding section. Build on what the student knows — they already understand facts and definitions; they are adding depth and variety.

**Quotations at the fourth-grade level:** Students do not need to cite formal sources with page numbers or bibliographic entries. They should attribute quotations to a person or source: "According to..." or "Dr. Smith explains..." The goal is to learn that quotations come from somewhere and must be credited, not to master MLA or APA format.

**Domain-specific vocabulary — how much is enough?** At the fourth-grade level, using 3-5 domain-specific words in a multi-paragraph informative piece is excellent. The student should define unfamiliar terms for the reader (using "which means" or "which is" or a dash). The goal is not to overwhelm the reader with jargon but to use the right words when they add precision and authority.

**Model your own process:** When writing the model text, think aloud about your word choices: "I could say 'the frog is colorful,' but 'glowing bright blue' is more precise and helps the reader picture it. I could say 'ocean animals,' but 'marine invertebrates' is the domain-specific term, and I will define it for my reader."

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Topic Sentences** | Each body paragraph has a clear topic sentence | Topic sentences are present but vague | Missing topic sentences |
| **Types of Development** | At least 3 of 4 types used per paragraph (on-level) or all 4 (stretch) | Two types used per paragraph | Only one type; paragraphs are undeveloped |
| **Domain-Specific Vocabulary** | At least 2-3 domain-specific words used and defined for the reader | One domain-specific word used | No domain-specific vocabulary |
| **Precise Language** | Word choices are specific and vivid; avoids vague language | Mostly precise with a few vague spots | Language is consistently vague or generic |
| **Conclusion** | Summary restates key points (not word-for-word from intro); lasting thought connects to reader | Conclusion is present but weak or repetitive | No conclusion |
| **Headings** | Each paragraph has a clear, specific heading | Headings are present but vague | No headings |
