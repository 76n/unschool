# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Opinion Writing: Stating a Claim

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between a casual opinion and a formal claim/thesis statement
- Write a clear claim statement that introduces a topic and states a debatable position
- Organize an opinion introduction that hooks the reader and leads to the claim

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write an opinion piece with a clear opinion, three organized reasons with transition words, and a conclusion (Grade 3, W.3.1)
- Understanding of the difference between a fact and an opinion
- Ability to write complete paragraphs with topic sentences, supporting details, and concluding sentences

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils or highlighters (3 colors)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling (optional but recommended)
- Index cards or sticky notes (for sorting activity)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about opinion writing from Grade 3 and introduce the concept of a "claim."

**Activity: Opinion Upgrade**

Say to the student: "Last year in Grade 3, you learned to write opinion pieces with an introduction, three reasons, and a conclusion. You are already an opinion writer! Today we are going to upgrade your opinion writing to make it even more powerful. But first, let me test your memory."

Read these statements one at a time. Have the student sort each one as FACT or OPINION.

1. "The Sahara Desert is the largest hot desert in the world." (Fact — this can be measured and verified.)
2. "Summer is the best season for kids." (Opinion — people can disagree.)
3. "Students should be allowed to use calculators on every math test." (Opinion — this is debatable.)
4. "There are fifty states in the United States." (Fact — this can be counted and confirmed.)
5. "Every family should own a pet." (Opinion — not everyone would agree.)

Say: "Great. Now here is a question: which of those opinions sounds most like something you would read in a newspaper editorial or a persuasive essay? Number 3 and number 5, right? Those are not just opinions — they are CLAIMS. Today, we are going to learn the difference."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the difference between a casual opinion and a formal claim; model writing a strong claim statement with an engaging introduction.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Opinion:** What you think, believe, or feel about something — reasonable people can disagree
- **Claim:** A specific, debatable statement that you will defend with reasons and evidence in your writing — it is your main argument
- **Thesis statement:** Another name for a claim; the sentence in your introduction that tells the reader exactly what you will argue
- **Debatable:** Something that reasonable people could disagree about — if everyone already agrees, it is not a good claim
- **Introduction:** The opening paragraph of your opinion piece — it hooks the reader, introduces the topic, and states your claim

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Casual Opinion vs. Formal Claim**

Say: "In Grade 3, you wrote opinions like 'I think pizza is the best food.' That is a perfectly fine opinion. But in Grade 4, we are going to make our opinions STRONGER by turning them into CLAIMS."

Write these two columns where the student can see them:

| Casual Opinion | Formal Claim |
|---|---|
| I like dogs. | Dogs make the best family pets because they provide companionship, encourage exercise, and teach responsibility. |
| School lunch is bad. | Schools should serve healthier lunches to help students focus better in class. |
| Reading is boring. | Students who read for 20 minutes every day perform better on tests and develop stronger vocabularies. |

Say: "Look at the left column. These are casual opinions — they tell you what someone FEELS, but they do not give you anything to argue about. Now look at the right column. These are CLAIMS. What do you notice is different?"

Guide the student to observe:
- Claims are specific, not vague
- Claims include a reason or direction ("because..." or "to help...")
- Claims are debatable — someone could argue the opposite
- Claims do not start with "I think" or "I like" — they make a bold statement

Say: "Here is the big rule: A CLAIM is a debatable statement that tells the reader WHAT you believe and hints at WHY. It is the most important sentence in your whole opinion piece because everything else will support it."

**Step 2: The "Can Someone Argue the Opposite?" Test**

Say: "To check if something is a good claim, ask yourself: Could a reasonable person argue the opposite? Watch."

Test these together:
- "Chocolate ice cream is delicious." Can someone argue the opposite? (Yes, but it is a matter of taste, not something you can support with evidence. This is too vague for a good claim.)
- "Schools should add an extra 30 minutes of recess every day." Can someone argue the opposite? (Yes! Someone might say students need more academic time. This IS a good claim because you can support it with evidence.)
- "Water is wet." Can someone argue the opposite? (Not really. This is a fact, not a claim.)

**Step 3: Writing a Strong Introduction**

Say: "Now that you know what a claim is, let me show you how to write an introduction that LEADS to your claim. A good introduction does three things: it hooks the reader, it introduces the topic, and it states your claim."

Write the formula where the student can see it:

**Opinion Introduction Formula:**
1. **Hook** — An interesting sentence that grabs the reader's attention (a question, a surprising fact, or a bold statement)
2. **Background** — 1-2 sentences that introduce the topic and give context
3. **Claim** — Your thesis statement — what you will argue and defend

### Worked Examples

**Model Text 1: Strong Introduction with a Claim**

Say: "Watch me write an introduction. I am going to think out loud so you can hear my process."

Write this in front of the student, narrating each part:

**Topic: Should schools require students to learn a musical instrument?**

Say: "First, I need a hook — something that grabs the reader's attention."
Write: **"What if there were one activity that could make you smarter, more disciplined, and happier — all at the same time?"**
Say: "That is my hook. It is a question that makes the reader curious."

Say: "Now I need some background to introduce the topic."
Write: **"Many schools across the country are cutting music programs to save money. But research shows that learning to play a musical instrument has powerful benefits for young people."**
Say: "Now the reader knows what I am writing about — music programs in schools."

Say: "Finally, my claim — the most important sentence."
Write: **"Every school should require students to learn a musical instrument because it improves brain development, builds self-discipline, and creates a lifelong skill."**
Say: "That is my claim. It tells the reader WHAT I believe and hints at my three reasons: brain development, self-discipline, and a lifelong skill."

Read the complete introduction aloud:

> What if there were one activity that could make you smarter, more disciplined, and happier — all at the same time? Many schools across the country are cutting music programs to save money. But research shows that learning to play a musical instrument has powerful benefits for young people. Every school should require students to learn a musical instrument because it improves brain development, builds self-discipline, and creates a lifelong skill.

Say: "Let's color-code this. Use one color for the hook, one for the background, and one for the claim." Have the student highlight or underline each part.

**Model Text 2: Weak Introduction vs. Strong Introduction (Comparison)**

Say: "Now let me show you a WEAK introduction so you can see the difference."

Write or read aloud:

> I think kids should play instruments. Instruments are cool. I play the piano and it is fun. In this essay I will tell you why.

Say: "What is wrong with this introduction?"

Guide the student to notice:
- "I think" makes it sound casual, not formal
- "Instruments are cool" is vague — it does not tell the reader anything specific
- "I play the piano and it is fun" is a personal detail, not a hook or background
- "In this essay I will tell you why" is a weak lead-in — it tells the reader what you WILL do instead of actually doing it
- There is no real claim with reasons previewed

Say: "See how the strong introduction hooks the reader, gives context, and states a specific claim? That is what we are aiming for in Grade 4."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice identifying claims and writing a strong introduction together.

**Activity Part 1: Claim or Not a Claim? (3 minutes)**

Say: "Let's practice. I am going to read some statements. Tell me: is it a CLAIM or is it NOT a claim? If it is not, tell me why."

1. "I love summer." (Not a claim — too casual and vague, no direction for argument.)
2. "Schools should start one hour later to help students get enough sleep." (Claim — specific, debatable, hints at reasons.)
3. "Pizza has cheese on it." (Not a claim — this is a fact, not debatable.)
4. "Kids who play team sports develop better social skills than kids who do not." (Claim — specific, debatable, can be supported with evidence.)
5. "Homework is dumb." (Not a claim — too casual and vague. Upgrade: "Teachers should assign less homework on weekends so students have time for family activities and rest.")

**Activity Part 2: Build an Introduction Together (7-9 minutes)**

Say: "Now let's build an introduction together. Here is our topic."

**Topic: Should kids be allowed to have cell phones?**

**Step 1: Decide on a position.**
Ask: "What do you think — should kids your age be allowed to have cell phones? Pick a side." (Let the student choose. Either side works.)

**Step 2: Write a claim.**
Say: "Now let's turn your opinion into a claim. Remember: a claim is specific, debatable, and hints at your reasons."

If the student says "yes," help them write something like: "Children in fourth and fifth grade should be allowed to have cell phones because phones help them stay safe, learn responsibility, and access educational tools."

If the student says "no," help them write something like: "Children under twelve should not have cell phones because phones distract from schoolwork, reduce face-to-face social time, and can expose kids to unsafe content."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student writes something vague like "Kids should have phones because they are cool," ask: "That is a start. But WHY are they useful for kids specifically? Can you give me three specific reasons? Let's put those in your claim."*

**Step 3: Add a hook.**
Say: "Now let's write a hook. You could ask a question, share a surprising fact, or make a bold statement."

Help the student compose a hook. Example: "Imagine being lost at the mall with no way to reach your parents." OR "Nearly 40% of kids between ages 8 and 12 now own a smartphone."

**Step 4: Add background.**
Say: "Now we need 1-2 sentences of background to introduce the topic before we get to our claim."

Help the student write background. Example: "The debate about whether kids should have cell phones is one that parents and teachers discuss all the time. Some adults worry about screen time, while others see phones as a safety tool."

**Step 5: Put it together and read aloud.**
Have the student read their complete introduction. Together, check:
- Does the hook grab the reader?
- Does the background introduce the topic?
- Does the claim state a specific, debatable position?
- Does the claim hint at the reasons that will follow?

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently writes a claim and introduction for an opinion piece.

**Directions:** "Choose ONE of the topics below. First, write a CLAIM — a specific, debatable statement with reasons previewed. Then write a complete introduction with a hook, background, and your claim."

**Topic Choices:**
- Should students be required to wear school uniforms?
- Should kids have a say in what is served for dinner at home?
- Is it better to read books on paper or on an electronic device?
- A topic of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a claim that states your opinion and includes at least two reasons previewed. Write an introduction with a hook, 1-2 sentences of background, and your claim. The introduction should be 4-6 sentences total.

**Stretch:** Write a claim that previews three specific reasons. Write an introduction with a creative hook (question, surprising fact, or vivid scenario), 2-3 sentences of background that show you understand both sides of the issue, and your claim. The introduction should be 5-8 sentences.

**Challenge:** Write TWO introductions for the SAME topic — one arguing FOR and one arguing AGAINST. Each introduction should have a hook, background, and a strong claim. Compare how the same topic can be argued from two different perspectives.

### Interleaved Review

After completing the introduction, answer these review questions:

1. **Reading Review (Unit 1 connection):** "What is the difference between a THEME and a TOPIC? Give an example of each." (A topic is a single word or phrase like "friendship." A theme is a complete message about the topic, like "True friends support each other even when things are difficult.")

2. **Writing Review (Grade 3 connection):** "Name the five parts of an opinion piece you learned in Grade 3." (Introduction, Reason 1, Reason 2, Reason 3, Conclusion.)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. What is the difference between a casual opinion and a claim? (A casual opinion is vague and just says how you feel. A claim is a specific, debatable statement that you will defend with reasons and evidence.)
2. What are the three parts of a good opinion introduction? (A hook, background information, and a claim/thesis statement.)
3. Read this statement: "Everyone should exercise for at least 30 minutes every day because it strengthens your heart, improves your mood, and helps you sleep better." Is this a claim? Why? (Yes — it is specific, debatable, and previews three reasons.)

**Mastery = Student correctly explains the difference between an opinion and a claim AND identifies all three parts of an introduction.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the casual opinion vs. formal claim chart from Direct Instruction
- Practice the "Can Someone Argue the Opposite?" test with 3-4 additional statements
- Give the student a claim frame to complete: "___ should/should not ___ because ___, ___, and ___."
- Before the next lesson, have the student write two claims on topics they care about

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 2 (Supporting Opinions with Evidence) next session
- Preview: "Today you learned to write a powerful claim. Next time, we are going to learn how to SUPPORT that claim with evidence — facts, examples, and details that PROVE your reasons are strong."

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Claim Detective:** Find an opinion or editorial in a newspaper, magazine, or online article (age-appropriate). Can the student identify the writer's claim? Does the introduction have a hook and background? Discuss what makes it effective or ineffective.
- **Claim Challenge:** The facilitator and student each pick the same topic but argue opposite sides. Each person writes a claim. Compare: whose claim is more specific? More debatable? More compelling?
- **Claim Collection:** Over the next few days, the student collects 3-5 claims they hear in everyday life (on TV, in conversations, in books). Write each one down and evaluate: Is it a strong claim? How could it be improved?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can be completed in one session for students with strong Grade 3 opinion writing foundations. If the student needs more time to grasp the distinction between a casual opinion and a formal claim, split into two sessions: Direct Instruction and Guided Practice in session one, Independent Practice and Extension in session two.

**Connection to Grade 3:** In Grade 3, students wrote opinion introductions that stated their topic and opinion. The new skills in Grade 4 are: (1) upgrading from a casual opinion to a formal claim/thesis statement, (2) previewing reasons in the claim, and (3) crafting an introduction with a hook and background before the claim. Make these distinctions explicit. Do not dismiss Grade 3 skills — build on them.

**The word "thesis":** Some students may encounter the word "thesis statement" in other contexts. For Grade 4, "claim" is the more accessible and accurate term. You may introduce "thesis statement" as a synonym, but focus on "claim" as the primary vocabulary.

**Common difficulty — vague claims:** The most common issue is students writing claims that are too vague ("I think reading is good"). When this happens, ask three questions: "Good for WHOM?" "Good HOW?" "Can you be more specific?" Guide the student to revise: "Students who read for at least 20 minutes every day build larger vocabularies and become better writers."

**Modeling is essential:** Write in front of the student. Let them see you draft a hook, pause, cross it out, try again, and then build toward the claim. The invisible process of writing becomes visible through think-alouds.
