# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3 Assessment

## Unit: Writing

**Assessment Type:** Summative (end-of-unit)
**Estimated Time:** 55-70 minutes (may be split across two sessions)
**Facilitation:** The facilitator reads directions aloud. The student completes written portions independently. The student may use a dictionary for spelling support during writing sections. If technology is available, the student may type Section D on a computer.
**Standards Assessed:** W.4.1, W.4.2, W.4.3, W.4.5, W.4.6

## Scoring Guide

### Section A: Opinion Writing (8 points total)

**Opinion Writing Rubric:**

| Criterion | Advanced (2 pts) | Proficient (1.5 pts) | Developing (1 pt) | Beginning (0.5 pts) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Introduction & Claim (2 pts)** | Introduction includes an engaging hook, relevant background, and a specific, debatable claim that clearly previews reasons. Reader is immediately drawn in and knows the writer's position. | Introduction includes a hook and a clear claim that previews reasons. Background may be brief. | Introduction states an opinion but the claim is vague or does not preview reasons. Hook is missing or weak. | No clear introduction or claim. Writing begins with reasons or details without establishing a position. |
| **Reasons & Evidence (2 pts)** | Three or more reasons, each supported by specific, relevant evidence (facts, examples, or details). Evidence is well-chosen and convincing. | Three reasons with at least one piece of evidence each. Evidence is relevant but may be general. | Two reasons with limited evidence, or three reasons with no evidence. | Fewer than two reasons; no evidence provided. |
| **Linking Words (2 pts)** | Four or more different linking words used correctly. Transitions connect claim to reasons, reasons to evidence, and ideas across paragraphs. Writing flows smoothly. | Three to four linking words used correctly. Most connections between ideas are smooth. | One to two linking words used, or linking words are used incorrectly. Writing is somewhat choppy. | No linking words. Ideas feel disconnected. |
| **Conclusion (2 pts)** | Conclusion restates the claim in a fresh way and leaves the reader with a compelling final thought. The ending feels strong and complete. | Conclusion restates the claim and wraps up the piece. | Conclusion is present but just repeats the introduction or says "The End." | No conclusion, or the writing simply stops. |

### Section D: Revising, Editing, and Publishing (6 points total)

**Part 1 — Revising for Word Choice and Transitions (2 points):**

The student should revise the paragraph by replacing vague words and adding transitions. A strong revision might look like:

> Our neighbor's golden retriever, Biscuit, was enormous — nearly as tall as my waist when he stood on all four paws. He had thick, caramel-brown fur that always smelled like grass and sunshine. Despite his size, Biscuit was one of the gentlest dogs I had ever met, and he loved nothing more than chasing tennis balls in the backyard. In fact, he would sprint across the yard in great bounding leaps, easily clearing the garden fence and the recycling bin. After an hour of play, however, Biscuit would finally flop down on the cool grass, his tongue hanging out, and fall fast asleep. He was, without a doubt, the best dog on the block.

**Scoring:**
- 2 points: At least three vague words replaced with precise alternatives AND at least two transitions added. The paragraph flows smoothly and the word choices are vivid.
- 1.5 points: Two to three vague words replaced and one to two transitions added. Improvement is clear.
- 1 point: One to two vague words replaced OR one transition added, but not both.
- 0.5 points: Minimal changes made; paragraph is mostly unchanged.

## Reteaching Guide

### If Section A (Opinion Writing) is below 5/8:

**Claim and introduction issues:**
- Return to Lesson 1 and review the difference between a casual opinion and a formal claim
- Practice writing claims using the frame: "___ should/should not ___ because ___, ___, and ___."
- Write several hook sentences for the same topic and choose the strongest one
- Review the three-part introduction formula: hook, background, claim

**Evidence and linking word issues:**
- Return to Lesson 2 and review the three types of evidence (fact, example, detail)
- Practice daily: give the student a reason and have them provide one piece of evidence
- Post the linking words chart and require the student to use at least three different linking words in every opinion paragraph
- Read published opinion editorials together and identify reasons, evidence, and linking words

---

### If Section B (Informative Writing) is below 5/8:

**Organization and grouping issues:**
- Return to Lesson 3 and practice the index card sorting activity — brainstorm facts, write each on a card, sort into groups, label each group with a heading
- Practice writing topic sentences for informative paragraphs: "What is this paragraph ABOUT? Say it in one sentence."
- Review the informative introduction formula: hook, topic statement, preview

**Development and vocabulary issues:**
- Return to Lesson 4 and review the four types of development (fact, definition, detail, quotation)
- Practice writing definitions using "which means" or "which is" frames
- Build domain-specific vocabulary by reading short nonfiction articles and collecting new terms
- Take an undeveloped paragraph and practice adding one fact, one definition, and one detail

---

### If Section C (Narrative Writing) is below 6/10:

Identify which rubric criteria scored lowest:

**Setting issues:**
- Return to Lesson 5 and practice the five senses chart for familiar locations
- Daily practice: describe one place in 3-4 sentences using at least three senses
- Read openings of published stories and identify sensory details

**Character issues:**
- Return to Lesson 5 and review the four methods of character introduction
- Create character profile cards for practice: name, appearance, personality, one action, one line of dialogue
- Practice introducing a family member or friend using at least two methods

**Dialogue issues:**
- Return to Lesson 6 and review the four dialogue punctuation patterns
- Practice 3-5 dialogue punctuation exercises daily until patterns become automatic
- Practice writing purposeful dialogue: give the student a situation and ask "What would the character SAY that would reveal their personality?"
- Use color-coding: highlight spoken words in one color, tags/action beats in another

**Pacing issues:**
- Return to Lesson 6 and review the pacing chart (fast vs. slow)
- Practice rewriting the same scene in fast pacing (short sentences, action) and slow pacing (long sentences, description)
- Use the movie analogy: "When does a movie use quick cuts? When does it use slow close-ups?"

**Ending issues:**
- Practice writing endings for incomplete stories — provide the beginning and middle, and the student writes the ending
- Review ending strategies: resolve the problem, show character feelings, share what was learned
- Read endings of published stories and discuss what makes them satisfying

---

### If Section D (Revising, Editing, Publishing) is below 4/6:

**Revision issues:**
- Practice the five revision strategies one at a time: spend one session on word choice upgrades, another on sentence variety, another on transitions
- Create a "Vague to Precise" reference chart the student can use during writing
- Daily practice: revise one weak paragraph per day

**Editing issues:**
- Practice daily "error hunt" exercises: one sentence or paragraph with 3-5 errors to find and fix
- Focus on the student's most common error types first (homophones, capitalization, dialogue punctuation)
- Use the self-editing checklist from Lesson 8 systematically — check one category at a time
- Read aloud as an editing strategy — many errors become audible when read aloud

**Publishing knowledge issues:**
- Review home row positioning and practice keyboarding for 10-15 minutes daily
- Review the formatting checklist from Lesson 9
- Practice setting up a document: title, author name, font, spacing

## Answer Key

### Section A — Sample Mastery-Level Response

**Topic: Fourth graders should have at least one hour of unstructured free time during the school day.**

> Have you ever noticed that your best ideas come when you are not trying to think of them — maybe while you are swinging on a swing, building with blocks, or just staring at the clouds? That is not an accident. Unstructured free time is when the brain does some of its most creative and important work. Fourth graders should have at least one hour of free time during every school day because free time reduces stress, boosts creativity, and helps students become more independent thinkers.
>
> First, free time helps students manage stress. By fourth grade, students juggle reading assignments, math problems, writing tasks, science projects, and tests — sometimes all in the same week. Without a break, this pressure builds up and can lead to anxiety, headaches, and burnout. For example, a study by the American Psychological Association found that children who have regular unstructured play time report feeling happier and less anxious than those whose schedules are completely packed. Even 30 to 60 minutes of free time during the school day can help students reset and return to their work with a calmer, clearer mind.
>
> In addition, unstructured time fuels creativity. When students are told exactly what to do every minute, they never get the chance to invent, imagine, or explore on their own. During free time, a student might sketch a comic book character, build a tower out of classroom supplies, or write a song — activities that exercise creative thinking. Specifically, research shows that children who engage in free play develop stronger problem-solving skills because they have to make their own decisions instead of following instructions.
>
> Most importantly, free time teaches independence and decision-making. When students are given an hour with no assignments and no screens, they have to decide for themselves what to do. This is a skill that many adults struggle with, and the earlier children practice it, the better. For instance, a student who chooses to spend free time reading a book they selected themselves is developing self-direction — a skill that will serve them in middle school, high school, and beyond.
>
> For all these reasons, schools should build at least one hour of unstructured free time into every school day. A student who is less stressed, more creative, and more independent is not just a happier student — they are a better learner.

This response demonstrates: an engaging hook (question), background, a specific claim with three previewed reasons, three body paragraphs with evidence (study, research, example), multiple linking words (First, For example, In addition, Specifically, Most importantly, For instance, For all these reasons), and a conclusion with a restated claim and compelling final thought.

### Section B — Sample Mastery-Level Response

**Topic: The Water Cycle**

> Every glass of water you drink has been on an incredible journey — a journey that has been repeating itself for billions of years. The water cycle is the continuous process by which water moves from the Earth's surface into the atmosphere and back again. Understanding the water cycle means understanding how rain forms, where rivers come from, and why water is one of the most important substances on our planet. This piece will explain the three main stages of the water cycle: evaporation, condensation, and precipitation.
>
> ## Evaporation: Water Rises
>
> The water cycle begins with evaporation, which is the process of liquid water turning into water vapor — an invisible gas. When the sun heats the surface of oceans, lakes, rivers, and even puddles, the water molecules gain energy and rise into the atmosphere. The warmer the temperature, the faster evaporation occurs. In fact, the sun's energy evaporates about 1.4 billion cubic meters of water from the Earth's surface every single day. Plants also contribute to evaporation through a process called transpiration, which is when water travels from a plant's roots through its stems and leaves and then evaporates into the air.
>
> ## Condensation and Precipitation: Water Returns
>
> Once water vapor rises high into the atmosphere, it cools down and undergoes condensation — the process of gas turning back into liquid. Tiny water droplets cling to dust particles and other microscopic matter in the air, forming clouds. As more and more droplets collect, the clouds grow heavier. When the droplets become too heavy to stay suspended in the air, they fall back to Earth as precipitation. Precipitation can take several forms depending on the temperature: rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Most precipitation falls directly into the oceans, but some falls on land, where it flows into rivers, soaks into the ground, or collects in lakes — eventually making its way back to the ocean to begin the cycle again.
>
> ## Why the Water Cycle Matters
>
> The water cycle is essential to all life on Earth. Without evaporation, there would be no rain. Without rain, crops could not grow, rivers would dry up, and freshwater would become scarce. Dr. Peter Gleick, a leading water scientist, explains that "the water cycle is the engine that drives weather, shapes landscapes, and sustains every ecosystem on the planet." Understanding this cycle helps scientists predict droughts, floods, and storms — and it reminds us that every drop of water is precious and shared by all living things.

This response demonstrates: a hook (every glass of water), clear topic statement, preview of subtopics, two body paragraphs with headings focused on distinct subtopics, multiple types of development (facts, definitions, concrete details, quotation), domain-specific vocabulary (evaporation, condensation, precipitation, transpiration, water vapor, microscopic), precise language, and a concluding section with a summary and lasting thought.

>
> Elijah shifted his heavy backpack onto one shoulder and spun the combination lock on locker 247. The dial was stiff, and he had to try twice before the latch clicked open. He swung the door wide — and froze.
>

A faint blue-green light pulsed from inside the jar, as if whatever was in there was breathing. Elijah leaned closer. The light was warm on his face. Inside the jar, dozens of tiny creatures — no bigger than grains of rice — drifted and swirled like living stars.
>
> "What in the world..." he whispered.
>
> He reached out and picked up the jar carefully, holding it at eye level. The creatures responded immediately, pressing against the glass nearest his fingertips as if they recognized him. A folded note was taped to the bottom of the jar. Elijah peeled it off with trembling hands and unfolded it.
>
> *These are yours now. Keep them safe. Do NOT let them get cold.*
>
> The handwriting was neat but unfamiliar. No signature.
>
> Elijah's heart was hammering. He glanced down the hallway. Empty. He looked back at the jar. The creatures pulsed brighter, almost cheerfully, like they were glad someone had finally found them.
>
> "Okay," Elijah said quietly, sliding the jar into his backpack and zipping it shut. He could feel the warmth through the fabric. "I don't know what you are. But I'll figure it out."
>
> He walked toward the exit, his backpack glowing faintly, a secret swimming against his spine. After a moment, he stopped at the front door and looked back down the long, empty hallway. Whoever had left that jar knew his locker number. Whoever it was knew his name.
>
> The question was not WHAT the creatures were. The question was WHO had put them there — and why.
