# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 2

## Topic
Supporting Opinions with Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between a reason and supporting evidence (facts, examples, details)
- Select and organize reasons logically from strongest to weakest or by category
- Use linking words and phrases (for example, because, in addition, furthermore, specifically) to connect opinions to reasons and reasons to evidence

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a clear claim/thesis statement that introduces a topic and states a debatable position (Lesson 1)
- Understanding of the five-part opinion structure from Grade 3 (introduction, three reasons, conclusion)
- Ability to write complete paragraphs with topic sentences and supporting details

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils or highlighters (2 colors — one for reasons, one for evidence)
- Index cards or sticky notes (for sorting reasons)
- The introduction written during Lesson 1 (if continuing the same topic) or a new topic
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the concept of a claim from Lesson 1 and introduce the idea that reasons need evidence.

**Activity: Reason or Evidence?**

Say to the student: "Last lesson, you learned to write a powerful claim. Today we are going to learn how to SUPPORT that claim so your reader is truly convinced. But first, let's see if you can tell the difference between a REASON and EVIDENCE."

Say: "A REASON tells WHY you believe your claim. EVIDENCE is the proof — a fact, example, or detail that backs up your reason. Listen to each pair and tell me which one is the reason and which one is the evidence."

**Pair 1:**
- "Playing outside keeps kids healthy." (Reason — this is WHY outdoor play matters.)
- "A study found that children who play outside for 60 minutes a day have stronger bones and better heart health." (Evidence — this is a specific fact that proves the reason.)

**Pair 2:**
- "For example, my neighbor's dog learned to sit, stay, and shake hands in just two weeks." (Evidence — this is a specific example.)
- "Dogs are easy to train." (Reason — this is WHY dogs make good pets.)

**Pair 3:**
- "Students who eat breakfast score 15% higher on math tests." (Evidence — a specific fact.)
- "Eating breakfast helps students do better in school." (Reason — WHY breakfast matters.)

Say: "Great! A reason answers the question 'WHY do you believe your claim?' Evidence answers the question 'How do you KNOW that reason is true?' Today, we are going to build opinion pieces where every reason is backed up with solid evidence."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to find and select evidence, organize reasons logically, and use linking words to connect ideas.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Reason:** A statement that explains WHY you hold your opinion or claim — it answers "Why do you believe that?"
- **Evidence:** A fact, example, or specific detail that supports your reason — it answers "How do you know? Prove it."
- **Linking words/phrases:** Words that connect your claim to your reasons and your reasons to your evidence (because, for example, in addition, furthermore, specifically, for instance, such as, according to)
- **Logical order:** Arranging reasons in a way that makes sense — from least important to most important, or grouped by category

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Types of Evidence**

Say: "In Grade 3, you supported your opinions with reasons. Now in Grade 4, you need to support your reasons with EVIDENCE. There are three main types of evidence you can use."

Write this chart where the student can see it:

| Type of Evidence | What It Is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fact** | Something that can be proven true | "Dogs have been domesticated for over 14,000 years." |
| **Example** | A specific instance that illustrates your point | "For example, my family's dog learned to fetch in just one afternoon." |
| **Detail** | A specific piece of information that adds depth | "Golden retrievers are known for their gentle temperament, which makes them ideal for families with young children." |

Say: "The best opinion writing uses a MIX of these types. Facts are powerful because they are hard to argue with. Examples make your writing feel real and personal. Details add depth and specificity."

**Step 2: Linking Words and Phrases**

Say: "In Grade 3, you used transition words like First, Also, and Finally to move between reasons. In Grade 4, you will still use those, but you will also learn LINKING words that connect your reasons to your evidence. These are the glue that holds your argument together."

Write the linking words chart:

**Linking Words for Opinion Writing:**
- **To introduce a reason:** First, Second, Additionally, In addition, Another reason, Furthermore, Most importantly
- **To connect a reason to evidence:** because, for example, for instance, specifically, such as, according to, in fact, one example is
- **To add more evidence:** also, in addition, furthermore, moreover
- **To conclude:** For these reasons, Therefore, In conclusion, As a result, Clearly

Say: "Watch how linking words work. Without them: 'Students should have longer recess. It helps them focus. Kids get restless.' With them: 'Students should have longer recess BECAUSE it helps them focus. FOR EXAMPLE, after a 30-minute recess, kids return to class feeling refreshed and ready to learn. IN ADDITION, physical activity reduces restlessness, WHICH MEANS students can sit and concentrate for longer periods.' See how the linking words create a smooth, connected argument?"

**Step 3: Organizing Reasons Logically**

Say: "Once you have your reasons and evidence, you need to put them in a logical order. Here are two strategies."

- **Strategy 1: Least Important to Most Important.** Start with a good reason and end with your STRONGEST reason. This way, the reader finishes with your most powerful argument fresh in their mind.
- **Strategy 2: Group by Category.** If your reasons fall into natural groups (health reasons, social reasons, academic reasons), group them together.

Say: "Most of the time, Strategy 1 works great for fourth-grade opinion writing. Save your best reason for last."

### Worked Examples

**Model Text: Complete Opinion Piece with Evidence**

Say: "Watch me write a complete opinion piece. I am going to focus on supporting each reason with evidence and using linking words to connect everything."

Write the following in front of the student, narrating your thinking:

**Topic: Schools should have a garden where students grow their own food.**

Say: "I already have my claim: 'Every school should have a garden where students grow fruits and vegetables because it teaches science, promotes healthy eating, and builds teamwork.' Now I need to support each reason with evidence."

> Every school should have a garden where students grow fruits and vegetables. A school garden is not just a patch of dirt with some seeds — it is a living classroom that teaches lessons no textbook can match.
>
> First, a school garden is one of the best ways to teach science. When students plant seeds and watch them grow, they learn about the life cycle of plants, photosynthesis, and ecosystems. For example, students can observe how bees pollinate flowers, how worms enrich the soil, and how too much or too little water affects growth. These are hands-on science lessons that students remember far longer than reading about them in a book.
>
> In addition, school gardens encourage students to eat healthier foods. According to a study by the American Society for Horticultural Science, children who grow their own vegetables are more likely to eat them. Specifically, students who participated in school garden programs ate 25% more fruits and vegetables than students who did not. When a student grows a tomato from a tiny seed, they feel proud of their work and are excited to taste what they created.
>
> Most importantly, working in a garden builds teamwork and responsibility. Students must cooperate to plant, water, weed, and harvest the garden. For instance, one student might be in charge of watering while another tracks the growth of each plant in a journal. If someone forgets their job, the whole garden suffers. This teaches students that their actions affect others — a lesson that goes far beyond the garden.
>
> For all these reasons, every school should create a garden. A school garden feeds students' minds, their bodies, and their sense of community.

Say: "Now let's look at what I did." Use two colors to highlight:
- Color 1: Reasons (underline the reason sentence in each body paragraph)
- Color 2: Evidence (underline the facts, examples, and details that support each reason)

Point out the linking words: "First," "For example," "In addition," "According to," "Specifically," "Most importantly," "For instance," "For all these reasons."

Say: "Notice that I organized my reasons from good to BEST. My strongest reason — teamwork and responsibility — comes LAST, so it is the one the reader remembers."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice selecting evidence and using linking words to build a supported opinion paragraph.

**Activity: Build a Body Paragraph Together**

Say: "Let's build ONE body paragraph together. I will give you a claim and a reason. Your job is to help me find evidence and connect it with linking words."

**Claim:** "Students should be allowed to listen to music while doing homework."
**Reason:** "Music helps students concentrate."

**Step 1: Brainstorm evidence.**
Say: "Our reason is that music helps students concentrate. What evidence could we use to support this? Let's brainstorm."

Guide the student to generate evidence. Accept all ideas and write them on index cards or sticky notes. Possible evidence:
- Some studies show that calm music (like classical or lo-fi) helps people focus on repetitive tasks
- When I listen to music, I can study for longer without feeling bored or restless
- Music blocks out distracting noises like a TV in the next room or a sibling playing

**Step 2: Choose the best evidence.**
Say: "Which two or three pieces of evidence are STRONGEST? Remember: facts and specific examples are more convincing than vague statements."

Help the student select 2-3 strong pieces.

**Step 3: Write the paragraph with linking words.**
Say: "Now let's write this paragraph. Start with a transition to introduce the reason, then add the evidence using linking words."

Guide the student to write something like:

> First, music helps students concentrate on their homework. For example, when I listen to calm instrumental music, I can focus on my math problems for 30 minutes without getting distracted. In addition, research shows that certain types of music, such as classical or lo-fi beats, activate the part of the brain that controls attention. Music also blocks out background noise. Specifically, if a sibling is watching TV in the next room, music in headphones creates a quiet bubble that helps a student stay on task.

**Step 4: Check the linking words.**
Say: "Let's circle every linking word we used." (First, For example, In addition, such as, also, Specifically)

Say: "See how each linking word has a JOB? 'First' introduces the reason. 'For example' introduces a personal example. 'In addition' adds more evidence. 'Such as' gives specific types. 'Specifically' adds a concrete detail."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently writes an opinion piece (or body paragraphs) with reasons supported by evidence and linked with connecting words.

**Directions:** "Choose ONE of the topics below. Write a complete opinion piece OR write the body paragraphs (three body paragraphs, one per reason). Each reason must be supported by at least one piece of evidence. Use at least four different linking words."

**Topic Choices:**
- Should kids be allowed to stay up later on weekends?
- Is it better to play on a sports team or do individual activities (like drawing or reading)?
- Should schools replace textbooks with tablets and laptops?
- Continue from the topic and introduction you wrote in Lesson 1

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write three body paragraphs (one per reason). Each paragraph should include the reason, at least one piece of evidence (fact, example, or detail), and at least one linking word. Paragraphs should be 3-5 sentences each.

**Stretch:** Write a complete opinion piece (introduction, three body paragraphs, conclusion). Each body paragraph should include at least two pieces of evidence. Use at least six different linking words across the piece. Organize reasons from least important to most important.

**Challenge:** Write a complete opinion piece AND write a one-paragraph counterargument — acknowledge what someone who DISAGREES with you might say, and explain why your position is still stronger. Use the phrase "Some people may argue that... however..." to introduce the counterargument.

### Interleaved Review

After completing the opinion writing, answer these review questions:

1. **Reading Review (Unit 2 connection):** "What is the difference between a main idea and a supporting detail in informational text?" (The main idea is the most important point the author is making. Supporting details are the facts, examples, and explanations that back up the main idea.)

2. **Vocabulary Review (Grade 3 connection):** "What does the word 'evidence' mean?" (Evidence is proof — facts, examples, or details that show something is true or support a claim.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to read this paragraph and identify the reason, the evidence, and the linking words:

> Furthermore, playing team sports teaches kids how to work with others. According to the National Youth Sports Alliance, children who play team sports learn to communicate, share responsibility, and handle disagreements constructively. For instance, a soccer player must pass the ball to a teammate in a better position, even if they want to shoot themselves. This kind of selfless decision-making carries over into the classroom and beyond.

1. What is the reason? ("Playing team sports teaches kids how to work with others.")
2. What evidence is provided? (The statistic from the National Youth Sports Alliance AND the soccer example.)
3. Name three linking words used. (Furthermore, According to, For instance)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the reason, at least one piece of evidence, and at least two linking words.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the Direct Instruction chart showing Types of Evidence (fact, example, detail)
- Give the student a reason and three possible evidence statements — have them choose which is strongest and explain why
- Practice adding linking words to disconnected sentences: "Dogs are loyal. My dog waits by the door every day." becomes "Dogs are loyal. FOR EXAMPLE, my dog waits by the door every single day until I come home from school."
- Before the next lesson, have the student find one example of evidence in a nonfiction text and identify the linking word used

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 3 (Informative Writing: Introducing a Topic) next session
- Preview: "You have now mastered opinion writing — you can state a claim and support it with evidence. Next, we are switching gears to INFORMATIVE writing. Instead of trying to convince someone, you will be trying to TEACH someone about a topic."

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Evidence Scavenger Hunt:** Read an age-appropriate opinion editorial or persuasive article. The student highlights reasons in one color and evidence in another. How many linking words can they find?
- **Debate Prep:** The facilitator and student take opposite sides of a topic. Each person writes three reasons with evidence. Then debate! Whose evidence is more convincing?
- **Linking Words Challenge:** The student rewrites a paragraph from their opinion piece, replacing every linking word with a DIFFERENT linking word that serves the same purpose. (Example: replace "For example" with "For instance" or "One example is.")

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can be completed in one session for students who quickly grasp the distinction between reasons and evidence. If the student needs more practice, split into two sessions: Direct Instruction and Guided Practice in session one, Independent Practice with a full opinion piece in session two.

**Connection to Grade 3:** In Grade 3, students provided reasons to support their opinions. The new skill in Grade 4 is EVIDENCE — the specific facts, examples, and details that support each reason. In Grade 3, "First, dogs are loyal" was sufficient. In Grade 4, students must add: "For example, studies show that dogs can recognize their owner's voice and will wait by the door for hours." The reason stays the same; the evidence makes it convincing.

**Linking words matter more than you think:** Many students can write reasons and evidence, but the writing feels choppy because linking words are missing. Spend time explicitly teaching linking words as "the glue that holds an argument together." A posted linking words chart that the student can reference during all writing activities is invaluable.

**Evidence does not need to be from published sources:** At the fourth-grade level, evidence can include personal experiences ("For example, when my family adopted a dog..."), logical reasoning ("This means that..."), and common knowledge facts. Students do not need to cite formal sources unless they are doing research writing. The goal is to develop the habit of supporting reasons with specific, concrete information.

**Organizing reasons:** Most fourth graders benefit from the "least to most important" strategy because it gives them a clear organizational principle. If a student is advanced, introduce the concept of grouping reasons by category (e.g., health reasons, social reasons, academic reasons).

## Answer Key / Rubric

**Warm-Up — Reason or Evidence:**
- Pair 1: "Playing outside keeps kids healthy" = Reason. "A study found..." = Evidence.
- Pair 2: "For example, my neighbor's dog..." = Evidence. "Dogs are easy to train" = Reason.
- Pair 3: "Students who eat breakfast score 15% higher..." = Evidence. "Eating breakfast helps students do better in school" = Reason.

**Guided Practice:** Answers will vary. The paragraph should contain: a reason introduced with a transition word, at least two pieces of evidence, and multiple linking words connecting ideas.

**Independent Practice:** Answers will vary by topic and tier. Evaluate using these criteria:

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Reasons (per paragraph)** | Each body paragraph has a clear reason that supports the claim | Reason is present but vague or not clearly connected to the claim | No clear reason; paragraph wanders |
| **Evidence (per paragraph)** | At least one piece of specific evidence (fact, example, or detail) supports the reason | Evidence is attempted but vague or generic | No evidence; reason stands alone |
| **Linking Words** | At least four different linking words are used correctly across the piece | Two or three linking words are used, or linking words are used incorrectly | Fewer than two linking words; ideas feel disconnected |
| **Organization** | Reasons are arranged logically (least to most important or by category) | Some logical order but one reason is out of place | No clear organizational strategy |

**Interleaved Review:**
1. The main idea is the most important point the author is making. Supporting details are facts, examples, and explanations that back up the main idea.
2. Evidence is proof — facts, examples, or details that show something is true or support a claim.

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Reason: "Playing team sports teaches kids how to work with others."
2. Evidence: The statistic from the National Youth Sports Alliance (fact) and the soccer player example (specific example).
3. Linking words: Furthermore, According to, For instance. (Also accept "This kind of" as a reference phrase.)
