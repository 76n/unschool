# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Narrative Writing: Setting and Characters

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Establish a situation by describing a setting with sensory details (sight, sound, smell, touch, taste)
- Introduce characters through description, action, thought, and personality — not just by naming them
- Orient the reader by grounding them in the who, where, and when of the story from the opening paragraph

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a narrative with characters, setting, dialogue, sequential events, and a satisfying ending (Grade 3, W.3.3)
- Understanding of the five senses and how to describe experiences using sensory language
- Ability to describe characters and explain how their actions contribute to events (RL.3.3, RL.4.3)

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils (for illustrating character or setting details — optional)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Index cards (for character planning)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate sensory awareness and review the concept of setting from reading.

**Activity: Where Am I?**

Say to the student: "I am going to describe a place using my five senses. Your job is to guess where I am. Listen carefully."

Read this description aloud:

"The air smells like salt and sunscreen. I can hear waves crashing on the shore and seagulls calling overhead. The sand between my toes is warm and gritty. I squint against the bright sunlight that bounces off the blue-green water stretching out to the horizon. Somewhere nearby, someone is eating a hot dog with mustard, and the smell drifts toward me on the breeze."

Ask: "Where am I?" (A beach.)

Ask: "How did you know? What clues did I give you?" (Salt, waves, sand, sunlight, water, seagulls, hot dog — all sensory details.)

Say: "I never SAID the word 'beach.' But you could picture it perfectly because I used SENSORY DETAILS — details that appeal to your five senses. Today, we are going to learn how to use sensory details to create vivid settings AND how to introduce characters that your readers will care about."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to establish a setting with sensory details and introduce characters with depth.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Setting:** The time and place where a story happens — it includes the location, the time of day, the season, the weather, and the mood of the environment
- **Sensory details:** Descriptions that appeal to the five senses: sight, sound, smell, touch, and taste — they help the reader experience the scene
- **Character introduction:** The way a writer first presents a character to the reader — through name, appearance, personality, actions, or thoughts
- **Orient the reader:** To ground the reader in the story right away so they know WHO the story is about, WHERE it takes place, and WHEN it happens
- **Narrator:** The person telling the story — in first person, the narrator is a character in the story using "I"; in third person, the narrator is outside the story using "he/she/they"

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Creating a Vivid Setting**

Say: "In Grade 3, you learned to include a setting in your narratives. In Grade 4, we are going to make settings come ALIVE by using all five senses."

Write the Five Senses Setting Chart:

| Sense | Question to Ask | Example (Forest Setting) |
|---|---|---|
| **Sight** | What does the place look like? | Tall pine trees with shafts of golden sunlight filtering through the branches |
| **Sound** | What can be heard? | Birds singing, twigs snapping underfoot, a stream gurgling nearby |
| **Smell** | What does it smell like? | Fresh pine needles, damp earth, wildflowers |
| **Touch** | What can be felt? | Cool shade, rough bark, soft moss |
| **Taste** | What can be tasted? (Not always applicable) | The crisp, clean taste of mountain air |

Say: "You do not need to include ALL five senses in every setting description. But aim for at least THREE. The more senses you include, the more real the setting feels to the reader."

Say: "Also, your setting is not just scenery — it sets the MOOD. A dark, stormy forest feels very different from a sunny, peaceful forest. The setting tells the reader how to feel before anything even happens."

**Step 2: Introducing Characters with Depth**

Say: "In Grade 3, you might have introduced a character by writing 'There was a girl named Emma.' That tells the reader a name, but nothing else. In Grade 4, we introduce characters so the reader can PICTURE them and start to understand their personality."

Write the Four Ways to Introduce a Character:

1. **Appearance:** What they look like — but focus on details that reveal personality, not just hair color
2. **Action:** What they are DOING when we first meet them — actions reveal personality
3. **Thought/Feeling:** What they are thinking or feeling — this shows their inner world
4. **Dialogue:** What they SAY — the words a character chooses reveal who they are

Say: "You do not need all four in your first paragraph, but try to use at least two or three. The goal is for the reader to feel like they are MEETING a real person, not just reading a name."

Write the comparison:

**Flat introduction:**
> There was a boy named Marcus. He was 10 years old. He went to Lakewood Elementary.

**Vivid introduction:**
> Marcus adjusted his oversized glasses for the third time that morning and stared at the science fair sign-up sheet on the bulletin board. His heart hammered in his chest. He wanted to sign up more than anything — he had already designed an experiment about whether plants grow faster with music — but his hand refused to pick up the pen. What if his project was not good enough? What if the other kids laughed?

Say: "What do we know about Marcus from the vivid introduction? We know he wears glasses. We know he is interested in science. We know he is excited but nervous. We know he worries about what others think. And we know all of this without me TELLING you — I SHOWED you through appearance, action, thought, and feeling."

### Worked Examples

**Model Text: Opening of a Narrative with Setting and Character**

Say: "Watch me write the opening of a story. I will create a setting with sensory details and introduce a character with depth."

Write in front of the student, thinking aloud:

**Story idea: A girl discovers something unexpected in her grandmother's attic.**

Say: "First, let me establish the setting."

Write: **"The attic smelled like old books and dust, with a faint sweetness underneath — like dried flowers that had been sitting in a vase for years. Afternoon light slanted through a small, grimy window, casting long golden stripes across the wooden floor. Boxes towered in crooked stacks against every wall, their cardboard sides soft and sagging with age. Somewhere in the rafters above, a pigeon cooed."**

Say: "I used four senses: smell (old books, dust, dried flowers), sight (afternoon light, grimy window, golden stripes, sagging boxes), touch (soft cardboard, wooden floor), and sound (pigeon cooing). The reader can picture this attic."

Say: "Now let me introduce my character."

Write: **"Amara climbed the last creaky step and stood at the top of the attic stairs, brushing cobwebs from her braids. She wrinkled her nose at the dust but could not stop the grin spreading across her face. She loved old things — old coins, old maps, old stories. And Grandma Josephine's attic was the oldest, most mysterious room in the whole house. 'There is treasure up here somewhere,' Amara whispered to herself, pulling a flashlight from her back pocket. 'I just have to find it.'"**

Say: "What do we know about Amara? She has braids (appearance). She wrinkles her nose but grins (action and feeling — she is excited despite the dust). She loves old things (personality). She whispers to herself about treasure (dialogue and thought — she is adventurous and imaginative). I used THREE of the four introduction methods: appearance, action, and dialogue."

Read the complete opening aloud, then ask the student: "Can you picture the attic? Can you picture Amara? Do you want to know what she finds?" (Yes to all three is the goal.)

**Model Text 2: Weak Opening for Comparison**

Say: "Now let me show you a weak opening so you can see the difference."

> Amara went to her grandma's attic. It was dusty. She looked around. She wanted to find something cool.

Say: "What is missing?"
- No sensory details — "dusty" is one detail, and it is generic
- No vivid character introduction — we know nothing about Amara except that she is looking around
- No mood or atmosphere — the reader cannot picture the scene
- No personality — "wanted to find something cool" is vague

Say: "Same story idea, but the strong version makes you FEEL like you are there. That is the power of setting and character description."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice creating a setting and character introduction together using sensory details and character depth.

**Activity: Build an Opening Together**

Say: "Let's write the opening of a story together. I will give you the story situation, and we will build the setting and character."

**Story situation: A kid arrives at a new school on the first day.**

**Step 1: Plan the setting.**
Say: "Where does the story start — outside the school? In the hallway? At the front door? Pick a specific location." (Let the student choose.)

Say: "Now let's brainstorm sensory details for that location." Draw the five senses chart and fill it in together.

*Example for "standing outside the school front doors":*
- Sight: large brick building, glass doors reflecting sunlight, kids streaming through the entrance, a flagpole with the flag flapping in the wind
- Sound: bus engines rumbling, kids laughing and shouting, a bell ringing in the distance
- Smell: fresh-cut grass from the lawn, exhaust from the buses
- Touch: backpack straps digging into shoulders, warm September morning air
- Taste: (maybe not applicable, or: the dry, nervous taste in the back of the throat)

**Step 2: Write the setting (2-3 sentences).**
Guide the student to write something like:

> The school was bigger than any building Jaylen had ever seen — a long wall of red brick with rows of windows that glinted in the September sun. Buses growled in the parking lot, and kids poured through the front doors in a noisy river of backpacks and sneakers. The warm morning air smelled like fresh-cut grass and bus exhaust.

**Step 3: Plan the character.**
Say: "Now let's introduce our main character. What is their name? What do they look like? Most importantly, how do they FEEL about starting at a new school?"

Use index cards to plan:
- Name and one appearance detail
- One action they are doing
- One feeling or thought

**Step 4: Write the character introduction (2-3 sentences).**
Guide the student to write something like:

> Jaylen gripped the straps of his backpack so tightly his knuckles turned white. He stood frozen at the edge of the sidewalk, watching the other kids walk in like they owned the place. They all seemed to know exactly where to go. Jaylen did not even know which door to use. *Just walk in*, he told himself. *Act like you belong here.*

**Step 5: Read aloud and check.**
- Can the reader picture the school? (Yes — sensory details.)
- Can the reader picture Jaylen? (Yes — white knuckles, frozen, watching.)
- Does the reader know how Jaylen feels? (Yes — nervous, uncertain.)
- Do you want to keep reading? (Ideally yes.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently writes a story opening with a vivid setting and character introduction.

**Directions:** "Choose ONE of the story situations below. Write the opening of the story (1-2 paragraphs). Your opening must include: a setting described with at least three sensory details, and a character introduced through at least two methods (appearance, action, thought/feeling, dialogue)."

**Story Situations:**
- A kid wakes up to find that it snowed three feet overnight and school is canceled
- A girl or boy is exploring a tide pool at the beach and discovers an unusual creature
- Two siblings are left home alone for the first time during a thunderstorm
- A kid opens a birthday present and finds something completely unexpected
- A story situation of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write 1-2 paragraphs (6-10 sentences). Include at least three sensory details and introduce the main character through at least two methods. The reader should be able to picture the setting and understand the character's personality or feelings.

**Stretch:** Write 2-3 paragraphs (10-15 sentences). Include at least four sensory details across at least three different senses. Introduce the main character through at least three methods. Include one line of dialogue or internal thought. Establish a clear mood (exciting, mysterious, nervous, peaceful, etc.).

**Challenge:** Complete the stretch tier. Then write a SECOND version of the same opening, changing the mood entirely. (For example: the first version of "waking up to snow" is joyful and excited; the second version is eerie and unsettling because something about the snow is not quite right.) Compare how sensory details change when the mood changes.

### Interleaved Review

After completing the narrative opening, answer these review questions:

1. **Informative Writing Review (Lessons 3-4):** "Name the four types of development used in informative writing." (Facts, definitions, concrete details, and quotations.)

2. **Opinion Writing Review (Lessons 1-2):** "What makes a claim different from a casual opinion?" (A claim is specific, debatable, and previews reasons. A casual opinion is vague and personal, like "I like dogs.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this opening paragraph and answer the questions:

> The old wooden dock groaned under Mia's sneakers as she walked to the edge and peered into the green-black water below. The lake was perfectly still — not a ripple, not a splash, not even a dragonfly skimming the surface. The air hung heavy and warm, thick with the smell of mud and pine. Mia crouched down and dipped her fingers into the water. It was colder than she expected, almost icy. She yanked her hand back and wiped it on her shorts, frowning. Something about this lake did not feel right.

1. Name two sensory details in this paragraph and identify which sense each appeals to. (Examples: "green-black water" = sight; "dock groaned" = sound; "smell of mud and pine" = smell; "colder than she expected, almost icy" = touch; "air hung heavy and warm" = touch.)
2. How is Mia introduced — through appearance, action, thought, or feeling? (Action: walking, peering, crouching, dipping fingers, yanking hand back. Feeling: frowning, sensing something is not right.)
3. What mood does the setting create? (Mysterious, uneasy, ominous — the stillness and the "something not right" create tension.)

**Mastery = Student identifies at least two sensory details with their senses, correctly identifies at least one character introduction method, and names the mood.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Five Senses Setting Chart and practice describing a familiar room (the student's bedroom, the kitchen) using at least three senses
- Practice character introduction by describing a family member using at least two methods (appearance + action, or thought + dialogue)
- Read the opening of a favorite book together and identify the sensory details and character introduction methods used
- Before the next lesson, have the student write a 3-sentence setting description of a place they visited recently

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 6 (Narrative Writing: Dialogue and Pacing) next session
- Preview: "Today you learned to create settings and characters that feel real. Next time, we are going to bring those characters to LIFE by writing dialogue — what they say and how they say it — and we are going to learn how to control the SPEED of our stories."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Setting Swap:** Take the opening the student wrote and rewrite it with a completely different setting. How does the new setting change the mood and the character's experience? (Example: the new-school story could move from September to January, from sunny to blizzard.)
- **Character Sketch:** The student creates a detailed character profile on an index card: name, age, appearance, personality traits, biggest fear, greatest wish, and a habit or quirk. This character can be used in future stories.
- **Mentor Text Study:** Read the opening chapter of a favorite novel together. How does the author introduce the setting? How many senses are used? How is the main character introduced? What can we learn from professional writers?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can be completed in one session if the student is comfortable with descriptive writing. If the student tends to write flat, undeveloped descriptions, allow two sessions — one focused on setting (with the five senses chart and practice), one focused on character introduction.

**Connection to Grade 3:** In Grade 3, students established a situation by introducing characters and setting. The new skills in Grade 4 are: (1) using SENSORY details across multiple senses to make settings vivid, (2) introducing characters through multiple methods (appearance, action, thought, dialogue) rather than just naming them, and (3) orienting the reader immediately so they know who, where, and when. Build on what the student knows — they already create characters and settings; now they are adding depth.

**Connection to reading:** If the student has completed Unit 1 (Reading Literature), they have practiced describing characters and settings in depth (RL.4.3). Writing is the mirror of reading — the same skills used to ANALYZE characters in published stories are used to CREATE characters in their own stories.

**Show, don't tell:** This is the lesson where "show, don't tell" becomes concrete. Instead of telling the reader "Marcus was nervous" (telling), we show: "His hand refused to pick up the pen" (showing). Practice this distinction frequently. When the student writes a "telling" sentence, ask: "How could you SHOW that instead? What would the character DO or THINK if they felt that way?"

**The mood of the setting matters:** Help the student understand that setting is not just decoration — it creates the EMOTIONAL ATMOSPHERE of the story. A sunny meadow creates a different feeling than a dark basement. When planning a story, ask: "What mood do you want the reader to feel? How can your setting create that mood?"

## Answer Key / Rubric

**Warm-Up — Where Am I?:** Beach. Clues: salt, waves, sand, sunlight, water, seagulls, hot dog.

**Guided Practice:** The opening should include a school setting with at least three sensory details and a character (Jaylen or another name) introduced through at least two methods. See the model in the Guided Practice section.

**Independent Practice:** Answers will vary by story situation and tier. Evaluate using these criteria:

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Setting — Sensory Details** | At least three sensory details from at least two different senses | One or two sensory details, or all from the same sense | No sensory details; setting is named but not described |
| **Setting — Mood** | The setting creates a clear mood (excited, mysterious, peaceful, tense, etc.) | Some atmosphere but inconsistent | No mood established |
| **Character — Introduction Methods** | At least two methods used (appearance, action, thought/feeling, dialogue) | One method used (e.g., name and appearance only) | Character is named but not described or developed |
| **Character — Personality/Feeling** | Reader can infer the character's personality or feelings from the introduction | Personality or feelings are told ("She was happy") rather than shown | No personality or feelings conveyed |
| **Orientation** | Reader immediately knows who, where, and when | Two of three elements are clear | Reader is disoriented; unclear who, where, or when |

**Interleaved Review:**
1. Facts, definitions, concrete details, and quotations.
2. A claim is specific, debatable, and previews reasons. A casual opinion is vague and personal.

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Any two: "green-black water" (sight), "dock groaned" (sound), "smell of mud and pine" (smell), "colder than she expected, almost icy" (touch), "air hung heavy and warm" (touch). Student must identify the sense for each.
2. Action (walking, peering, crouching, dipping, yanking, wiping) and feeling (frowning, sensing something is not right).
3. Mysterious, uneasy, ominous, or tense. (Accept any reasonable answer that reflects the unsettling quality of the stillness and Mia's discomfort.)
