# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 9

## Topic
Typing and Publishing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Demonstrate correct keyboarding posture and home row finger placement
- Type a minimum of one page of writing in a single sitting
- Format and publish a polished piece of writing using a word processor or neat handwriting

## Prerequisites
- A revised and edited draft from Lesson 8 (or a polished piece from Lessons 1-7)
- Basic familiarity with a computer, tablet, or word processing tool (helpful but not required)
- Understanding of the revision and editing process (Lesson 8)

## Materials Needed
- A computer, tablet, or device with a word processor (Google Docs, Microsoft Word, Pages, or similar)
- If no technology is available: lined paper, a ruler, and colored pencils for a polished handwritten copy
- The student's revised and edited draft from Lesson 8
- A timer (for keyboarding practice)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for displaying typing tips
- A printed keyboard diagram (optional but helpful for home row instruction)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build excitement about publishing and introduce the idea that writers produce finished products for an audience.

**Activity: What Does "Published" Mean?**

Say to the student: "When you hear the word 'published,' what do you think of?" (Let the student respond. They may say books, magazines, websites, etc.)

Say: "Published just means your writing is in a FINAL form and SHARED with an audience. A published novel in a bookstore is one kind of published writing. But so is a typed essay printed and hung on the refrigerator. Or a story posted on a class blog. Or a neatly written letter sent to Grandma. When you publish, you are saying: 'This is my best work, and I want someone to read it.'"

Say: "Today you are going to take the draft you revised and edited in Lesson 8 and turn it into a PUBLISHED piece. You are going to learn keyboarding skills and type your writing on a computer. By the end of today, you will have a finished, polished piece of writing that you can be proud of."

Ask: "Why do you think it is important to learn to type?" (Let the student share ideas.)

Say: "In school and in life, you will be asked to produce written work on a computer. Reports, essays, emails, applications — typing is a skill you will use for the rest of your life. The standard for Grade 4 is to be able to type at least one page in a single sitting. Let's work toward that today."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach keyboarding fundamentals, word processing basics, and formatting for a published piece.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Keyboarding:** The skill of typing on a keyboard using correct finger placement and technique
- **Home row:** The middle row of letter keys on a keyboard (A, S, D, F, G, H, J, K, L) where your fingers rest when not actively typing
- **Word processor:** A computer program used to type, format, and print written documents (such as Google Docs, Microsoft Word, or Pages)
- **Formatting:** How a document looks — including font size, spacing, margins, headings, bold text, and indentation
- **Publishing:** Producing a final, polished version of writing to share with an audience

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Keyboarding Fundamentals**

Say: "Before we type your piece, let's learn the basics of keyboarding. Good typing starts with correct hand position."

Demonstrate (or draw on the board) the home row:

```
Left Hand:               Right Hand:
Pinky  → A               J  ← Index finger
Ring   → S               K  ← Middle finger
Middle → D               L  ← Ring finger
Index  → F               ;  ← Pinky
        (thumb on space bar)
```

Say: "Your fingers should rest lightly on these keys. This is called the HOME ROW. Every time you finish typing a word, your fingers should return to the home row, just like a runner returns to home base."

Key rules:
1. **Sit up straight.** Feet flat on the floor. Screen at eye level. Wrists should float above the keyboard, not rest on the desk.
2. **Use all your fingers.** Each finger is responsible for specific keys. Your left index finger types F, R, T, G, V, B. Your right index finger types J, U, Y, H, N, M. Over time, this becomes automatic.
3. **Do not look at the keyboard.** This is the hardest part for beginners. Try to look at the screen, not your hands. It is okay to glance down at first, but the goal is to type by feel.
4. **Accuracy first, speed second.** It is better to type slowly and correctly than fast and full of errors.

Say: "If you have never typed before, do not worry. Typing is a skill that improves with practice, just like handwriting. Today, we are going to focus on getting your piece typed — even if it takes some time."

**Step 2: Word Processing Basics**

Say: "Now let me show you the basics of a word processor." (Demonstrate on the actual device, or describe the steps if working from a printed reference.)

Essential skills for today:
- **Opening a new document:** Start a blank page
- **Typing text:** Just type — words will wrap to the next line automatically (no need to press Enter at the end of each line)
- **Enter/Return key:** Press only to start a NEW paragraph
- **Backspace/Delete:** Erase mistakes
- **Shift key:** Hold Shift and press a letter to type a capital letter
- **Tab key:** Indent the first line of a paragraph (optional — some formats use a blank line between paragraphs instead)
- **Save:** Save your work frequently (Ctrl+S on Windows, Cmd+S on Mac, or the auto-save feature in Google Docs)

**Step 3: Formatting a Published Piece**

Say: "A published piece should look clean, organized, and professional. Here are the formatting guidelines."

Write the formatting checklist:

```
PUBLISHING FORMAT CHECKLIST
[ ] Title centered at the top of the page, in bold or slightly larger font
[ ] Author name below the title
[ ] Font: a readable font like Times New Roman, Arial, or Calibri
[ ] Font size: 12 point for body text; 14-16 point for the title
[ ] Spacing: double-spaced (or 1.5 spacing) for readability
[ ] Margins: standard (about 1 inch on all sides)
[ ] Paragraphs: indented OR separated by a blank line
[ ] Headings: bold, on their own line (for informative pieces)
[ ] No spelling, grammar, or punctuation errors (this is your FINAL version)
```

Say: "If you are publishing by hand instead of on a computer, the same rules apply — neat handwriting, a clear title, consistent spacing, and a clean page with no scratch-outs. Use a ruler to draw lines if needed."

### Worked Examples

**Model: What a Published Piece Looks Like**

Say: "Here is what a published opinion piece might look like when it is finished."

Show (or describe) a formatted example:

```
                    Why Every School Needs a Garden
                         by [Student Name]

     What if there were a classroom with no walls, no desks,
and no textbooks — but where students learned science, math,
and teamwork every single day? That classroom exists, and it is
called a school garden. Every school should create a garden
where students grow fruits and vegetables because it teaches
science, promotes healthy eating, and builds teamwork.

     First, a school garden is one of the best ways to teach
science. When students plant seeds and watch them grow, they
learn about the life cycle of plants, photosynthesis, and
ecosystems. For example, students can observe how bees
pollinate flowers and how too much or too little water affects
growth. These are hands-on science lessons that students
remember far longer than reading about them in a book.

     [continues...]
```

Say: "Notice the centered title, the author name, the indented paragraphs, and the clean formatting. This looks like a real published piece. THAT is what you are creating today."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice keyboarding with a warm-up exercise, then begin typing the student's published piece together.

**Activity Part 1: Keyboarding Warm-Up (5 minutes)**

Say: "Before you type your whole piece, let's warm up your fingers. I am going to give you some short sentences to type. Focus on correct finger placement and accuracy, not speed."

Have the student type these practice sentences (read them aloud if the student cannot see them):

1. The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog. (Uses every letter in the alphabet.)
2. Jack and Jill went up the hill to fetch a pail of water.
3. She sells seashells by the seashore.
4. A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.

Say: "Good. Now let's check your work. Did you capitalize the first word of each sentence? Did you include the period at the end? Any spelling errors? Fix them now."

Time the student on the fourth sentence. Say: "Let's see how long this takes. Remember — accuracy first."

**Activity Part 2: Begin Typing the Published Piece (5-7 minutes)**

Say: "Now let's start typing your revised and edited piece. First, set up your document."

Guide the student through:
1. Open a new document
2. Type the title (centered, bold or larger font)
3. Type the author name
4. Set the font to 12 point, double-spaced
5. Begin typing the first paragraph

Say: "I will sit with you while you type the first paragraph. Take your time. Look at your draft and type one sentence at a time. When you finish a sentence, check it against your handwritten draft. Then move to the next sentence."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student is very slow, do not rush them. Encourage: "You are building a new skill. Every professional typist started exactly where you are right now. Speed comes with practice."*

*If no technology is available:* Guide the student to create a polished handwritten copy. Use lined paper, write the title centered at the top, indent each paragraph, and use the student's best handwriting. The focus shifts from keyboarding to presentation and neatness.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently types (or neatly handwrites) their complete published piece.

**Directions:** "Continue typing your piece. Your goal is to type at least one full page (approximately 250 words, or about 20-25 sentences). If your piece is shorter than one page, add details to develop your ideas further. Use the formatting checklist to make sure your published piece looks polished."

Say: "Set a timer for 12 minutes. Type as much as you can. If you finish before the timer goes off, proofread your typed version against your handwritten draft to catch any new errors."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Type at least one full page (approximately 250 words). Use correct formatting: title, author name, readable font, double-spacing. Proofread the typed version for any new typos introduced during typing.

**Stretch:** Type the complete piece (one page or more). Add formatting enhancements: bold headings (for informative writing), indented paragraphs, or italics for emphasis where appropriate. After finishing, type a one-sentence dedication at the end: "This piece is dedicated to..." or an author's note: "About the Author: [Student name] is a fourth-grade student who enjoys..."

**Challenge:** Complete the stretch tier. Then type a SECOND piece from this unit (a different writing type). If your first piece was opinion, type a narrative or informative piece. Aim to produce two full typed pages across both pieces. Compare: which type of writing was easier to type? Why?

### Interleaved Review

After completing (or making significant progress on) the typing, answer these review questions orally:

1. **Revision Review (Lesson 8):** "What is the difference between revising and editing?" (Revising improves the content — ideas, organization, word choice, sentence variety. Editing corrects errors — spelling, grammar, capitalization, punctuation.)

2. **Writing Review (All lessons):** "Name the three types of writing you have learned in this unit and one key feature of each." (Opinion: states a claim supported by reasons and evidence. Informative: teaches about a topic with facts, definitions, and details. Narrative: tells a story with characters, setting, dialogue, and events.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Evaluate the student's published piece (even if incomplete) against these criteria:

1. Is the title formatted correctly (centered, bold or larger)? (Yes/No)
2. Is the font readable and the spacing consistent? (Yes/No)
3. Did the student type at least half a page? (Yes/No — if not, check for keyboarding difficulties and plan additional practice time.)
4. Does the typed version match the revised draft (no new errors introduced)? (Check 3-4 sentences.)

Additionally, ask the student:

5. "What are the home row keys for your left hand?" (A, S, D, F)
6. "Why is it important to save your work frequently?" (So you do not lose your writing if the computer crashes, loses power, or the program closes unexpectedly.)

**Mastery = Student produced at least half a page of typed text with correct formatting AND can identify the home row keys.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student struggles with typing speed, schedule 10-15 minutes of daily keyboarding practice using a free online typing program (e.g., TypingClub, Typing.com, or Dance Mat Typing by the BBC)
- If the student is frustrated, reassure them that typing fluency develops over weeks and months, not in a single session
- Allow the student to type only the introduction and first body paragraph today; complete the rest in the next session
- If no technology is available and the student struggles with handwriting neatness, focus on one paragraph at a time with careful attention to letter formation and spacing

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 10 (Writing Celebration and Portfolio) next session
- If the student did not finish typing, allow time at the start of the next session to complete and print the piece
- Preview: "You have typed and published your writing. Next session is our CELEBRATION. You will choose your best work from this entire unit, put together a writing portfolio, and share your writing with an audience."

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Daily Typing Practice:** Set a goal of 10-15 minutes of keyboarding practice per day for the next two weeks using a free online typing program. Track progress: how many words per minute can the student type? The average fourth grader types about 10-15 words per minute; the goal is steady improvement, not a specific speed.
- **Design Your Page:** If using a word processor, the student can experiment with formatting: add a border, change the font color for the title, insert a relevant clipart image, or create a decorative header. This introduces basic digital design skills.
- **Create a Cover Page:** The student designs a cover page for their published piece — like a book cover. Include the title, the author's name, and an illustration. This makes the published piece feel like a real book.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson benefits from two sessions. Session one: keyboarding instruction, warm-up practice, and beginning to type the piece. Session two: finishing the typed piece, formatting, proofreading, and printing. If the student is an experienced typist, one session may be sufficient.

**Technology flexibility:** The standard (W.4.6) requires students to "use technology, including the Internet, to produce and publish writing." If a computer or tablet is available, use it. If not, a polished handwritten final copy with careful attention to formatting (title, spacing, margins, neatness) meets the spirit of the standard. The keyboarding practice can be added when technology becomes available.

**Typing expectations for Grade 4:** The standard says "demonstrate sufficient command of keyboarding skills to type a minimum of one page in a single sitting." For most fourth graders, especially those new to typing, one page (approximately 250 words) may take 20-30 minutes. This is normal. Do not expect touch-typing fluency at this stage — correct posture, home row awareness, and building comfort with the keyboard are the goals.

**Ergonomics matter:** Correct posture prevents strain and builds good habits. The student should sit with feet flat on the floor, back straight, elbows at about 90 degrees, wrists floating (not resting on the desk or keyboard), and eyes on the screen. A chair that is too high or too low, or a screen at the wrong angle, can make typing uncomfortable and frustrating.

**Celebrate the published product:** When the piece is typed and printed (or neatly handwritten), treat it as a real accomplishment. Display it prominently. The student has taken an idea from brainstorming to drafting to revising to editing to publishing — the complete writing process. That is a significant achievement.

**Common challenges with typing at this age:**
- Hunting and pecking (using one or two fingers instead of all ten) — gently redirect to home row position
- Looking at the keyboard instead of the screen — this is normal; improvement comes with practice
- Frustration with speed — reassure that every expert typist was once a beginner
- Introducing new errors while typing (spelling mistakes, missing words) — emphasize proofreading the typed version against the handwritten draft

## Answer Key / Rubric

**Warm-Up:** Answers will vary. The student should recognize that "published" means writing is in a final form and shared with an audience.

**Guided Practice — Keyboarding Warm-Up:** Evaluate typed sentences for:
- Correct capitalization at the start of each sentence
- Correct punctuation at the end of each sentence
- Correct spelling
- Reasonable typing speed (accuracy is more important than speed at this stage)

**Independent Practice:** Evaluate the published piece using these criteria:

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Length** | At least one full page (approximately 250 words) typed in a single sitting | Half a page to one page | Less than half a page |
| **Title and Author** | Title is centered and formatted (bold or larger font); author name is present | Title is present but not formatted | No title |
| **Font and Spacing** | Readable font (12 pt), consistent spacing (double or 1.5) | Font is readable but spacing is inconsistent | Difficult to read; inconsistent formatting throughout |
| **Paragraph Formatting** | Paragraphs are indented or separated by blank lines; structure is clear | Some paragraphs are formatted correctly, others are not | No paragraph breaks; text is one continuous block |
| **Accuracy** | Typed version matches the revised draft; no new errors introduced | 1-3 new typos introduced during typing | Many new errors; typed version is less accurate than handwritten draft |
| **Content Quality** | The content reflects the revisions from Lesson 8; writing is polished and complete | Content mostly matches the revised draft | Content does not reflect revisions; appears to be an unedited first draft |

**Interleaved Review:**
1. Revising improves content (ideas, organization, word choice). Editing corrects errors (spelling, grammar, capitalization, punctuation).
2. Opinion: states a claim supported by reasons and evidence. Informative: teaches about a topic with facts, definitions, and details. Narrative: tells a story with characters, setting, dialogue, and events.

**Exit Ticket:**
1-4. Yes/No evaluation of the published piece (see criteria above).
5. Home row keys for left hand: A, S, D, F.
6. Saving frequently prevents losing work if the computer crashes, loses power, or the program closes.
