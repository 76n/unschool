# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 6

## Topic
Narrative Writing: Dialogue and Pacing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write dialogue that develops characters and advances the plot (not empty or purposeless dialogue)
- Punctuate dialogue correctly, including quotation marks, commas, end punctuation, and dialogue tags
- Control pacing by alternating between fast action, dialogue, and slow descriptive moments

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write narrative dialogue with correct punctuation (quotation marks, commas, dialogue tags) from Grade 3 (W.3.3)
- Ability to establish a setting and introduce characters with sensory details and depth (Lesson 5)
- Understanding of narrative structure (beginning, middle, end)

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils (one color for spoken words, one for dialogue tags, one for action/description)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Index cards (for dialogue practice)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review dialogue punctuation from Grade 3 and introduce the idea that dialogue should do work in a story.

**Activity: Purposeful or Pointless?**

Say to the student: "In Grade 3, you learned to write dialogue with quotation marks and tags. Today we are going to take your dialogue skills to the next level. But first, let me show you two conversations. Tell me which one is PURPOSEFUL and which one is POINTLESS."

**Conversation A:**
> "Hi," said Maya.
> "Hi," said Liam.
> "How are you?" asked Maya.
> "Fine," said Liam.
> "That's good," said Maya.

**Conversation B:**
> "Liam, I found something in the woods behind the school," Maya whispered, glancing over her shoulder to make sure no one else was listening.
> Liam leaned closer. "What kind of something?"
> "A door," Maya said. "A wooden door, standing in the middle of the clearing. No walls, no building. Just a door."
> Liam stared at her. "You're making that up."
> "I'm not." Maya's voice was steady. "And Liam? It was open."

Ask: "Which conversation is purposeful? Why?" (Conversation B. It reveals character personality, creates mystery, advances the plot, and makes the reader want to know more.)

Ask: "What is wrong with Conversation A?" (It does not reveal anything about the characters. It does not advance the story. It is filler — it could be removed without losing anything.)

Say: "In Grade 4, every line of dialogue in your stories should do at least ONE of two jobs: reveal something about the character OR move the story forward. If it does not do either, cut it."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach purposeful dialogue, review and extend dialogue punctuation, and introduce pacing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Dialogue:** The spoken words of characters in a story, placed inside quotation marks
- **Dialogue tag:** The phrase that tells the reader who is speaking and how (said, whispered, shouted, asked, replied, muttered, exclaimed)
- **Pacing:** The speed at which a story moves — fast pacing uses short sentences, action, and dialogue; slow pacing uses description and detail
- **Action beat:** A sentence describing what a character does, placed before or after dialogue instead of a dialogue tag — it shows who is speaking through action
- **Characterization through dialogue:** Revealing what a character is like through the words they choose and how they speak

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Review Dialogue Punctuation Rules**

Say: "Let's review the rules for punctuating dialogue. You learned these in Grade 3, but we are going to make sure they are solid before we add new skills."

Write the four dialogue patterns:

**Pattern 1: Tag after the dialogue**
> "I found a door in the woods," Maya whispered.
- Spoken words inside quotation marks
- Comma BEFORE the closing quotation mark (if the tag follows)
- Lowercase for the tag ("whispered," not "Whispered")
- Period at the end

**Pattern 2: Tag before the dialogue**
> Liam asked, "What kind of door?"
- Comma after the tag
- Capital letter starts the spoken words
- End punctuation INSIDE the quotation marks

**Pattern 3: Question or exclamation with tag after**
> "Are you serious?" Liam asked.
> "I'm completely serious!" Maya exclaimed.
- Question mark or exclamation point replaces the comma
- Still inside the quotation marks
- Tag is still lowercase

**Pattern 4: Dialogue with action beat (no tag)**
> Liam crossed his arms. "You're making that up."
- No comma needed — the action beat is a separate sentence
- The action beat replaces the tag; it shows who is speaking through action

Say: "Pattern 4 is new for Grade 4. An ACTION BEAT is when you describe what a character DOES instead of using 'said.' Watch: instead of writing '"I don't believe you," Liam said,' you can write 'Liam crossed his arms. "I don't believe you."' The action shows who is speaking AND reveals how Liam feels — skeptical."

**Step 2: Two Jobs of Dialogue**

Say: "Remember: every line of dialogue must do at least one of two jobs."

Write on the board:

**Job 1: Reveal Character**
- What a character says shows their personality
- A brave character speaks differently from a timid character
- Word choice, tone, and sentence length all reveal character

**Job 2: Advance the Plot**
- Dialogue can share new information
- Dialogue can create conflict or tension
- Dialogue can show a turning point or decision

Say: "Let me show you examples of each."

**Dialogue that reveals character:**
> "Relax, everybody. I've got this under control." (This character is confident, maybe overconfident.)
> "Are you sure this is safe? Maybe we should wait for an adult." (This character is cautious and anxious.)
> "Last one to the top is a rotten egg!" (This character is playful and competitive.)

**Dialogue that advances the plot:**
> "The bridge is out. We'll have to find another way across." (New information that creates a problem.)
> "I'm not going with you. I'm done." (A decision that changes the direction of the story.)
> "Wait — did you hear that?" (Builds tension and moves toward a new event.)

**Step 3: Controlling Pacing**

Say: "Pacing is the SPEED of your story. Some moments should feel FAST. Some should feel SLOW. A good writer controls pacing like a movie director controls the camera."

Write the pacing chart:

| Pacing | When to Use It | How to Create It |
|---|---|---|
| **Fast** | Action scenes, chases, surprises, turning points | Short sentences. Quick dialogue. Less description. Paragraph breaks. |
| **Medium** | Conversations, regular events, transitions | Mix of dialogue and description. Normal sentence length. |
| **Slow** | Setting the scene, building mood, emotional moments | Long sentences. Sensory details. Character thoughts. Description. |

Say: "Watch how the same story moment feels different with different pacing."

**Fast pacing:**
> Maya grabbed Liam's arm. "Run."
> They ran. Branches slapped their faces. The ground was uneven, and Liam stumbled, caught himself, kept going. Behind them, the sound grew louder.
> "Faster!" Maya gasped.

**Slow pacing:**
> Maya stood at the edge of the clearing, staring at the door. It was old — ancient, really — with iron hinges that had turned the color of dried blood. The wood was dark and cracked, like the bark of a tree struck by lightning. She reached out slowly, her fingertips trembling. The handle was cold. She turned it.

Say: "The fast version feels urgent and breathless. Short sentences. Quick actions. The slow version feels tense and deliberate. Long sentences. Careful description. BOTH are effective — the key is choosing the right pacing for the right moment."

### Worked Examples

**Model Text: Narrative Scene with Purposeful Dialogue and Varied Pacing**

Say: "Watch me write a scene that uses dialogue, action beats, and pacing changes."

Write in front of the student:

> The classroom was quiet except for the scratch of pencils on paper. Ms. Rivera walked between the rows, her heels clicking softly on the tile floor.
>
> Jaylen stared at his math test. Question seven. He had studied this. He KNEW this. But the numbers swam in front of his eyes, rearranging themselves like they were playing a game he did not understand.
>
> A folded piece of paper landed on his desk.
>
> Jaylen glanced sideways. Diego, sitting one row over, raised his eyebrows and nodded at the paper. Jaylen's stomach dropped. He knew what it was. Diego was trying to give him the answer.
>
> Jaylen shook his head slightly.
>
> Diego frowned and mouthed, "Just look at it."
>
> "Mr. Santos." Ms. Rivera's voice cut through the silence like a bell. She was standing right behind Diego. "Is that a note?"
>
> Diego's face went pale. "No, ma'am. I was just — "
>
> "Bring it to me, please." Her voice was calm, but her eyes were not.
>
> Diego stood up slowly, the paper crumpling in his fist. Jaylen watched him walk to the front of the room, and a wave of guilt crashed over him. He should say something. He should tell Ms. Rivera that he did not ask for the note, that he did not read it, that Diego was just trying to help. But the words stuck in his throat like peanut butter, thick and impossible to swallow.

Say: "Let's analyze what I did."

- **Slow pacing at the start:** Long sentences, sensory details (scratch of pencils, clicking heels), Jaylen's thoughts about the test. This builds the scene.
- **Fast pacing in the middle:** Short paragraph — "A folded piece of paper landed on his desk." One sentence. Sudden. Surprising.
- **Dialogue does WORK:** Diego's "Just look at it" reveals his personality (bold, willing to break rules). Ms. Rivera's "Is that a note?" advances the plot (creates consequences). Diego's "No, ma'am. I was just —" reveals his fear.
- **Action beats replace tags:** "Diego frowned and mouthed" — the action shows who is speaking and how he feels without needing "Diego said."
- **Slow pacing at the end:** Jaylen's guilt, the simile about peanut butter. The story slows down for an emotional moment.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice writing purposeful dialogue with correct punctuation and varied pacing.

**Activity Part 1: Fix the Punctuation (3 minutes)**

Say: "Let's warm up with some dialogue punctuation practice. I will give you sentences with errors. Fix them."

1. Incorrect: Maya said I found something amazing.
   Correct: Maya said, "I found something amazing."

2. Incorrect: "where are we going" asked Liam
   Correct: "Where are we going?" asked Liam.

3. Incorrect: "Be careful"! she shouted.
   Correct: "Be careful!" she shouted.

4. Incorrect: "I don't think this is a good idea." Said Marcus.
   Correct: "I don't think this is a good idea," said Marcus.

**Activity Part 2: Write a Dialogue Scene Together (7-9 minutes)**

Say: "Now let's write a short dialogue scene together. Here is the situation."

**Situation: Two friends are in a canoe on a lake. One of them sees something large moving under the water.**

**Step 1: Plan the dialogue.**
Say: "What would each character say? Let's make them different — one is calm and curious, the other is nervous and wants to go back to shore."

Brainstorm 4-6 lines of dialogue on index cards.

**Step 2: Add action beats and description.**
Say: "Now let's add action beats between some of the dialogue lines. Instead of always using 'said,' let's show what the characters are DOING."

**Step 3: Write the scene together.**
Guide the student to write something like:

> Ava leaned over the side of the canoe and squinted at the dark water. "Did you see that?"
>
> "See what?" Noah's paddle froze mid-stroke.
>
> "Something moved down there. Something big." Ava pointed at a shadow gliding just below the surface.
>
> Noah pulled his paddle out of the water and laid it across his knees. "We should go back," he said, his voice tight.
>
> "Wait." Ava held up her hand. "It's coming closer."
>
> The shadow drifted upward. Noah gripped the sides of the canoe. The water rippled, and then — a turtle. A massive snapping turtle, its shell wider than a dinner plate, surfaced and blinked at them with ancient, golden eyes.
>
> Ava laughed. "See? Nothing to worry about."
>
> "Nothing to worry about?" Noah's voice cracked. "That thing is the size of a manhole cover!"

**Step 4: Check together.**
- Is every line of dialogue punctuated correctly? (Check quotation marks, commas, tags.)
- Does the dialogue reveal character? (Ava: calm, curious. Noah: nervous, cautious.)
- Does the dialogue advance the plot? (Yes — the mystery of the shadow is revealed.)
- Is there pacing variety? (Slow description of the shadow, then fast dialogue, then the reveal.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student independently writes a narrative scene with purposeful dialogue and pacing.

**Directions:** "Choose ONE of the situations below. Write a scene (1-2 pages) that includes at least SIX lines of dialogue between two characters. Your dialogue must be correctly punctuated, must reveal character personality, and must advance the story. Include at least two action beats (instead of 'said' tags). Vary your pacing — include at least one fast moment and one slow moment."

**Story Situations:**
- Two kids are building a treehouse and discover they disagree about the design
- A child is trying to convince a parent to let them adopt a stray animal they found
- Two friends are lost in a corn maze and hear footsteps behind them
- A kid finds out they won a contest and calls their best friend to share the news
- A situation of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a scene with at least six lines of correctly punctuated dialogue between two characters. Use at least two action beats. Each character should sound different from the other. Include a brief setting description (2-3 sentences) to ground the scene.

**Stretch:** Write a scene with at least eight lines of dialogue. Use at least three action beats. Vary your dialogue tags (whispered, exclaimed, muttered, replied — not just "said"). Include at least one moment of fast pacing (short sentences, quick action) and one moment of slow pacing (description, character thought). Total: 15-25 sentences.

**Challenge:** Complete the stretch tier. Then add a THIRD character to the scene — someone who interrupts or arrives partway through. The new character should have a distinct voice that sounds different from the other two. How does the arrival of a third character change the dynamic?

### Interleaved Review

After completing the dialogue scene, answer these review questions:

1. **Narrative Review (Lesson 5):** "Name the four ways to introduce a character in a story." (Appearance, action, thought/feeling, and dialogue.)

2. **Informative Writing Review (Lessons 3-4):** "What is domain-specific vocabulary, and why is it important in informative writing?" (Words that belong to a particular subject area, like 'ecosystem' in science or 'peninsula' in geography. They make writing sound knowledgeable and teach the reader new terms.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this dialogue excerpt and answer the questions:

> Grandpa set down his newspaper and peered over his reading glasses. "You want to do WHAT?"
>
> "Build a rocket," Sonia repeated, holding up her sketch. "A real one. Well, a model one. But it actually flies."
>
> Grandpa took the sketch and studied it for a long time. A slow smile crept across his face. "You know," he said, "when I was your age, I built one too."
>
> Sonia's eyes went wide. "You did?"
>
> "Blew up the neighbor's mailbox." Grandpa chuckled. "But the flight part was magnificent."

1. What do you learn about Sonia from her dialogue? (She is ambitious, creative, and excited — she wants to build a rocket and has already drawn a plan.)
2. What do you learn about Grandpa from his dialogue? (He is supportive, has a sense of humor, and has his own history with building things — he is relatable and encouraging.)
3. Find one action beat in this passage. ("Grandpa set down his newspaper and peered over his reading glasses" or "Grandpa took the sketch and studied it for a long time" or "A slow smile crept across his face" or "Sonia's eyes went wide.")

**Mastery = Student identifies what dialogue reveals about at least one character AND correctly identifies one action beat.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the four dialogue punctuation patterns from the instruction section
- Practice writing 3-4 lines of dialogue with correct punctuation using a familiar scenario (a conversation between the student and a family member)
- Return to the "Two Jobs of Dialogue" chart and practice: for each line of dialogue, ask "What does this reveal about the character?" or "How does this move the story forward?"
- Provide a dialogue template card for reference: [quotation mark] [spoken words] [comma/question mark/exclamation point] [quotation mark] [tag or action beat]

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 7 (Transitional Words and Phrases) next session
- Preview: "You have now learned to write opinion pieces, informative pieces, and narrative scenes with settings, characters, and dialogue. Next, we are going to focus on the GLUE that holds all writing together: transitional words and phrases."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Dialogue Journal:** For the next few days, the student listens to real conversations (at home, with friends, in public) and jots down interesting lines of dialogue they overhear. How do real people talk? How could they use real speech patterns to make their characters sound authentic?
- **Character Voice Challenge:** Write the same scene twice — once from the perspective of a bold, confident character, and once from the perspective of a shy, cautious character. How does the dialogue change when the personality changes?
- **Pacing Experiment:** Take a scene the student has already written and rewrite one moment in SLOW MOTION (adding sensory details, character thoughts, and description) and another moment in FAST FORWARD (short sentences, quick actions). Compare the effect.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson benefits from two sessions. Session one: review dialogue punctuation and teach the two jobs of dialogue. Session two: teach pacing and complete independent practice. Dialogue and pacing are both demanding skills, and rushing through both in one session may result in surface-level understanding.

**Connection to Grade 3:** In Grade 3, students learned the mechanics of dialogue punctuation. The new skills in Grade 4 are: (1) making dialogue PURPOSEFUL (revealing character and advancing plot), (2) using ACTION BEATS instead of dialogue tags, and (3) controlling PACING by varying sentence length, description, and dialogue frequency. The mechanical skills from Grade 3 should be solid; if they are not, spend extra time on punctuation review before introducing the craft elements.

**Action beats are a game-changer:** Many students overuse "said" because it is the only dialogue tag they are comfortable with. Action beats solve two problems: they eliminate "said said said" and they add characterization. Encourage the student to replace at least half of their "said" tags with action beats. Example: Instead of '"I can't believe it," she said,' write 'She pressed her hands against her cheeks. "I can't believe it."'

**Pacing is intuitive once demonstrated:** Most students have an intuitive sense of pacing from movies and TV shows. Use this analogy: "In a movie, sometimes the camera moves fast — quick cuts, action, running. Sometimes the camera slows down — close-ups, music, quiet moments. Writing works the same way. Short sentences are like quick cuts. Long, descriptive sentences are like slow close-ups."

**Common dialogue errors at this level:**
- Putting the period outside the quotation marks ("I agree". should be "I agree.")
- Capitalizing the tag after dialogue ("She Said" should be "she said")
- Forgetting to start a new paragraph when the speaker changes
- Using "said" for every single tag (encourage variety: whispered, muttered, exclaimed, replied)

## Answer Key / Rubric

**Warm-Up:** Conversation B is purposeful because it reveals character personality (Maya is adventurous and observant; Liam is skeptical), introduces a mystery (the door), and makes the reader want to know more. Conversation A is pointless because it communicates no meaningful information and reveals nothing about the characters.

**Guided Practice — Fix the Punctuation:**
1. Maya said, "I found something amazing."
2. "Where are we going?" asked Liam.
3. "Be careful!" she shouted.
4. "I don't think this is a good idea," said Marcus.

**Independent Practice:** Answers will vary by situation and tier. Evaluate using these criteria:

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dialogue Punctuation** | All dialogue correctly punctuated (quotation marks, commas, end punctuation, tags) | Minor errors (1-2 punctuation mistakes) that do not impede reading | Major errors throughout; dialogue is difficult to read |
| **Purposeful Dialogue** | Dialogue reveals character personality AND advances the story | Dialogue does one job (reveals character OR advances plot) but not both | Dialogue is pointless — empty greetings or filler |
| **Action Beats** | At least 2 action beats used effectively to replace "said" tags | One action beat used | No action beats; all lines use "said" or "asked" |
| **Distinct Character Voices** | The two characters sound different — different word choices, tones, or speaking styles | Some difference between characters | Both characters sound identical |
| **Pacing** | At least one fast moment (short sentences, quick action) and one slow moment (description, thought) | Some pacing variation but not deliberate | Pacing is uniform throughout |
| **Setting** | Scene is grounded with a brief setting description | Minimal setting | No setting; dialogue floats in empty space |

**Interleaved Review:**
1. Appearance, action, thought/feeling, and dialogue.
2. Domain-specific vocabulary: words that belong to a particular subject area (e.g., "ecosystem" in science). Important because they make writing sound knowledgeable and teach the reader new terms.

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Sonia: ambitious, creative, excited, prepared (she has a sketch).
2. Grandpa: supportive, humorous, has personal experience, encouraging.
