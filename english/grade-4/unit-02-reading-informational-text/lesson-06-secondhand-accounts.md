# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 2, Lesson 6

## Topic
Secondhand Accounts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define what a secondhand account is and identify examples of secondhand accounts
- Recognize the features of secondhand accounts (third-person pronouns, factual tone, broader perspective)
- Explain how a secondhand account's perspective differs from a firsthand account's perspective

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify the features of a firsthand account (Lesson 5)
- Understanding of personal pronouns and author's perspective
- Ability to identify main idea and key details (Lesson 1)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages (encyclopedia entries, textbook passages, news reports)
- Secondhand Account Features chart
- Firsthand vs. Secondhand comparison chart (from Lesson 5, for reference)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review firsthand accounts from Lesson 5 and introduce the concept of secondhand accounts.

**Activity: Who Is Telling the Story?**

Say: "In our last lesson, we learned about firsthand accounts — descriptions written by people who were there. We read a diary entry, a soldier's letter, and an interview. What made those firsthand accounts? Who can tell me?"

Allow students to respond. Reinforce: personal pronouns (I, me, my), personal feelings, direct observations, limited to one person's experience.

Say: "But what if someone who was NOT at the event wrote about it? What if a historian read Thomas's letter from Valley Forge and then wrote a textbook chapter about what life was like for soldiers? That historian was not at Valley Forge. She did not feel the cold or see General Washington walking through camp. She learned about it from other sources — letters, documents, records."

Say: "When someone who was not there writes about an event, that is called a secondhand account. Today we are going to learn what secondhand accounts look like and how they are different from firsthand accounts."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify secondhand accounts and understand their features and perspective.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Secondhand account:** A description of an event written or told by someone who did NOT experience it directly; the writer learned about the event from other sources
- **Third-person pronouns:** Words like he, she, they, them, his, her, their — used when the author is writing about other people, not themselves
- **Objective tone:** A writing style that presents facts without showing the author's personal feelings
- **Sources:** The materials (books, interviews, documents, records) that a secondhand account writer uses to learn about the event

### Instruction Notes

Say: "A secondhand account is written by someone who researched or learned about an event but was not there to see it happen. Common types of secondhand accounts include textbook entries, encyclopedia articles, biographies, news reports, and reference books."

Write on the board:
**Features of a Secondhand Account:**
1. Uses third-person pronouns: he, she, they, them, it
2. Has an objective or factual tone — focuses on facts, not personal feelings
3. Often includes a broader perspective — the "big picture"
4. May include information from multiple sources or viewpoints
5. Typically organized in a logical, structured way

Say: "Notice how different these features are from a firsthand account. A firsthand account uses 'I' and shares personal feelings. A secondhand account uses 'he,' 'she,' or 'they' and sticks mostly to facts."

Say: "Let me show you what a secondhand account looks like."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: Encyclopedia Entry — "Valley Forge"**

> Valley Forge was the site of the Continental Army's winter camp from December 1777 to June 1778, during the American Revolutionary War. General George Washington chose this location in southeastern Pennsylvania because it was close enough to the British-occupied city of Philadelphia to monitor enemy movements but far enough away to avoid a surprise attack. Conditions at Valley Forge were extremely harsh. The army of roughly 12,000 soldiers suffered from a lack of food, clothing, and shelter. Many soldiers did not have proper shoes or coats, and they endured freezing temperatures in poorly built log huts. Disease spread rapidly through the camp. Typhus, smallpox, and pneumonia killed nearly 2,000 soldiers during the six-month encampment — far more than died in battle that winter. Despite these terrible conditions, the army did not fall apart. Washington worked to maintain morale by visiting his troops regularly. A Prussian military officer named Baron von Steuben arrived at the camp in February 1778 and trained the soldiers in European military drills and discipline. By the time the army left Valley Forge in June, it was a stronger, more organized fighting force. Historians consider Valley Forge a turning point in the war because the army that emerged was better prepared to challenge the British.

Say: "Let me check this against our secondhand account features."

Say: "Feature 1 — Does this use third-person pronouns? Yes. 'Washington chose,' 'the army suffered,' 'they endured,' 'soldiers did not have.' The author never says 'I' because the author was not there."

Say: "Feature 2 — Is the tone objective and factual? Yes. The passage presents facts: numbers (12,000 soldiers, 2,000 deaths), dates (December 1777 to June 1778), names (Washington, Baron von Steuben). It does not say 'I felt' or 'I think.'"

Say: "Feature 3 — Does it give a broader perspective? Yes. It tells us about the whole army, not just one soldier. It mentions the strategic reasons for choosing Valley Forge, the overall death toll, the training by Baron von Steuben, and the outcome for the whole army."

Say: "Feature 4 — Does it use multiple sources? We can tell the author gathered information from many places — military records, historical documents, probably soldiers' letters. The author knows things that no single soldier would have known, like the total number of deaths and the strategic reasons Washington chose that location."

Say: "Feature 5 — Is it organized logically? Yes. It follows a clear structure: why Valley Forge was chosen, the conditions, the suffering, the recovery, and the outcome."

Say: "Now, think back to Thomas's letter from Lesson 5. Thomas described his worn-out boots and his personal hope. This encyclopedia entry describes the whole army's experience with facts and numbers. Both are about Valley Forge, but they tell us very different things because the perspectives are different."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying secondhand account features with facilitator support.

Say: "Let us read another secondhand account together. This time, I want you to identify the features."

**Passage B: Textbook Entry — "The Great Oklahoma Tornado Outbreak of 2011"**

> On May 24, 2011, a series of powerful tornadoes struck central Oklahoma, causing widespread destruction and loss of life. The most devastating tornado of the day touched down near the town of El Reno at approximately 2:15 in the afternoon. It was classified as an EF-5 tornado, the most powerful category on the Enhanced Fujita Scale, with wind speeds exceeding 200 miles per hour. The tornado traveled across open farmland for approximately 75 minutes, carving a path of destruction nearly a mile wide and 63 miles long. In total, the May 2011 outbreak produced more than 15 tornadoes across the state. Emergency response teams worked through the night to rescue survivors and assess damage. Nine people were killed, and more than 180 were injured. Property damage was estimated at over 2 billion dollars. The National Weather Service had issued warnings well in advance, and meteorologists believe that early warnings saved many lives. The 2011 outbreak led to improvements in tornado prediction technology and emergency alert systems.

Say: "Work with your partner. Find evidence for each of the five secondhand account features in this passage. You have two minutes."

Allow 2 minutes. Then discuss as a class:

Say: "Feature 1 — Pronouns?"
Expected: Third-person: "it was classified," "the tornado traveled," "nine people were killed." No "I" or "my."

Say: "Feature 2 — Tone?"
Expected: Objective and factual. Includes specific data: dates (May 24, 2011), numbers (200 mph, 75 minutes, 63 miles, 15 tornadoes, 9 killed, 180 injured, 2 billion dollars), official classifications (EF-5).

Say: "Feature 3 — Broader perspective?"
Expected: Yes. Covers the entire outbreak, not just one person's experience. Mentions multiple tornadoes, total casualties, total damage, and changes in technology afterward.

Say: "Feature 4 — Multiple sources?"
Expected: The author used data from the National Weather Service, emergency response teams, and damage assessments. No single eyewitness would have all this information.

Say: "Feature 5 — Organization?"
Expected: Organized chronologically and logically: the event, the specific tornado, the damage, the response, and the outcome.

Say: "Now think about Elena's interview from Lesson 5. Elena described one tornado she personally saw — the greenish-gray sky, the roaring sound, her pounding heart. This textbook describes the entire outbreak with facts and numbers. How are the two accounts different in their focus?"

Allow students to respond. Guide them to see: Elena focused on her personal experience of one tornado; the textbook focuses on the broader event with data and facts.

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze a secondhand account.

**Passage C: News Report — "City Opens New Community Garden"**

> The city of Riverside officially opened its newest community garden last Saturday morning in a ceremony attended by Mayor Linda Chen, city council members, and more than 100 local residents. The Elm Street Community Garden, located on a vacant lot that had been unused for over a decade, features 40 raised garden beds, a tool shed, a rainwater collection system, and a small greenhouse. The project was funded by a $50,000 grant from the State Department of Agriculture and an additional $15,000 raised through community donations.
>
> According to city planner David Morales, the idea for the garden began two years ago when residents of the Elm Street neighborhood submitted a petition requesting a green space. "The community spoke up, and the city listened," Morales said at the opening ceremony. Volunteer coordinator Rosa Gutierrez noted that more than 60 residents had already signed up to tend garden plots. She added that the garden would also host free workshops on composting and organic gardening throughout the summer.
>
> The Elm Street garden is the third community garden established in Riverside in the past five years. City officials say they plan to open two more gardens in underserved neighborhoods by next year. Studies have shown that community gardens can improve neighborhood health, reduce food costs for families, and bring neighbors closer together.

**Questions:**
1. Is this a firsthand or secondhand account? Explain your reasoning with at least two pieces of evidence.
2. What pronouns does the author use? How does this differ from a firsthand account?
3. Describe the tone of this passage. Is it personal and emotional or factual and objective? Give two examples from the text.
4. How does this secondhand account provide a "bigger picture" that a single person at the ceremony might not have included?
5. The passage includes quotes from David Morales and Rosa Gutierrez. Are their quoted words firsthand or secondhand? Explain.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1–4 as written above.
**Stretch:** Answer all five questions, with a detailed answer for question 5.
**Challenge:** Answer all five questions. Then rewrite the first paragraph of the passage as if you were a resident who attended the ceremony, changing it to a firsthand account. Include personal pronouns and at least one personal feeling.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Lesson 5 review) Name three features that would be present if this were a firsthand account instead of a secondhand account.
7. (Lesson 2 review) Write a two-sentence summary of this passage.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following short passage:

> The eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD destroyed the Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum. The volcano released a massive cloud of ash and pumice that reached heights of 20 miles. Temperatures near the volcano exceeded 500 degrees. Approximately 2,000 people in Pompeii were killed, many of them buried under layers of ash. The cities remained hidden beneath volcanic debris for nearly 1,700 years until they were rediscovered by archaeologists in the 1700s.

1. Is this a firsthand or secondhand account? How do you know? (Expected: Secondhand. It uses third-person language, presents facts and data, covers the "big picture" of the event, and was clearly written long after the eruption.)
2. Name two features of a secondhand account that you can find in this passage. (Expected: Any two of — third-person pronouns, factual/objective tone, broader perspective, includes data from multiple sources, organized logically.)
3. What information does this secondhand account include that an eyewitness in Pompeii probably would NOT have known? (Expected: The total number of people killed, the height of the ash cloud, the exact temperature, the fact that the cities were rediscovered 1,700 years later.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct, with specific evidence cited**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Secondhand Account Features chart side by side with the Firsthand Account Features from Lesson 5
- Reread Passage A together, circling third-person pronouns and underlining factual data
- Use a simple comparison: "If Thomas the soldier wrote about Valley Forge, that is firsthand. If a historian who lived 200 years later wrote about Valley Forge, that is secondhand."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7 on comparing firsthand and secondhand accounts
- Preview: "Next time, we will put firsthand and secondhand accounts side by side and see how they describe the same event differently."

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Rewrite Challenge:** Take the soldier's letter from Lesson 5 (Passage B) and rewrite the information as a secondhand account, as if writing an encyclopedia entry. Compare the two versions.
2. **Account Sort:** Give students five or six short passages (a diary entry, a textbook paragraph, a letter, a news article, an interview, a biography excerpt). Have them sort each one as firsthand or secondhand and explain their reasoning.
3. **Research a Topic:** Students choose a topic they are interested in and find a secondhand account about it (a textbook entry, an article, a reference book page). They identify the five features and share their findings with the class.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students may wonder whether a news reporter who attends an event is writing a firsthand or secondhand account. The answer is nuanced: a reporter who witnesses an event may include firsthand observations, but a standard news report is typically written in the secondhand style — third person, factual, with quotes from others. For Grade 4, focus on the features rather than edge cases.
- The passage about the community garden (Passage C) includes direct quotes. This is a good opportunity to discuss how secondhand accounts sometimes include firsthand elements — the quotes from Morales and Gutierrez are their firsthand words embedded within the reporter's secondhand account.
- Continually contrast secondhand accounts with the firsthand accounts from Lesson 5. Building this comparison prepares students well for Lesson 7.
- Some students may assume secondhand accounts are "worse" or "less true" than firsthand accounts. Emphasize that both types are valuable. Secondhand accounts often provide more complete, organized information because the writer can consult multiple sources.
- The Mount Vesuvius exit ticket connects to social studies and provides a strong example: no one alive today was at Pompeii, so everything written about it now is secondhand, based on archaeological evidence and ancient records.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice (Passage B):**
- Feature 1: Third-person pronouns — "it was classified," "the tornado traveled," "nine people were killed"
- Feature 2: Factual tone — specific data (EF-5, 200 mph, 75 minutes, 63 miles, 9 killed, 180 injured, $2 billion)
- Feature 3: Broader perspective — covers the entire outbreak, not one person's experience
- Feature 4: Multiple sources — NWS data, emergency response reports, damage assessments
- Feature 5: Organized logically — event, specific tornado, damage, response, outcome

**Independent Practice (Passage C):**
1. Secondhand account. Evidence: Uses third-person pronouns ("the city," "Mayor Linda Chen," "she added"); factual and objective tone with specific data ($50,000 grant, 40 garden beds, 60 residents signed up); gives the broader context (third community garden in five years, plans for two more, research about benefits).
2. The author uses third-person pronouns (he, she, they, it) and names of people. A firsthand account would use "I," "me," and "my."
3. The tone is factual and objective. Examples: Specific numbers like "$50,000 grant" and "40 raised garden beds"; references to studies showing benefits of community gardens. The author does not share personal feelings or reactions.
4. The secondhand account includes the history of the project (two-year timeline, resident petition), the funding details ($50,000 grant plus $15,000 donations), the broader context (third garden in five years, plans for more), and research about community garden benefits. A single attendee would likely focus on what they personally saw and felt at the ceremony.
5. Their quoted words are firsthand — Morales and Gutierrez are speaking from their own experience and knowledge. However, they are embedded within a secondhand account written by a reporter who is describing the event from the outside.
6. (Review) If firsthand: personal pronouns (I, me, my), personal feelings and reactions (e.g., "I was so excited"), and direct sensory observations (what the author saw, heard, and felt at the ceremony).
7. (Review) Sample summary: "The city of Riverside opened the Elm Street Community Garden, its third community garden in five years, funded by a state grant and community donations. The garden features 40 plots, and over 60 residents have signed up to participate."

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Secondhand. Uses third-person language ("the volcano released," "approximately 2,000 people"), presents historical facts and data, and was written long after the event. No personal experience or feelings are included.
2. Any two of: third-person pronouns, objective/factual tone, broader perspective (covers both cities, total deaths, rediscovery 1,700 years later), organized logically.
3. An eyewitness would not have known: the total number killed, the exact height of the ash cloud, the temperature, or that the cities would be buried for 1,700 years and rediscovered by archaeologists.
