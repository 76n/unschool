## Part 1: Passage A — "The Incredible Salmon Run"

Read the passage and the bar graph. Then answer questions 1–8.

> Every year, one of nature's most dramatic events takes place in the rivers of the Pacific Northwest. Millions of salmon make an exhausting journey from the ocean back to the freshwater streams where they were born. This journey is called a salmon run, and it is one of the most remarkable migrations in the animal kingdom.
>
> The life cycle of a Pacific salmon begins in a freshwater stream. Female salmon lay their eggs in shallow nests called redds, which they dig into the gravel of the streambed. After hatching, young salmon, called fry, spend several months to a few years in freshwater, depending on the species. When they are large enough, they travel downstream to the ocean, where they spend one to five years growing and feeding. During their time in the ocean, salmon can travel thousands of miles.
>
> When it is time to reproduce, adult salmon begin the most challenging part of their lives. They stop eating and start swimming upstream — against the current — back to the exact stream where they were born. Scientists believe the salmon use their sense of smell to recognize the unique chemical signature of their home stream. The journey can be hundreds of miles long, and the salmon must swim through rapids, leap over waterfalls, and avoid predators like bears and eagles along the way. The physical effort is so intense that the salmon's bodies begin to change shape and color during the trip.
>
> When the salmon finally reach their home stream, the females dig redds and lay their eggs while the males fertilize them. Tragically, most Pacific salmon species die within days of spawning. Their bodies decompose in the stream and provide important nutrients to the surrounding forest ecosystem. In this way, the salmon's journey ends where it began, completing a cycle that has been repeating for millions of years.

## Part 2: Passages B and C — Two Accounts of the Same Event

Read both passages. Then answer questions 9–15.

### Passage B: Firsthand Account — "My Day at the Redwood Forest"

> *From the journal of Maria Santos, age 10, written on July 18*
>
> Today my family visited the Redwood National Park in California, and I have never seen anything like it in my life. When we got out of the car and I looked up at the first redwood tree, my mouth dropped open. It was so tall that I could not even see the top — it just disappeared into the sky. My dad said some of these trees are over 300 feet tall, but standing next to one, it felt even taller than that. The bark was reddish-brown and rough like the skin of an elephant. I reached out and touched it, and it felt soft and spongy, not hard like I expected.
>
> We walked along a trail through the forest, and it was so quiet. The trees blocked most of the sunlight, so it was dim and cool even though it was hot outside. I felt like I was in a cathedral — that is what my mom called it, a "cathedral of trees." I saw a banana slug on a fern leaf, which was gross but also kind of amazing because it was bright yellow and about six inches long. At one point, we walked through a fallen redwood that had been hollowed out, and our whole family fit inside. I could not believe that a single tree could be that big. This was the best field trip of my entire life, and I never want to forget how tiny I felt standing among those giant trees.

### Passage C: Secondhand Account — "The Coast Redwoods"

> The coast redwood (*Sequoia sempervirens*) is the tallest tree species on Earth. These remarkable trees grow naturally only in a narrow strip along the Pacific coast, from southern Oregon to central California. The tallest known redwood, named Hyperion, stands at 380.3 feet — taller than a 35-story building. Coast redwoods can live for more than 2,000 years, making them among the oldest living organisms on the planet.
>
> Redwood trees thrive in the foggy, moist climate of the Pacific coast. Their bark, which can be up to 12 inches thick, protects them from fire, insects, and disease. The bark contains a chemical called tannin that makes it resistant to decay. Redwoods have shallow but widespread root systems that interlock with the roots of neighboring trees, helping the entire grove stand firm against strong winds and storms.
>
> Redwood forests are home to a diverse ecosystem. The forest floor supports ferns, mosses, and a variety of fungi. Animals such as the northern spotted owl, the marbled murrelet, and the Pacific giant salamander depend on old-growth redwood forests for their habitat. Banana slugs, which can grow up to 10 inches long, are common on the forest floor and play an important role in breaking down decaying plant material.
>
> Logging in the 1800s and 1900s destroyed approximately 96 percent of the original old-growth redwood forests. Today, only about 120,000 acres of old-growth redwoods remain, mostly protected in national and state parks. Conservation groups continue to work to preserve and restore these ancient forests.

## Part 3: Passage D — "The Story of Bridges"

Read the passage and the timeline. Then answer questions 16–20.

> Bridges have been an important part of human civilization for thousands of years. The earliest bridges were simple logs or flat stones placed across streams. Over time, people developed more advanced designs to cross wider rivers and deeper valleys. The ancient Romans were among the greatest bridge builders in history. They invented the stone arch bridge, which used a curved design to spread the weight of the bridge evenly. Many Roman arch bridges are still standing after 2,000 years.
>
> During the Middle Ages, bridge building continued to improve. Some bridges were built with houses and shops on top of them, like the famous Old London Bridge, which stood for over 600 years. In the 1800s, the Industrial Revolution brought a major change to bridge design: iron and steel replaced stone as the primary building material. These new materials were stronger and lighter, allowing engineers to build bridges that were longer and taller than ever before.
>
> The suspension bridge was one of the most important innovations in bridge design. A suspension bridge hangs the roadway from thick cables that are strung between tall towers. This design allows the bridge to span very long distances without needing support pillars in the middle. The Brooklyn Bridge, completed in 1883, was one of the first major suspension bridges and remains an icon of engineering.
>
> Today, engineers continue to push the boundaries of bridge design. Modern bridges use advanced materials like reinforced concrete and high-strength steel cables. Computer technology helps engineers test designs before construction begins, making bridges safer and more efficient. The longest bridge in the world, the Danyang-Kunshan Grand Bridge in China, stretches an incredible 102.4 miles.

## Remediation Guidance

If a student does not meet mastery on a specific standard, use the following targeted reteaching plan:

**RI.4.2 (Main Idea and Details):**
- Reteach using Lesson 1, focusing on the Three-Step Check (Topic → Main Idea → Key Details)
- Provide two additional short passages for practice before reassessing
- Use the Main Idea Web graphic organizer

**RI.4.3 (Explaining Events and Concepts):**
- Reteach using Lessons 3–4, focusing on sequence signal words and cause-effect relationships
- Provide a simple step-by-step passage and guide the student through numbering each step
- Use the sequence graphic organizer and Cause-Effect T-chart

**RI.4.6 (Firsthand and Secondhand Accounts):**
- Reteach using the features charts from Lessons 5–6
- Provide a simple paired set of passages (firsthand and secondhand) about a familiar topic
- Use the Venn diagram to structure the comparison

**RI.4.7 (Visual Information):**
- Reteach using Lessons 8–9, reviewing the three steps for reading visual elements
- Provide a simple bar graph with an accompanying paragraph and walk through the connection
- Practice reading labels, titles, and data before asking interpretation questions
