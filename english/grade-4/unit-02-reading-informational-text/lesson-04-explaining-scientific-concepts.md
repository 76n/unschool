# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 2, Lesson 4

## Topic
Explaining Scientific Concepts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read a science text and identify the concept or procedure being explained
- Explain a scientific concept or procedure step by step using specific details from the text
- Use text details to describe how and why a scientific process works

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify main idea and key details (Lesson 1)
- Ability to identify sequence and cause-effect in informational text (Lesson 3)
- Familiarity with basic science topics at a Grade 3–4 level

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Step-by-step graphic organizer (numbered boxes with arrows)
- Cause-and-Effect T-chart
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge and show students that they already explain scientific processes in everyday life.

**Activity: Explain It to Me**

Say: "Before we read about science today, I want you to try explaining something you already know. Can anyone explain to me how it rains? Just tell me what you know in your own words."

Allow two or three students to share. Responses may be incomplete or slightly inaccurate, and that is fine.

Say: "You just did something that scientists and science writers do all the time — you tried to explain how something in nature works, step by step. When we read science texts, the author does the same thing. They break down a concept or a process into steps so that we can understand it. Today we are going to practice reading science texts and explaining the concepts we find in them."

Say: "In Lesson 3, we practiced explaining historical events by finding sequence and cause-and-effect. We are going to use those same skills today, but with science topics instead of history."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to read a scientific text and explain a concept or procedure using specific text details.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Concept:** A big idea or topic in science (e.g., photosynthesis, erosion, the water cycle)
- **Procedure:** A series of steps that describe how something is done or how something happens
- **Process:** A natural series of events that produces a change or result
- **Technical text:** A text that explains how something works, often using specific terms and step-by-step descriptions

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When we read a science text, the author usually wants us to understand how something works or why something happens. Our job is to find the steps in the process and explain them clearly."

Write on the board:
**How to Explain a Scientific Concept:**
1. Identify the concept or process being explained.
2. Find the steps or stages in order.
3. Look for cause-and-effect relationships (what makes each step happen).
4. Explain the concept in your own words, using specific details from the text.

Say: "Let me show you with a passage about how plants make food."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "How Plants Make Their Own Food"**

> Unlike animals, green plants do not need to eat food from other living things. Instead, they make their own food through a process called photosynthesis. Photosynthesis begins when the plant's roots absorb water from the soil. At the same time, tiny openings in the leaves called stomata take in carbon dioxide from the air. The leaves also capture energy from sunlight using a green substance called chlorophyll. Inside the leaf, the plant uses the energy from sunlight to combine the water and carbon dioxide. This chemical reaction produces glucose, a type of sugar that the plant uses for energy and growth. Oxygen is also produced during this process and is released into the air through the stomata. This is why plants are so important for life on Earth — they not only feed themselves, but they also produce the oxygen that people and animals need to breathe.

Say: "Step 1 — What concept is being explained? The passage tells us about photosynthesis, the process by which plants make their own food."

Say: "Step 2 — What are the steps in the process? Let me find them in order."

Write on the board:
1. Roots absorb water from the soil.
2. Stomata (tiny openings in leaves) take in carbon dioxide from the air.
3. Chlorophyll in the leaves captures energy from sunlight.
4. The plant uses sunlight energy to combine water and carbon dioxide.
5. This reaction produces glucose (sugar) for the plant.
6. Oxygen is produced and released through the stomata.

Say: "Step 3 — What cause-and-effect relationships do I see?"
- Because roots absorb water and leaves take in carbon dioxide, the plant has the raw materials it needs.
- Because chlorophyll captures sunlight energy, the plant can power the chemical reaction.
- As a result of photosynthesis, the plant gets glucose for energy and oxygen is released into the air.

Say: "Step 4 — Now let me explain photosynthesis in my own words: Plants make their own food through photosynthesis. First, the roots take in water and the leaves absorb carbon dioxide and sunlight. Then, using energy from the sun, the plant combines water and carbon dioxide to make glucose, which is a sugar the plant needs to grow. This process also creates oxygen, which is released into the air for people and animals to breathe."

Say: "Notice that I used the specific details from the text — I mentioned roots, water, carbon dioxide, sunlight, chlorophyll, glucose, and oxygen. I put them in order. And I explained how each step connects to the next."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice explaining a scientific concept with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us try one together. Listen carefully as I read this passage about erosion."

**Passage B: "How Erosion Shapes the Land"**

> The surface of the Earth is constantly being changed by a slow but powerful process called erosion. Erosion happens when natural forces like water, wind, and ice wear away rock and soil and move it from one place to another. Water is the most common cause of erosion. When rain falls on bare soil, it loosens small particles and washes them downhill. Over time, flowing water carves channels into the ground that become streams and rivers. Rivers can cut through rock over thousands of years, creating deep valleys and canyons. The Grand Canyon in Arizona was carved by the Colorado River over millions of years. Wind erosion is most common in dry areas with little plant cover. Strong winds pick up loose sand and dust and carry them long distances, sometimes wearing down rocks into unusual shapes. Glaciers, which are massive sheets of slow-moving ice, also cause erosion by scraping across the land and pushing rocks and soil ahead of them. Although erosion is a natural process, human activities like cutting down forests and farming steep hillsides can speed it up by removing the plants that hold soil in place.

Say: "Step 1 — What concept is being explained here? Talk to your partner."

Expected: "Erosion — how natural forces wear away and move rock and soil."

Say: "Good. Step 2 — The author describes three types of erosion. What are they? Find them in the text."

Allow 1 minute. Discuss:
- Water erosion: Rain loosens soil; rivers cut through rock over time.
- Wind erosion: Wind picks up loose sand and dust, wears down rocks.
- Glacier erosion: Massive ice sheets scrape the land and push rocks and soil.

Say: "Step 3 — What cause-and-effect relationships did you notice? Find at least two with your partner."

Allow 1 minute. Discuss:
- Because rain falls on bare soil, small particles are loosened and washed downhill.
- Because rivers flow over rock for thousands of years, deep valleys and canyons form.
- Because wind picks up loose sand, rocks can be worn into unusual shapes.
- Because humans cut down forests, erosion speeds up (plants no longer hold soil).

Say: "Step 4 — Now write a three- to four-sentence explanation of erosion in your own words. Use specific details from the text."

Allow 2–3 minutes. Call on a volunteer.

Sample: "Erosion is a natural process in which water, wind, and ice wear away rock and soil and move it to new places. Water is the most common cause — rain washes soil downhill, and rivers can carve deep canyons over millions of years. Wind erosion picks up loose sand in dry areas and can shape rocks over time. Glaciers scrape the land as they move slowly across it. Human activities like cutting down trees can make erosion happen faster."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently read a science passage and explain a concept using text details.

**Passage C: "How Animals Survive Winter"**

> When winter brings cold temperatures, shorter days, and less food, animals must find ways to survive. Different animal species have developed different strategies. Some animals migrate, meaning they travel long distances to warmer places where food is easier to find. For example, many birds fly south in autumn and return in spring. Gray whales swim thousands of miles from the icy waters of Alaska to the warm coast of Mexico each year. Other animals hibernate, which means they go into a deep sleep that lasts for weeks or even months. During hibernation, an animal's body temperature drops, its heartbeat slows, and it uses very little energy. Before hibernating, animals like bears and groundhogs eat large amounts of food to build up fat reserves that their bodies use for energy while they sleep. A third strategy is adaptation. Some animals grow thicker fur or feathers to stay warm through the winter. The Arctic fox, for example, grows a thick white coat that provides warmth and camouflage in the snow. Squirrels gather and store nuts and seeds during the fall, then retrieve them during the winter months. Each of these strategies helps animals survive the harsh conditions of winter until spring returns.

**Questions:**
1. What scientific concept is this passage explaining?
2. The passage describes three survival strategies. Name each strategy and explain it in one sentence using details from the text.
3. Pick one of the three strategies and identify a cause-and-effect relationship related to it.
4. In your own words, write a three- to four-sentence explanation of how animals survive winter. Use specific details from the passage.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1–4 as written above.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. For question 4, include at least one specific animal example for each survival strategy.
**Challenge:** Answer all questions. Then write a paragraph explaining which survival strategy you think is most effective and why, using evidence from the text to support your reasoning.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Lesson 2 review) Write a two-sentence summary of this passage using the SKIP method.
6. (Lesson 3 review) What signal words in the passage help show the sequence or organization of ideas? List at least three.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following short passage:

> When you breathe in, air enters your nose or mouth and travels down a tube called the trachea. The trachea splits into two smaller tubes called bronchi, one going to each lung. Inside the lungs, the bronchi branch into even tinier tubes that end in small air sacs called alveoli. Oxygen from the air passes through the walls of the alveoli and enters the blood. At the same time, carbon dioxide, a waste gas, moves from the blood into the alveoli and is breathed out.

1. What scientific process is being explained? (Expected: Breathing / how the lungs work / respiration)
2. List the steps in order. (Expected: Air enters nose or mouth; travels down trachea; trachea splits into bronchi; bronchi branch into tiny tubes ending in alveoli; oxygen passes into blood through alveoli walls; carbon dioxide moves from blood into alveoli and is breathed out.)
3. Name one cause-and-effect relationship. (Expected: Because the bronchi branch into tiny tubes ending in alveoli, oxygen can pass into the blood. OR Because carbon dioxide is a waste gas, it moves from the blood into the alveoli to be breathed out.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct, with at least four steps listed in order for question 2**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Reread the passage together, numbering each step as you go
- Use the step-by-step graphic organizer to map the process visually
- Focus on one step at a time and ask: "What happens next? How do you know?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 5 on firsthand accounts
- Encourage the student to read a science article at home and practice explaining the concept to a family member

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Science Explainer Video:** Students choose a simple science concept (how rainbows form, why the sky is blue, how seeds grow into plants) and write a script explaining it step by step, as if they were making a short educational video.
2. **Process Drawing:** Students create a labeled diagram showing the steps of photosynthesis, erosion, or another process from the lesson. Each label should include a brief explanation.
3. **Compare Two Processes:** Students read about two related science processes (e.g., water cycle and erosion) and write a paragraph explaining how they are connected, using details from both texts.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Science texts often contain unfamiliar vocabulary. Pre-teach key terms before reading and post them on the word wall. For this lesson, terms like photosynthesis, chlorophyll, stomata, erosion, glacier, migrate, and hibernate may be new.
- Some students confuse the overall concept with individual steps. Help them see that the concept is the "big picture" (e.g., photosynthesis), while the steps are the pieces that make up the process.
- Encourage students to use transition words (first, next, then, as a result, because) when writing their explanations. This connects to the skills from Lesson 3.
- The passages in this lesson are intentionally information-dense. If a student is overwhelmed, break the passage into chunks and discuss one paragraph at a time.
- For English learners, pair the text with simple diagrams or visuals whenever possible. Even drawing a quick sketch on the board (arrows showing water going from roots to leaves) can help make abstract processes concrete.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice (Passage B):**
- Concept: Erosion — how natural forces wear away rock and soil and move it to new places.
- Three types: Water erosion (rain loosens soil; rivers cut canyons), Wind erosion (wind carries sand and dust; wears rocks into shapes), Glacier erosion (ice sheets scrape land, push rocks).
- Cause-Effect: Because rain falls on bare soil, particles are loosened and washed downhill. Because the Colorado River flowed over rock for millions of years, the Grand Canyon was formed. Because humans remove plants, erosion speeds up.
- Sample explanation: "Erosion is the process of water, wind, and ice wearing away rock and soil and carrying it to new locations. Water erosion is the most common — rain washes soil downhill, and rivers can carve deep canyons like the Grand Canyon over millions of years. Wind erodes dry areas by carrying sand and dust, and glaciers scrape the land as they move. Human activities like deforestation can make erosion happen even faster."

**Independent Practice (Passage C):**
1. How animals survive winter.
2. Migration: Some animals, like birds and gray whales, travel long distances to warmer places where food is available. Hibernation: Some animals, like bears and groundhogs, go into a deep sleep for months, slowing their body functions and living off stored fat. Adaptation: Some animals, like Arctic foxes and squirrels, grow thicker coats or store food to survive the cold.
3. Example: Because winters bring less food, some animals migrate to warmer places where food is easier to find. OR Because bears eat large amounts of food before winter, they build fat reserves that give them energy during hibernation.
4. Sample: "When winter makes it hard to find food and stay warm, animals use three main strategies to survive. Some animals migrate to warmer areas — gray whales swim from Alaska to Mexico, and many birds fly south. Other animals hibernate, slowing their bodies down and living off fat they stored up during fall. Animals that stay active through winter, like Arctic foxes and squirrels, grow thick coats or store food to get through the cold months."
5. (Review) Sample summary: "Animals survive winter using three strategies: migration, hibernation, and adaptation. Some travel to warmer places, some sleep through the cold months, and others grow thick fur or store food."
6. (Review) Signal words: "some," "other," "a third," "for example," "during," "each of these." (Accept any three valid organizational signal words from the passage.)

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Breathing / how the lungs work / how oxygen gets into the blood.
2. (1) Air enters nose or mouth; (2) air travels down trachea; (3) trachea splits into two bronchi; (4) bronchi branch into tiny tubes ending in alveoli; (5) oxygen passes through alveoli walls into blood; (6) carbon dioxide moves from blood into alveoli and is breathed out.
3. Because bronchi branch into tiny tubes ending in alveoli, oxygen can pass through the alveoli walls into the blood. (Accept other valid cause-effect pairs.)
