# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 2, Lesson 10

## Topic
Informational Text Review

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the main idea and supporting key details in an informational passage (RI.4.2)
- Explain events and concepts using specific details from the text (RI.4.3)
- Distinguish between firsthand and secondhand accounts and compare perspectives (RI.4.6)
- Interpret visual information (charts, graphs, diagrams) and connect it to text (RI.4.7)

## Prerequisites
- Completion of Lessons 1–9 in this unit
- Familiarity with all key vocabulary from previous lessons
- Experience with all graphic organizers used in this unit

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Main Idea Web, Venn Diagram, and comparison charts (from earlier lessons)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Briefly review the key strategies from each skill area before students apply them in an integrated activity.

### Key Vocabulary
(Review of all unit vocabulary)
- **Main idea:** The most important point the author makes about the topic
- **Key details:** Specific facts that support the main idea
- **Summary:** A short retelling with the main idea and key details, no opinions
- **Cause and effect:** Why something happened (cause) and what happened as a result (effect)
- **Firsthand account:** Written by someone who was there — uses I/me/my, includes feelings
- **Secondhand account:** Written by someone who was not there — uses he/she/they, factual tone, broader view
- **Diagram/Chart/Graph/Timeline:** Visual elements that present information in a visual format to support the text

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Let me quickly review the strategies we learned for each skill."

Write on the board:

**Main Idea and Details (Lessons 1–2):**
- Three-Step Check: Topic → Main Idea → Key Details
- SKIP for summaries: State the main idea, Keep key details, In your own words, Pull out opinions

**Explaining Events and Concepts (Lessons 3–4):**
- Find sequence using signal words (first, next, then, finally)
- Find cause-effect using signal words (because, as a result, due to)
- Explain the process step by step using text details

**Firsthand vs. Secondhand Accounts (Lessons 5–7):**
- Firsthand: I/me/my, personal feelings, direct observations, limited view
- Secondhand: he/she/they, factual tone, broader view, multiple sources
- Compare: focus, information, perspective

**Visual Information (Lessons 8–9):**
- Three steps: Read the title, Read the labels, Connect to the text
- Types: bar graphs, line graphs, pie charts, diagrams, timelines, maps

Say: "Now we are going to apply all of these skills to a real informational text about honeybees."

### Worked Examples

Say: "Before we start the integrated activity, let me model how I would approach a passage that requires multiple skills. Here is a short example."

**Quick Model Passage: "Lightning"**

> Lightning is a powerful electrical discharge that occurs during thunderstorms. When ice particles inside a cloud collide, they create an electrical charge. The top of the cloud becomes positively charged, and the bottom becomes negatively charged. When the difference in charge becomes strong enough, a bolt of lightning strikes — either between clouds or from a cloud to the ground. A single bolt of lightning can reach temperatures of 30,000 degrees Kelvin, which is about five times hotter than the surface of the sun. Lightning strikes the Earth approximately 100 times every second.

Say: "If I were asked to find the main idea, I would say: Lightning is a powerful electrical discharge caused by electrical charges building up inside storm clouds."

Say: "If I were asked to explain the process, I would say: Ice particles collide, creating charges. The top becomes positive, the bottom negative. When the charge difference is strong enough, a bolt strikes."

Say: "If I were asked how a diagram would help, I might say: A diagram of a cloud showing the positive charges at the top and negative charges at the bottom, with a lightning bolt striking downward, would help readers see how lightning forms."

Say: "See how I used multiple skills on the same passage? That is what you will do next."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students work through an integrated passage applying all four skill areas with facilitator support.

Say: "Now we are going to read a longer passage together and practice using all our skills. This passage is about honeybees, and it includes both text and a diagram."

**Passage: "The Remarkable World of Honeybees"**

> Honeybees are among the most important insects on the planet. While most people think of honey when they think of bees, honeybees actually play a much bigger role in our world by pollinating plants. Pollination happens when bees carry pollen from one flower to another, allowing plants to produce fruits, vegetables, and seeds. Scientists estimate that honeybees pollinate about one-third of the food we eat, including apples, almonds, blueberries, and cucumbers.
>
> A honeybee colony is an incredibly organized society. Every bee has a specific job. The queen bee is the only bee that lays eggs — she can lay up to 2,000 eggs in a single day. Worker bees, which are all female, perform many tasks depending on their age. Young workers clean cells and feed larvae. Older workers build honeycomb from wax, guard the hive entrance, and fly out to collect nectar and pollen. Male bees, called drones, have only one job: to mate with a new queen. A typical colony can have between 20,000 and 80,000 bees, all working together.
>
> The process of making honey is fascinating. Worker bees visit flowers and suck up nectar using a long tube-like tongue called a proboscis. They store the nectar in a special honey stomach separate from their regular stomach. When they return to the hive, they pass the nectar to other worker bees through mouth-to-mouth transfer. These bees chew the nectar, mixing it with enzymes that break down the sugars. Then they spread the liquid into honeycomb cells and fan it with their wings to evaporate the water. Once the honey is thick enough, the bees seal the cell with a wax cap. The honey is stored as food for the colony, especially during winter when flowers are not blooming.

```
[Middle section: BROOD AREA]

```

Say: "Let us work through this together, using all four skills."

**Skill 1 — Main Idea and Details (RI.4.2):**
Say: "What is the main idea of this whole passage? Talk to your partner."

Allow 1 minute. Discuss:
Expected: "Honeybees are remarkably important and organized insects that pollinate plants and make honey through a complex process."

Say: "What key details support this? Name at least three."
Expected: Bees pollinate about one-third of the food we eat; every bee has a specific job; the queen lays up to 2,000 eggs per day; the honey-making process involves multiple steps.

**Skill 2 — Explaining a Process (RI.4.3):**
Say: "The third paragraph describes how honey is made. With your partner, list the steps in order."

Allow 2 minutes. Discuss:
Expected steps:
1. Worker bees visit flowers and suck up nectar with their proboscis.
2. They store nectar in a special honey stomach.
3. At the hive, they pass nectar to other bees mouth-to-mouth.
4. Bees chew the nectar and mix it with enzymes.
5. They spread the liquid into honeycomb cells.
6. Bees fan it with their wings to evaporate water.
7. Once thick, bees seal the cell with a wax cap.

**Skill 3 — Account Type (RI.4.6):**
Say: "Is this passage a firsthand or secondhand account? How do you know?"

Expected: "Secondhand. It uses third-person pronouns (they, bees, the queen), has a factual tone, includes scientific data, and covers the topic broadly. The author was not a bee!"

**Skill 4 — Visual Information (RI.4.7):**
Say: "Look at the diagram. What information does it show that the text describes?"

Expected: The diagram shows the honey storage area (matching the honey-making description), the brood area with queen and nurse bees (matching the colony roles), and the guard and forager bees at the entrance (matching the worker bee descriptions).

Say: "What does the diagram add that the text alone does not make as clear?"

Expected: The diagram shows where each type of bee works within the hive — honey storage is at the top, the brood area is in the middle, and the entrance is at the bottom. The text describes the roles but not the physical layout.

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently apply all four skills to a new passage.

**Passage: "The Amazing Journey of Sea Turtles"**

> Sea turtles are some of the ocean's most remarkable travelers. These ancient reptiles have been swimming the world's oceans for more than 100 million years — they existed alongside the dinosaurs. Today, seven species of sea turtles survive, and most of them are endangered. The largest species, the leatherback, can grow up to seven feet long and weigh as much as 2,000 pounds.
>
> Every year, female sea turtles make an incredible journey back to the beach where they were born to lay their eggs. Scientists are not entirely sure how the turtles find their way, but they believe the turtles use Earth's magnetic field as a kind of natural compass. When a female turtle reaches her nesting beach, she crawls up above the high-tide line and digs a deep hole in the sand with her back flippers. She then lays between 80 and 120 eggs, covers the nest with sand, and returns to the ocean. The entire nesting process takes about two hours.
>
> The eggs incubate in the warm sand for approximately 60 days. An interesting fact about sea turtle eggs is that the temperature of the sand determines whether the babies will be male or female — warmer temperatures produce more females, and cooler temperatures produce more males. When the baby turtles hatch, they are only about two inches long. They dig their way out of the sand, usually at night, and scramble toward the ocean, guided by the moonlight reflecting off the water. This journey from nest to sea is one of the most dangerous moments in a sea turtle's life, as predators like birds, crabs, and raccoons wait nearby.
>
> Scientists and volunteers around the world work to protect sea turtles and their nesting beaches. Conservation programs monitor nests, relocate eggs that are in danger, and educate the public about the threats sea turtles face, including pollution, fishing nets, and habitat loss.

**Questions (All Four Skills):**

**Main Idea and Details (RI.4.2):**
1. What is the main idea of this passage?
2. List three key details that support the main idea.
3. Write a two- to three-sentence summary of the passage.

**Explaining Events and Concepts (RI.4.3):**
4. Explain the sea turtle nesting process step by step, from the mother's arrival at the beach to the babies reaching the ocean. Use at least five steps.
5. Identify one cause-and-effect relationship in the passage.

**Firsthand vs. Secondhand Accounts (RI.4.6):**
6. Is this passage a firsthand or secondhand account? Give two reasons for your answer.
7. If a marine biologist who spent a summer protecting sea turtle nests wrote about her experience, would that be a firsthand or secondhand account? What features would it have?

**Visual Information (RI.4.7):**
8. According to the bar graph, which species has the most nesting females per year? Which has the fewest?
9. The passage says most sea turtle species are endangered. How does the bar graph help you understand which species might be most at risk?
10. How does the bar graph add to your understanding of the passage?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 10.
**Stretch:** Answer questions 1–6, 8, and 10.
**Challenge:** Answer all ten questions with detailed, evidence-based responses.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following short passage:

> Glaciers are massive rivers of ice that move very slowly across the land. They form when snow piles up year after year and compresses into thick ice. As glaciers move, they carve out valleys and push rocks and soil ahead of them. Today, glaciers cover about 10 percent of Earth's land surface. However, because of rising global temperatures, many glaciers are shrinking. Scientists measure glacier size using satellite images and on-the-ground surveys.

1. What is the main idea of this passage? (Expected: Glaciers are massive rivers of ice that shape the land, but they are shrinking due to rising temperatures.)
2. Explain how glaciers form, using details from the text. (Expected: Snow piles up year after year. Over time, it compresses into thick ice, forming a glacier.)
3. Would a diagram showing a glacier carving a valley be helpful with this passage? Why or why not? (Expected: Yes, it would show how the glacier pushes rocks and soil ahead of it and carves valleys — a process that is hard to picture from text alone.)
4. Is this passage a firsthand or secondhand account? (Expected: Secondhand — it uses third-person pronouns, has a factual tone, and presents scientific information from research.)

**Mastery = 4 out of 4 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Identify which specific skill area(s) the student struggled with
- Review the key strategy for that skill area (Three-Step Check, sequence steps, account features, visual reading steps)
- Provide targeted practice on the weak area before the unit assessment

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is well prepared for the Unit Assessment
- Encourage the student to review their notes and graphic organizers from all nine lessons before the assessment

2. **Teach a Friend:** Students choose one of the four skill areas and prepare a two-minute mini-lesson to teach a partner. They must include an example passage and at least one practice question.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This is a review lesson, so the pacing should be brisk. Students have already learned each skill individually; now they are putting it all together. Spend more time on skills that students have found difficult and less time on skills they have mastered.
- The honeybee passage in Guided Practice is intentionally longer than passages from earlier lessons. This mirrors the complexity students will encounter on the Unit Assessment.
- Use the exit ticket strategically. If a student misses a question, note which skill area it falls under and provide targeted review before the assessment.
- The Independent Practice includes ten questions covering all four skill areas. For students who work slowly, prioritize the On-level tier (six questions) and use the remaining questions as optional enrichment.
- This is a good lesson to use cooperative learning structures. Students can work in pairs or small groups during Guided Practice and then work independently during Independent Practice to demonstrate their own understanding.
- If time permits, end the lesson with a brief discussion: "Which skill from this unit was the hardest for you? Which was the easiest? What strategies helped you the most?" This metacognitive reflection helps students prepare for the assessment.

**Independent Practice (Sea Turtles):**
