# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 2, Lesson 5

## Topic
Firsthand Accounts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define what a firsthand account is and identify examples of firsthand accounts
- Recognize the features of firsthand accounts (personal pronouns, feelings, direct observations)
- Identify the author's perspective in a firsthand account

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify main idea and key details (Lesson 1)
- Ability to explain events using details from a text (Lesson 3)
- Understanding that different texts can describe the same topic in different ways

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages (diary entries, letters, interviews)
- Firsthand Account Features chart (to record personal pronouns, feelings, observations)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters (optional)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build understanding of what it means to experience something firsthand versus hearing about it from someone else.

**Activity: Were You There?**

Say: "I want you to imagine two different situations. In the first situation, you went to a school assembly where a magician performed amazing tricks. You saw everything with your own eyes. In the second situation, you were home sick that day, and your friend told you about the assembly at recess the next day."

Say: "If someone asked you to describe the assembly, how would your description be different from your friend's description? Turn to a partner and talk about this."

Allow 1 minute. Call on a few students.

Say: "If you were there, you could describe exactly what you saw, heard, and felt. You might say things like 'I was amazed when the magician pulled a rabbit out of the hat' or 'I could hear everyone gasping.' But if your friend told you about it, you would describe what your friend said happened. You might say, 'My friend said the magician pulled out a rabbit.'"

Say: "When you describe something you experienced yourself, that is called a firsthand account. Today we are going to learn what makes firsthand accounts special and how to recognize them when we read."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify firsthand accounts and analyze the author's perspective.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Firsthand account:** A description of an event written or told by someone who experienced it directly
- **Perspective:** The way a person sees or thinks about something, based on their experiences and feelings
- **Personal pronouns:** Words like I, me, my, we, our, and us that show the author is talking about their own experience
- **Eyewitness:** A person who saw an event happen with their own eyes

### Instruction Notes

Say: "A firsthand account is written by someone who was there — an eyewitness. They saw it, heard it, or lived through it. Firsthand accounts include diary entries, personal letters, journal entries, interviews, memoirs, and autobiographies."

Write on the board:
**Features of a Firsthand Account:**
1. Uses personal pronouns: I, me, my, we, our, us
2. Includes personal feelings and reactions: "I felt scared," "I was so excited"
3. Describes what the author saw, heard, smelled, tasted, or touched
4. Shares the author's own perspective — their unique point of view
5. May not include the "big picture" because the author only knows what they experienced

Say: "Let me read you a firsthand account and show you how to spot these features."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: Diary Entry — "My First Day at the New School"**

> *September 8, 1952*
>
> Dear Diary,
>
> Today was my first day at Lincoln Elementary, and I was so nervous my hands were shaking when Mama dropped me off. The building was much bigger than my old school back in Georgia. I walked through the front doors and could smell the floor polish and chalk dust. The hallways were so long that I was sure I would get lost. When I found my classroom, the teacher, Mrs. Patterson, smiled at me and told me to sit in the third row. The other children stared at me, and I wished I could disappear. At lunch, a girl named Dorothy sat next to me and shared her apple slices. She asked me where I was from, and when I told her Georgia, she said she had a cousin there. By the end of the day, I felt a little better. I still miss my old friends, but maybe this school will not be so bad after all.

Say: "Let us go through our checklist. Feature 1 — Does this use personal pronouns?"

Highlight or underline: I, my, me

Say: "Yes. 'I was so nervous,' 'my hands were shaking,' 'Mama dropped me off.' The author is talking about herself."

Say: "Feature 2 — Does the author share personal feelings?"

Highlight: "I was so nervous," "I wished I could disappear," "I felt a little better," "I still miss my old friends"

Say: "Absolutely. We know exactly how this person felt — nervous, scared, lonely, then a little hopeful."

Say: "Feature 3 — Does the author describe what she saw, heard, or smelled?"

Highlight: "smell the floor polish and chalk dust," "The hallways were so long," "the other children stared at me"

Say: "Yes. She describes sights and smells from being right there in the building."

Say: "Feature 4 — What is the author's perspective? How does she see this experience?"

Say: "She sees the first day through the eyes of a nervous newcomer. She focuses on her fears and her loneliness, but she also notices the kindness of Dorothy. Her perspective is personal and emotional."

Say: "Feature 5 — Is there anything missing? Does she describe the 'big picture'?"

Say: "She does not tell us about the whole school or what other students thought. She only describes what she personally saw and felt. That is typical of a firsthand account."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying firsthand account features with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us read another firsthand account together. This time, I want you to help me find the features."

**Passage B: Letter Home — "A Soldier's Letter from Valley Forge"**

> *January 15, 1778*
>
> Dear Mother,
>
> I write to you from our camp at Valley Forge. The cold here is something I have never known before. My boots have worn through, and I wrap my feet in rags to keep them from freezing. Many of the men in our regiment have fallen ill, and we have very little medicine to help them. Each morning, I wake up hoping that supply wagons will arrive with food and blankets, but most days we receive nothing. Despite all of this, our spirits have not been completely broken. General Washington walks through the camp each day, speaking to the men and reminding us of what we are fighting for. When I see him standing tall in the freezing wind, I find the courage to carry on. I do not know when this war will end, but I believe we are on the right side of it. Please give my love to Father and the children. I hope to see you all again before too long.
>
> Your loving son,
> Thomas

Say: "This is a letter from a soldier during the American Revolution. Let us find the five features of a firsthand account. Work with your partner on this."

Allow 2 minutes for partner discussion. Then go through each feature:

Say: "Feature 1 — Personal pronouns. What do you find?"

Expected: I, my, me, we, our — "I write to you," "My boots," "we have very little medicine," "our spirits"

Say: "Feature 2 — Personal feelings. What feelings does Thomas share?"

Expected: Suffering from cold and hunger, hope for supplies, admiration for General Washington, courage, belief in the cause, love for his family, uncertainty about when the war will end.

Say: "Feature 3 — What does Thomas see, hear, or feel physically?"

Expected: Boots worn through, wrapping feet in rags, cold, men falling ill, seeing Washington walk through camp.

Say: "Feature 4 — What is Thomas's perspective?"

Expected: He sees the war from the ground level — the daily suffering of an ordinary soldier. He is cold, hungry, and worried, but he is also inspired by Washington and believes in the cause.

Say: "Feature 5 — What does Thomas NOT tell us?"

Expected: He does not describe the overall strategy of the war, what is happening in other parts of the country, or the reasons the supplies are delayed. He only knows what he sees at Valley Forge.

Say: "This is why firsthand accounts are so valuable — they show us what it was really like to be there. But they also have limits because the author can only describe their own experience."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze a firsthand account.

**Passage C: Interview Transcript — "Storm Chaser Elena Ruiz"**

> **Interviewer:** Elena, can you tell us about the tornado you witnessed in Oklahoma last spring?
>
> **Elena:** I will never forget it. My team and I had been tracking the storm system all morning. We could see the sky turning a greenish-gray color, which is usually a sign that conditions are right for a tornado. Around 2:30 in the afternoon, I spotted a funnel cloud forming about three miles to the west. My heart started pounding. I grabbed my camera and started recording while my partner, Jake, kept track of our distance from the storm.
>
> **Interviewer:** What happened next?
>
> **Elena:** The funnel cloud touched down, and within seconds it kicked up a huge cloud of dust and debris. The sound was incredible — people always say tornadoes sound like a freight train, and I can tell you that is exactly right. We could hear it roaring even from two miles away. I watched it move across a field, tearing up fence posts and scattering hay bales like they were made of paper. Thankfully, it did not hit any homes in that area.
>
> **Interviewer:** Were you frightened?
>
> **Elena:** Absolutely. No matter how many storms I have chased, there is always a moment of fear when a tornado touches down. But I have also learned to channel that fear into focus. My job is to gather data that helps scientists understand tornadoes better, so staying alert is essential. After about twelve minutes, the tornado lifted back into the clouds and was gone. I remember standing there with dust settling around us, feeling this strange mix of relief and amazement.

**Questions:**
1. Is this a firsthand account? How do you know? Give at least two pieces of evidence.
2. List three personal feelings or reactions Elena shares in this account.
3. List three specific observations Elena describes (things she saw, heard, or felt).
4. What is Elena's perspective on the tornado? How does her experience as a storm chaser shape what she notices and how she describes the event?
5. What information about the tornado might be missing from Elena's account because she can only describe what she personally experienced?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1–4 as written above.
**Stretch:** Answer all five questions, with detailed answers for questions 4 and 5.
**Challenge:** Answer all five questions. Then write a short paragraph (3–4 sentences) from Elena's perspective describing the moment the tornado touched down, as if you were Elena writing in a personal diary that night.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Lesson 1 review) What is the main idea of Elena's account? Write it as one sentence.
7. (Lesson 3 review) List three events from Elena's account in the order they happened.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following short passage:

> I remember the moment the lights went out during the big storm of 2003. I was sitting in our kitchen doing homework when suddenly everything went dark. My little sister started crying, and I felt my way to the drawer where we kept the flashlights. The wind outside was howling so loudly that I could barely hear my mom telling us to stay calm. We huddled together in the hallway until the storm passed. I was scared, but I also felt proud that I found the flashlight so quickly.

1. Is this a firsthand account? Name one feature that tells you. (Expected: Yes. It uses personal pronouns like "I" and "my," shares personal feelings like "I was scared" and "I felt proud," and describes what the author saw and heard.)
2. What is the author's perspective on the storm? (Expected: The author experienced the storm as a child in their home. They were scared but also proud of themselves for helping. They focus on what happened inside the house — the personal, family experience.)
3. Name one thing about the storm that the author probably does NOT know from their experience. (Expected: How widespread the power outage was, what caused it, what happened in other neighborhoods, how long it lasted overall, or what the storm looked like from outside.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct, with at least one specific feature identified for question 1**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the five features of a firsthand account using the chart from Direct Instruction
- Reread Passage A together and highlight each feature with a different color
- Ask guiding questions: "Who is telling this story? Were they there? How do you know?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 6 on secondhand accounts
- Preview: "Next time, we will learn about secondhand accounts — descriptions written by people who were NOT there."

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Write Your Own Firsthand Account:** Students write a diary entry about a memorable event they experienced (a field trip, a birthday party, a weather event). They must include at least three of the five firsthand account features.
2. **Interview a Family Member:** Students interview a parent, grandparent, or other family member about an interesting experience and write up the interview as a firsthand account, noting the features.
3. **Firsthand Account Hunt:** Give students two or three short passages and have them sort them into "firsthand account" and "not a firsthand account," explaining their reasoning for each.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The concept of "perspective" can be abstract for fourth graders. Ground it in concrete examples: "Elena sees the tornado as a scientist gathering data. A farmer nearby might see the same tornado as a threat to their crops. Same event, different perspectives."
- Some students may confuse firsthand accounts with fiction written in first person. Clarify that a firsthand account is nonfiction — it describes a real event that the author actually experienced.
- The soldier's letter (Passage B) provides a natural tie-in to social studies. If students are studying the American Revolution, use this passage to deepen their understanding.
- Interviews are a type of firsthand account that students may not immediately recognize. Point out that even though the interviewer is asking questions, Elena's responses are firsthand because she is describing her own experience.
- This lesson sets up Lesson 6 (secondhand accounts) and Lesson 7 (comparing accounts). Save the passages from this lesson so students can compare them to secondhand accounts later.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice (Passage B):**
- Personal pronouns: I, my, me, we, our
- Feelings: Cold and suffering, hope, admiration for Washington, courage, belief in the cause, love for family, uncertainty
- Observations: Boots worn through, wrapping feet in rags, men falling ill, seeing Washington walk through camp in freezing wind
- Perspective: An ordinary soldier experiencing the hardships of Valley Forge firsthand — cold, hungry, worried, but inspired by Washington
- Missing information: Overall war strategy, events elsewhere, reasons for supply delays

**Independent Practice (Passage C):**
1. Yes, it is a firsthand account. Evidence: Elena uses "I" and "my" (personal pronouns); she shares feelings like "my heart started pounding" and "fear"; she describes what she saw and heard (greenish-gray sky, funnel cloud, roaring sound).
2. Feelings: Heart pounding (excitement/fear), fear when tornado touched down, focus/alertness, relief and amazement after it ended. (Accept any three.)
3. Observations: Sky turning greenish-gray, funnel cloud forming three miles west, huge cloud of dust and debris, roaring sound like a freight train, fence posts torn up, hay bales scattered, dust settling. (Accept any three.)
4. Elena sees the tornado as both dangerous and scientifically important. As an experienced storm chaser, she notices specific weather signs (greenish-gray sky), measures distance, and gathers data. She acknowledges fear but channels it into focus. Her perspective combines personal emotion with professional purpose.
5. Missing information might include: how strong the tornado was rated, how much total damage it caused, whether anyone was hurt in other areas, how long the overall storm system lasted, or what the tornado looked like from other vantage points.
6. (Review) Main idea: Storm chaser Elena Ruiz describes witnessing a powerful tornado in Oklahoma, sharing her observations and emotions during the event.
7. (Review) Sequence: (1) Elena's team tracked the storm system all morning; (2) Around 2:30 PM, Elena spotted a funnel cloud forming; (3) The funnel cloud touched down and kicked up dust and debris; (4) The tornado moved across a field for about twelve minutes; (5) The tornado lifted back into the clouds.

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Yes. Uses personal pronouns ("I," "my"); shares feelings ("I was scared," "I felt proud"); describes observations (lights going out, wind howling, sister crying).
2. The author was a child experiencing a scary storm at home. They focus on the family experience — the fear, the dark, the noise, and the pride of helping.
3. Accept any: How widespread the outage was, what caused it, what happened outside or in the neighborhood, how long the outage lasted, or what the storm looked like.
