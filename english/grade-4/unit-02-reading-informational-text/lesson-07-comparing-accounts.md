# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 2, Lesson 7

## Topic
Comparing Accounts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare a firsthand and a secondhand account of the same event
- Identify similarities and differences in focus, information provided, and perspective
- Explain why reading both types of accounts gives a more complete understanding of an event

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify features of firsthand accounts (Lesson 5)
- Ability to identify features of secondhand accounts (Lesson 6)
- Understanding of perspective and how it shapes what an author includes

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages (paired firsthand and secondhand accounts)
- Venn diagram graphic organizer
- Comparison chart (columns: Focus, Information, Perspective, Pronouns, Tone)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review firsthand and secondhand account features and set up the comparison task.

**Activity: Quick Sort**

Say: "Over the past two lessons, we have learned about firsthand accounts and secondhand accounts. Let me see how well you remember the differences."

Write the following on the board and ask students to sort each item into "Firsthand" or "Secondhand":
- Uses "I" and "my" (Firsthand)
- Includes personal feelings (Firsthand)
- Uses "he," "she," and "they" (Secondhand)
- Gives the "big picture" with facts and data (Secondhand)
- Describes what the author saw and felt directly (Firsthand)
- May include information from multiple sources (Secondhand)
- Written by someone who was there (Firsthand)
- Written by someone who researched the event (Secondhand)

Say: "Great. Today we are going to do something new. We are going to read two accounts of the same event — one firsthand and one secondhand — and compare them. We will look at what is the same, what is different, and why both accounts are valuable."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model how to compare a firsthand and secondhand account of the same event.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Compare:** To look at two or more things and find what is the same
- **Contrast:** To look at two or more things and find what is different
- **Focus:** What the author chooses to emphasize or spend the most time on
- **Venn diagram:** A graphic organizer with two overlapping circles used to show similarities and differences

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When we compare two accounts of the same event, we look at three main areas."

Write on the board:
1. **Focus:** What does each author choose to emphasize? What do they spend the most time on?
2. **Information:** What facts or details does each account include? What does each one leave out?
3. **Perspective:** How does each author see the event? What is their point of view?

Say: "Let me show you with two passages about the same event."

### Worked Examples

**Event: The First Moon Landing (July 20, 1969)**

**Passage A: Firsthand Account — Astronaut's Memoir**

> When I stepped off the ladder and my boot pressed into the moon's surface for the first time, I could hardly believe it was real. The ground was covered in a fine gray powder, and my footprint stayed perfectly still — there is no wind on the moon. I looked up and saw the Earth hanging in the black sky, a bright blue marble surrounded by darkness. It was the most beautiful and lonely sight I had ever seen. My heart was pounding inside my spacesuit, and I could hear my own breathing echoing in my helmet. I thought about my family back home and wondered if they were watching. The surface was eerily quiet — no birds, no wind, no sound at all except the hum of my life-support system. I spent two and a half hours walking on the surface, collecting rock samples and setting up scientific instruments. Every step felt like a dream. When I climbed back into the lunar module, I took one last look at the footprints we had left behind, knowing they would remain there for millions of years.

**Passage B: Secondhand Account — Encyclopedia Entry**

> On July 20, 1969, the Apollo 11 mission successfully landed the first humans on the moon. Astronauts Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin descended to the lunar surface in the Eagle lunar module while Michael Collins orbited above in the command module. Armstrong was the first to step onto the moon at 10:56 PM Eastern Time, famously declaring, "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." Aldrin joined him shortly after. The astronauts spent approximately two and a half hours on the surface, collecting 47.5 pounds of lunar rock and soil samples and deploying scientific instruments, including a seismometer and a laser reflector. An estimated 600 million people worldwide watched the event on television, making it one of the most-watched broadcasts in history. The Apollo 11 mission was a major achievement in the space race between the United States and the Soviet Union. The astronauts returned safely to Earth on July 24, splashing down in the Pacific Ocean. The mission fulfilled President John F. Kennedy's 1961 goal of landing a man on the moon before the end of the decade.

Say: "These two passages describe the same event — the first moon landing. But notice how different they are. Let me compare them using our three areas."

**Focus:**
Say: "Passage A focuses on the astronaut's personal experience — what he saw, felt, and thought. Passage B focuses on the facts and historical significance of the mission."

**Information:**
Say: "Passage A gives us sensory details: gray powder, footprints, Earth in the sky, silence, his breathing. Passage B gives us factual data: specific date and time, names of all three astronauts, weight of samples (47.5 pounds), number of TV viewers (600 million), and the historical context (the space race, Kennedy's goal)."

Say: "What does Passage A leave out? The names of the other astronauts, the date, the number of viewers. What does Passage B leave out? The personal feelings, the silence, the beauty of seeing Earth from the moon."

**Perspective:**
Say: "Passage A shows us the moon landing through the eyes of someone who was there — it is emotional, personal, and focused on the moment. Passage B shows us the moon landing as a historical event — it is factual, broad, and places the event in context."

Draw a Venn diagram on the board:
- Firsthand Only: personal feelings, sensory details (gray powder, silence, Earth in sky), individual experience, emotional tone
- Both: astronauts walked on moon, collected rock samples, spent about 2.5 hours on surface, deployed scientific instruments
- Secondhand Only: exact date and time, all three astronaut names, weight of samples, number of TV viewers, space race context, Kennedy's goal, return to Earth details

Say: "See how reading both accounts gives us a much more complete picture? The firsthand account helps us feel what it was like to be there. The secondhand account helps us understand the facts and significance."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice comparing paired accounts with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us try comparing two accounts together. These are about the same event — a school science fair."

**Event: Lincoln Elementary Science Fair**

**Passage A: Firsthand — Student's Journal Entry**

> Today was the science fair, and I was so nervous I almost could not eat breakfast. I have been working on my project about which type of soil helps bean plants grow the fastest for three whole weeks. When I set up my display in the gym, my hands were trembling. I had my three bean plants lined up — the one in sandy soil was the smallest, the one in potting soil was the tallest, and the one in clay soil was somewhere in between. The judges came to my table around 10:00 in the morning. They asked me questions about my hypothesis and my results, and I was surprised at how well I answered. I could tell they were impressed when I showed them my data chart. My best friend Amara's project on magnets was right next to mine, and we kept giving each other encouraging looks. At the awards ceremony after lunch, my name was called for second place! I was so happy I almost knocked over my plants running up to get my ribbon. It was the best day of fourth grade so far.

**Passage B: Secondhand — School Newsletter Report**

> Lincoln Elementary held its annual science fair last Friday in the school gymnasium. A total of 54 students from grades three through five presented projects on topics ranging from plant growth and magnetism to water filtration and weather patterns. Projects were judged on scientific method, creativity, presentation, and data analysis by a panel of three judges, including local high school science teacher Mr. David Park and two parent volunteers. First place was awarded to fifth-grader Marcus Webb for his project on water filtration methods. Second place went to fourth-grader Lily Chen for her experiment comparing bean plant growth in different soil types. Third place was awarded to third-grader Amara Johnson for her demonstration of magnetic fields. Principal Margaret Holloway praised the students' hard work and curiosity. "Every student who participated is a winner in my eyes," Holloway said. "These projects show real scientific thinking." The science fair was attended by approximately 200 parents and family members.

Say: "Let us compare these two accounts. I will ask you questions, and you will work with your partner."

Say: "First — what is the focus of each account?"

Allow 1 minute. Discuss:
Expected: Passage A focuses on one student's personal experience — her nervousness, her project, her friend, her feelings when she won. Passage B focuses on the overall event — the number of students, the judging criteria, the winners, and the principal's comments.

Say: "Second — what information does each account include that the other does not?"

Allow 1 minute. Discuss:
Expected: Only in A: personal feelings (nervousness, happiness), sensory details (trembling hands, knocking over plants), the experience of being questioned by judges. Only in B: total number of students (54), judging criteria, all three winners and their projects, the judges' names, number of attendees (200), principal's quote.

Say: "Third — how are the perspectives different?"

Expected: The student sees the science fair as a personal experience — a nervous, exciting day. The newsletter sees it as a school event worth reporting on for the broader community.

Say: "Now, fill in your Venn diagram with at least two items in each section: Firsthand Only, Both, and Secondhand Only."

Allow 2 minutes. Circulate and check for accuracy.

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently compare a firsthand and secondhand account of the same event.

**Event: The Blizzard of January 2018**

**Passage A: Firsthand — Personal Blog Post**

> I woke up on January 4th to the sound of absolute silence — which is how I knew something was wrong. Usually I can hear traffic on Route 9 from my bedroom window, but that morning there was nothing. I pulled back the curtain and gasped. Snow was piled up past the bottom of our windows, and it was still coming down hard. My dad came in and said the schools were closed and the roads were blocked. We spent the whole day inside. My little brother and I played board games while Mom made hot chocolate and Dad tried to shovel the driveway — he came back inside after twenty minutes saying it was useless. The power went out around noon, and we used flashlights and candles for the rest of the day. It was cold in the house, so we all slept in the living room with extra blankets. I have to admit, even though it was a little scary, part of me loved it. It felt like an adventure. We did not get our power back for two full days.

**Passage B: Secondhand — Local Newspaper Report**

> A powerful winter storm struck the region on January 4, 2018, dropping between 18 and 24 inches of snow across the county and bringing wind gusts of up to 55 miles per hour. The storm, which meteorologists had been tracking for several days, caused widespread power outages affecting approximately 35,000 homes and businesses. All schools in the county were closed for three consecutive days. Governor Patricia Morales declared a state of emergency and activated the National Guard to assist with snow removal and emergency services. Road crews worked around the clock, but many secondary roads remained impassable for more than 48 hours. Emergency shelters were opened at four locations for residents who lost heat. Three storm-related deaths were reported in the county, and dozens of people were treated at hospitals for injuries from falls and car accidents. Power was fully restored to all customers by January 7. County officials estimated total damage from the storm at approximately 12 million dollars.

**Questions:**
1. Complete a Venn diagram comparing these two accounts. Write at least two items in each section (Firsthand Only, Both, Secondhand Only).
2. What is the focus of each account? How are the focuses different?
3. What information does the firsthand account include that the secondhand account does not?
4. What information does the secondhand account include that the firsthand account does not?
5. How does each author's perspective shape the way they describe the storm? Use specific examples from both passages.
6. Why is it valuable to read BOTH accounts of this event? What do you gain from each one?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1–5 as written above.
**Stretch:** Answer all six questions, with a detailed response for question 6.
**Challenge:** Answer all six questions. Then write a paragraph explaining which account you think gives a better understanding of the blizzard and why. Support your opinion with specific evidence from both passages.

### Interleaved Review
7. (Lesson 1 review) What is the main idea of Passage B? Write it as a complete sentence.
8. (Lesson 3 review) Identify two cause-and-effect relationships in Passage B.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read these two short passages about the same event:

**Firsthand:** "I stood in the crowd as the mayor cut the big red ribbon across the entrance to the new playground. My little sister squeezed my hand because she was so excited. I could smell popcorn from the snack stand, and I could hear people clapping and cheering. It was a great day for our neighborhood."

**Secondhand:** "On Saturday, March 15, Mayor Sandra Kim officially opened the Riverside Park Playground. The $250,000 project, funded by city and state grants, features new climbing structures, swings, a splash pad, and an accessible ramp. Approximately 300 residents attended the ceremony."

1. Name one difference in focus between the two accounts. (Expected: The firsthand account focuses on the personal experience of being in the crowd. The secondhand account focuses on facts about the project and the ceremony.)
2. What information is in the secondhand account but NOT in the firsthand account? (Expected: The date, the mayor's full name, the cost, the funding sources, the specific playground features, and the number of attendees.)
3. What does the firsthand account include that the secondhand account does not? (Expected: Personal feelings and sensory details — the sister's excitement, the smell of popcorn, the sound of clapping and cheering.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct, with specific details from both passages cited**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Use the Venn diagram graphic organizer to visually map the two accounts side by side
- Review the three comparison areas (focus, information, perspective) one at a time
- Reread the worked examples from Direct Instruction and talk through the comparison aloud

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8 on reading charts and graphs
- Congratulate the student: "You have now completed our three lessons on firsthand and secondhand accounts. These skills will help you be a more critical reader."

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Write Both Accounts:** Give students a brief scenario (e.g., a school field trip to a museum). Have them write a three- to four-sentence firsthand account and a three- to four-sentence secondhand account of the same event. Then have a partner compare the two.
2. **Historical Comparison:** Find a brief firsthand account from history (a short excerpt from a diary or letter) and a brief secondhand account about the same event (a textbook entry). Have students fill in a comparison chart.
3. **Debate: Which Is More Valuable?** Divide students into two groups — one argues that firsthand accounts are more valuable, the other argues for secondhand accounts. After the debate, conclude that both types are important and they work best together.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is the culmination of the Lessons 5–6–7 arc on accounts. Students should come in with a solid understanding of both types. If they seem uncertain, spend a few extra minutes on the warm-up review.
- The Venn diagram is a critical tool for this lesson. Make sure students know how to use it: items that are true of BOTH accounts go in the overlapping center section.
- Some students may struggle to see that both accounts describe the same event because they look so different. Start by identifying the shared event ("What event are both passages about?") before comparing details.
- Emphasize that neither type of account is "better" — they serve different purposes. Firsthand accounts bring emotional depth and personal detail. Secondhand accounts bring factual breadth and context.
- The science fair passages in Guided Practice are a relatable example for students. Point out that they would write a firsthand account if they described their own science fair project, but a secondhand account if they wrote a report about the whole school event.
- For the blizzard independent practice, the contrast is especially clear: one family's cozy-but-scary experience vs. the devastating regional impact. This helps students see how perspective shapes what details are included.

- Perspectives: The student sees the fair as a personal milestone. The newsletter sees it as a school community event.

**Independent Practice (Blizzard):**
1. Venn Diagram:
   - Firsthand Only: Personal feelings (excited, scared, adventurous), sensory details (silence, gasping at snow), family activities (board games, hot chocolate), sleeping in living room
   - Both: Snow storm on January 4, schools closed, power outages, roads blocked/impassable, power out for about two days
   - Secondhand Only: 18–24 inches of snow, 55 mph wind gusts, 35,000 homes lost power, governor declared state of emergency, National Guard activated, emergency shelters, 3 deaths, $12 million in damage, power restored January 7
2. Passage A focuses on one family's experience at home during the storm. Passage B focuses on the storm's impact across the entire county with data and official responses.
3. Firsthand includes: personal emotions (gasping, fear, excitement), sensory details (silence, seeing snow piled past windows), family activities, the experience of losing power and sleeping in the living room.
4. Secondhand includes: snowfall totals, wind speeds, number of homes affected, governor's emergency declaration, National Guard deployment, shelter locations, deaths and injuries, total damage estimate, full timeline of power restoration.
5. The firsthand author sees the storm as a personal adventure — scary but also exciting, focused on family togetherness. The secondhand author sees the storm as a regional emergency — focused on public safety, damage, and government response. For example, the firsthand author describes playing board games and drinking hot chocolate, while the secondhand author reports three deaths and $12 million in damage.
6. The firsthand account helps us understand what it was actually like to live through the storm — the personal experience. The secondhand account helps us understand the full scope and impact of the storm. Together, they give us both the human story and the factual picture.
7. (Review) Main idea: A powerful winter storm struck the region on January 4, 2018, causing major damage, power outages, and disruptions across the county.
8. (Review) Cause-effect: Because the storm dropped 18–24 inches of snow with 55 mph winds, approximately 35,000 homes lost power. Because roads were impassable, the governor declared a state of emergency and activated the National Guard.
