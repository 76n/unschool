# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic
Diagrams, Timelines, and Maps

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read and interpret information presented in diagrams, timelines, and maps
- Explain how a visual element (diagram, timeline, or map) supports and adds to the information in a text
- Use information from both the text and a visual element to answer questions

## Prerequisites
- Ability to read and interpret bar graphs, line graphs, and pie charts (Lesson 8)
- Ability to identify main idea and key details (Lesson 1)
- Understanding of sequence and cause-effect in informational text (Lesson 3)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages with accompanying diagrams, timelines, and maps (described in text format)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Blank paper for student-drawn diagrams or timelines
- Pencils, colored pencils, and rulers
- Highlighters (optional)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect to Lesson 8 on graphs and introduce three new types of visual information.

**Activity: Three Ways to Show Information**

Say: "In our last lesson, we learned how to read bar graphs, line graphs, and pie charts. Today we are going to learn about three more types of visual information that appear in informational texts: diagrams, timelines, and maps."

Write on the board:
- **Diagram:** A labeled drawing that shows the parts of something or how something works
- **Timeline:** A visual that shows events in the order they happened along a line
- **Map:** A visual that shows the location of places or how places are connected

Say: "Let me give you a quick example of each. If you were reading about the parts of a flower, you might see a diagram with labels pointing to the petals, stem, and roots. If you were reading about the history of flight, you might see a timeline showing when the Wright Brothers flew and when the first jet was built. If you were reading about Lewis and Clark, you might see a map showing the route they traveled."

Say: "These visuals are not just decoration — they give us information that the text alone cannot always show clearly. Today we are going to practice reading all three types."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to read and interpret diagrams, timelines, and maps alongside informational text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Diagram:** A labeled drawing that shows the parts of something, how something is built, or how a process works
- **Timeline:** A line that shows events arranged in the order they happened, usually with dates or time periods
- **Map:** A drawing that shows the location, size, or shape of places on Earth
- **Label:** A word or phrase on a diagram, timeline, or map that identifies a specific part or feature
- **Caption:** A short explanation that appears below or beside a visual element

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Just like with graphs, we use three steps when reading any visual element in a text."

Write on the board:
1. **Read the title or caption.** What is this visual about?
2. **Read the labels.** What parts, events, or places are identified?
3. **Connect to the text.** How does this visual support or add to what the passage says?

Say: "Let me show you how this works with each type of visual."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Diagram**

**Passage: "Inside a Volcano"**

> A volcano is an opening in Earth's surface where hot melted rock, called magma, pushes up from deep below. When a volcano erupts, the magma rises through a central channel called the conduit and reaches the surface. Once it flows out onto the ground, it is called lava. The opening at the top of the volcano is called the crater. Over time, layers of cooled lava and ash build up around the crater, forming the cone-shaped mountain that we recognize as a volcano. Deep below the surface, a large pool of magma called the magma chamber feeds the volcano.

**Diagram: "Cross-Section of a Volcano"**
```
                    [Crater] — the opening at the top
                    /        \
                   /          \
                  /   [Lava]   \  — flowing down the sides
                 / flows here   \
                /________________\
               |                  |
               |   [Conduit]      |  — the central channel
               |   (tube going    |
               |    straight      |
               |    down)         |
               |__________________|
               |                  |
               | [Magma Chamber]  |  — the pool of magma deep underground
               |__________________|

Labels with arrows pointing to:
- Crater (top opening)
- Lava (flowing out of crater and down the sides)
- Conduit (vertical tube from magma chamber to crater)
- Magma Chamber (large pool at the bottom)
- Layers of lava and ash (the sloped sides of the cone)
```

Say: "Step 1 — The title says this is a cross-section of a volcano. A cross-section means we are looking at the inside, as if we sliced it in half."

Say: "Step 2 — The labels show us the crater at the top, lava flowing down the sides, the conduit going straight down through the middle, and the magma chamber deep underground."

Say: "Step 3 — How does this diagram connect to the text? The passage tells us that magma rises through the conduit. The diagram shows us where the conduit actually is — a tube running from the magma chamber to the crater. The passage tells us that layers of lava and ash form the cone shape. The diagram shows us those layers on the sloped sides. The diagram makes it much easier to picture the structure that the passage describes in words."

**Example 2: Timeline**

**Passage: "The History of Powered Flight"**

> The dream of human flight became reality in the early 1900s and has advanced rapidly ever since. The Wright Brothers made the first successful powered airplane flight in 1903. Their flight lasted only 12 seconds and covered 120 feet. Over the next few decades, airplanes became faster and more reliable. Charles Lindbergh made the first solo nonstop flight across the Atlantic Ocean in 1927. During World War II, jet engines were developed, and the first jet-powered aircraft flew in 1942. After the war, commercial jet travel became available to the public, with the first regular jet passenger service beginning in 1952. In 1969, the supersonic Concorde made its first flight, traveling faster than the speed of sound. Today, modern aircraft can carry hundreds of passengers across oceans in a matter of hours.

**Timeline: "Key Events in the History of Powered Flight"**
```
1903 -------- 1927 -------- 1942 -------- 1952 -------- 1969
  |             |             |             |             |
  Wright        Lindbergh     First         First         Concorde
  Brothers'     flies solo    jet-powered   regular jet   makes
  first         across the    aircraft      passenger     first
  flight        Atlantic      flies         service       flight
  (12 sec,                                  begins        (faster than
  120 ft)                                                 sound)
```

Say: "This timeline shows five key events from the passage, arranged in order along a line."

Say: "The passage describes these events in paragraphs. The timeline lets me see all of them at once and understand how much time passed between each one. I can see that 24 years passed between the Wright Brothers' flight and Lindbergh's flight, but only 10 years between the first jet aircraft and regular passenger service. The timeline makes the pace of change visible in a way the text alone does not."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice reading a map alongside text with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us practice with a map. This passage is about the Oregon Trail. Read the passage, then look at the map."

**Passage: "The Oregon Trail"**

> In the mid-1800s, hundreds of thousands of American settlers traveled west on the Oregon Trail, one of the longest overland routes in North American history. The trail began in Independence, Missouri, near the Missouri River. From there, travelers headed northwest across the Great Plains of present-day Kansas and Nebraska. The trail then followed the North Platte River into present-day Wyoming, where settlers crossed the Rocky Mountains through a gap called South Pass. After crossing the mountains, the trail continued through present-day Idaho, following the Snake River. The final stretch of the journey took settlers through the Blue Mountains and into the Willamette Valley in Oregon. The entire trip covered about 2,000 miles and took four to six months by covered wagon. Travelers faced many dangers along the way, including river crossings, harsh weather, illness, and difficult mountain terrain.

**Map: "The Oregon Trail Route"**

  |
  v

  |
  v

  |

  |

```

Say: "Step 1 — What is this map about?"

Expected: "The route of the Oregon Trail."

Say: "Step 2 — What labels and features do you see? Work with your partner to list at least five."

Allow 1 minute. Discuss:
Expected: Independence (Missouri), Kansas, Nebraska, Wyoming, Idaho, Oregon, North Platte River, South Pass, Snake River, Blue Mountains, Willamette Valley, Missouri River.

Say: "Step 3 — How does the map connect to the text? Let me give you two connections, and then you find two more with your partner."

Examples:
- The passage says the trail went from Independence, Missouri to the Willamette Valley in Oregon. The map shows both of these places marked with stars.
- The passage says travelers crossed the Rocky Mountains through South Pass. The map shows South Pass labeled in Wyoming with mountain symbols.

Allow 1 minute for students to find more connections.

Expected student connections:
- The passage says travelers followed the North Platte River. The map shows this river in Nebraska.
- The passage says the trail went through Idaho following the Snake River. The map shows the Snake River in Idaho.

Say: "The map helps us see the geography — where the trail went, which states it crossed, and how rivers and mountains shaped the route. The text tells us the human story — the dangers, the time it took, and why people made the journey. Together, they give us a much richer understanding."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently read text with a diagram and answer questions.

**Passage: "The Water Cycle"**

> Water is constantly moving between Earth's surface and the atmosphere in a process called the water cycle. The cycle begins when the sun heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers. This heat causes some of the water to evaporate, which means it turns from liquid water into water vapor, an invisible gas that rises into the air. As the water vapor rises higher into the atmosphere, it cools down. When it cools enough, it condenses, meaning it turns back into tiny water droplets. These droplets gather together to form clouds. When the droplets in a cloud become heavy enough, they fall back to Earth as precipitation — rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Some of this precipitation flows across the land into streams and rivers, eventually reaching the ocean. Some soaks into the ground and becomes groundwater. Then the cycle begins again as the sun heats the water once more.

**Diagram: "The Water Cycle"**
```
                        [CLOUDS]
                     (condensation happens
                      here — water vapor
                      cools into droplets)
                    /                    \
                   /                      \
          [EVAPORATION]              [PRECIPITATION]
          (sun heats water;          (rain, snow, sleet,
           liquid turns to            or hail falls from
           water vapor;               clouds back to Earth)
           vapor rises)                    |
              ^                            v
              |                    [COLLECTION]
              |                    (water flows into rivers,
              |                     streams, lakes, and oceans;
              |                     some soaks into ground
              |                     as groundwater)
              |                            |
              +----------------------------+
                    (cycle repeats)

Labels:
- Sun (drawn with rays, heating the water below)
- Ocean / Lake (body of water at the bottom)
- Water vapor (invisible gas rising upward)
- Clouds (formed by condensed water droplets)
- Rain / Snow (falling from clouds)
- Rivers and streams (water flowing across land)
- Groundwater (water soaking into the earth)

Arrows show: water moves from collection → evaporation → condensation → precipitation → collection
```

**Questions:**
1. What process does this diagram show?
2. According to the diagram, what happens to water during evaporation?
3. The passage says water vapor "condenses" when it cools. Where does condensation appear in the diagram, and what does the diagram show happening at that stage?
4. Name two things the diagram shows that make the water cycle easier to understand than reading the text alone.
5. The passage mentions groundwater. Where does groundwater appear in the diagram, and what role does it play in the water cycle?
6. Using both the passage and the diagram, explain the water cycle in four steps, starting with evaporation.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1–5 as written above.
**Stretch:** Answer all six questions, with question 6 written in your own words.
**Challenge:** Answer all six questions. Then explain what might happen to the water cycle if there were no sun. Use details from both the text and the diagram.

### Interleaved Review
7. (Lesson 4 review) This passage explains a scientific process. List the steps of the water cycle in order, as you learned to do in Lesson 4.
8. (Lesson 8 review) If you wanted to show the average monthly rainfall in a city over one year, which type of graph from Lesson 8 would be the best choice? Explain why.

**Exit Ticket:**

Read the passage and timeline, then answer the questions.

**Timeline: "Early American Space Milestones"**

  Alan          John Glenn    First U.S.    First         Apollo 11:
  Shepard:      orbits        spacewalk     spacecraft    first
  first         Earth         (Ed White)    docks in      moon
  American                                  orbit         landing

```

1. According to the timeline, what happened in 1965? (Expected: The first U.S. spacewalk, performed by Ed White.)
2. How many years passed between the first American in space and the first moon landing? (Expected: 8 years, from 1961 to 1969.)
3. How does the timeline support the passage's claim that the space program made "remarkable progress in less than a decade"? (Expected: The timeline shows that in just 8 years, the program went from putting one person briefly into space to landing astronauts on the moon. You can see five major milestones happening in a short span of time.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct, with question 3 connecting the visual to the text**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three steps for reading visual elements (title, labels, connect to text)
- Return to the volcano diagram and walk through it step by step with the student
- Practice reading a simple timeline with only three events before moving to more complex examples

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10 (unit review)
- Encourage the student to look for diagrams, timelines, and maps in their textbooks and describe what information they add

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Create a Timeline:** Students create a timeline of their own life with at least five important events (year born, first day of school, a family event, a move, starting fourth grade).
2. **Diagram Challenge:** Students draw and label a diagram of something they know well — a bicycle, a fish tank, a plant, the inside of a house. They then write a short paragraph that the diagram would accompany.
3. **Map Your Neighborhood:** Students draw a simple map of their neighborhood or school, labeling key locations. They write a short paragraph describing a route from one place to another, and a partner follows the route on the map to see if the paragraph and map match.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Visual elements are described in text format in this lesson. Whenever possible, draw or project actual visuals for students. A quick whiteboard sketch of the volcano cross-section or the water cycle diagram significantly enhances understanding.
- Students may need reminders about how to read a map (orientation, symbols, labels) or a timeline (left-to-right or top-to-bottom = earlier to later). Review these conventions briefly if needed.
- The Oregon Trail map passage provides a natural connection to social studies. If your students are studying westward expansion, this passage will reinforce content knowledge.
- The water cycle diagram is a classic example of how a diagram can show a process more clearly than text alone. Emphasize that the arrows in the diagram show the direction of movement — water evaporates upward, condenses in clouds, precipitates downward, and collects below.
- Some students may struggle with the concept of cross-sections (the volcano diagram). Compare it to cutting a sandwich in half to see the layers inside.
- This lesson and Lesson 8 together cover RI.4.7 comprehensively. Reinforce that graphs show data (numbers), diagrams show parts or processes, timelines show sequences, and maps show locations — but all of them add to the reader's understanding of the text.
