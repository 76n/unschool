# English Language Arts — Grade 4 — Unit 2, Lesson 8

## Topic
Reading Charts and Graphs

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read and interpret data presented in bar graphs, line graphs, and pie charts
- Connect information from a chart or graph to information in the accompanying text
- Explain how a visual element adds to the reader's understanding of a text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify main idea and key details (Lesson 1)
- Basic understanding of numbers and simple data (from math instruction)
- Ability to read informational text and locate specific details

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages with accompanying charts/graphs (described in text format)
- Graph paper or printed chart templates
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils, colored pencils, and paper for student responses
- Ruler (optional, for drawing graphs)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about graphs and build excitement for reading data visually.

**Activity: Data All Around Us**

Say: "Today we are going to learn how to read charts and graphs. Before we start, think about this: Where have you seen charts or graphs in real life? Maybe on a weather report, in a math textbook, on a food label, or on a sports scoreboard?"

Allow students to share examples.

Say: "Charts and graphs are everywhere because they make information easier to see and understand. Imagine if I said, 'In our class, 12 students like pizza best, 8 like tacos, 5 like spaghetti, and 3 like salad.' That is a lot of numbers to remember. But what if I showed you a bar graph?"

Draw a simple bar graph on the board:

```
Favorite Lunch Foods
Pizza:      ||||||||||||  (12)
Tacos:      ||||||||      (8)
Spaghetti:  |||||         (5)
Salad:      |||           (3)
```

Say: "Now you can see the information at a glance. Pizza is the most popular, and salad is the least popular. That is the power of a graph — it turns numbers into a picture."

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to read three types of graphs and connect visual data to text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Bar graph:** A graph that uses rectangular bars of different lengths to compare amounts or quantities
- **Line graph:** A graph that uses points connected by lines to show how something changes over time
- **Pie chart:** A circular graph divided into sections (like slices of a pie) to show parts of a whole
- **Axis:** The horizontal (bottom) or vertical (side) line of a graph that shows labels and numbers
- **Title:** The name of the graph that tells you what data it shows
- **Key/Legend:** A guide that explains what colors, patterns, or symbols in the graph mean

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When you come across a chart or graph while reading, follow these three steps."

Write on the board:
1. **Read the title.** What is this graph about?
2. **Read the labels.** What do the axes, sections, or columns represent?
3. **Read the data.** What specific numbers or amounts does the graph show?

Say: "Then ask yourself: How does this graph connect to what I just read in the text? Let me show you with each type of graph."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Bar Graph**

Say: "Read this passage, and then look at the bar graph that goes with it."

**Passage: "Pets in Fourth Grade"**

> Mrs. Delgado's fourth-grade class conducted a survey to find out what types of pets students had at home. Every student in the class participated, and some students had more than one type of pet. The results showed that dogs were the most popular pet, followed by cats. Fish and birds were less common, and only a few students had reptiles like lizards or turtles. The class displayed their results in a bar graph on the hallway bulletin board.

**Bar Graph: "Types of Pets Owned by Mrs. Delgado's Class"**
```
Vertical axis (number of students): 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14
Horizontal axis (type of pet): Dogs, Cats, Fish, Birds, Reptiles

Dogs:     14 students (bar reaches 14)
Cats:     9 students (bar reaches 9)
Fish:     6 students (bar reaches 6)
Birds:    4 students (bar reaches 4)
Reptiles: 2 students (bar reaches 2)
```

Say: "Step 1 — Read the title. This graph is about types of pets owned by Mrs. Delgado's class."

Say: "Step 2 — Read the labels. The bottom shows the types of pets. The side shows the number of students."

Say: "Step 3 — Read the data. Dogs: 14 students. Cats: 9 students. Fish: 6. Birds: 4. Reptiles: 2."

Say: "Now, how does this graph connect to the text? The passage says dogs were the most popular pet. The graph confirms this — dogs have the tallest bar at 14. The passage says fish and birds were less common. The graph shows fish at 6 and birds at 4. The graph gives us the exact numbers that the text describes in words."

**Example 2: Line Graph**

**Passage: "Temperature Changes in March"**

> During the month of March, the weather in Oakville changed dramatically. The first week of March was still cold, with temperatures barely reaching the high 30s. By the middle of the month, a warm front moved in and temperatures climbed into the 50s. The last week of March brought a brief cold snap before temperatures rose again at the very end of the month.

**Line Graph: "Average Weekly Temperature in Oakville, March"**
```
Vertical axis (temperature in °F): 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60
Horizontal axis (week): Week 1, Week 2, Week 3, Week 4

Week 1: 36°F (point at 36)
Week 2: 48°F (line rises to 48)
Week 3: 42°F (line dips to 42)
Week 4: 54°F (line rises to 54)

Points connected by lines showing: rise from Week 1 to 2, dip at Week 3, rise again at Week 4.
```

Say: "A line graph shows change over time. The line goes up when the value increases and down when it decreases."

Say: "The passage says the first week was cold — the graph shows 36 degrees. The passage says temperatures climbed by mid-month — the graph shows a jump to 48 degrees in Week 2. The passage mentions a 'brief cold snap' — the graph shows a dip to 42 degrees in Week 3. Then the passage says temperatures rose again — the graph ends at 54 degrees in Week 4."

Say: "The graph and the text tell us the same story, but the graph makes the pattern easier to see. You can literally see the line going up, dipping down, and going up again."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice reading a graph and connecting it to text with facilitator support.

Say: "Now let us try one together. Read the passage and the pie chart, and then answer my questions."

**Passage: "How Students Get to School"**

> Maple Elementary School surveyed all 200 of its students to find out how they travel to school each morning. The most common way to get to school was by car, with nearly half of all students being driven by a parent or guardian. The school bus was the second most popular choice. A smaller number of students walked to school, and a very small group rode bicycles. The school used the results to plan safer crosswalks and additional bicycle racks.

**Pie Chart: "How Maple Elementary Students Travel to School"**
```
Total: 200 students

Car:     90 students  (45% of the circle — the largest slice, nearly half)
Bus:     60 students  (30% of the circle — the second largest slice)
Walk:    36 students  (18% of the circle — a smaller slice)
Bicycle: 14 students  (7% of the circle — the smallest slice)
```

Say: "Step 1 — What is this pie chart about? Talk to your partner."

Expected: "How Maple Elementary students travel to school."

Say: "Step 2 — What do the sections represent?"

Expected: Four ways of getting to school: car, bus, walk, bicycle.

Say: "Step 3 — What does the data tell us? What is the biggest slice? The smallest?"

Expected: Car is the biggest (90 students, 45%). Bicycle is the smallest (14 students, 7%).

Say: "Now, how does the pie chart connect to the passage? The text says 'nearly half' of students are driven by car. Does the chart support that?"

Expected: Yes — 90 out of 200 is 45%, which is nearly half.

Say: "The text also says the school used the results to plan safer crosswalks and bicycle racks. Which data supports that decision?"

Expected: 36 students walk (so crosswalks matter) and 14 ride bicycles (so bike racks are needed).

Say: "Excellent. The text tells us what the school decided. The graph shows us the data that led to that decision. Together, they give us a fuller understanding."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently read text with an accompanying graph and answer questions.

**Passage: "Rainfall in Riverdale"**

> The town of Riverdale experienced an unusual year of rainfall. The year started dry, with very little rain in January and February. Spring brought moderate rainfall in March and April, which helped local farmers prepare their fields. However, May and June were much wetter than usual, and some low-lying areas experienced minor flooding. July and August returned to drier conditions as summer heat set in. The fall months of September and October saw average rainfall, but November and December were drier than expected. Town officials used this data to plan for water conservation during dry months and flood prevention during wet ones.

**Bar Graph: "Monthly Rainfall in Riverdale (in inches)"**
```
Vertical axis (inches of rain): 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6
Horizontal axis (month): Jan, Feb, Mar, Apr, May, Jun, Jul, Aug, Sep, Oct, Nov, Dec

January:   0.8 inches
February:  1.0 inches
March:     2.5 inches
April:     3.0 inches
May:       5.2 inches
June:      5.8 inches
July:      1.5 inches
August:    1.2 inches
September: 2.8 inches
October:   2.5 inches
November:  1.3 inches
December:  0.9 inches
```

**Questions:**
1. According to the graph, which month had the most rainfall? How many inches fell?
2. The passage says the year "started dry." What data from the graph supports this statement?
3. The passage mentions "minor flooding" in some areas. Which months likely caused this flooding? Use the graph to support your answer.
4. The passage says July and August "returned to drier conditions." What does the graph show for those months?
5. How does the bar graph help you understand the passage better? What can you see in the graph that is harder to see in the text alone?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1–5 as written above.
**Stretch:** Answer all five questions. Then calculate the total rainfall for the year by adding all twelve monthly amounts.
**Challenge:** Answer all questions and calculate the yearly total. Then write a paragraph explaining which three months town officials should focus on most for flood prevention and why, using both the text and the graph as evidence.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Lesson 1 review) What is the main idea of the passage about Riverdale's rainfall?
7. (Lesson 2 review) Write a two-sentence summary of the passage using the SKIP method.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short passage and graph:

**Passage:** "The fourth-grade classes at Hillside School read a total of 340 books during the spring reading challenge. Mrs. Kim's class read the most, while Mr. Santos's class read the fewest."

**Bar Graph: "Books Read During Spring Reading Challenge"**
```
Mrs. Kim's class:    120 books
Ms. Patel's class:   95 books
Mr. Santos's class:  55 books
Mrs. Lee's class:    70 books
```

1. Which class read the most books? How many? (Expected: Mrs. Kim's class, 120 books)
2. Does the graph support the passage's claim that Mr. Santos's class read the fewest? (Expected: Yes — Mr. Santos's class read 55 books, which is the lowest bar on the graph.)
3. How does the graph add to your understanding of the passage? (Expected: The passage says 340 books were read total, but the graph shows exactly how many each class read, letting you compare the classes.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct, with question 3 explaining how the graph adds information beyond the text**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three steps for reading a graph (title, labels, data)
- Practice with the warm-up bar graph example, asking simple questions about the data
- Have the student point to specific bars/sections as they answer to build the connection between visual and verbal

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9 on diagrams, timelines, and maps
- Challenge the student to find a graph in a newspaper, magazine, or website and explain what it shows

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Create Your Own Graph:** Students survey classmates about a topic (favorite season, number of siblings, hours of sleep) and create their own bar graph to display the results. Then they write a short paragraph that the graph could accompany.
2. **Graph Detective:** Give students a graph without any accompanying text. They must write a short paragraph that accurately describes the data shown in the graph.
3. **Misleading Graphs:** Show students two graphs of the same data — one with an accurate scale and one with a manipulated scale that exaggerates differences. Discuss how graphs can sometimes be misleading and why it is important to read the numbers carefully.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Since this is a language arts lesson (not math), the focus is on interpreting graphs as part of informational text, not on constructing graphs mathematically. Students should understand how a graph adds meaning to a passage.
- Graphs are described in text format in this lesson. If possible, draw or project actual visual graphs for students. The text descriptions are designed to be easily converted into visuals on a whiteboard or printed handout.
- Some students may need help reading the scale on a graph. Review how to read the vertical axis (each line or number represents a specific amount) before asking interpretation questions.
- Emphasize the connection between text and graph. Always ask: "What does the text say? What does the graph show? How do they work together?" This is the core of RI.4.7.
- Pie charts can be challenging because students need to understand parts of a whole. If needed, use a pizza analogy: "If the whole pizza represents all 200 students, this slice is 90 students — almost half the pizza."
- For students who are strong in math, the Stretch and Challenge tiers provide opportunities to calculate totals and percentages, reinforcing cross-curricular skills.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice (Pie Chart):**
- Title: How Maple Elementary Students Travel to School
- Sections: Car (90, 45%), Bus (60, 30%), Walk (36, 18%), Bicycle (14, 7%)
- Biggest slice: Car. Smallest slice: Bicycle.
- Connection to text: "Nearly half" = 45% car; crosswalks needed for 36 walkers; bike racks needed for 14 cyclists.

**Independent Practice (Rainfall):**
1. June had the most rainfall: 5.8 inches.
2. January had 0.8 inches and February had 1.0 inches — both are the lowest amounts on the graph, supporting the statement that the year started dry.
3. May (5.2 inches) and June (5.8 inches) likely caused the flooding. These are by far the tallest bars on the graph, showing much more rain than any other months.
4. July had 1.5 inches and August had 1.2 inches — both dropped significantly from the May/June highs, confirming "drier conditions."
5. The graph shows the exact amounts for each month and makes it easy to compare them visually. In the text, it is harder to picture the differences between "moderate," "much wetter," and "drier." The graph shows the pattern at a glance.
6. (Review) Main idea: Riverdale experienced an unusual year of rainfall with very dry and very wet periods, and town officials used the data to plan for water conservation and flood prevention.
7. (Review) Sample summary: "Riverdale had an unusual year of rainfall, starting dry and then getting much wetter than normal in May and June. Town officials used the rainfall data to plan for both water conservation and flood prevention."

**Stretch:** Total rainfall = 0.8 + 1.0 + 2.5 + 3.0 + 5.2 + 5.8 + 1.5 + 1.2 + 2.8 + 2.5 + 1.3 + 0.9 = 28.5 inches.

**Challenge:** The three months to focus on for flood prevention are May (5.2 inches), June (5.8 inches), and April (3.0 inches). May and June had the highest rainfall and caused minor flooding according to the text. April also had above-average rain. By preparing for these months, officials could reduce flooding risks in low-lying areas.

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Mrs. Kim's class, 120 books.
2. Yes. Mr. Santos's class read 55 books, which is the shortest bar and the fewest books of any class.
3. The passage only tells us the total (340) and which class read the most and fewest. The graph shows the exact number for each class, letting us see how they compare — for example, Ms. Patel's class (95) read more than Mrs. Lee's class (70), which the passage does not mention.
