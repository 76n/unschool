# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 4, Lesson 7

## Topic
Using Quotation Marks in Dialogue

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Place quotation marks around the exact words a character speaks
- Use a comma to separate the spoken words from the speaker tag (e.g., said, asked)
- Capitalize the first word of spoken dialogue
- Begin a new paragraph for each new speaker

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 6: Commas in a series (general comma awareness)
- Experience with narrative writing from Unit 3 (characters speaking in stories)
- Ability to write multi-sentence paragraphs

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Colored pencils (two colors — one for spoken words, one for narration)
- A children's book with dialogue (any book where characters talk — for reference)
- Index cards (for the dialogue puzzle activity)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate awareness of dialogue in stories and introduce quotation marks.

**Activity: Spot the Talking**

Write this short passage on paper:

> Mom looked at the clock and said it was time for bed. Leo groaned. But I am not tired he said. Yes you are Mom replied with a smile. You have been yawning for the last ten minutes.

Say: "Read this passage. Something is wrong — it is VERY hard to tell who is talking and what they are saying. Can you figure out what Leo says? What about Mom?"

Let the student struggle with it for a moment. Then say: "It is confusing, right? That is because this passage is missing QUOTATION MARKS and some other punctuation. Quotation marks are the marks that go around the exact words someone says. By the end of today's lesson, you will be able to fix this passage perfectly."

**Lesson 6 Review:** "Add commas to this sentence: 'I packed a sandwich an apple and a juice box for lunch.'" (I packed a sandwich, an apple, and a juice box for lunch.)

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the four key rules for punctuating dialogue.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Dialogue:** The words that characters say in a story — their spoken conversation
- **Quotation marks:** Punctuation marks (" ") that go around the exact words someone speaks
- **Speaker tag:** The part of the sentence that tells WHO is speaking (e.g., "said Mom," "Leo asked," "she whispered")
- **New paragraph:** Starting on a new line (indented) each time a different person speaks

### Instruction Notes

**Rule 1: Quotation Marks Around Spoken Words (3 minutes)**

Say: "Quotation marks look like tiny pairs of curved marks — one pair opens and one pair closes. They go around the EXACT WORDS that someone says out loud. Only the spoken words go inside the marks — not the speaker tag."

Write:
- **"I love pizza,"** said Marco.

Say: "The words Marco actually says out loud are: 'I love pizza.' Those words go inside the quotation marks. The 'said Marco' part is the speaker tag — it tells us who spoke. It stays OUTSIDE the quotation marks."

More examples:
- **"Let's go to the park,"** said Dad.
- Mom asked, **"Did you finish your homework?"**
- **"Watch out!"** yelled the coach.

Say: "Notice how the quotation marks hug the spoken words tightly. They open right before the first spoken word and close right after the last spoken word (and its punctuation)."

**Rule 2: Comma Between Spoken Words and Speaker Tag (3 minutes)**

Say: "The spoken words and the speaker tag are separated by a comma. Where the comma goes depends on whether the speaker tag comes BEFORE or AFTER the spoken words."

Write:

**Pattern A — Speaker tag AFTER:**
- **"I am hungry,"** said Leo.

Say: "When the speaker tag comes after, the comma goes INSIDE the quotation marks, right after the last spoken word. Notice: 'hungry-COMMA' then close quotation mark."

**Pattern B — Speaker tag BEFORE:**
- Mom said, **"Dinner is almost ready."**

Say: "When the speaker tag comes first, the comma goes AFTER the speaker tag, right BEFORE the opening quotation mark. Notice: 'said-COMMA' then open quotation mark."

**Special cases — questions and exclamations:**
- **"Where are my shoes?"** asked Dad. (Question mark replaces the comma.)
- **"Look out!"** shouted Maria. (Exclamation point replaces the comma.)

Say: "If the spoken words are a question or exclamation, the ? or ! goes inside the quotation marks and you do NOT need an additional comma."

**Rule 3: Capitalize the First Word of Spoken Words (2 minutes)**

Say: "The first word that someone says out loud ALWAYS starts with a capital letter, even if it is in the middle of the sentence."

Write:
- Mom said, **"D**inner is almost ready." (Capital D — start of spoken words.)
- **"I** am hungry," said Leo. (Capital I — start of spoken words.)
- Leo asked, **"C**an I have a snack?" (Capital C — start of spoken words.)

Say: "Think of it this way: the spoken words are like a sentence inside the sentence. And every sentence starts with a capital letter."

**Rule 4: New Paragraph for Each New Speaker (4 minutes)**

Say: "When you write a conversation between two or more characters, you start a NEW PARAGRAPH every time a different person speaks. This helps the reader keep track of who is talking."

Write this correctly formatted dialogue:

> Mom looked at the clock.
>
> "It is time for bed," she said.
>
> "But I am not tired!" Leo groaned.
>
> "Yes, you are," Mom replied with a smile. "You have been yawning for the last ten minutes."

Say: "See how each time a new person speaks, the words start on a new line? Mom speaks — new paragraph. Leo speaks — new paragraph. Mom speaks again — new paragraph. This makes the conversation easy to follow."

Now compare to the Warm-Up passage: "Remember how confusing the passage was at the beginning of the lesson? Let's fix it now using all four rules."

Work through the correction together, applying each rule one at a time.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Add quotation marks and a comma: *She said I like your drawing.*

Think aloud: "Who is the speaker? 'She.' What does she say? 'I like your drawing.' I put quotation marks around the spoken words and a comma after 'said': She said, 'I like your drawing.' Wait — I also need to capitalize the first spoken word. 'I' is already capitalized. Done!"

Answer: She said, "I like your drawing."

**Example 2:** Add quotation marks and a comma: *This is fun said Jake.*

Think aloud: "The speaker tag 'said Jake' comes AFTER the spoken words. The spoken words are 'This is fun.' I put quotation marks around them and a comma before the closing quotation mark: 'This is fun,' said Jake."

Answer: "This is fun," said Jake.

**Example 3:** Add quotation marks and correct punctuation: *Where did you go asked Mom.*

Think aloud: "The spoken words are a question: 'Where did you go?' I need a question mark, not a comma, inside the quotation marks."

Answer: "Where did you go?" asked Mom.

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice punctuating dialogue with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Color-Coding Dialogue (5 minutes)**

Write these sentences. The student uses one colored pencil to underline the SPOKEN WORDS and another color to underline the SPEAKER TAG. Then they add the correct punctuation.

1. Come here said the teacher.
   - Spoken words: Come here
   - Speaker tag: said the teacher
   - Corrected: "Come here," said the teacher.

2. Dad asked Are you ready to go
   - Spoken words: Are you ready to go
   - Speaker tag: Dad asked
   - Corrected: Dad asked, "Are you ready to go?"

3. This is the best day ever shouted Maya
   - Spoken words: This is the best day ever
   - Speaker tag: shouted Maya
   - Corrected: "This is the best day ever!" shouted Maya.

4. The coach said You did a great job today
   - Spoken words: You did a great job today
   - Speaker tag: The coach said
   - Corrected: The coach said, "You did a great job today."

5. I found it whispered Sam
   - Spoken words: I found it
   - Speaker tag: whispered Sam
   - Corrected: "I found it," whispered Sam.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student puts quotation marks around the speaker tag too (e.g., "Come here," "said the teacher."), say: "The quotation marks only go around what the person actually SAYS out loud. Did the teacher say 'said the teacher'? No! She said 'Come here.' The 'said the teacher' part tells us who spoke — it stays outside."*

**Activity 2: Write the Dialogue (7 minutes)**

Say: "Now let's practice WRITING dialogue from scratch. I will describe a situation, and you write what the characters say, using correct punctuation."

1. A boy named Ben asks his mom if he can go outside.
   (Example: "Can I go outside?" asked Ben.)

2. Mom says yes, but he needs to wear a jacket.
   (Example: "Yes, but wear your jacket," said Mom.)

3. Ben says thank you and runs to get his jacket.
   (Example: "Thank you!" said Ben.)

4. Write a short exchange (2-3 lines of dialogue) between two friends deciding what game to play. Remember to start a new paragraph for each speaker.

*Facilitator prompt: Walk the student through the punctuation step by step if needed: "First, what does the character say? Write those words. Now put quotation marks around them. Add the speaker tag. Put a comma (or ? or !) in the right place. Capitalize the first spoken word."*

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices dialogue punctuation independently.

**Part A: Fix the Dialogue (4 minutes)**

Each sentence has dialogue errors. Rewrite each sentence correctly:

1. I am going to the store said Mom.
   ("I am going to the store," said Mom.)

2. Dad asked do you want to come with me
   (Dad asked, "Do you want to come with me?")

3. Yes please! said Emma
   ("Yes, please!" said Emma.)

4. Dont forget your shoes said Dad
   ("Don't forget your shoes," said Dad.)

5. She whispered i think i see a deer.
   (She whispered, "I think I see a deer.")

**Part B: Add Punctuation to a Passage (4 minutes)**

Rewrite this passage with correct quotation marks, commas, capitalization, and new paragraphs for each speaker:

> Look at that rainbow said Mia. Wow it is so bright said Leo. I wish I could touch it Mia said. My teacher said there is a pot of gold at the end Leo laughed.

(Correctly punctuated:

"Look at that rainbow!" said Mia.

"Wow, it is so bright!" said Leo.

"I wish I could touch it," Mia said.

"My teacher said there is a pot of gold at the end," Leo laughed.)

**Part C: Write Your Own Dialogue (4 minutes)**

### Difficulty Tiers

- **On-level:** Write a 4-line conversation between two characters. Use correct quotation marks, commas, and new paragraphs for each speaker.
- **Stretch:** Write a 6-line conversation between two characters. Include at least one question, one exclamation, and one regular statement. Use correct punctuation for all three types.
- **Challenge:** Write a short story scene (8-10 sentences) that includes dialogue between two characters AND narration (actions and descriptions between the dialogue). Punctuate all dialogue correctly.

### Interleaved Review

6. **Lesson 5 Review:** Write the superlative of: tall (tallest), good (best), beautiful (most beautiful).

7. **Lesson 1 Review:** Write the past tense of: bake (baked), stop (stopped), hurry (hurried).

8. **Unit 3 Review:** What are "temporal words," and why do writers use them? (Temporal words show the order of events in a story: first, next, then, after that, finally, last. They help the reader follow the sequence.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick assessment of dialogue punctuation.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Punctuate this sentence correctly: *I love ice cream said Ana*
   ("I love ice cream," said Ana.)

2. Punctuate this sentence correctly: *Dad asked Where are you going*
   (Dad asked, "Where are you going?")

3. Why do we start a new paragraph when a different character speaks? (So the reader can tell who is talking.)

**Mastery = Both sentences punctuated correctly AND a reasonable explanation for Rule 4.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on one rule at a time. Master Rule 1 (quotation marks around spoken words) before adding the other rules.
- Use the color-coding strategy: spoken words in one color, speaker tags in another. This visual separation helps.
- Practice with very short dialogue: "Hi," said Sam. "Hi," said Max. Start simple and build up.
- Look at dialogue in a book together and point out each punctuation mark: "See the quotation marks? The comma? The capital letter?"

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 8 (Using a Dictionary). Preview: "You are becoming a punctuation pro! Next lesson, we switch to vocabulary. You will learn how to use a DICTIONARY — a tool that helps you find the meaning, spelling, and pronunciation of any word."

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**

- **Dialogue Rewrite:** Take a scene from a familiar story (a fairy tale, a movie scene, etc.) and write it as a dialogue. Example: Write the conversation between Goldilocks and the Three Bears when they come home and find her sleeping.

- **Comic Strip to Dialogue:** Draw a simple comic strip with speech bubbles. Then rewrite the comic as a narrative passage with properly punctuated dialogue. This helps the student see the connection between speech bubbles and quotation marks.

- **Dialogue Detective:** Read a page from a book that has dialogue. Count how many dialogue rules the author followed. Find examples of: (1) quotation marks, (2) commas, (3) capital letters, and (4) new paragraphs for new speakers.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This is one of the most rule-heavy lessons in the unit. There are four separate rules to learn, and they all need to work together. If the student seems overwhelmed, split this into two sessions. Session 1: Rules 1 and 2 (quotation marks and commas). Session 2: Rules 3 and 4 (capitalization and new paragraphs) plus practice.

**The comma inside the quotation marks:** This is the rule students forget most often. They tend to put the comma after the closing quotation mark ("I am hungry", said Leo) instead of before it ("I am hungry," said Leo). Be patient and consistent — this takes practice. Some students benefit from the phrase: "The comma snuggles up inside the quotation marks."

**New paragraphs for new speakers:** This rule is critical for readable dialogue but can be confusing if the student has not written multi-paragraph pieces before. Show them in a published book how each new speaker starts on a new line. If the student's handwriting makes indentation unclear, use a ruler or draw a line to show where each new paragraph starts.

**Connection to narrative writing:** This lesson connects directly to the narrative writing skills from Unit 3. If the student wrote stories with dialogue in Unit 3, they were probably missing proper punctuation. Now they have the tools to go back and fix those stories. Consider pulling out an old writing piece and adding quotation marks as a practice activity.

**Speaker tags beyond "said":** While "said" is the most common speaker tag, encourage the student to use varied tags: asked, whispered, shouted, yelled, replied, exclaimed, laughed, cried, answered, muttered. Each tag adds character and emotion to the dialogue. However, "said" is perfectly fine for most dialogue — do not overuse exotic tags.

**Do not mark up creative flow:** When the student is writing dialogue creatively, let them get their ideas down first. Then go back and fix the punctuation. Stopping to correct every comma in the middle of a creative burst can kill motivation. Draft first, edit second.
