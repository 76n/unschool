# Facilitator Guide — Grade 3 ELA, Unit 4: Grammar & Vocabulary

**Grade:** 3
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 4 — Grammar & Vocabulary
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Partially facilitated (adult introduces the lesson, supports guided practice, then monitors independent work; student can do warm-up and independent practice more autonomously)
**Standards:** L.3.1d, L.3.1g, L.3.2c, L.3.4d

---

## Unit Overview

This is the final unit in Grade 3 ELA, and it brings together two essential skill areas: grammar and vocabulary. Grammar and vocabulary are not separate subjects to be memorized and forgotten — they are the tools your student uses every time they read a sentence, write a story, or have a conversation. This unit teaches these tools in the context of real reading and writing, so your student sees immediately how grammar makes their own writing clearer and more interesting.

The unit is organized into three blocks:

**Block 1: Verbs (Lessons 1-3)** — Students learn to form regular past tense verbs (adding -ed with its three spellings and three sounds), memorize common irregular past tense verbs (go/went, see/saw, run/ran, etc.), and then put it all together by working with present, past, and future tenses. The goal is for students to use verb tenses correctly and consistently in their own writing.

**Block 2: Adjectives and Punctuation (Lessons 4-7)** — Students learn to compare using adjectives (taller, tallest; more beautiful, most beautiful), master commas in a series, and learn the rules for punctuating dialogue with quotation marks. These lessons connect directly to the narrative writing skills from Unit 3, giving students new tools to make their stories more polished.

**Block 3: Vocabulary Strategies (Lessons 8-9)** — Students learn to use a dictionary and glossary independently, and they practice combining context clues, word parts (prefixes and suffixes), and reference tools to figure out the meanings of unfamiliar words. These are lifelong reading skills.

**Lesson 10** is a cumulative review that brings all the skills together through editing, writing, and games.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Form and use past tense of regular verbs, including spelling rules (doubling, dropping silent e, changing y to i)
- Recognize and use common irregular past tense verbs (went, saw, ran, ate, gave, took, came, made, said, thought)
- Identify and use present, past, and future tense verbs and maintain consistent tense in writing
- Form and use comparative adjectives (-er) and superlative adjectives (-est), including irregular forms (good/better/best, bad/worse/worst)
- Use "more" and "most" with longer adjectives
- Use commas correctly in a series of three or more items
- Punctuate dialogue correctly using quotation marks, commas, and capital letters
- Use a dictionary and glossary to find word meanings, pronunciations, and parts of speech
- Use context clues, word parts (prefixes and suffixes), and reference tools to determine meanings of unfamiliar words
- Edit writing for grammar and punctuation errors

**Why this matters:** Grammar is not about memorizing rules for a test — it is about communicating clearly. A student who can control verb tenses tells better stories. A student who can use commas and quotation marks writes dialogue that readers can follow. A student who can look up words in a dictionary and use context clues becomes an independent reader who does not get stuck on unfamiliar vocabulary. These are practical, everyday skills that strengthen everything your student does in reading and writing.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Read and write complete, multi-sentence paragraphs (Units 1-3)
- [ ] Identify nouns, verbs, and adjectives in simple sentences
- [ ] Write a narrative with characters, setting, and sequenced events (Unit 3)
- [ ] Use basic capitalization and end punctuation correctly
- [ ] Understand that verbs are action words (run, jump, eat, think)
- [ ] Understand that adjectives describe nouns (big, red, happy, tall)
- [ ] Read grade-level text with accuracy and expression (Unit 1)

If the student cannot identify verbs and adjectives in simple sentences, spend a few minutes reviewing before Lesson 1. Use sentences like "The cat jumped over the fence" and ask: "Which word tells what the cat DID?" (jumped — that is the verb) and "Which word describes the cat or the fence?" This basic part-of-speech knowledge is essential for the grammar work in this unit.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Pencil and paper** (used in every lesson)
- **Index cards** (for sorting games, flashcards, and matching activities — you will use these frequently)
- **Colored pencils or markers** (for color-coding parts of sentences — optional but helpful)
- **A children's dictionary** (a physical dictionary is ideal for Lesson 8; online dictionaries work too but a physical book teaches page-navigation skills)
- **A textbook or nonfiction book with a glossary** (for Lesson 8 — any book with a glossary in the back will work)
- **Lined writing paper** (for writing activities)
- **A folder or writing notebook** to collect work across the unit
- **Timer** (optional, for timed sorting games)
- **Sticky notes** (for marking words and sentences)

All practice texts, sentences, word lists, and game materials are included in the lessons. You do not need to purchase any special materials.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions | Notes |
|--------|-------|----------|-------|
| 1 | Regular Verbs — Past Tense | 1 session | Foundation for Lessons 2-3 |
| 2 | Irregular Verbs | 1-2 sessions | Memorization-heavy; some students need extra time |
| 3 | Verb Tenses — Present, Past, Future | 1 session | Pulls together Lessons 1-2 |
| 4 | Comparative Adjectives (-er) | 1 session | Pairs with Lesson 5 |
| 5 | Superlative Adjectives (-est) | 1 session | Builds directly on Lesson 4 |
| 6 | Commas in a Series | 1 session | Punctuation lesson; tends to go quickly |
| 7 | Quotation Marks in Dialogue | 1-2 sessions | Many rules; take two sessions if needed |
| 8 | Using a Dictionary and Glossary | 1-2 sessions | Hands-on; pacing depends on familiarity |
| 9 | Context Clues and Word Meanings | 1 session | Ties together vocabulary strategies |
| 10 | Grammar and Vocabulary Review | 1-2 sessions | Cumulative; can split into review + writing |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session (40-50 minutes) | |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lessons 1-3 (verbs) build on each other sequentially. Do not skip Lesson 1 to get to Lesson 2 — the regular verb patterns are needed before tackling irregular verbs.
- Lessons 4-5 (adjectives) are a natural pair. If the student grasps comparatives quickly, you can move to superlatives in the same session.
- Lesson 7 (quotation marks) has many rules. If the student seems overwhelmed, split it into two sessions: one for the basic rules and one for practice.
- Lesson 8 (dictionary skills) depends heavily on whether the student has used a dictionary before. If this is completely new, plan two sessions and go slowly.
- If the student finishes the unit quickly and scores well on the assessment, the extra time can be used for creative writing projects that apply all the grammar and vocabulary skills.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student adds -ed to irregular verbs ("goed," "runned," "eated") | The student is overgeneralizing the regular past tense rule — this is actually a sign of learning! The student understands that -ed makes past tense, but has not yet memorized the exceptions. | Do not punish this error. Say: "That makes sense — most verbs DO add -ed! But 'go' is a tricky verb that changes to 'went.' Some verbs have to be memorized." Provide extra practice with the irregular verbs list from Lesson 2. Repeated exposure is key. |
| Student writes "more taller" or "most tallest" (doubling the comparison) | The student knows both strategies for comparing (add -er/-est AND use more/most) but does not yet know that you only use ONE at a time. | Say: "You have two good ideas here! With a short word like 'tall,' we just add -er: taller. We do NOT need 'more' because the -er already does that job. 'More' is for longer words like 'more beautiful.'" Practice with a sorting game: short words get -er/-est, long words get more/most. |
| Student writes "goodest" or "baddest" instead of "best" or "worst" | The student is applying the regular -est rule to irregular adjectives. | Say: "'Good' and 'bad' are special words that change completely when we compare. Good becomes better and best. Bad becomes worse and worst. These just have to be memorized — kind of like irregular verbs!" Post the irregular adjective chart where the student can see it. |
| Student puts quotation marks around everything, not just spoken words | The student understands that quotation marks are important but is unsure which words get them. | Return to the rule: "Quotation marks go around the EXACT words someone says out loud — only the spoken words, not the 'she said' part." Use the color-coding strategy from Lesson 7: highlight spoken words in one color and narration in another. |
| Student forgets the comma before the closing quotation mark in dialogue | This is a fine-grained punctuation rule that takes practice. Many adults get this wrong too. | Show the pattern repeatedly: "Hello," said Mom. Point out that the comma goes INSIDE the quotation marks, right after the last spoken word. Have the student practice with five short dialogue sentences until the pattern becomes automatic. |
| Student looks up a word in the dictionary and picks the wrong definition | Dictionaries list multiple definitions, and the student may not know how to choose the right one. | Teach the "try it out" strategy: read the sentence with each definition substituted in. Which one makes sense? Model this process aloud: "The first definition says 'a stick used in baseball.' Let me try that in the sentence... does that make sense? No? Let's try the next one." |
| Student relies only on context clues and refuses to use a dictionary ("I can figure it out") | Context clues are a great first strategy, but sometimes they are not enough for precise understanding. | Validate the strategy: "Using context clues is exactly what good readers do! Sometimes, though, context clues give you a general idea but not the exact meaning. A dictionary gives you the precise definition. Let's try both — use context clues first to make a guess, then check with the dictionary." |
| Student struggles to keep verb tense consistent within a paragraph (switches between past and present) | Tense consistency is a sophisticated writing skill that develops over time. The student may not yet hear the shifts. | Read the paragraph aloud together and exaggerate the verbs: "Yesterday, I WALK to school..." Does that sound right? Train the student's ear first. Then use the "tense check" strategy from Lesson 3: underline every verb and check that they all match. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to reinforce grammar and vocabulary thinking:

- "I heard you say 'I runned to the door.' Can you remember the tricky past tense of 'run'?" (ran)
- "Which is bigger — a dog or a horse? How would you say that using a comparing word?" (A horse is bigger than a dog.)
- "Can you name three things you ate today? Say it in a sentence with commas." (I ate cereal, an apple, and a sandwich.)
- "If a character in your story is talking, how would you write what they say? What punctuation do you need?"
- "You just read a word you did not know. What could you do to figure out what it means?"
- "Tell me about your day yesterday — but use past tense for everything. I walked, I ate, I played..."
- "What is the superlative of 'fast'? What about 'beautiful'?" (fastest; most beautiful)
- "Let's look up a random word in the dictionary. What did we find? What part of speech is it?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- The student cannot identify verbs in simple sentences after multiple explanations (may need more foundational grammar instruction before this unit)
- The student consistently produces irregular verb forms from a different dialect (e.g., "I seen it," "she done it") and shows frustration or confusion when learning standard forms — approach with cultural sensitivity and distinguish between oral and written conventions
- Despite repeated practice, the student cannot retain any irregular verb forms from one session to the next (possible memory or processing concern)
- The student cannot sequence events in order or use any tense markers in speech (may indicate a broader language development need)
- The student shows significant difficulty with alphabetical order, making dictionary use extremely challenging (may need more foundational sequencing practice)
- The student becomes highly frustrated or anxious around grammar activities, even with games and encouragement

If you see these signs, consider:
- Reducing the number of items to memorize at one time (e.g., focus on five irregular verbs instead of ten)
- Using songs, chants, or rhymes to support memory (there are many verb-tense songs available online)
- Allowing the student to create a personal grammar reference notebook that they keep and use during writing
- Breaking lessons into shorter segments with movement breaks in between
- Consulting with a literacy specialist or speech-language pathologist if language concerns persist

## What Comes Next

After completing this unit, Grade 3 ELA is complete. The student is ready to move to **Grade 4 ELA**, which deepens and extends every skill area:

- **Grade 4 Reading Literature** introduces theme, deeper character analysis, and comparing points of view across texts
- **Grade 4 Writing** demands greater sophistication in opinion, informative, and narrative writing, with more developed use of transitional words and typed compositions
- **Grade 4 Grammar & Language** (Unit 4) builds directly on this unit:
  - Relative pronouns and adverbs (who, which, that, where, when)
  - Progressive verb tenses (was walking, is walking, will be walking) — an extension of the three tenses learned here
  - Ordering adjectives within sentences — an extension of comparative/superlative work
  - Choosing words and phrases for effect — applying vocabulary skills in writing

All of the grammar and vocabulary skills from this unit will continue to appear in warm-up reviews throughout Grade 4. Encourage your student to keep their irregular verbs flashcards and grammar reference materials handy for ongoing practice.

Between units, encourage daily reading and writing. Every time the student reads a book and encounters an unfamiliar word, they can practice their vocabulary strategies. Every time they write a story, letter, or journal entry, they can practice verb tenses, comparisons, commas, and quotation marks. Grammar is not something you study once and put away — it is something you use every day.
