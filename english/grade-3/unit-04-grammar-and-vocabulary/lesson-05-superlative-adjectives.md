# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 4, Lesson 5

## Topic
Superlative Adjectives — Comparing Three or More Things

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Form superlative adjectives by adding -est, applying the correct spelling rules
- Use irregular comparative and superlative forms: good/better/best, bad/worse/worst
- Use "more" and "most" for longer adjectives (more beautiful, most beautiful)
- Distinguish between comparative (-er, for two) and superlative (-est, for three or more)

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 4: Comparative adjectives (-er and "than")
- Understanding of spelling rules: doubling, dropping e, changing y to i
- Ability to write complete sentences

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Index cards (for sorting game)
- Three objects of different sizes (e.g., three books, three pencils, three stuffed animals — for hands-on comparison)
- Colored pencils (optional)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review comparative adjectives and introduce the need for superlatives.

**Activity: Two vs. Three**

Say: "Last lesson, you learned to compare TWO things using -er. Quick review!"

1. tall: A giraffe is _______ than a horse. (taller)
2. fast: A jet is _______ than a car. (faster)
3. big: An elephant is _______ than a dog. (bigger)

Now say: "Great! But what if I have THREE things? Look:"

Hold up three objects of different sizes (or draw three things on paper — a small circle, a medium circle, and a large circle).

Say: "This one is big. This one is bigger than the first one. But this one is the... what? What word do I use for the MOST big of all three? The BIGGEST! When I compare three or more things, I use -est instead of -er. Today, we are going to learn how."

**Lesson 4 Review:** "What word do we always use after a comparative adjective?" (than)

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach superlative adjectives (-est), irregular forms, and the more/most rule for longer adjectives.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Superlative adjective:** The form of an adjective that compares THREE or more things and shows which one is the MOST (tallest, fastest, biggest)
- **Irregular adjective:** An adjective that does not follow the regular -er/-est pattern (good, bad)
- **"More" and "most":** Words used instead of -er/-est for longer adjectives

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: The Basic Rule — Adding -est (3 minutes)**

Say: "Comparative is for TWO things. Superlative is for THREE or more. Comparative uses -er. Superlative uses -est."

Write this chart:

| Adjective | Comparative (two things) | Superlative (three or more) |
|-----------|--------------------------|----------------------------|
| tall | tall**er** | tall**est** |
| fast | fast**er** | fast**est** |
| cold | cold**er** | cold**est** |
| long | long**er** | long**est** |
| old | old**er** | old**est** |

Say: "See the pattern? -er for two, -est for three or more."

Write example sentences:
- "Marco is taller than Jake." (Two people — comparative.)
- "Marco is the tallest student in the class." (More than two — superlative.)
- "The cheetah is the fastest animal in the world." (More than two — superlative.)

Say: "Notice that superlatives often use the word 'the' before them: 'the tallest,' 'the fastest,' 'the coldest.' That is because there is only ONE thing that is the most."

**Step 2: Spelling Rules for -est (3 minutes)**

Say: "The same spelling rules from -er apply to -est."

Write this chart:

| Rule | Comparative (-er) | Superlative (-est) |
|------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Just add -er / -est | tall → taller | tall → tall**est** |
| Double the consonant | big → bigger | big → big**gest** |
| Drop the silent e | large → larger | large → larg**est** |
| Change y to i | happy → happier | happy → happ**iest** |

More examples:
- hot → hotter → hot**test**
- nice → nicer → nic**est**
- funny → funnier → funn**iest**
- thin → thinner → thin**nest**

Say: "If you know the spelling rule for -er, you already know it for -est. Same rules!"

**Step 3: Irregular Comparatives and Superlatives (3 minutes)**

Say: "Just like verbs have irregular forms, some adjectives do too. These are the most important ones."

Write this chart in large, clear letters:

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| **good** | **better** | **best** |
| **bad** | **worse** | **worst** |

Say: "'Good' does NOT become 'gooder' or 'goodest.' It becomes 'better' and 'best.' You already know these words! 'She is better than me at drawing.' 'He is the best player on the team.'"

Say: "'Bad' does NOT become 'badder' or 'baddest.' It becomes 'worse' and 'worst.' 'This cold is worse than last time.' 'That was the worst storm of the year.'"

Write example sentences:
- "This cookie is **good**. That one is **better**. But THIS one is the **best**!"
- "Monday's weather was **bad**. Tuesday's was **worse**. Wednesday's was the **worst**."

Say: "These are irregular — you just have to memorize them. But you probably already use 'better,' 'best,' 'worse,' and 'worst' when you talk."

**Step 4: "More" and "Most" for Longer Adjectives (3 minutes)**

Say: "Remember from last lesson, longer adjectives use 'more' instead of -er? The same thing happens with superlatives — they use 'most' instead of -est."

Write this chart:

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| beautiful | more beautiful | **most** beautiful |
| interesting | more interesting | **most** interesting |
| important | more important | **most** important |
| careful | more careful | **most** careful |
| dangerous | more dangerous | **most** dangerous |

Say: "Short adjectives get -er and -est. Long adjectives (three or more syllables) get 'more' and 'most.' NEVER use both: do NOT write 'most tallest' or 'more beautifuler.'"

Write the rule:
- **1-2 syllables:** -er / -est (taller, tallest)
- **3+ syllables:** more / most (more beautiful, most beautiful)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "Fill in: 'Of all the animals at the zoo, the elephant is the _____ (heavy).'"

Think aloud: "'Heavy' — two syllables, ends in consonant + y. I change y to i and add -est: heaviest. And I am comparing three or more animals, so I need the superlative. 'The elephant is the heaviest.'"

**Example 2:** "Fill in: 'This book is the _____ (interesting) book I have ever read.'"

Think aloud: "'Interesting' — four syllables. Too long for -est! I use 'most' instead: 'most interesting.' 'This book is the most interesting book I have ever read.'"

**Example 3:** "Fill in: 'Of the three flavors, chocolate is the _____ (good).'"

Think aloud: "'Good' is irregular! It does not become 'goodest.' The superlative of 'good' is 'best.' 'Of the three flavors, chocolate is the best.'"

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice superlative adjectives and distinguish them from comparatives.

**Activity 1: Complete the Chart (5 minutes)**

Say: "Let's fill in this chart together. I will give you the adjective — you give me the comparative AND the superlative."

| Adjective | Comparative | Superlative |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| small | _______ | _______ |
| hot | _______ | _______ |
| pretty | _______ | _______ |
| wide | _______ | _______ |
| good | _______ | _______ |
| bad | _______ | _______ |
| exciting | _______ | _______ |

(Answers: smaller/smallest, hotter/hottest, prettier/prettiest, wider/widest, better/best, worse/worst, more exciting/most exciting)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student writes "goodest," say: "Tricky one! 'Good' is irregular — it does not follow the rules. The comparative is 'better' and the superlative is 'best.' Just like irregular verbs, these have to be memorized."*

**Activity 2: Comparative or Superlative? (7 minutes)**

Say: "Now I will give you a scenario. You tell me whether to use the comparative or superlative, and then write the sentence."

1. "Compare the height of two buildings." (comparative — Building A is taller than Building B.)
2. "Compare the speed of three runners to find who is the fastest." (superlative — Maria is the fastest runner of the three.)
3. "Compare two desserts — which tastes better?" (comparative — The cake tastes better than the pie.)
4. "Out of all the students in class, who is the funniest?" (superlative — Jake is the funniest student in the class.)
5. "Compare the weather today and yesterday." (comparative — Today is warmer than yesterday.)
6. "Of all the planets, which is the biggest?" (superlative — Jupiter is the biggest planet.)

*Facilitator prompt: The key question is: "Are we comparing TWO things or THREE or more?" Two things = comparative (-er). Three or more = superlative (-est).*

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices forming and using superlative adjectives independently.

**Part A: Write the Superlative Form (3 minutes)**

Write the superlative (-est or "most") form:

1. fast → _______ (fastest)
2. big → _______ (biggest)
3. safe → _______ (safest)
4. happy → _______ (happiest)
5. bad → _______ (worst)
6. beautiful → _______ (most beautiful)
7. good → _______ (best)
8. thin → _______ (thinnest)

**Part B: Fill in the Blank (4 minutes)**

Choose the correct form — comparative or superlative:

9. An oak tree is _______ (tall) than a bush. (taller)
10. Mount Everest is the _______ (tall) mountain in the world. (tallest)
11. This math problem is _______ (hard) than the last one. (harder)
12. That was the _______ (hard) test I have ever taken. (hardest)
13. My sister's cookies are _______ (good) than the store ones. (better)
14. Grandma's cookies are the _______ (good) of all. (best)

**Part C: Write Your Own Superlatives (3 minutes)**

### Difficulty Tiers

- **On-level:** Write three sentences using superlative adjectives about things you know (e.g., animals, foods, places).
- **Stretch:** Write one sentence using each: a regular superlative (-est), an irregular superlative (best or worst), and "most" with a long adjective.
- **Challenge:** Write a paragraph (4-5 sentences) comparing three of something (three animals, three foods, three places). Use at least three superlative adjectives and one comparative. Underline each adjective form.

### Interleaved Review

15. **Lesson 3 Review:** Identify the tense of each verb: "ran" (past), "will eat" (future), "plays" (present).

16. **Unit 2 Review:** What is the "main idea" of a paragraph? How is it different from a detail? (The main idea is the most important point of the paragraph. Details are the smaller facts that support or explain the main idea.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick assessment of superlative adjective formation.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Write the comparative AND superlative of: fast (faster, fastest)
2. Write the comparative AND superlative of: good (better, best)
3. Write a sentence using a superlative adjective.

**Mastery = All four forms correct (faster, fastest, better, best) AND a correct superlative sentence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Create a three-column chart (Adjective / Comparative / Superlative) and practice filling it in with common adjectives
- Focus on the irregular forms (good/better/best, bad/worse/worst) with daily oral practice
- Use physical objects: line up three things by size and say "small, smaller, smallest"
- Post the irregular adjective chart where the student can see it during writing

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6 (Commas in a Series). Preview: "You are doing great with adjectives! Now we are going to shift to punctuation. Next lesson, you will learn how to use commas when you list three or more items in a sentence."

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**

- **Record Book:** Create a "Book of Records" modeled after the Guinness Book. Write entries like: "The blue whale is the largest animal. The cheetah is the fastest land animal. The Sahara is the hottest desert." Illustrate each page. This is a fun, creative project that practices superlatives.

- **Comparative vs. Superlative Quiz Game:** The facilitator says a sentence with a blank, and the student must quickly say whether to use the comparative or superlative form AND give the correct word. Score one point for each correct answer. Can the student get 10 in a row?

- **"More" vs. "-er" Sort:** Write 15 adjectives on index cards (mix of short and long). The student sorts them into two piles: "Use -er/-est" and "Use more/most." Discuss any borderline cases (e.g., "clever" can use either form).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** If the student mastered the spelling rules in Lessons 1 and 4, the -est forms should come easily because the same rules apply. The new content in this lesson is: (1) irregular forms (good/better/best, bad/worse/worst) and (2) the more/most rule for long adjectives. Spend the most time on these.

**The "double comparison" error revisited:** This lesson is where students are most likely to write "most tallest" or "more better." Be ready with the correction: "You only need one! Short words use -est. Long words use most. Irregular words (good, bad) have their own special forms. Pick one."

**When to use -er/-est vs. more/most:** The general rule (1-2 syllables = -er/-est, 3+ syllables = more/most) works for most cases. Two-syllable adjectives are sometimes ambiguous — "gentle" can be "gentler" or "more gentle." For third graders, the simple rule is sufficient. Do not overthink edge cases.

**Connection to Lesson 4:** This lesson is a direct extension of Lesson 4. If the student struggled with comparatives, it may be wise to review Lesson 4 briefly before jumping into superlatives. The concepts are parallel: -er → -est, more → most.

**Irregular forms are high-priority:** "Better/best" and "worse/worst" are among the most common words in English. Students will encounter them constantly in reading and need them in writing. If there is one thing to solidify from this lesson, it is these four irregular forms.

**Making it tangible:** Use real objects throughout the lesson. Line up three books: "This book is thick. This one is thicker. This one is the thickest." Line up three people (or stuffed animals): "This one is tall. This one is taller. This one is the tallest." The concrete experience helps the abstract grammar concept stick.
