# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3 Assessment

## Unit: Writing

**Assessment Type:** Summative (end-of-unit)
**Estimated Time:** 45-55 minutes (may be split across two sessions)
**Facilitation:** The facilitator reads directions aloud. The student completes written portions independently. For Section A, the facilitator may conduct the oral portion conversationally before the student writes. For Section D, the facilitator provides the source passage.
**Standards Assessed:** W.3.1, W.3.2, W.3.3, W.3.7

---

## Assessment Overview

This assessment measures the student's mastery of all skills taught in Unit 3:

| Section | Skill Area | Lessons Assessed | Points |
|---------|-----------|------------------|--------|
| **Section A** | Opinion Writing | Lessons 1-2 (structure, reasons, evidence) | 5 points |
| **Section B** | Informative Writing | Lessons 3-4 (topic sentence, grouping, facts, definitions) | 5 points |
| **Section C** | Narrative Writing | Lessons 5-7 (characters, setting, dialogue, sequence, closure) | 8 points |
| **Section D** | Research Skills | Lesson 8 (sources, note-taking, paraphrasing) | 4 points |
| **Section E** | Editing and Revision | Lesson 9 (self-editing, revision strategies) | 3 points |
| | | **Total** | **25 points** |

**Timing suggestion:** Sections A and B can be completed in one session (20-25 minutes). Sections C, D, and E can be completed in a second session (25-30 minutes). If the student has strong stamina, the entire assessment can be done in one sitting with a short break between Sections B and C.

## Section A: Opinion Writing (5 points)

### Oral Component (1 point)

**Instructions to facilitator:** Before the student writes, have a brief conversation to activate thinking.

Ask the student: "If you could change ONE rule at school (or at home), what would you change? Tell me your opinion and give me at least two reasons why."

Listen for:
- A clear opinion statement
- At least two reasons connected to the opinion
- The ability to distinguish between an opinion and a fact

**Scoring:**
- 1 point: Student states a clear opinion and provides at least two connected reasons
- 0.5 points: Student states an opinion but gives only one reason or reasons are disconnected
- 0 points: Student cannot state a clear opinion or confuses opinion with fact

## Scoring Guide

### Section A: Opinion Writing (5 points total)

**Oral Component (1 point):**
- 1 point: Clear opinion with at least two connected reasons
- 0.5 points: Opinion stated but only one reason or weak connection
- 0 points: No clear opinion or cannot distinguish opinion from fact

**Written Component — Opinion Paragraph Rubric (4 points):**

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| **Introduction & Opinion (1 pt)** | Introduction clearly states the topic and the writer's opinion. Reader immediately knows what the writer thinks. | Opinion is present but vague or buried in the paragraph. | No clear opinion statement. |
| **Reasons with Transitions (1 pt)** | Three reasons are present, each introduced with a transition word (First, Also/Another reason, Finally or equivalent). | Two reasons with transitions, or three reasons but missing transitions. | Fewer than two reasons; no transition words used. |
| **Evidence (1 pt)** | At least one reason includes evidence connected with "because" or "for example." Evidence is specific and relevant. | Evidence is attempted but vague or not clearly connected to a reason. | No evidence provided; reasons stand alone without support. |
| **Conclusion (1 pt)** | Conclusion restates the opinion in a different way and wraps up the paragraph. | Conclusion is present but just repeats the introduction or says "The End." | No conclusion. |

---

### Section B: Informative Writing (5 points total)

**Informative Paragraph Rubric (5 points):**

| Criterion | Full Credit | Partial Credit | No Credit |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| **Topic Sentence (1 pt)** | Clear topic sentence that introduces the topic. The reader knows immediately what the paragraph is about. | Topic is mentioned but the sentence is vague or reads like an opinion. | No topic sentence; paragraph jumps straight into facts. |
| **Facts and Details (1 pt)** | At least three facts or details about the topic. Information is accurate and relevant. | Two facts or details, or three facts that are vague or only partially relevant. | Fewer than two facts; information is mostly opinion or off-topic. |
| **Definition (1 pt)** | At least one key term is defined using "which means," "which is," or an equivalent structure. | A definition is attempted but unclear or not smoothly integrated. | No definitions provided. |
| **Logical Grouping (1 pt)** | Related information is grouped together. The paragraph does not jump randomly between subtopics. | Some grouping is visible, but a few facts are out of place. | No grouping; facts are randomly ordered. |
| **Concluding Sentence (1 pt)** | A concluding sentence wraps up the topic. | Concluding sentence is present but weak or just repeats the topic sentence. | No concluding sentence. |

---

### Section C: Narrative Writing (8 points total)

**Narrative Rubric (8 points):**

| Criterion | Full Credit (2 pts) | Partial Credit (1 pt) | No Credit (0 pts) |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|-----------|
| **Characters & Setting (2 pts)** | Characters are introduced with personality or detail (not just a name). Setting is described with at least two sensory details. The reader can picture the scene. | Characters are named but flat. Setting is mentioned but has one or no sensory details. | Characters and/or setting are missing or extremely vague. |
| **Dialogue (2 pts)** | At least two lines of dialogue are included. Quotation marks, commas/end punctuation, and dialogue tags are used correctly. Dialogue reveals character personality or advances the plot. | Dialogue is attempted but has punctuation errors (missing quotation marks, misplaced commas). OR dialogue is present but does not contribute to the story. | No dialogue is included, or dialogue is so poorly punctuated that it is unreadable. |
| **Sequence with Temporal Words (2 pts)** | Events are told in a clear, logical order. At least three temporal words or phrases are used (first, next, then, meanwhile, later, finally, etc.). | Events are mostly in order but one or two are out of place. OR fewer than three temporal words are used. | Events are randomly ordered with no temporal words. Sequence is confusing. |
| **Ending/Closure (2 pts)** | The ending resolves the situation. The reader knows how the character feels or what they learned. The story feels complete. Uses at least one closure strategy (resolve problem, show feelings, share lesson). | An ending is present but abrupt, vague, or does not fully resolve the situation. May just say "The End." | No real ending; the story just stops. |

### Section D: Research Skills (4 points total)

**Question 4 — Identifying Good Sources (1 point):**
- 1 point: Correctly identifies A, C, and E as good sources (and does NOT select B or D)
- 0.5 points: Identifies at least two correct sources but also selects one incorrect source (B or D)
- 0 points: Cannot identify good sources or selects mostly incorrect sources

**Question 5 — Note-Taking and Paraphrasing (2 points, 1 point per note card):**
- 1 point per card: The note contains a fact from the passage AND is written in the student's own words (not copied word-for-word). Minor overlap in individual words (like "butterfly" or "chrysalis") is acceptable, but the sentence structure must be different from the source.
- 0.5 points per card: The note contains a fact but is mostly copied from the source with only one or two words changed.
- 0 points per card: The note is copied word-for-word, or the fact is inaccurate, or no note is written.

**Question 6 — Research Question (1 point):**
- 1 point: The student writes a genuine question that the passage does NOT answer. Examples: "How long does metamorphosis take?" "What do adult butterflies eat?" "How far can butterflies fly?" "Why do butterflies have colorful wings?"
- 0 points: The question IS answered by the passage, or no question is written.

## Reteaching Guide

### If Section A (Opinion Writing) is below 3/5:

**Structure and transitions (Questions about introduction, reasons, conclusion):**
- Return to Lesson 1 and review the five-part opinion structure using the sandwich analogy
- Practice orally: give the student a topic, have them state their opinion, three reasons with transition words, and a conclusion — all out loud before writing
- Create a visual anchor: a five-part template the student fills in before drafting

**Evidence (Question about "because" and "for example"):**
- Return to Lesson 2 and review the difference between reasons and evidence
- Practice the sentence frames daily: "___ because ___" and "For example, ___"
- Give the student a reason and have them brainstorm 2-3 pieces of evidence before writing

---

### If Section B (Informative Writing) is below 3/5:

**Topic sentences and grouping:**
- Return to Lesson 3 and practice creating planning webs for simple topics
- Practice writing topic sentences for 3-4 different topics, checking that each is informative (not opinion)
- Use index cards: write facts about a topic, then physically sort them into groups before writing

**Facts, definitions, and details:**
- Return to Lesson 4 and practice the three types of informative support (facts, definitions, details)
- Practice adding definitions using the "which means" or "which is" frame
- Read short informational texts together and identify where the author uses facts, definitions, and details

---

### If Section C (Narrative Writing) is below 5/8:

Identify which rubric criterion scored lowest:

**Characters & Setting:**
- Return to Lesson 5. Practice introducing characters through action, detail, or thought
- Practice describing familiar settings using the five senses chart
- Do daily "setting snapshots": describe one room or place in 3-4 sentences with sensory details

**Dialogue:**
- Return to Lesson 6. Review the four dialogue patterns and the reference card
- Practice 3-5 dialogue punctuation exercises daily until the patterns become automatic
- Color-code dialogue: highlight the exact spoken words in one color and everything else in another

**Sequence:**
- Return to Lesson 7. Review the temporal words list
- Practice sequencing with daily activities: "Tell me about your morning using at least four temporal words"
- Use the story mountain graphic organizer for every narrative planning session

**Ending/Closure:**
- Return to Lesson 7 (closure strategies). Practice writing endings for incomplete stories
- Review the three strategies: resolve the problem, show feelings, share a lesson
- Read endings of familiar books and identify which strategies the author used

---

### If Section D (Research Skills) is below 3/4:

**Identifying good sources:**
- Practice source evaluation: give the student a research topic and a list of potential sources, then discuss which are reliable and which are not
- Key distinction: a good source has FACTS that can be checked; a bad source is a guess, a story, or someone's opinion

**Paraphrasing:**
- Return to Lesson 8 and practice the Read-Cover-Remember-Write strategy with simple sentences daily
- If the student copies word-for-word, have them tell you the fact ORALLY first, then write what they said (oral language is naturally paraphrased)
- Compare a source sentence with a paraphrased version: "These say the same thing but in different words. That is paraphrasing."

---

### If Section E (Editing and Revision) is below 2/3:

- Practice daily with "error hunt" sentences: write one sentence with 1-2 errors and have the student find and fix them
- Focus on the most common error types first: capitalization at the start of sentences, end punctuation, and common spelling words
- Use the self-editing checklist from Lesson 9 on SHORT pieces (2-3 sentences) before applying to longer writing
- Read aloud as an editing strategy: many errors become audible when read aloud that are invisible when read silently

## Answer Key

### Section A

**Oral Component:**
Answers will vary. The student should state a clear opinion and give at least two reasons connected to it. The opinion should be something that can be argued (not a fact).

**Written Component (Question 1):**
Answers will vary by topic. Evaluate using the rubric above.

**Sample mastery-level response (topic: Every kid should learn to play a musical instrument):**

> I believe every kid should learn to play a musical instrument. First, playing music helps your brain grow because studies show that learning an instrument improves memory, attention, and problem-solving skills. Also, playing an instrument teaches patience and discipline. For example, when I started learning piano, I had to practice the same song over and over before I could play it right, and that taught me not to give up. Finally, music is a way to express your feelings when words are not enough. When I am feeling sad, I can play a slow, quiet song, and when I am happy, I can play something fast and loud. For all these reasons, learning a musical instrument is one of the best things a kid can do.

This response demonstrates: clear opinion in the introduction, three reasons with transitions (First, Also, Finally), evidence using both "because" and "for example," and a conclusion that restates the opinion differently.

---

### Section B

**Question 2:**
Answers will vary by topic. Evaluate using the rubric above.

**Sample mastery-level response (topic: An animal — sea otters):**

> Sea otters are fascinating marine mammals that live along the coasts of the Pacific Ocean. They are covered in thick, dense fur, which is the thickest fur of any animal. This fur keeps them warm in the cold ocean water because sea otters do not have a thick layer of fat like whales and seals do. Sea otters eat sea urchins, crabs, clams, and fish. To open hard-shelled food, they use a rock as a tool — they float on their backs and smash the shell against the rock on their bellies. Baby sea otters, called pups, cannot swim at first, so their mothers wrap them in kelp, which is a type of seaweed, to keep them from floating away while the mother hunts for food. Sea otters play an important role in keeping the ocean healthy because they eat sea urchins that would otherwise destroy kelp forests.

This response demonstrates: clear topic sentence, multiple facts and details, two definitions ("which is the thickest fur..." and "which is a type of seaweed"), logical grouping (appearance, food/behavior, babies, importance), and a concluding sentence.

---

### Section C

**Question 3:**
Answers will vary by story choice. Evaluate using the rubric above.

**Sample mastery-level response (topic: A child finds a mysterious note in their mailbox):**

> On a gray Wednesday afternoon, Sofia walked down the driveway to check the mail. The air smelled like rain, and the wind was starting to pick up. Inside the mailbox, between two boring envelopes, she found a small folded piece of paper with her name written in blue ink.
>
> "That's weird," she muttered, turning the paper over in her hands. She unfolded it carefully. The note said: "Look under the big rock by the oak tree in the backyard. — A Friend."
>
> Sofia's heart beat faster. First, she looked around to see if anyone was watching. The street was empty. Then she walked slowly to the backyard, where the old oak tree stood with its branches stretching out like giant arms.
>
> She found the big rock right away. It was covered in moss and half-buried in the dirt. She knelt down and pushed with all her strength. The rock finally rolled to the side.
>
> Underneath was a small tin box with a ribbon around it. She opened the lid. Inside was a friendship bracelet made of purple and gold beads — her favorite colors — and another note that read: "Happy birthday, Sofia! From your secret friend, Aunt Carmen."
>
> "Aunt Carmen!" Sofia laughed. She slipped the bracelet onto her wrist and held it up to catch the light. Later that evening, she called her aunt to say thank you.
>
> "How did you know the exact right colors?" Sofia asked.
>
> "Because I know you, kiddo," Aunt Carmen replied with a laugh.
>
> Sofia smiled and looked at the bracelet again. She decided that this was the best birthday surprise she had ever received — not because of the bracelet itself, but because someone cared enough to make it an adventure.

This response demonstrates: character introduction with detail (Sofia, walking to the mailbox), setting with sensory details (gray afternoon, smell of rain, wind, mossy rock), multiple lines of correctly punctuated dialogue, temporal words (first, then, finally, later that evening), and a satisfying ending with resolution (the mystery is solved), feelings (Sofia smiles), and a lesson (the value of thoughtful gestures).

### Section D

**Question 4:**
Good sources: **A, C, and E.**
- A: A children's encyclopedia entry about butterflies (reliable reference source)
- C: A nature magazine article about the life cycle (relevant, factual source)
- E: A science website for kids with facts about metamorphosis (relevant, factual source)

NOT good sources:
- B: A friend's guess (a guess is not a reliable source)
- D: A fictional story about a talking butterfly (fiction is not a factual source for research)

**Question 5:**
Answers will vary. Each note card must contain a fact from the passage written in the student's own words.

Acceptable paraphrases include:
- "Butterflies change their bodies completely through a process called metamorphosis."
- "A butterfly starts as an egg, then becomes a caterpillar, then goes into a chrysalis, and finally comes out as a butterfly."
- "Caterpillars eat a lot of leaves to get bigger before they make a chrysalis."
- "The caterpillar builds a hard shell called a chrysalis around itself and changes inside."
- "When metamorphosis is complete, an adult butterfly comes out of the chrysalis and flies away."

NOT acceptable (copied too closely): "Butterflies go through a process called metamorphosis, which means a complete change in body form." (This is word-for-word from the passage.)

**Question 6:**
Any genuine question not answered by the passage. Examples:
- "How long does the whole metamorphosis process take?"
- "What do adult butterflies eat?"
- "How far can butterflies fly?"
- "Why are butterfly wings so colorful?"
- "How many eggs does a mother butterfly lay at one time?"
- "What predators do butterflies have?"

### Section E

**Question 7 — Corrected paragraph:**

> Last Saturday, my sister and I went to the zoo. We saw elephants, lions, and penguins. The penguins were my favorite because they waddled around and slid on their bellies. "Look at that one," said my sister. "It looks like it's wearing a tuxedo." I laughed and agreed. It was the best day ever.

**All 12 errors and their corrections:**

| # | Error | Correction |
|---|-------|-----------|
| 1 | "last" (lowercase) | "Last" (capitalize — start of sentence) |
| 2 | "saturday" (lowercase) | "Saturday" (capitalize — proper noun) |
| 3 | "me and my sister" | "my sister and I" (grammar — subject pronoun) |
| 4 | "too" (wrong homophone) | "to" |
| 5 | "we" (lowercase) | "We" (capitalize — start of sentence) |
| 6 | "elefants" (misspelled) | "elephants" |
| 7 | "the" before "penguins" (lowercase) | "The" (capitalize — start of sentence) |
| 8 | "favrite" (misspelled) | "favorite" |
| 9 | "there" (wrong homophone) | "their" (possessive) |
| 10 | "look at that one said my sister it looks like its wearing a tuxedo" (no dialogue punctuation) | "Look at that one," said my sister. "It looks like it's wearing a tuxedo." |
| 11 | "laffed" (misspelled) | "laughed" |
| 12 | "agread" (misspelled) | "agreed" |

Note: Error 10 is a complex dialogue error that encompasses missing quotation marks, missing comma, missing period, missing capitalization of "It" as a new sentence within dialogue, and "its" should be "it's" (contraction). A student who correctly adds quotation marks and basic dialogue punctuation should receive credit even if some sub-elements are imperfect.
