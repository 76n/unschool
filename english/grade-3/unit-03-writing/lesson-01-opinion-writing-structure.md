# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Opinion Writing Structure

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the five parts of a structured opinion piece (introduction, reason 1, reason 2, reason 3, conclusion)
- Write an opinion introduction that clearly states the topic and the writer's opinion
- Organize reasons using transition words (first, also/another reason, finally)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a complete sentence with correct capitalization and end punctuation
- Ability to write a simple opinion piece with a topic, opinion, and at least one reason (Grade 1-2 skill)
- Understanding of the difference between a fact and an opinion (Grade 2 skill)

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils or highlighters (5 colors)
- Index cards or sticky notes (optional, for organizing reasons)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling (optional but recommended)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about opinions and review fact vs. opinion from earlier grades.

**Activity: Opinion or Fact?**

Say to the student: "Before we start writing today, let's warm up our brains. I am going to read some statements. For each one, tell me: is it an OPINION or a FACT? Remember — an opinion is what someone thinks or believes; a fact is something that can be proven true."

Read these statements one at a time. Have the student respond orally.

1. "Dogs have four legs." (Fact — this can be observed and proven.)
2. "Dogs are the best pets in the world." (Opinion — someone else might prefer cats or fish.)
3. "Recess is the best part of the school day." (Opinion — this is what someone believes.)
4. "There are 12 months in a year." (Fact — this can be counted and confirmed.)
5. "Chocolate ice cream tastes better than vanilla." (Opinion — this is a personal preference.)

Say: "Great! Today we are going to learn how to write our opinions in a way that is organized and convincing. In earlier grades, you wrote opinions with one or two reasons. Now in Grade 3, we are going to build a STRUCTURE — like a building with five floors. Each floor has a job to do."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Opinion:** What you think, believe, or feel about something — reasonable people can disagree about opinions
- **Reason:** A "why" — it explains WHY you hold your opinion
- **Structure:** The organized plan or framework for a piece of writing — how the parts are arranged
- **Introduction:** The opening of your writing — it tells the reader what you are writing about and what you think
- **Conclusion:** The ending of your writing — it wraps up your ideas and restates your opinion in a new way
- **Transition words:** Words that connect ideas and show the reader you are moving to the next point (first, also, another reason, finally)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the Five-Part Opinion Structure**

Say: "In Grade 2, you might have written an opinion like this:"

Write this on the board or paper:

> I think recess should be longer. It is fun. We need to run. The end.

Say: "That is a start, but it is not very organized. A reader might think, 'OK, you think recess should be longer, but WHY? Can you convince me?' In Grade 3, we are going to use a five-part structure that makes our opinions clear and convincing."

Write the structure where the student can see it:

**Opinion Writing Structure:**
1. **Introduction** — Tell the reader your topic and your opinion
2. **Reason 1** — "First, ..." (your first reason why)
3. **Reason 2** — "Also, ..." or "Another reason is ..." (your second reason)
4. **Reason 3** — "Finally, ..." (your third and strongest reason)
5. **Conclusion** — Restate your opinion in a new way; wrap it up

Say: "Think of this like a sandwich. The introduction and conclusion are the bread — they hold everything together. The three reasons are the filling — they are the meaty, important part."

**Step 2: Explain Each Part**

Say: "Let me explain each part."

**Introduction:** "The introduction does two jobs. First, it tells the reader what topic you are writing about. Second, it clearly states YOUR opinion. The reader should never have to guess what you think."

**Reasons with transition words:** "Each reason starts with a transition word. Transition words are like road signs — they tell the reader, 'Here comes my next point.' Watch: FIRST tells the reader 'here is my first reason.' ALSO or ANOTHER REASON tells the reader 'I have more to say.' FINALLY tells the reader 'this is my last and best reason.'"

**Conclusion:** "The conclusion does NOT just say 'The End.' It restates your opinion using DIFFERENT words and leaves the reader with a final thought. Think of it as your last chance to convince the reader."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Modeling a Complete Opinion Piece**

Say: "Watch me write an opinion piece using the five-part structure. I am going to think out loud so you can hear what is happening in my brain."

Write this in front of the student, narrating your thinking:

**Topic: Should students have longer recess?**

*Writing the Introduction:*
Say: "First, I need my introduction. I need to tell the reader what I am writing about and state my opinion clearly."
Write: **"I believe that students should have longer recess every day."**
Say: "See how the reader immediately knows my topic (recess) and my opinion (it should be longer)? I used the words 'I believe' to make my opinion clear."

*Writing Reason 1:*
Say: "Now I need my first reason. I will start with the transition word 'First.'"
Write: **"First, longer recess gives students more time to exercise and stay healthy. Running, jumping, and playing games keeps our bodies strong."**
Say: "I stated my reason AND I added a detail to explain it. The reason is 'more time to exercise.' The detail is 'running, jumping, and playing games.'"

*Writing Reason 2:*
Say: "Here comes reason two. I will use the transition 'Also.'"
Write: **"Also, recess helps students focus better in class. After playing outside, our brains feel refreshed and ready to learn."**
Say: "My second reason is about focusing. I explained WHY recess helps with focus — because our brains feel refreshed."

*Writing Reason 3:*
Say: "Time for my third and final reason. I will use 'Finally.'"
Write: **"Finally, recess is when students learn to get along with each other. We practice sharing, taking turns, and solving problems on the playground."**
Say: "My strongest reason is about social skills. I listed specific things students learn: sharing, taking turns, solving problems."

*Writing the Conclusion:*
Say: "Now I need a conclusion. I will NOT just say 'The End.' I will restate my opinion in a different way."
Write: **"For all these reasons, schools should give students more time for recess. A longer recess means healthier, happier, and more focused students."**
Say: "I restated my opinion ('schools should give students more time for recess') and I ended with a strong final thought about what would happen if they did."

Read the complete piece aloud:

> I believe that students should have longer recess every day. First, longer recess gives students more time to exercise and stay healthy. Running, jumping, and playing games keeps our bodies strong. Also, recess helps students focus better in class. After playing outside, our brains feel refreshed and ready to learn. Finally, recess is when students learn to get along with each other. We practice sharing, taking turns, and solving problems on the playground. For all these reasons, schools should give students more time for recess. A longer recess means healthier, happier, and more focused students.

Say: "Let's count the five parts." Use five different colors to underline or highlight each part:
1. Introduction (color 1)
2. Reason 1 with "First" (color 2)
3. Reason 2 with "Also" (color 3)
4. Reason 3 with "Finally" (color 4)
5. Conclusion (color 5)

**Worked Example 2: A Weaker Example for Comparison**

Say: "Now let me show you an opinion piece that is NOT as strong. Let's figure out what is missing."

Write or read aloud:

> I like pizza. Pizza is good. It has cheese. I also like the sauce. Pizza is my favorite food. The End.

Ask: "What is the opinion here?" (Pizza is the writer's favorite food — but it is not stated clearly until the end.)
Ask: "Does the writer use transition words?" (No.)
Ask: "Does the writer give reasons that explain WHY pizza is the favorite?" (Not really — "it has cheese" and "I also like the sauce" are details, not reasons connected to WHY it is the best.)
Ask: "Is there a real conclusion?" (No — "The End" is not a conclusion.)

Say: "This piece has an opinion, but it has no structure. Let's see how it would look with our five-part plan."

Write the improved version together:

> I think pizza is the best food in the world. First, pizza has so many delicious flavors — you can choose cheese, pepperoni, vegetables, or even pineapple. Also, pizza is a food you can share with friends and family, which makes it perfect for parties and movie nights. Finally, pizza is easy to eat because you can hold it in your hand without needing a fork or knife. For all these reasons, pizza is my number-one favorite food.

Say: "See the difference? Same topic, but the second one is organized, convincing, and complete."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice planning and writing an opinion piece together.

**Activity: Build an Opinion Together**

Say: "Now let's build an opinion piece together. I am going to give you a topic, and we will plan it using our five-part structure."

**Topic: What is the best season of the year?**

**Step 1: Choose an opinion.**
Ask: "Which season do you think is the best? Summer? Fall? Winter? Spring?" (Let the student choose. If they cannot decide, suggest summer.)

**Step 2: Brainstorm reasons.**
Say: "Now let's brainstorm. Why do you think ___ is the best season?" Write down all the student's reasons on scratch paper or index cards. Accept all ideas.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student only comes up with one or two reasons, ask guiding questions: "What activities can you do in this season? How does the weather make you feel? What foods or holidays happen in this season?"*

**Step 3: Choose the three best reasons.**
Say: "You came up with great reasons! Now pick your three BEST reasons — the ones that would most convince someone who disagrees with you. Put your STRONGEST reason last."

**Step 4: Write the introduction together.**
Say: "Let's write our introduction. Remember, we need to name the topic and state the opinion." Guide the student to write something like: "I believe ___ is the best season of the year."

**Step 5: Write the three reasons with transitions.**
Say: "Now add your first reason with the word 'First.'" Help the student write 1-2 sentences for each reason. Prompt: "Can you add a detail that explains your reason?"

Repeat for reasons 2 and 3, using "Also" (or "Another reason") and "Finally."

**Step 6: Write the conclusion.**
Say: "Now let's wrap it up. Restate your opinion in a different way. Do NOT just say 'The End.'" Help the student compose a conclusion.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles with the conclusion, offer a sentence starter: "For all these reasons, I think ___" or "That is why ___ will always be my favorite season."*

**Step 7: Read it aloud and check.**
Have the student read their complete piece aloud. Together, verify:
- Does the introduction state the opinion clearly?
- Are there three reasons with transition words?
- Does the conclusion restate the opinion?

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes an opinion piece independently using the five-part structure.

**Directions:** "Now it is your turn. Choose ONE of the topics below and write a complete opinion piece using our five-part structure. Plan first, then write."

**Topic Choices:**
- What is the best animal to have as a pet?
- Should kids have homework on weekends?
- What is the best book (or movie) you have ever read (or watched)?
- A topic of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a complete opinion piece with all five parts. Each reason should be 1-2 sentences. Use the transition words: First, Also/Another reason, Finally.

**Stretch:** Write a complete opinion piece with all five parts. Each reason should include a specific detail or example that supports it. Try using a transition word you have NOT used before (for example: In addition, Furthermore, Most importantly).

**Challenge:** Write TWO opinion pieces on the SAME topic — one arguing FOR and one arguing AGAINST. (For example: "Cats are the best pets" and "Cats are NOT the best pets.") This requires thinking about both sides of an issue.

### Interleaved Review

After completing the opinion piece, answer these review questions:

6. **Reading Review (Unit 1 connection):** "What is the difference between a main character and a supporting character in a story?" (A main character is who the story is mostly about; a supporting character helps the story but is not the focus.)

7. **Reading Review (Unit 2 connection):** "What is a main idea?" (The most important point or message of a text — what the text is mostly about.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. Name the five parts of an opinion piece in order. (Introduction, Reason 1, Reason 2, Reason 3, Conclusion)
2. What transition word can you use to introduce your LAST reason? (Finally; also accept "Most importantly" or "Last")
3. What should a conclusion do? (Restate the opinion in a different way; wrap up the ideas; NOT just say "The End")

**Mastery = Student correctly names all five parts and explains the role of the conclusion.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the five-part structure using the recess model from Direct Instruction
- Create a visual anchor: draw a five-layer sandwich with each layer labeled (Introduction, Reason 1, Reason 2, Reason 3, Conclusion)
- Before the next lesson, have the student identify the five parts in the model opinion piece by underlining with five colors
- Practice orally: give the student a simple opinion topic and have them state their opinion, three reasons, and a conclusion out loud before writing

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 2 (Opinion Writing with Evidence) next session. Preview: "Today you learned to organize your opinion with three reasons. Next time, we are going to make your reasons even MORE convincing by adding EVIDENCE — proof from real life or things you have read."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Opinion Debate:** The facilitator takes the OPPOSITE opinion on the same topic. Each person presents their three reasons. The student practices defending their position with organized arguments.
- **Opinion Letter:** Write the opinion piece as a letter to a real person (a parent, teacher, principal, or friend). Add a greeting and closing. Mail or deliver it!
- **Opinion Detective:** Find an opinion in a magazine, newspaper, or online article. Can the student identify the writer's opinion and their reasons? Are the reasons organized with transition words?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can be completed in one session for students with strong Grade 2 writing foundations. If the student needs more time to internalize the five-part structure, split the lesson into two sessions: Direct Instruction and Guided Practice in session one, Independent Practice and Extension in session two.

**Connection to earlier grades:** In Grade 2, students wrote opinion pieces with a topic, opinion, reasons, and a closing. The new skills in Grade 3 are: (1) organizing reasons with transition words, (2) providing three reasons instead of one or two, and (3) writing a conclusion that does more than just say "The End." Make these distinctions explicit.

**Modeling is essential:** The single most effective thing you can do in this lesson is WRITE IN FRONT OF THE STUDENT. Let them see you think out loud, make choices, cross things out, and build the piece one part at a time. This makes the invisible process of writing visible.

**Common confusion — reasons vs. details:** Students sometimes confuse reasons with details. A reason explains WHY you hold your opinion. A detail supports the reason. Example: "Recess should be longer" (opinion). "It helps us exercise" (reason). "We can run, jump, and play tag" (detail supporting the reason). If the student provides only details without reasons, ask: "That is an interesting detail. But WHY does that make ___ the best?"

**Growth mindset note:** Some students may feel intimidated by the five-part structure. Reassure them: "You already know how to write opinions from last year. This year, we are just adding more organization. Think of it as upgrading from a small building to a bigger one — same foundation, more floors."

- Stretch tier: uses varied transition words
- Challenge tier: both pieces present coherent arguments
