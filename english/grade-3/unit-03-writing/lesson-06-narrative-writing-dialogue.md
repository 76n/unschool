# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 6

## Topic
Narrative Writing: Dialogue

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Punctuate dialogue correctly using quotation marks, commas, and end punctuation
- Write dialogue that reveals character personality and advances the story
- Include dialogue tags (said, asked, shouted, whispered, etc.) and place them correctly

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a narrative opening with characters and a setting (Lesson 5)
- Understanding of character personality and how actions reveal character traits (Lesson 5)
- Ability to use correct capitalization and end punctuation in sentences

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils (two colors: one for the exact words characters say, one for dialogue tags)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling (strongly recommended for this lesson)
- Index cards (for dialogue punctuation reference card)
- The student's narrative opening from Lesson 5 (if available)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate awareness of how people talk and review character introduction skills from Lesson 5.

**Activity: Listen and Notice**

Say to the student: "Today we are going to learn something that makes stories come alive — DIALOGUE. Dialogue is when characters talk. Before we learn how to WRITE it, let's think about how people really talk."

Say: "I am going to describe two characters. Tell me what each one might SAY."

**Character 1: A shy, quiet child meeting someone new.**
Ask: "What might this character say when they meet a new kid at school?" (Examples: "Hi... I'm Sam," or "Um, do you... do you want to sit here?" — quiet, hesitant, few words.)

**Character 2: An excited, outgoing child who just won a prize.**
Ask: "What might this character say?" (Examples: "Oh my gosh, I won! I can't believe it! Did you see that?!" — loud, lots of words, exclamation marks.)

Say: "See how different characters talk DIFFERENTLY? The shy character speaks quietly and uses fewer words. The excited character speaks loudly and uses more words. When you write dialogue, you are not just putting words on the page — you are letting the reader HEAR your character's voice. Today we are going to learn exactly how to do that."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Dialogue:** The exact words that characters say in a story — written with quotation marks
- **Quotation marks:** The punctuation marks (" ") that go around the EXACT words a character says — they look like tiny 66 and 99
- **Dialogue tag:** The part that tells the reader WHO is speaking and HOW they said it (e.g., said Maya, asked Liam, whispered Rosa)
- **Speaker:** The character who is talking
- **Comma in dialogue:** A comma that separates the exact words from the dialogue tag

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce Quotation Marks**

Say: "Quotation marks are the most important punctuation in dialogue. They go around the EXACT words a character says — nothing more, nothing less."

Write this on the board or paper. Use a colored pencil to highlight the quotation marks:

**"I found a treasure map!"** Marcus shouted.

Say: "The quotation marks go AROUND the words Marcus actually says. 'I found a treasure map!' — those are his exact words. 'Marcus shouted' is the DIALOGUE TAG — it tells us who said it and how they said it."

**CRITICAL RULE — Write this where the student can see it throughout the lesson:**

> **Quotation marks go around ONLY the exact words the character says.**
> **Everything else stays OUTSIDE the quotation marks.**

**Step 2: Teach the Four Dialogue Patterns**

Say: "There are four ways to write dialogue. I am going to show you each one."

**Pattern 1: Dialogue tag AFTER the spoken words.**

Write: **"Let's go to the park," said Emma.**

Say: "Notice four things:"
1. The quotation marks open BEFORE 'Let's' and close AFTER 'park.'
2. There is a COMMA after 'park' but BEFORE the closing quotation mark.
3. The dialogue tag (said Emma) comes AFTER the quotation marks.
4. The period goes at the very END of the whole sentence.

Write the rule:
> **Pattern 1:** "_____ ," said [name].

**Pattern 2: Dialogue tag BEFORE the spoken words.**

Write: **Emma said, "Let's go to the park."**

Say: "This time, the dialogue tag comes FIRST. Notice:"
1. There is a COMMA after 'said' — before the spoken words begin.
2. The quotation marks open AFTER the comma.
3. The period goes INSIDE the closing quotation mark.

Write the rule:
> **Pattern 2:** [Name] said, "_____ ."

**Pattern 3: Question in dialogue.**

Write: **"Can we go to the park?" asked Emma.**

Say: "When a character ASKS a question, we use a question mark INSTEAD of a comma — but the question mark still goes INSIDE the quotation marks."

Write the rule:
> **Pattern 3:** "_____ ?" asked [name].

**Pattern 4: Exclamation in dialogue.**

Write: **"Watch out!" shouted Marcus.**

Say: "When a character says something with strong feeling, we use an exclamation mark INSIDE the quotation marks."

Write the rule:
> **Pattern 4:** "_____ !" shouted [name].

**Step 3: Create a Reference Card**

Say: "Let's make a reference card you can keep next to you when you write dialogue."

On an index card, write:

```
DIALOGUE REFERENCE CARD
-----------------------
Pattern 1: "Words words words," said Name.
Pattern 2: Name said, "Words words words."
Pattern 3: "Words words words?" asked Name.
Pattern 4: "Words words words!" shouted Name.

REMEMBER:
- Quotation marks around ONLY the exact words
- Comma or ? or ! goes INSIDE the quotation marks
- Capitalize the first word inside the quotation marks
- New speaker = new line (start a new paragraph)
```

Say: "Keep this card next to you every time you write dialogue for the rest of this unit."

**Step 4: Teach Dialogue Tags Beyond "Said"**

Say: "The word 'said' works fine, but there are MANY other words you can use to show HOW a character speaks."

Write a list:

| Instead of "said" | Use when the character... |
|---|---|
| whispered | speaks very quietly |
| shouted / yelled | speaks very loudly |
| asked | asks a question |
| replied / answered | responds to someone |
| exclaimed | says something with excitement or surprise |
| mumbled | speaks unclearly |
| laughed | speaks while laughing |
| cried | speaks while crying or speaks urgently |
| sighed | speaks with tiredness or frustration |
| begged | asks desperately |

Say: "Choosing the right dialogue tag is like choosing the right color for a painting. 'Whispered' paints a different picture than 'shouted.' The dialogue tag helps the reader HEAR the character's voice."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Writing Dialogue in a Story**

Say: "Watch me add dialogue to a story. I am going to write a short scene where two characters talk to each other."

Write in front of the student, thinking aloud:

**Scene: Marcus finds something in the backyard.**

Say: "I am going to write what is happening first, then add dialogue."

Write: **Marcus was digging in the backyard when his shovel hit something hard. He brushed the dirt away and saw the corner of a rusty metal box.**

Say: "Now Marcus is going to speak. I need quotation marks around his exact words."

Write: **"Dad, come here! I found something!" Marcus shouted, waving his arms.**

Say: "I used Pattern 4 — exclamation, then the tag. The exclamation mark is INSIDE the quotation marks. Now Dad responds."

Write: **His dad jogged over and knelt beside the hole. "What is it?" he asked, peering at the box.**

Say: "I used Pattern 3 — a question. The question mark is INSIDE the quotation marks. Notice I started a new line because a DIFFERENT character is speaking."

Write: **"I think it might be a treasure box," Marcus whispered. His eyes were wide.**

Say: "Pattern 1 — statement with comma, then tag. I chose 'whispered' because Marcus is excited but also a little in awe."

Write: **Dad carefully lifted the box out of the ground. "Only one way to find out," he said with a grin. "Let's open it."**

Say: "Dad speaks twice in a row, so I do not start a new line. Both pieces of dialogue are in the same paragraph because the SAME person is talking."

Read the complete passage:

> Marcus was digging in the backyard when his shovel hit something hard. He brushed the dirt away and saw the corner of a rusty metal box.
>
> "Dad, come here! I found something!" Marcus shouted, waving his arms.
>
> His dad jogged over and knelt beside the hole. "What is it?" he asked, peering at the box.
>
> "I think it might be a treasure box," Marcus whispered. His eyes were wide.
>
> Dad carefully lifted the box out of the ground. "Only one way to find out," he said with a grin. "Let's open it."

Say: "Let's check our dialogue rules." Go through each line of dialogue and verify with the student:
- Quotation marks around the exact words? Yes.
- Comma, question mark, or exclamation mark inside the quotation marks? Yes.
- First word inside quotation marks capitalized? Yes.
- New speaker starts a new line? Yes.
- Dialogue tags are varied (shouted, asked, whispered, said)? Yes.

**Worked Example 2: Finding and Fixing Dialogue Errors**

Say: "Now let me show you some dialogue with MISTAKES. Let's fix them together."

Write these one at a time. For each, ask: "What is wrong?"

**Mistake 1:** Marcus said I found something.
Fix: Marcus said, **"I found something."**
(Missing quotation marks and comma.)

**Mistake 2:** "What is it" asked Dad.
Fix: **"What is it?"** asked Dad.
(Missing question mark — it should go inside the quotation marks.)

**Mistake 3:** "Let's open it." Said Dad.
Fix: "Let's open it," **said** Dad.
(Period should be a comma because the dialogue tag follows. Also, "said" should not be capitalized because it is part of the same sentence.)

**Mistake 4:** "I think" said Marcus "it might be treasure."
Fix: **"I think,"** said Marcus, **"it might be treasure."**
(Needs a comma after "think" inside the quotation marks, and a comma after "Marcus.")

Say: "Dialogue punctuation takes practice. Even adult writers have to think carefully about where the quotation marks and commas go. The reference card will help you."

**Worked Example 3: Showing How Dialogue Reveals Character**

Say: "Good dialogue does not just fill space — it shows us what the characters are like."

Write two versions of the same scene:

**Version A (dialogue reveals nothing):**
> "Hi," said Sarah.
> "Hi," said Tom.
> "Want to play?" said Sarah.
> "OK," said Tom.

**Version B (dialogue reveals character):**
> "Hey! You're the new kid, right? I'm Sarah. Come on, I'll show you where the best climbing tree is!" Sarah grabbed Tom's arm and pulled him toward the playground.
> "Uh, sure," Tom mumbled, tripping over his own feet as he tried to keep up. "I'm not really a climbing person, though."
> "You will be!" Sarah laughed.

Say: "In Version A, we learn nothing about the characters. In Version B, we can HEAR Sarah's personality — she is outgoing, enthusiastic, bossy in a friendly way. We can hear Tom's personality — he is shy, uncertain, not very adventurous. The dialogue SHOWS us who they are."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice writing dialogue with correct punctuation together.

**Activity 1: Punctuate This Dialogue (5 minutes)**

Say: "I am going to give you some dialogue without any punctuation. Add the quotation marks, commas, question marks, and exclamation marks."

Write these without punctuation. The student adds punctuation:

1. Watch out for the puddle yelled Kayla
   **"Watch out for the puddle!" yelled Kayla.**

2. Mom asked Where did you put your shoes
   **Mom asked, "Where did you put your shoes?"**

3. I finished my homework said Desi with a big smile
   **"I finished my homework," said Desi with a big smile.**

4. Can I have another cookie begged Eli
   **"Can I have another cookie?" begged Eli.**

5. This is the best day ever whispered Ava
   **"This is the best day ever," whispered Ava.**

*Facilitator prompt: Have the student refer to the dialogue reference card. If they make an error, do not just correct it — point them to the pattern that matches and let them figure out the fix.*

**Activity 2: Add Dialogue to a Scene (7 minutes)**

Say: "Here is a scene with NO dialogue. Let's add at least three lines of dialogue to bring it to life."

Write or read:

> Mia and her brother Carlos stood at the edge of the lake. The water was perfectly still. Then, something splashed right in the middle.

Together, write dialogue for this scene. Guide the student:
- What would Mia say when she sees the splash? (Use her personality — is she scared? Excited? Curious?)
- How would Carlos respond? (Is he different from Mia?)
- What would they say next?

Write 3-4 lines of dialogue together, checking punctuation for each line.

Example:
> "Did you see that?!" Mia gasped, pointing at the ripples.
>
> "It was probably just a fish," Carlos said, crossing his arms.
>
> "That was way too big to be a fish," Mia whispered. She took a step closer to the water's edge.
>
> Carlos grabbed her arm. "Don't get too close," he warned.

*Facilitator prompt: After writing each line, ask: "Did we put the quotation marks in the right place? Is the comma or question mark inside the quotation marks? Did we capitalize the first word of the spoken words?"*

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes a narrative scene with dialogue independently.

**Directions:** "Write a short narrative scene (8-12 sentences) that includes at least FOUR lines of dialogue. Your scene should have two characters who are having a conversation. Remember to use your dialogue reference card. Make sure your dialogue shows the characters' personalities and moves the story forward."

**Scene Choices:**
- Two friends discover a mysterious door in the school basement
- A child tries to convince a parent to let them get a pet
- Two siblings argue about what game to play, then figure it out together
- A scene of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a scene with at least four lines of dialogue. Use at least two different dialogue patterns from the reference card. Punctuate all dialogue correctly. Use at least two different dialogue tags (not just "said" for everything).

**Stretch:** Write a scene with at least six lines of dialogue. Use at least three different dialogue patterns. Include at least one line where you describe what the character is DOING while they speak (e.g., "she said, twisting her hands nervously"). Use at least four different dialogue tags.

**Challenge:** Write a scene with dialogue between THREE characters. Make each character sound different based on their personality. After writing, go back and highlight each character's lines in a different color. Can a reader tell WHO is speaking just from the words and how they talk, even without the dialogue tags?

### Interleaved Review

After completing the scene, answer these review questions:

5. **Lesson 3-4 Review:** What is the purpose of a planning web in informative writing? (To organize information into groups before writing, so related facts stay together.)

6. **Lesson 1-2 Review:** In an opinion piece, what does the conclusion do? (It restates the opinion in a different way and wraps up the ideas. It does NOT just say "The End.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer in writing:

1. Punctuate this dialogue correctly: Come here said Mom I have something to show you
   **Answer:** "Come here," said Mom. "I have something to show you."

2. Where do quotation marks go in a line of dialogue? (Around ONLY the exact words the character says.)

3. Write one line of dialogue where a character asks a question. Punctuate it correctly. (Example: "Where are we going?" asked Jake. Accept any correctly punctuated question in dialogue.)

**Mastery = Student correctly punctuates item 1 AND writes a correctly punctuated line of dialogue for item 3.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the four dialogue patterns using the reference card
- Practice with physical manipulation: write spoken words on one color of paper and tags on another color, then physically arrange them with punctuation cards (quotation marks, commas, question marks)
- Do 3-5 dialogue punctuation exercises each day as a warm-up until the patterns become automatic
- Key error to watch for: If the student consistently puts the comma or period OUTSIDE the quotation marks, use this memory trick: "The comma cuddles up INSIDE the quotation marks — it stays warm inside, not cold outside."

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 7 (Narrative Writing: Sequence and Closure) next session. Preview: "You now know how to create characters, describe settings, and write dialogue. Next time, we are going to learn how to organize the EVENTS of your story in order using special words, and how to write an ending that makes the reader feel satisfied."

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Dialogue-Only Story:** Write a very short story (10-15 lines) using ONLY dialogue — no narration at all. Can the reader figure out what is happening, who is talking, and where they are just from what the characters say? This is an advanced challenge that forces the student to convey everything through dialogue.
- **Comic Strip:** Draw a 4-6 panel comic strip with speech bubbles, then rewrite the same scene as a narrative paragraph with proper dialogue punctuation. Compare the two versions.
- **Dialogue Revision:** Take the narrative opening from Lesson 5 and add at least two lines of dialogue to it. How does the dialogue change the feel of the opening?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This is the most technically challenging lesson in the unit. Plan for TWO sessions. In session one, cover Direct Instruction and Guided Practice. In session two, have the student complete Independent Practice. Dialogue punctuation requires repeated practice before it becomes automatic.

**The reference card is essential.** Keep the dialogue reference card visible during EVERY writing session for the rest of this unit. Even after the student "knows" the rules, having the visual reference prevents errors and builds confidence. Professional writers refer to style guides; there is no shame in using a reference card.

**Common errors and how to address them:**

| Error | What the student writes | How to fix |
|---|---|---|
| Missing quotation marks | Come here said Mom. | "Which words did Mom actually SAY? Put quotation marks around those and only those." |
| Period instead of comma before tag | "Come here." said Mom. | "The tag (said Mom) is part of the same sentence. Use a comma, not a period, to connect them." |
| Comma outside quotation marks | "Come here", said Mom. | "The comma cuddles INSIDE the quotation marks." |
| No capital after opening quote | Mom said, "come here." | "The first word after the opening quotation mark always gets a capital letter." |
| Quotation marks around the tag | "Come here" "said Mom." | "Quotation marks go around ONLY the words the character speaks. 'Said Mom' is not something she said — it tells us she is speaking." |

**Color-coding is your friend.** Use one color for the exact words a character says and a different color for everything else (tags, narration). This visual distinction helps the student see exactly where quotation marks belong.

**Dialogue advances plot.** Good dialogue is not just characters chatting — it moves the story forward. When reviewing the student's Independent Practice, ask: "Does this dialogue tell us something new? Does it move the story forward? Or are the characters just saying 'hi' and 'bye'?"

**New speaker, new line.** This rule is essential for readability. Each time a DIFFERENT character speaks, the writer starts a new paragraph. If the SAME character speaks twice in a row (with some narration in between), it can stay in the same paragraph. Enforce this rule consistently.

**Growth mindset note:** Dialogue punctuation is genuinely hard. Adult writers struggle with it. If the student makes errors, say: "You are tackling one of the trickiest parts of writing. Professional writers use reference guides for this too. Every time you practice, it gets a little more automatic."
