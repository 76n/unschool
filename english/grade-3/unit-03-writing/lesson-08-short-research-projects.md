# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 8

## Topic
Short Research Projects

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Choose a focused research topic and generate 2-3 research questions
- Find and gather information from 2-3 sources, taking simple notes in their own words
- Organize notes into a logical structure and write a short research report (1-2 paragraphs)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write informative text with facts, definitions, and details (Lessons 3-4)
- Ability to create a planning web to group related information (Lesson 3)
- Ability to write topic sentences and concluding sentences
- Basic reading comprehension skills (ability to read short informational passages and identify key information)

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Index cards or half-sheets of paper (for note-taking)
- Blank paper for planning web
- 2-3 simple informational sources about the student's chosen topic (see suggestions below)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Colored pencils (for color-coding notes by source)

**Source suggestions (prepare before the lesson):**
Choose one of these topic areas and gather 2-3 short, accessible sources:
- **Animals:** A children's encyclopedia entry, a printed article from a kid-friendly website (National Geographic Kids, DK Find Out), and/or a short nonfiction book
- **Weather:** Similar source types about a specific weather phenomenon
- **Community helpers:** Similar source types about firefighters, doctors, veterinarians, etc.

If printed sources are not available, the facilitator can read information aloud from a screen while the student takes notes, or the facilitator can prepare 2-3 short "fact sheets" (half-page summaries with 6-8 facts each).

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate thinking about where information comes from and review informative writing skills from Lessons 3-4.

**Activity: Where Would You Find Out?**

Say to the student: "Today we are going to learn how to do RESEARCH — that means finding information about a topic from different sources and then writing about what you learned. Before we start, let's think about where information comes from."

Ask: "If you wanted to find out the answer to each of these questions, where would you look?"

1. "How tall is the tallest building in the world?" (A book about buildings, an encyclopedia, a trusted website.)
2. "What do sea turtles eat?" (A book about sea turtles, a nature website, an article, asking a marine biologist.)
3. "What was your town like 100 years ago?" (A history book about the town, the local library, asking an older relative, old photographs.)
4. "How do you make chocolate chip cookies?" (A cookbook, a recipe website, asking someone who bakes.)

Say: "Researchers do not just make things up — they find information from sources they can trust. Today, you are going to be a researcher. You will pick a topic, find information from different sources, take notes, and write a short research report."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Research:** The process of finding information about a topic by looking at different sources
- **Source:** A place where you find information — books, articles, websites, experts, videos
- **Notes:** Short written reminders of the important facts you find during research — notes are in YOUR words, not copied
- **Paraphrase:** To say or write something in your OWN words instead of copying the exact words from a source
- **Research question:** A question you want to answer through your research — it guides what you look for
- **Research report:** A piece of informative writing based on what you learned from your research

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain the Research Process**

Say: "Research has four steps. Think of it as a recipe."

Write:

**The Research Recipe:**
1. **Choose a topic and ask questions.** Pick something you are curious about. Write 2-3 questions you want to answer.
2. **Find sources and take notes.** Read 2-3 sources. Write down important facts on note cards — in your OWN words.
3. **Organize your notes.** Group your notes by subtopic (just like the planning web from Lesson 3).
4. **Write your report.** Turn your organized notes into an informative piece with a topic sentence, grouped facts, and a conclusion.

Say: "Today we are going to go through all four steps."

**Step 2: Teach How to Choose a Topic and Write Research Questions**

Say: "A good research topic is specific enough to research but broad enough to find information about."

Write:
- Too broad: "Animals" (There are millions of animals! Where would you even start?)
- Too narrow: "What my cat ate for breakfast" (You cannot research this in a book.)
- Just right: "How dolphins communicate with each other" (Specific, interesting, researchable.)

Say: "Once you have a topic, you write research questions. These are the questions you want your research to answer."

**Example:**
Topic: How dolphins communicate

Research questions:
1. What sounds do dolphins make?
2. Do dolphins use body language to communicate?
3. Why is communication important for dolphins?

Say: "These questions guide your research. When you read your sources, you are looking for the ANSWERS to these questions."

**Step 3: Teach Note-Taking with Paraphrasing**

Say: "When you find a fact in a source, you do NOT copy the whole sentence word-for-word. That is called copying, and it is not research. Instead, you PARAPHRASE — you read the information, think about what it means, and write it in YOUR words."

Teach the **Read-Cover-Remember-Write** strategy:

Write:
1. **READ** the sentence or paragraph from your source.
2. **COVER** it (turn the page over or cover it with your hand).
3. **REMEMBER** the key idea — what is the most important thing?
4. **WRITE** it in your own words on a note card.

**Demonstration:**

Write a "source" sentence on the board:
> Source: "Dolphins produce a variety of sounds including clicks, whistles, and burst-pulse sounds to communicate with members of their pod."

Say: "I just read that sentence. Now I am going to cover it up." (Cover it.)
Say: "What do I remember? Dolphins make different sounds — clicks, whistles, and other sounds — to talk to their group."
Write the note: **"Dolphins make clicks, whistles, and other sounds to communicate with their group (called a pod)."**

Say: "See? I did NOT copy the sentence. I wrote the same idea in MY words. That is paraphrasing."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Modeling the Complete Research Process**

Say: "Watch me do a mini-research project from start to finish."

**Step 1: Choose a topic and questions.**
Say: "My topic is: How ants work together in a colony."
Write research questions:
1. What jobs do different ants have?
2. How do ants communicate with each other?
3. How do ants find food?

**Step 2: Read sources and take notes.**

Say: "I have two sources. Let me read them and take notes."

Present Source 1 (read aloud or write on the board):
> "An ant colony can contain thousands or even millions of ants. Each ant has a specific job. The queen's job is to lay eggs. Worker ants find food, build the nest, and take care of baby ants. Soldier ants protect the colony from enemies."

Say: "Let me use Read-Cover-Remember-Write." Cover the source.
Write on an index card: **Source 1: Ant colonies have lots of ants. Different ants have different jobs — the queen lays eggs, workers find food and build, and soldiers protect the colony.**

Present Source 2:
> "Ants communicate by releasing chemicals called pheromones. When an ant finds food, it leaves a pheromone trail on the ground so other ants can follow the trail to the food source. Ants also communicate by touching each other with their antennae."

Cover the source. Write on another card: **Source 2: Ants talk to each other using chemicals called pheromones. When one ant finds food, it leaves a chemical trail so others can find it. They also touch each other with their antennae to share information.**

**Step 3: Organize the notes.**

Say: "Now I will organize my notes into groups."

Draw a quick planning web:
```
              [Ants Working Together]
             /          |           \
     [Jobs in        [How they      [Finding
      the colony]    communicate]    food]
     queen: eggs    pheromones      leave trails
     workers: food  touch antennae  others follow
     soldiers:
       protect
```

**Step 4: Write the report.**

Write in front of the student:

> Ants are tiny insects that do amazing things by working together in groups called colonies. Each ant in the colony has a specific job. The queen ant lays all the eggs. Worker ants find food, build the nest, and care for baby ants. Soldier ants have the important job of protecting the colony from enemies.
>
> Ants communicate with each other using chemicals called pheromones, which are special scents that carry messages. When a worker ant finds food, it leaves a pheromone trail on the ground like an invisible path. Other ants smell the trail and follow it straight to the food source. Ants also communicate by touching each other with their antennae, which are the two thin parts sticking out from their heads. By working together and sharing information, ants can accomplish things that no single ant could do alone.

Say: "My report has two paragraphs. The first is about jobs in the colony. The second is about communication and finding food. I grouped related information just like we learned in Lesson 3. I included definitions ('pheromones, which are special scents' and 'antennae, which are the two thin parts'). I used information from BOTH sources. And I wrote everything in my own words — I did not copy any sentences directly."

**Worked Example 2: What NOT to Do — Copying**

Say: "Let me show you what copying looks like so you can avoid it."

**Source sentence:** "Ants communicate by releasing chemicals called pheromones."
**Copied note:** "Ants communicate by releasing chemicals called pheromones." (This is word-for-word the same.)
**Paraphrased note:** "Ants talk to each other using special chemicals called pheromones." (Same idea, different words.)

Say: "Always paraphrase. If you find yourself writing the exact same words as the source, stop, cover the source, and try again in your own words."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice the research process together with real sources.

**Activity: Mini-Research Together**

Say: "Now let's do a mini-research project together. We are going to go through all four steps."

**Step 1: Choose a topic.**
Let the student choose from:
- A specific animal (polar bears, octopuses, eagles, etc.)
- A type of weather (thunderstorms, hurricanes, tornadoes, blizzards)
- A community helper (veterinarians, firefighters, librarians)

**Step 2: Write 2-3 research questions together.**
Guide the student: "What do you want to learn about this topic? What questions do you have?" Write the questions down.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles to form questions, offer starters: "Where does/do ___ live?" "What does/do ___ eat?" "How does/do ___ ___?" "Why is/are ___ important?"*

**Step 3: Read sources and take notes.**
Use the sources you prepared before the lesson. Read Source 1 together (or have the student read independently if able). After reading a section:
- Say: "What is the most important fact here?"
- Have the student cover the source and paraphrase the fact onto a note card.
- Repeat for 3-4 facts from Source 1.

Do the same for Source 2.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student copies word-for-word, gently say: "Great fact! Now cover the source and tell me in YOUR words. You can change the words as long as the meaning stays the same."*

**Step 4: Organize notes.**
Together, sort the note cards into 2-3 groups. Draw a quick planning web.

Say: "We will use this plan to write the report in Independent Practice."

---

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes a short research report using notes from Guided Practice.

**Directions:** "Using the notes and planning web you created in Guided Practice, write a short research report (1-2 paragraphs, 8-14 sentences). Your report must include: a topic sentence that introduces your topic, facts from at least TWO sources, at least ONE definition, grouped information, and a concluding sentence. Write in your OWN words — do not copy from the sources."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a 1-paragraph research report (8-10 sentences) using notes from at least two sources. Include a topic sentence, facts, at least one definition, and a concluding sentence. Paraphrase all information.

**Stretch:** Write a 2-paragraph research report (10-14 sentences) with clear grouping (each paragraph covers a different subtopic). Include at least two definitions and transitions between paragraphs. Add an illustration or diagram with labels.

**Challenge:** Write a 2-paragraph research report, then add a "Questions for Further Research" section: 2-3 new questions that your sources did NOT answer and where you might look to find the answers. Also write a "Sources" section listing where you found your information.

### Interleaved Review

After completing the research report, answer these review questions:

5. **Lesson 6 Review:** Write one line of dialogue where a character asks a question. Punctuate it correctly. (Example: "What kind of bird is that?" asked Mia. — Accept any correctly punctuated question in dialogue.)

6. **Lesson 7 Review:** Name three temporal words you can use to sequence events in a narrative. (Accept any three: first, next, then, after that, meanwhile, later, finally, eventually, before long, at last, etc.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. What are the four steps of research? (Choose a topic and ask questions, find sources and take notes, organize your notes, write your report.)
2. What does "paraphrase" mean? (To write information in your OWN words instead of copying the exact words from the source.)
3. Read this source sentence: "Polar bears have thick fur and a layer of fat called blubber that keeps them warm in freezing temperatures." Now cover it and write a paraphrased version. (Example: "Polar bears stay warm in the cold because of their thick fur and a layer of fat under their skin called blubber." Accept any version that conveys the same meaning in different words.)

**Mastery = Student correctly names the four research steps AND writes a successful paraphrase.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice the Read-Cover-Remember-Write strategy with simple sentences every day for a week
- Give the student a short paragraph (3-4 sentences) and have them take notes using note cards — one fact per card, paraphrased
- Practice choosing topics and writing research questions without doing the full research process
- Before the next lesson, have the student paraphrase one sentence per day from a book or article they are reading

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 9 (Revision and Editing) next session. Preview: "You have now written opinion pieces, informative pieces, narratives, and a research report. Next time, we are going to learn how to go back and make all of that writing EVEN BETTER through revision and editing."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Expert Presentation:** The student uses their research report to give a short "expert presentation" to a family member. They present the information orally, show their diagram, and answer questions from the "audience."
- **Source Comparison:** Give the student two sources about the same topic that have slightly different information. The student writes about what both sources agree on and what is different. This introduces the concept of comparing sources.
- **Expanded Research:** The student chooses one of their "Questions for Further Research" and finds the answer using an additional source. They add a third paragraph to their report.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This is the most time-intensive lesson in the unit. Plan for 2-3 sessions:
- Session 1: Direct Instruction and beginning of Guided Practice (choosing topic, writing questions, beginning to read sources)
- Session 2: Completing Guided Practice (note-taking, organizing) and Independent Practice (writing the report)
- Session 3 (if needed): Finishing the report, adding illustrations, Extension activities

**Source preparation is critical.** The biggest logistical challenge of this lesson is having appropriate sources available. Prepare these BEFORE the lesson. Options:
- Print articles from kid-friendly websites (National Geographic Kids, Britannica Kids, DK Find Out)
- Use children's nonfiction books from a library or home collection
- Create simple "fact sheets" yourself (half-page documents with 6-8 facts about a topic, written in clear, simple language)
- Read information aloud from a screen while the student takes notes

**Paraphrasing is hard.** This is genuinely one of the most challenging academic skills to learn. Many adults struggle with it. Be patient. The Read-Cover-Remember-Write strategy helps because it physically separates the student from the source text. If the student produces something that is still very close to the original, say: "That is close to the source's words. Can you say it in a totally different way? Pretend you are explaining this to a friend who has not read the article."

**Do not expect perfection in the research report.** This is the student's first research project. The goal is to learn the PROCESS (topic → questions → sources → notes → organize → write), not to produce a polished final product. If the report has good paraphrased facts, a topic sentence, and some organization, that is excellent for a first attempt.

**Connection to informative writing (Lessons 3-4).** Research writing IS informative writing — the difference is that the information comes from external sources rather than the student's own knowledge. Make this connection explicit: "Remember how we wrote informative paragraphs in Lessons 3 and 4? A research report is the same thing — topic sentence, facts, definitions, details, conclusion — but the facts come from SOURCES you read."

**Common issue — the student only uses one source.** Gently redirect: "You found great information in that book! Now let's see if our second source tells us anything NEW — something the first source did not mention." Finding different information across sources is a key skill.

**Growth mindset note:** Research can feel overwhelming. Break it into small, manageable steps. After each step, celebrate: "You wrote great research questions! That is step one done." / "Look at all these notes! Step two complete." The four-step process gives the student a clear roadmap.
