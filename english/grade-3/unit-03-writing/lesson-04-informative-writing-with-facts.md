# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 4

## Topic
Informative Writing with Facts, Definitions, and Details

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Use facts, definitions, and specific details to develop an informative topic
- Write a complete multi-paragraph informative piece with logically grouped information
- Add an illustration or diagram when it helps explain the topic

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a topic sentence for informative writing (Lesson 3)
- Ability to create a planning web to group related information (Lesson 3)
- Understanding of the difference between opinion writing and informative writing (Lesson 3)

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Blank paper for illustrations or diagrams
- Colored pencils for drawings and for color-coding different types of information
- Planning web from Lesson 3 (if available)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review informative writing organization from Lesson 3 and warm up with fact vs. opinion sorting.

**Activity: Fact, Definition, or Detail?**

Say to the student: "In informative writing, we use three kinds of information to teach our readers: facts, definitions, and details. Let me explain the difference, and then we will practice sorting."

Write:
- **Fact:** Something that is true and can be checked or proven (e.g., "Frogs are amphibians.")
- **Definition:** An explanation of what a word or term means (e.g., "An amphibian is an animal that lives part of its life in water and part on land.")
- **Detail:** A specific piece of information that adds more to a fact (e.g., "Frogs breathe through their skin when they are underwater.")

Read these statements. Ask: "Is this a fact, a definition, or a detail?"

1. "A tornado is a spinning column of air that reaches from a storm cloud to the ground." (Definition)
2. "Tornadoes can have wind speeds over 300 miles per hour." (Fact)
3. "The sky often turns a greenish-yellow color just before a tornado forms." (Detail)
4. "A thermometer is a tool used to measure temperature." (Definition)
5. "The highest temperature ever recorded on Earth was 134 degrees Fahrenheit." (Fact)

Say: "Great! Today we are going to use all three — facts, definitions, and details — to write informative pieces that really teach our readers."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Fact:** Information that is true and can be verified or checked
- **Definition:** An explanation of what a word means — helps the reader understand unfamiliar terms
- **Detail:** A specific piece of information that adds depth — helps the reader picture or understand the topic better
- **Develop:** To make writing fuller and more complete by adding information
- **Illustration:** A picture or drawing that helps explain a topic
- **Diagram:** A drawing with labels that shows how something works or what something looks like — a diagram teaches with pictures and words together

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Show How Facts, Definitions, and Details Work Together**

Say: "In Lesson 3, you learned to group information and write topic sentences. Today, we are going to make our informative writing RICHER by using three kinds of information together: facts, definitions, and details."

Write this example:

**Thin version (just facts):**
> Bees make honey. Bees live in hives. Bees can sting.

**Rich version (facts + definitions + details):**
> Honey bees are insects that make honey, a thick, sweet liquid that people use in cooking and baking. Bees live in hives, which are structures made of wax that can hold thousands of bees. Inside the hive, each bee has a job. The queen lays eggs, worker bees collect nectar from flowers, and guard bees protect the entrance. Bees can sting to defend themselves, but a honey bee can only sting once because its stinger gets stuck and pulls out.

Say: "The thin version has facts, but it does not teach us much. The rich version has facts AND definitions (what honey is, what a hive is) AND details (each bee has a job, the stinger gets stuck). The rich version makes you feel like you actually LEARNED something."

**Step 2: Teach When to Add a Definition**

Say: "You should add a definition when you use a word that your reader might NOT know. Ask yourself: 'Would someone who knows nothing about this topic understand this word?' If not, define it."

Write examples:
- Without definition: "The frog goes through metamorphosis." (A reader might not know this word.)
- With definition: "The frog goes through metamorphosis, which means its body changes completely as it grows from a tadpole into an adult frog." (Now the reader understands!)

- Without definition: "Penguins eat krill." (What is krill?)
- With definition: "Penguins eat krill, which are tiny shrimp-like creatures that live in the ocean." (Clear!)

Say: "See how the definition is tucked right into the sentence using 'which means' or 'which are'? That is a smooth way to teach your reader a new word without stopping the flow."

**Step 3: Teach When to Add an Illustration or Diagram**

Say: "Sometimes, a picture helps more than words. If you are writing about something that has parts, stages, or a specific appearance, an illustration or diagram can help your reader understand."

Show examples of when illustrations help:
- Writing about the parts of a flower? A labeled diagram showing petals, stem, and leaves.
- Writing about the water cycle? A diagram showing evaporation, condensation, and precipitation with arrows.
- Writing about a type of animal? A drawing showing what it looks like, with labels pointing to special features.

Say: "You do NOT need an illustration for every piece of writing. But when you think 'It would be easier to SHOW this,' that is a great time to add a diagram."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Writing a Complete Informative Piece with Facts, Definitions, and Details**

Say: "Watch me write a complete informative piece. I am going to use our planning web approach AND make sure I include facts, definitions, and details."

**Topic: The Water Cycle**

*Step 1: Quick planning web (draw in front of the student):*
```
              [Water Cycle]
             /      |      \
    [Evaporation] [Condensation] [Precipitation]
     sun heats     water vapor    rain, snow,
     water turns   cools into     sleet, hail
     to vapor      tiny droplets  falls to ground
                   forms clouds
```

*Step 2: Write the piece, thinking aloud:*

Write: **"The water cycle is the process that moves water around the Earth over and over again."**
Say: "That is my topic sentence. It introduces the topic AND includes a definition of what the water cycle is."

Write: **"The water cycle has three main stages: evaporation, condensation, and precipitation."**
Say: "I am previewing the three groups for the reader. This helps them know what is coming."

Write: **"In the first stage, evaporation, the sun heats water in oceans, lakes, and rivers. The water turns into water vapor, which is water in the form of an invisible gas that rises into the air."**
Say: "I defined 'water vapor' because my reader might not know that term. I also gave specific details about where the water comes from."

Write: **"In the second stage, condensation, the water vapor rises high into the sky where the air is cooler. The cold air causes the vapor to turn back into tiny water droplets. Millions of these droplets clump together to form clouds."**
Say: "More definitions and details. I explained what condensation actually IS."

Write: **"In the third stage, precipitation, the water falls back to the earth. Precipitation can take many forms, including rain, snow, sleet, and hail. The type of precipitation depends on the temperature."**
Say: "I defined precipitation and gave specific examples."

Write: **"Once the water reaches the ground, it flows into rivers, lakes, and oceans, and the cycle starts all over again. The water cycle never stops — it has been running for billions of years."**
Say: "My concluding sentences wrap up the topic and add an interesting final detail."

Say: "Now, this topic would be PERFECT for a diagram. Let me draw one."

Draw a simple water cycle diagram on a separate piece of paper: arrows showing water rising from a lake (evaporation), forming clouds (condensation), and falling as rain (precipitation). Label each stage.

Say: "This diagram helps the reader SEE how the cycle works. Sometimes a picture explains something better than words alone."

**Worked Example 2: Identifying What Is Missing**

Say: "Now let's look at a piece that has facts but is missing definitions and details."

Write or read:

> Frogs are amphibians. They go through metamorphosis. First they are eggs, then tadpoles, then froglets, then adult frogs. Frogs eat insects. Some frogs are poisonous.

Say: "This has the right facts, but it does not TEACH very much. Let me show you how to develop it."

Work together with the student to add definitions and details:

- "Frogs are amphibians" → ADD: **", which means they can live both in water and on land."**
- "They go through metamorphosis" → ADD: **", a process in which their bodies completely change as they grow."**
- "First they are eggs, then tadpoles, then froglets, then adult frogs" → ADD: **"Tadpoles look like tiny fish with tails and live entirely in water. As they grow, they develop legs, lose their tails, and eventually can live on land."**
- "Some frogs are poisonous" → ADD: **"For example, the poison dart frog has bright colors that warn other animals not to eat it."**

Read the developed version aloud. Ask: "Which version teaches you more?"

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice writing informative text with facts, definitions, and details together.

**Activity: Develop an Informative Paragraph Together**

Say: "Let's write an informative paragraph together about a topic we both know something about."

**Topic: Community Helpers — Firefighters**

**Step 1: Planning web (create together):**
```
              [Firefighters]
            /       |        \
   [What they    [Equipment    [Training]
    do]           they use]
   fight fires   trucks        go to school
   rescue people hoses         practice drills
   teach safety  axes          stay fit
                 protective
                 gear
```

**Step 2: Write the topic sentence together.**
Ask: "What should our topic sentence say?" Guide the student toward something like: "Firefighters are community helpers who keep people safe in many important ways."

**Step 3: Write about each group, adding definitions and details.**

For "What they do":
Guide: "What do firefighters do? Let's use a fact and add details."
Together, write something like: "Firefighters respond to emergencies, which are dangerous situations that need help right away. They put out fires in homes and buildings, rescue people who are trapped, and teach communities how to stay safe from fires."

*Facilitator prompt: After each section, ask: "Is there a word here that a reader might not know? Should we add a definition?"*

For "Equipment":
Together, write something like: "Firefighters use special equipment to do their jobs. They drive fire trucks that carry long hoses, ladders, and tools. They wear protective gear, including helmets, boots, and coats made of heat-resistant material that keeps them safe from flames."

*Facilitator prompt: Ask: "Would a diagram help here? What could we draw?" (A labeled picture of a firefighter in full gear.)*

**Step 4: Write a concluding sentence together.**
Guide: "How should we wrap up?" Together: "Without firefighters, our communities would be much less safe. The next time you see a fire truck, remember the brave people inside who are ready to help."

**Step 5: Add an illustration.**
Have the student draw a quick labeled diagram of a firefighter in gear OR a fire truck with labeled parts. Talk about how the illustration adds to the writing.

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes a complete informative piece with facts, definitions, and details independently.

**Directions:** "Choose ONE topic below. Create a quick planning web, then write a complete informative piece (8-12 sentences) that includes: a topic sentence, grouped information, at least TWO definitions, at least THREE specific details, and a concluding sentence. Add an illustration or diagram if it would help explain your topic."

**Topic Choices:**
- An animal you find interesting (what it looks like, where it lives, what it eats)
- A type of weather (how it forms, what it looks like, how to stay safe)
- Your favorite sport (how it is played, equipment needed, rules)
- A topic of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a complete informative piece with a topic sentence, information from at least two groups, at least two definitions, and a concluding sentence. Add one illustration or diagram with labels.

**Stretch:** Write a complete informative piece with information from three groups, at least three definitions, specific details for each fact, and smooth transitions between groups. Add one or more diagrams with labels and captions (a caption is a sentence under the picture that explains it).

**Challenge:** Write a complete informative piece on a topic, then write 2-3 questions that your writing does NOT answer. Explain where you might find the answers to those questions. (This previews research skills in Lesson 8.)

### Interleaved Review

After completing the informative piece, answer these review questions:

5. **Lesson 2 Review:** In opinion writing, what two connector words can you use to attach evidence to a reason? ("Because" and "for example.")

6. **Unit 1 Reading Connection:** "What does it mean to refer to the text when answering a question about a story?" (It means to point to specific words, sentences, or details in the story as proof for your answer — not just guessing or remembering vaguely.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. Name the three types of information you can use to develop an informative topic. (Facts, definitions, and details.)
2. When should you add a definition in informative writing? (When you use a word that the reader might not know or understand.)
3. Read this sentence: "Caterpillars eat leaves." Add a detail to make it more informative. (Example: "Caterpillars eat leaves, and some caterpillars can eat an entire leaf in just one day." OR "Caterpillars eat the leaves of the specific plant where their eggs were laid." Accept any sentence that adds a meaningful detail.)

**Mastery = Student names all three types of information AND adds a meaningful detail to the sample sentence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice identifying facts, definitions, and details in a short informational passage — read a paragraph together and highlight each type in a different color
- Give the student a simple fact ("Dogs can learn tricks") and practice adding a definition and a detail to make it richer
- Use the "thin version / rich version" comparison from Direct Instruction with new topics
- Before the next lesson, have the student pick a topic and write just THREE sentences: one fact, one definition, one detail

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 5 (Narrative Writing: Characters and Setting) next session. Preview: "You have now learned two types of writing — opinion and informative. Next, we are going to start the third type: NARRATIVE writing. This is storytelling. You are going to learn how to create characters, build a setting, and take your reader on an adventure."

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Mini-Encyclopedia Entry:** The student writes a mini-encyclopedia entry about a topic of their choice, complete with a topic sentence, grouped information, definitions of key terms, an illustration with labels, and a "See Also" section that names related topics.
- **Teach a Lesson:** The student uses their informative writing to actually teach the facilitator about the topic. They read their piece aloud, show their diagram, and then the facilitator asks 2-3 questions. Can the student answer from their writing?
- **Informative Poster:** Transform the informative piece into a poster with a title, organized text sections, illustrations, and a border. Display it somewhere in the home.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson can be completed in one session if the student grasped Lesson 3 well. If the student still struggles with grouping information, split this into two sessions: one for understanding facts/definitions/details (Direct Instruction), and one for writing (Guided and Independent Practice).

**The illustration component is not optional filler.** Drawing a diagram or illustration is a genuine writing skill. It forces the student to think about which information is most important and how to represent it visually. Encourage the student to label their diagrams with words — this connects visual and written communication.

**Definitions are a new skill.** Many third graders have never been asked to define a term in their writing. The sentence frame "which means" or "which is" makes this much easier: "Metamorphosis, which means a complete change in body form, happens to frogs and butterflies." Practice this frame repeatedly.

**Common issue — copying from sources.** If the student tries to write a fact word-for-word from a book or article, this is a good teaching moment. Say: "You found a great fact! Now, can you say it in YOUR words? Close the book and tell me what you learned. Then write THAT." This prepares for the paraphrasing skill in Lesson 8.

**Writing quality levels for this lesson:**

*Developing:* "Dogs are pets. They eat food. They are fun. Some dogs are big and some are small." (Has facts but no definitions, no details, no grouping.)

*Approaching:* "Dogs are popular pets. They come in many sizes, from tiny Chihuahuas to huge Great Danes. Dogs eat dog food and treats. Some dogs help people, like guide dogs." (Some grouping, some details, but no definitions and loose organization.)

*Mastery:* "Dogs are one of the most popular pets in the world. They come in hundreds of breeds, which means different types with different sizes, shapes, and abilities. Some breeds are tiny, like the Chihuahua, which weighs only about five pounds. Others are huge, like the Great Dane, which can stand taller than a person when on its hind legs. Dogs eat special dog food that gives them the nutrients they need, but they also enjoy treats like bones and peanut butter. Some dogs have important jobs. Guide dogs, which are dogs trained to help people who cannot see, lead their owners safely through busy streets and buildings. Police dogs use their powerful sense of smell to help officers find missing people or catch criminals. Whether they are small or large, working or playing, dogs are loyal companions that bring joy to millions of families." (Clear grouping, definitions included, specific details, concluding sentence.)
