# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Narrative Writing: Characters and Setting

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Establish a narrative situation by introducing a narrator/main character and at least one other character
- Describe a setting using sensory details (what the character sees, hears, feels, smells)
- Write a narrative opening (4-6 sentences) that introduces characters and setting in a way that draws the reader in

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of characters, setting, and events as elements of a narrative (Grade 2, Unit 3)
- Ability to write complete sentences with description
- Familiarity with sensory details (sight, sound, touch, smell, taste) from Grade 2 writing

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Blank paper for character sketches (optional)
- Colored pencils or crayons (for character and setting illustrations)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Index cards (optional, for character trait cards)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about narrative writing from Grade 2 and transition from informative writing.

**Activity: Three Types of Writing Check-In**

Say to the student: "We have been learning three types of writing this unit. Let me check — can you tell me what each one does?"

Ask:
1. "What is the goal of opinion writing?" (To convince the reader by sharing your opinion with reasons and evidence.)
2. "What is the goal of informative writing?" (To teach the reader about a topic using facts, definitions, and details.)
3. "What do you think the goal of NARRATIVE writing is?" (To tell a story — to entertain the reader or share an experience.)

Say: "Exactly! In Grade 2, you wrote stories with a beginning, middle, and end. In Grade 3, we are going to make your stories much richer. You are going to learn to create characters that feel REAL, describe settings that the reader can PICTURE, add DIALOGUE so your characters actually TALK, and build stories with a clear sequence and a satisfying ending."

**Quick sensory review:**
Say: "Before we start, let's review our five senses."
Ask: "Name the five senses." (Sight, hearing, touch, smell, taste.)
Say: "We are going to use ALL of these to describe our settings today. If you can make the reader see, hear, feel, and smell your setting, they will feel like they are RIGHT THERE in the story."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Narrative:** A story — writing that tells about events that happen to characters
- **Narrator:** The person telling the story — in Grade 3, this is usually the main character (first person: "I") or an outside storyteller (third person: "she/he")
- **Character:** A person or animal in a story — characters have names, personalities, and feelings
- **Setting:** Where and when a story takes place — a good setting uses details that help the reader picture the scene
- **Sensory details:** Descriptions that appeal to the five senses — what the character sees, hears, feels (touch), smells, or tastes
- **Establish a situation:** The first job of a narrative — introduce WHO the story is about, WHERE they are, and WHAT is happening at the beginning

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain "Establishing a Situation"**

Say: "Every story needs to do something important right at the beginning: establish a situation. That means the reader needs to know three things very quickly:"

Write:
1. **WHO** — Who is the main character? Who else is in the story?
2. **WHERE/WHEN** — Where does the story take place? What time is it?
3. **WHAT** — What is happening at the beginning? What is the character doing or feeling?

Say: "In Grade 2, you might have started a story like this: 'One day, a boy went to the park.' That tells us who (a boy) and where (the park), but it is not very interesting. In Grade 3, we are going to make our openings RICHER."

**Step 2: Teach How to Introduce Characters**

Say: "A character is not just a name. A good writer helps the reader KNOW the character by showing us what they are like."

Write:
**Ways to introduce a character:**
- **Name and a detail:** "Maya, who never went anywhere without her red notebook, sat on the front steps."
- **Action:** "Liam burst through the door, dropped his backpack on the floor, and raced to the kitchen."
- **Feeling or thought:** "Rosa stared out the window, wishing the rain would stop so she could go outside."
- **Dialogue:** "'I have the best idea!' Jayden shouted, running into the living room."

Say: "Each of these tells us something about the character. Maya loves writing. Liam is in a hurry and energetic. Rosa is bored and restless. Jayden is excited and loud. We learn about characters from what they DO, THINK, FEEL, and SAY."

**Step 3: Teach How to Describe a Setting with Sensory Details**

Say: "The setting is not just a place name. A good writer helps the reader FEEL like they are in that place."

Write two versions:

**Thin setting:** "It was a cold day at the park."

**Rich setting:** "The wind bit at Liam's cheeks as he crunched through the frosty grass in the park. Brown leaves swirled around his sneakers, and the metal slide gleamed with a thin coat of ice. Somewhere nearby, a dog barked."

Say: "The rich version uses sensory details. Let's find them."
- **Sight:** brown leaves swirling, metal slide gleaming with ice
- **Touch/feeling:** wind biting at his cheeks, frosty grass
- **Sound:** crunching through grass, a dog barking

Say: "I did not just SAY it was cold — I SHOWED it by describing what Liam sees, hears, and feels. The reader can picture this park. They can almost feel the cold themselves."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Modeling a Narrative Opening**

Say: "Watch me write a narrative opening. I need to establish a situation: introduce my character, describe the setting, and set up what is happening."

Think aloud and write in front of the student:

Say: "First, I need to decide on my character. Let me create someone interesting."
Write: **"Amara was the kind of girl who always had dirt under her fingernails and a bug jar in her pocket."**
Say: "That one sentence tells the reader a lot about Amara — she loves nature and the outdoors. I did not say 'Amara liked nature.' I SHOWED it."

Say: "Now I need my setting."
Write: **"On a sticky August morning, she stood at the edge of her grandmother's garden, where fat tomatoes hung from vines and sunflowers towered over her head. Bees hummed from flower to flower, and the air smelled like warm dirt and basil."**
Say: "I used sensory details: sight (fat tomatoes, towering sunflowers), sound (bees humming), smell (warm dirt and basil), and touch (sticky August morning). The reader can picture this garden."

Say: "Now I need to set up what is happening — the beginning of the story."
Write: **"Amara was on a mission. Somewhere in this garden, she was sure she would find the biggest, most beautiful beetle she had ever seen. Her grandmother had mentioned it at breakfast, and Amara had been thinking about nothing else since."**
Say: "Now the reader knows what Amara wants: to find a special beetle. This makes the reader curious — will she find it? That is how you hook a reader into a narrative."

Read the complete opening:

> Amara was the kind of girl who always had dirt under her fingernails and a bug jar in her pocket. On a sticky August morning, she stood at the edge of her grandmother's garden, where fat tomatoes hung from vines and sunflowers towered over her head. Bees hummed from flower to flower, and the air smelled like warm dirt and basil. Amara was on a mission. Somewhere in this garden, she was sure she would find the biggest, most beautiful beetle she had ever seen. Her grandmother had mentioned it at breakfast, and Amara had been thinking about nothing else since.

Say: "In six sentences, the reader knows WHO (Amara, a nature-loving girl), WHERE (grandmother's garden on an August morning), and WHAT (she is looking for a special beetle). The reader is hooked!"

**Worked Example 2: A Weaker Opening for Comparison**

Write or read:

> One day, a girl went to a garden. She wanted to find a bug. It was a nice day.

Say: "This has the basics — who, where, and what. But it does not make me care. The character has no name or personality. The setting is just 'a garden' with no details. And 'a nice day' tells me nothing I can picture."

Ask: "What would you add to make this opening stronger?" Guide the student to suggest: a character name and detail, sensory details about the garden, and something specific about the bug or the character's feelings.

**Worked Example 3: Quick Character Sketch**

Say: "Let me show you a quick way to plan a character before you start writing."

Draw a simple character web:

```
              [Character Name: Marcus]
             /        |         \
     [Looks like]  [Personality]  [Wants/Needs]
      tall for      curious,       wants to be
      his age,      brave but      the first to
      curly hair,   sometimes      explore the
      always wears  nervous        old house on
      his lucky                    the hill
      green hat
```

Say: "I do not need to put ALL of this in the story. But knowing my character helps me write about them in a way that feels real. When I write, I can show Marcus's curiosity through his actions and his nervousness through his thoughts."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice creating characters and describing settings together.

**Activity 1: Build a Character Together (5 minutes)**

Say: "Let's create a character together. I will ask you questions, and you decide."

Walk through these questions with the student:
1. "Is your character a boy or a girl?" (Student chooses.)
2. "What is their name?" (Student chooses.)
3. "What is ONE interesting thing about what they look like?" (e.g., freckles, always wears mismatched socks, has a scar on their knee)
4. "What is their personality like? Are they shy, bold, curious, funny, kind?" (Student chooses 1-2 traits.)
5. "What do they love to do?" (Student chooses.)
6. "What do they want or need in this story?" (Student chooses.)

Together, write a character web on paper. Then write ONE sentence that introduces this character by SHOWING their personality through action, appearance, or a detail. For example: "[Name], who could never resist peeking behind closed doors, leaned closer to the old wooden box."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student gives a flat introduction like "[Name] was nice," ask: "How could you SHOW that they are nice? What would a nice person DO?"*

**Activity 2: Describe a Setting Together (7 minutes)**

Say: "Now let's choose a setting for our character's story and describe it with sensory details."

Let the student choose from:
- A rainy morning at school
- A busy farmer's market on a Saturday
- A quiet, snowy forest
- A setting of their choice

Together, fill in a sensory details chart:

| Sense | What the Character Experiences |
|-------|-------------------------------|
| Sight | |
| Sound | |
| Touch/Feel | |
| Smell | |
| Taste (if relevant) | |

Then write 2-3 sentences describing the setting using at least three senses from the chart.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student only gives sight details, ask: "What would you HEAR in this place? What would the air feel like? Is there a smell?"*

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes a complete narrative opening independently.

**Directions:** "Now it is your turn. Create a character and a setting, then write a narrative opening (6-8 sentences) that establishes a situation. Your opening must include: a character introduction that shows their personality, a setting described with at least three sensory details, and a hint of what the story is about (what the character wants or what is about to happen)."

**Topic/Setting Choices:**
- A kid who discovers something unexpected in their backyard
- A new student arriving at school on their first day
- A child visiting a grandparent's house in the country for the summer
- A situation of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a narrative opening (6-8 sentences) that introduces one main character, describes the setting with sensory details (at least three senses), and hints at what the story will be about.

**Stretch:** Write a narrative opening (6-8 sentences) that introduces a main character AND a second character. Show how the two characters are different through their actions or reactions. Describe the setting with at least four senses. Include at least one sentence that reveals the character's thoughts or feelings.

**Challenge:** Write TWO different openings for the SAME story. In version A, tell the story in first person ("I walked into the room..."). In version B, tell it in third person ("She walked into the room..."). Which version do you like better? Why?

### Interleaved Review

After completing the narrative opening, answer these review questions:

5. **Lesson 3-4 Review:** What is the difference between a fact and a definition in informative writing? (A fact is something true that can be verified. A definition explains what a word or term means.)

6. **Lesson 1-2 Review:** Name three transition words you can use in opinion writing. (First, also/another reason, finally; also accept in addition, furthermore, most importantly.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. What three things does a narrative opening need to establish? (Who — the characters; Where/When — the setting; What — what is happening or what the character wants.)
2. Name two ways you can introduce a character without just listing facts about them. (Show them through action, through dialogue, through a detail about their appearance, through their thoughts or feelings — accept any two.)
3. Write one sentence describing a beach using at least two sensory details. (Example: "The warm sand squished between her toes as waves crashed and seagulls cried overhead." Accept any sentence with two or more sensory details.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the three elements of a narrative opening AND writes a sentence with at least two sensory details.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the "rich setting" vs. "thin setting" comparison and practice adding sensory details to thin descriptions
- Use real objects or a familiar room: have the student describe it using each of the five senses
- Practice character introductions orally: "Tell me about a character — but do not just list facts. SHOW me what they are like through something they do or say."
- Before the next lesson, have the student draw a character and setting, then write 3-4 sentences describing the picture

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6 (Narrative Writing: Dialogue) next session. Preview: "You have created great characters and vivid settings. Next time, we are going to make your characters come alive by having them TALK. You are going to learn to write dialogue — the exact words your characters say — complete with quotation marks."

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Setting Transformation:** Write two descriptions of the SAME setting at different times. For example: describe a park in summer (warm, green, buzzing with insects, children laughing) and the same park in winter (frozen, quiet, bare branches, crunching snow). Notice how the sensory details change.
- **Character Interview:** The student "interviews" their character by writing questions and answering them in the character's voice. "What is your favorite thing to do?" "What are you afraid of?" "What makes you laugh?" This builds character depth.
- **Opening Swap:** The student writes a narrative opening, then the facilitator writes a different opening for the same story. Compare: which one hooks the reader more? Why? What techniques did each writer use?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson introduces narrative writing, which most students find more enjoyable than opinion or informative writing because they get to make things up. Energy is usually high. The challenge is channeling that enthusiasm into structured writing with specific craft elements (sensory details, character introduction techniques). If the student is bursting with story ideas, use that energy — but keep guiding them toward the specific skills in this lesson.

**Connection to Grade 2:** In Grade 2, students wrote narratives with a beginning, middle, and end. The Grade 3 additions are: (1) establishing a situation (not just starting with "One day"), (2) introducing characters with personality and depth, (3) using sensory details to describe settings, and (4) eventual addition of dialogue (Lesson 6). Acknowledge what the student already knows: "You already know how to write a story with a beginning, middle, and end. Now we are going to make your beginnings STRONGER."

**"Show, don't tell" is the core concept.** The phrase "show, don't tell" captures the key skill of this lesson. Instead of telling the reader "She was brave," SHOW the reader: "She took a deep breath, squared her shoulders, and pushed open the creaky door." Instead of telling "It was a beautiful day," SHOW: "Sunlight sparkled on the lake and a warm breeze carried the smell of wildflowers." Return to this phrase often throughout the narrative writing lessons.

**Sensory details may be uneven.** Most students default to sight. That is natural — we experience the world primarily through vision. Push gently toward other senses: "I can see the forest in my mind. Now help me hear it. What sounds are there?" Eventually, sound and touch details will come more naturally.

**First person vs. third person:** Most third graders are more comfortable writing in first person ("I") because they naturally tell stories about themselves. Third person ("she/he") requires more craft. For this lesson, let the student choose. The Challenge tier introduces both perspectives. In future lessons, third person will be practiced more.

**Growth mindset note:** Narrative writing is deeply personal. Some students worry that their ideas are "not good enough" or that their story is "boring." Reassure them: "Every writer starts with a simple idea. The craft — the sensory details, the character depth, the dialogue — is what makes it come alive. You are learning that craft right now."
