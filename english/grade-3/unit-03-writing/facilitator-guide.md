# Facilitator Guide — Grade 3 ELA, Unit 3: Writing

**Grade:** 3
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 3 — Writing
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 5 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Partially facilitated (adult introduces the lesson and supports guided practice; student works more independently during warm-up and independent practice)
**Standards:** W.3.1, W.3.2, W.3.3, W.3.7

---

## Unit Overview

This unit is a milestone in your student's writing development. In Grades 1 and 2, your student learned to write simple opinion, informative, and narrative pieces with a topic, supporting details, and a closing. Those were important first steps. Now, in Grade 3, everything levels up.

Grade 3 writers move from "I can write a few sentences about my opinion" to "I can build a structured argument with organized reasons." They move from "I can list facts about a topic" to "I can group related information logically and use definitions and details." And in narrative writing, the biggest leap of all: Grade 3 is when students learn to write dialogue — real characters talking to each other on the page, complete with quotation marks, commas, and said/asked tags.

This unit also introduces research writing for the first time. Your student will learn to choose a topic, gather information from multiple sources, take simple notes, and organize those notes into a short research report. This is the foundation for every research paper they will ever write.

**The unit is organized into four strands:**

- **Lessons 1-2: Opinion Writing** — Structure opinions with organized reasons and supporting evidence
- **Lessons 3-4: Informative Writing** — Introduce topics clearly, group information logically, use facts and definitions
- **Lessons 5-7: Narrative Writing** — Establish characters and setting, add dialogue, sequence events with temporal words, write satisfying endings
- **Lessons 8-10: Research and Process** — Conduct short research projects, revise and edit using a checklist, celebrate and publish finished work

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Write an opinion piece that states a clear opinion, provides at least three organized reasons with evidence, and ends with a concluding statement
- Write an informative/explanatory piece that introduces a topic, groups related information together, uses facts and definitions, and provides a concluding statement
- Write a narrative with an established situation (characters, setting), dialogue punctuated correctly, events in sequence using temporal words, and a satisfying ending
- Conduct a short research project: choose a topic, find information from 2-3 sources, take notes, and write a research report of 1-2 paragraphs
- Revise and edit their own writing using a self-editing checklist (capitals, punctuation, spelling, paragraph structure, dialogue punctuation)
- Reflect on their growth as a writer and share their work with an audience

**Why this matters:** Writing is how students make their thinking visible. A student who can organize ideas into a structured opinion piece is learning to argue logically. A student who can write informative text with grouped facts is learning to teach others. A student who can craft a narrative with dialogue is learning to bring characters to life. And a student who can conduct research is learning to find, evaluate, and communicate information — a skill they will use for the rest of their lives. This unit does not just teach writing; it teaches thinking.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Write complete sentences with correct capitalization and end punctuation
- [ ] Write a multi-sentence paragraph with a topic sentence, supporting details, and a closing sentence
- [ ] Write a simple opinion piece with a topic, opinion, and at least one reason (Grade 1-2 skill)
- [ ] Write a simple informative piece with a topic and facts (Grade 1-2 skill)
- [ ] Write a simple narrative with a beginning, middle, and end (Grade 2 skill)
- [ ] Use temporal words like first, next, then, and last to sequence events (Grade 2 skill)
- [ ] Spell grade-appropriate words correctly and use phonetic spelling strategies for unfamiliar words
- [ ] Read grade-level text with accuracy, fluency, and comprehension (Grade 3 Units 1-2)

**If the student struggles with basic paragraph writing,** spend 2-3 sessions reviewing paragraph structure (topic sentence + supporting details + closing sentence) before beginning Lesson 1. The lessons in this unit assume the student can write a paragraph; they build on that foundation by adding structure, evidence, dialogue, and research skills.

**If Units 1-2 (Reading Literature and Reading Informational Text) have been completed,** the student will have a strong reading foundation that directly supports this writing unit. Reading comprehension skills — identifying main ideas, understanding text structure, analyzing characters — all feed into stronger writing. If those units have not been completed, this writing unit can still be taught, but you may need to provide extra support with reading source materials in Lesson 8.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Lined paper and pencils** (the student will write extensively throughout this unit)
- **Colored pencils or highlighters** (for coding writing elements and peer editing)
- **Index cards or sticky notes** (for note-taking in Lesson 8 and organizing ideas)
- **A writing folder or notebook** to collect all drafts and finished pieces across the unit
- **Chart paper or a large whiteboard** (optional but very helpful for modeling writing in front of the student)
- **A simple children's dictionary** or access to an online dictionary (for spelling during editing)
- **2-3 simple informational sources** for Lesson 8 (see that lesson for suggestions — short books, printed articles, or age-appropriate websites about animals, weather, or community helpers)
- **A printed or hand-drawn self-editing checklist** (template provided in Lesson 9)

All model texts, writing samples, graphic organizers, and rubrics are included in the lessons. No additional published books are required, though reading published examples of opinion, informative, and narrative writing is always beneficial.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Opinion Writing Structure | 1-2 sessions |
| 2 | Opinion Writing with Evidence | 1-2 sessions |
| 3 | Informative Writing Introduction | 1-2 sessions |
| 4 | Informative Writing with Facts | 1-2 sessions |
| 5 | Narrative Writing: Characters and Setting | 1-2 sessions |
| 6 | Narrative Writing: Dialogue | 2 sessions |
| 7 | Narrative Writing: Sequence and Closure | 1-2 sessions |
| 8 | Short Research Projects | 2-3 sessions |
| 9 | Revision and Editing | 1-2 sessions |
| 10 | Writing Celebration and Publishing | 1-2 sessions |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session (40-50 minutes) |

**Adjusting the pace:**

- **Lessons 1-2 (opinion writing)** build directly on Grade 2 opinion writing. If the student has a strong foundation, these may move quickly. If opinion writing is new or rusty, take two sessions per lesson.
- **Lesson 6 (dialogue)** is the most technically challenging lesson in the unit. Quotation mark placement, commas before tags, and capitalization rules are all new. Plan for two full sessions and expect the student to need ongoing practice with dialogue punctuation throughout the rest of the unit.
- **Lesson 8 (research)** is the most time-intensive lesson. Research requires choosing a topic, finding sources, reading and note-taking, organizing information, and writing a report. Plan for 2-3 sessions. If the student has not completed Grade 3 Unit 2 (Reading Informational Text), allow extra time for reading source materials.
- **Lessons 9-10 (revision and publishing)** work best as a two-session sequence: one session for revision and editing, one session for final publishing and celebration.
- If the student finds sustained writing physically tiring, break sessions into smaller segments with movement breaks. Writing stamina at age 8-9 is still developing. A typical Grade 3 student can sustain focused writing for 15-20 minutes before needing a break.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student confuses opinions with facts ("Dogs are the best pet" vs. "Dogs have four legs") | The student does not yet distinguish between something that can be argued and something that can be proven true | Use the "Can people disagree?" test. If reasonable people can disagree, it is an opinion. If it can be proven with evidence, it is a fact. Practice sorting statements into opinion vs. fact before writing. |
| Student lists reasons without connecting them to the opinion ("I like pizza. Pizza has cheese. Pizza is round.") | The student provides details but does not explain HOW or WHY those details support the opinion | Model using "because" and "for example" to connect reasons to opinions: "I like pizza BECAUSE it has warm, melty cheese." Show that a reason must answer the question "Why do you think that?" |
| Student writes informative text that reads like a list of random facts | The student has not yet learned to group related information together | Use a web or outline to sort facts into categories BEFORE writing. Model the difference between a disorganized paragraph and one where related facts are grouped under clear subtopics. |
| Student writes narratives without dialogue, even after instruction | Dialogue feels risky because of the punctuation rules; the student avoids it to avoid errors | Normalize imperfect dialogue punctuation. Praise any attempt to include dialogue. Provide a dialogue template card the student can reference while writing: [quotation mark] [words] [comma or period] [quotation mark] [said/asked] [character name] [period] |
| Student places quotation marks incorrectly (before the tag, around the whole sentence, or only at the beginning) | Quotation mark placement is genuinely confusing — it involves remembering rules about where to open, what to include, and where to close | Use color-coding: highlight the exact words the character says in one color, and the tag (said Mom) in another. The quotation marks go around ONLY the colored words. Practice this visually before asking the student to write from scratch. |
| Student writes a narrative where events happen randomly with no clear sequence | The student has not internalized the need for temporal order; events tumble out as they think of them | Return to the planning stage. Use a numbered list or story mountain graphic organizer to plan events in order BEFORE writing. Post a temporal words list (first, next, then, meanwhile, later, finally) and require the student to use at least 3-4 in their narrative. |
| Student resists revision ("It's done! I don't want to change it!") | Revision feels like punishment or criticism of their work, especially for students who find writing effortful | Frame revision as what real authors do — not as "fixing mistakes" but as "making your writing even better." Start with positive feedback (two things that are great) before suggesting changes. Let the student choose which revision to make first. Keep revision sessions short. |
| Student's research "report" is copied word-for-word from the source | The student does not yet know how to put information in their own words (paraphrasing) | Teach the "Read, Cover, Remember, Write" strategy: read a sentence from the source, cover it up, remember the key idea, then write it in your own words. Check that it sounds different from the original. This is explicitly taught in Lesson 8. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to reinforce writing thinking:

- "What is your opinion about ___? Can you give me three reasons why you think that?"
- "If you had to teach someone about ___, what are the most important facts they would need to know?"
- "What happened in your day today? Tell me the story — start with the setting, add some dialogue, and give me a good ending."
- "I read something interesting today. Do you think it was an opinion or a fact? How can you tell?"
- "If a character in your story were standing right here, what would they say? How would they say it?"
- "You just told me that story out loud — now try writing down exactly what you said. That is what writers do."
- "What is the most interesting fact you know about ___? Where did you learn it?"
- "If you were going to revise this piece, what is ONE thing you would change to make it even better?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- After multiple sessions, the student cannot write a complete paragraph (topic sentence, supporting sentences, closing sentence) even with scaffolding — they may need additional sentence and paragraph construction instruction before tackling the writing types in this unit
- The student can express opinions and ideas fluently in speech but produces very little writing — this gap between oral and written expression may indicate a fine motor, processing, or attention issue; consider allowing dictation or typing as an accommodation
- The student consistently cannot distinguish between an opinion and a fact, even after explicit instruction and practice
- The student cannot sequence events in logical order, even orally — this may indicate challenges with temporal reasoning that affect both narrative writing and reading comprehension
- Despite repeated instruction, the student cannot read or comprehend the source materials in Lesson 8 — they may need reading support before research writing is accessible
- The student shows significant frustration, avoidance, or distress around writing tasks even with scaffolding, encouragement, and choice of topic

If you see these signs, consider:

- Breaking writing tasks into very small steps: first plan (orally or with pictures), then write one sentence at a time, then combine
- Using speech-to-text tools or dictation so the student can focus on content and organization rather than the physical act of writing
- Reducing length expectations and increasing structure (provide sentence starters, graphic organizers, and word banks)
- Allowing the student to choose their own topics for all writing tasks — engagement increases dramatically when students write about what they care about
- Consulting with a literacy specialist if writing difficulties persist despite scaffolding and encouragement

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 4: Grammar & Vocabulary**, which teaches the language conventions that strengthen all types of writing: regular and irregular verbs, comparative and superlative adjectives, commas and quotation marks in dialogue (reinforcing Lesson 6 of this unit), and using dictionaries and glossaries to determine word meanings.

The writing skills from this unit carry forward throughout the rest of Grade 3 and beyond. Students will continue to write opinions, informative texts, and narratives with increasing sophistication. The research skills from Lesson 8 expand in Grades 4-5 to longer research projects with more sources and formal citations. Dialogue writing becomes a natural part of narrative craft. Revision and editing become habitual.

Between units, encourage daily writing practice. Even 10-15 minutes of journal writing, a letter to a friend or family member, a short opinion about something the student cares about, or a fictional story keeps writing muscles strong. Read published writing together and talk about what makes it effective — the more students read, the better they write.
