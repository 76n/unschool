# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 3

## Topic
Informative Writing Introduction

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between opinion writing and informative/explanatory writing
- Write a clear topic sentence that introduces an informative topic
- Use a planning web or outline to group related information before writing

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a structured opinion piece with reasons and evidence (Lessons 1-2)
- Understanding of main idea and supporting details (Grade 2 / Unit 2 reading skills)
- Ability to write complete sentences with correct capitalization and end punctuation

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Blank paper for planning webs (or use lined paper)
- Colored pencils (for organizing groups of information by color)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Index cards (optional, for sorting facts into groups)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review opinion writing from Lessons 1-2 and introduce the distinction between opinion and informative writing.

**Activity: What Type of Writing?**

Say to the student: "You have been doing great work writing opinions. Today, we are starting a new type of writing: informative writing. Before we begin, let's make sure we can tell the difference."

Read each statement. Ask: "Is this from an opinion piece or an informative piece?"

1. "I believe that soccer is the best sport because it is exciting to watch." (Opinion — "I believe" signals an opinion.)
2. "Soccer is played in over 200 countries around the world." (Informative — this is a fact that can be verified.)
3. "Butterflies go through four stages of life: egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, and adult." (Informative — this teaches the reader a fact.)
4. "Everyone should learn about butterflies because they are fascinating." (Opinion — "everyone should" and "fascinating" signal an opinion.)
5. "The White House has 132 rooms, 35 bathrooms, and 6 levels." (Informative — these are verifiable facts.)

Say: "Good! In opinion writing, the goal is to convince the reader to agree with you. In informative writing, the goal is to TEACH the reader about a topic. You are the teacher, and your writing is the lesson."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Informative writing:** Writing that teaches the reader about a topic using facts, definitions, and details — it does NOT try to convince or tell a story
- **Topic sentence:** The first sentence of a paragraph that tells the reader what the paragraph is about
- **Supporting details:** Facts, definitions, and examples that give the reader more information about the topic
- **Grouping:** Putting related information together so the reader can follow along easily
- **Planning web:** A graphic organizer with a main topic in the center and related subtopics branching out — used to organize ideas before writing
- **Outline:** A numbered list that shows the order and organization of your writing before you draft it

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain What Informative Writing Does**

Say: "In Lessons 1 and 2, you wrote opinion pieces. Your goal was to tell the reader what you THINK and convince them with reasons and evidence. Informative writing is different. Your goal is to TEACH the reader about a topic. You are like a little encyclopedia — you give facts, definitions, and details so the reader learns something new."

Write this comparison:

| | Opinion Writing | Informative Writing |
|---|---|---|
| **Goal** | Convince the reader | Teach the reader |
| **Uses** | Opinions, reasons, evidence | Facts, definitions, details |
| **Sounds like** | "I believe... because..." | "There are three types of..." |
| **Reader feels** | "I agree!" or "I disagree!" | "I learned something new!" |

**Step 2: Teach Topic Sentences for Informative Writing**

Say: "Every informative paragraph starts with a TOPIC SENTENCE. The topic sentence tells the reader what the paragraph is going to be about. It is like a title for the paragraph."

Write three examples of topic sentences:

1. "There are many interesting facts about dolphins."
2. "Volcanoes form when hot melted rock pushes up through the earth's surface."
3. "The water cycle has three main stages."

Say: "A good topic sentence is clear and specific. It does NOT include your opinion. Compare these:"

Write:
- Weak: "I am going to write about dogs." (Too vague — tells us nothing about what we will learn.)
- Weak: "Dogs are the best." (This is an opinion, not informative.)
- Strong: "Dogs come in hundreds of different breeds, each with unique sizes, shapes, and talents." (Clear topic, specific, informative.)

Ask the student: "Which topic sentence tells you the most about what you are going to learn?"

**Step 3: Teach Grouping with a Planning Web**

Say: "Before you start writing an informative piece, you need to PLAN. The most important part of planning is GROUPING — putting related information together. If you just dump all your facts in a pile, the reader gets confused. But if you organize your facts into groups, the reader can follow along."

Draw a planning web on paper:

```
                    [Dogs]
                   /  |  \
           [Sizes]  [Food]  [Jobs]
           /    \     |      /    \
     tiny  huge  kibble  police  guide
     dogs  dogs  water   dogs    dogs
```

Say: "See how I took the topic 'Dogs' and created three groups: Sizes, Food, and Jobs? Each group holds related facts. When I write my paragraphs, each group becomes its own section."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Modeling an Informative Paragraph from a Planning Web**

Say: "Watch me turn a planning web into an informative paragraph. I am going to plan first, then write."

**Step 1: Create the planning web in front of the student.**

Topic: Penguins

Draw:
```
                    [Penguins]
                   /     |     \
        [What they     [Where    [What they
         look like]    they live]   eat]
         /     \         |        /     \
     black &   cannot   Antarctica  fish   krill
     white     fly      & cold      squid
     feathers  waddle   places
```

**Step 2: Write a topic sentence.**
Say: "I need a topic sentence that introduces penguins as my topic."
Write: **"Penguins are one of the most interesting birds in the world."**

**Step 3: Write about each group.**
Say: "Now I will write about my first group — what penguins look like."
Write: **"Penguins have black and white feathers that make them look like they are wearing tiny tuxedos. Unlike most birds, penguins cannot fly. Instead, they waddle on land and are excellent swimmers."**

Say: "Now my second group — where penguins live."
Write: **"Most penguins live in cold places. Many species live in Antarctica, where temperatures can drop far below freezing. Some penguins also live in South America, South Africa, and New Zealand."**

Say: "Now my third group — what penguins eat."
Write: **"Penguins eat fish, squid, and tiny creatures called krill. They catch their food by diving into the ocean. Some penguins can hold their breath for over 20 minutes while hunting underwater."**

Say: "Finally, I need a concluding sentence."
Write: **"From their tuxedo-like feathers to their amazing underwater hunting skills, penguins are truly remarkable birds."**

Read the complete piece aloud:

> Penguins are one of the most interesting birds in the world. Penguins have black and white feathers that make them look like they are wearing tiny tuxedos. Unlike most birds, penguins cannot fly. Instead, they waddle on land and are excellent swimmers. Most penguins live in cold places. Many species live in Antarctica, where temperatures can drop far below freezing. Some penguins also live in South America, South Africa, and New Zealand. Penguins eat fish, squid, and tiny creatures called krill. They catch their food by diving into the ocean. Some penguins can hold their breath for over 20 minutes while hunting underwater. From their tuxedo-like feathers to their amazing underwater hunting skills, penguins are truly remarkable birds.

Say: "Notice how the information is GROUPED. All the facts about what penguins look like are together. All the facts about where they live are together. All the facts about food are together. I did not jump from food to appearance to food to location. That would confuse the reader."

**Worked Example 2: A Disorganized Example**

Say: "Now let me show you what happens when you do NOT group information."

Write or read:

> Penguins eat fish. They have black and white feathers. Some penguins live in Antarctica. They also eat krill. Penguins cannot fly. It is very cold in Antarctica. Penguins swim really well. They catch squid too.

Say: "These are all real facts about penguins. But it is hard to follow because the facts are mixed up — food, then appearance, then location, then back to food, then appearance again."

Ask: "Which version is easier to understand — the organized one or the mixed-up one?" (The organized one.)
Ask: "Why?" (Because related facts are grouped together, so you can focus on one topic at a time.)

Say: "That is why we PLAN before we write. The planning web helps us group our information BEFORE we start writing, so we do not end up with a jumbled pile of facts."

**Worked Example 3: Making a Planning Web Together (Quick)**

Say: "Let's practice making a planning web for a different topic."

Topic: Apples

Together with the student, create a web:
```
                    [Apples]
                   /    |    \
          [Types]    [How they   [Uses]
          /    \      grow]      /    \
       red   green   trees    eating  baking
       golden         seeds   juice   pies
```

Say: "Now we have a plan. If I were going to write about apples, I would write one section about types, one about how they grow, and one about uses. The plan keeps me organized."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice creating a planning web and writing topic sentences together.

**Activity 1: Build a Planning Web (6 minutes)**

Say: "Let's create a planning web together for a topic you know something about."

**Topic: Your favorite animal** (or let the student choose from: frogs, horses, cats, dogs, sharks)

Guide the student through these steps:

1. Write the animal name in the center of the paper.
2. Ask: "What are three things we could tell a reader about this animal?" Help the student identify 3 subtopics (e.g., what it looks like, where it lives, what it eats; OR: interesting abilities, habitat, babies).
3. For each subtopic, ask: "What facts do you know about this?" Help the student add 2-3 facts under each subtopic.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student cannot think of facts, it is fine to brainstorm together or look things up. The goal is to practice organizing, not to test the student's knowledge.*

**Activity 2: Write a Topic Sentence and First Group (6 minutes)**

Using the planning web from Activity 1:

1. Say: "Write a topic sentence that introduces your animal to the reader. Remember, it should be clear and specific."
2. Help the student write the topic sentence. Check: Does it tell the reader what the paragraph is about? Is it a fact, not an opinion?
3. Say: "Now choose ONE group from your web and write 2-3 sentences about it. Keep the related facts together."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student starts mixing facts from different groups, gently say: "That fact is about [different subtopic]. Let's save that for when we write about [that group]. Right now, let's stay focused on [current group]."*

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student creates a planning web and writes an informative paragraph independently.

**Directions:** "Choose ONE topic below. Create a planning web with at least three groups of information. Then write an informative paragraph with a topic sentence, information from at least two groups, and a concluding sentence."

**Topic Choices:**
- A sport you know a lot about
- Your neighborhood or town
- A type of weather (rain, snow, thunderstorms, tornadoes)
- A topic of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Create a planning web with three groups. Write an informative paragraph with a topic sentence, sentences from at least two groups, and a concluding sentence. Aim for 6-8 sentences total.

**Stretch:** Create a planning web with three or four groups. Write an informative piece with a topic sentence, sentences from ALL groups (with clear transitions between groups such as "Another interesting thing about..." or "In addition..."), and a concluding sentence. Aim for 8-12 sentences.

**Challenge:** Create a planning web for a topic you want to research and learn more about. For facts you are not sure about, write a question mark. After writing what you know, make a list of 3 questions you would like to answer through research. (This previews Lesson 8.)

### Interleaved Review

After completing the informative paragraph, answer these review questions:

5. **Lesson 1-2 Review:** What is the difference between a reason and evidence in opinion writing? (A reason explains WHY you hold your opinion. Evidence is proof — a detail, example, or fact — that shows the reason is true.)

6. **Unit 2 Reading Connection:** "What is a text feature? Name one type of text feature and explain how it helps a reader." (A text feature is a part of informational text that helps readers find or understand information. Examples: headings, bold words, diagrams, maps, captions, table of contents.)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. What is the goal of informative writing? (To teach the reader about a topic using facts, definitions, and details.)
2. What is a topic sentence? (The first sentence of a paragraph that tells the reader what the paragraph is about.)
3. Why is it important to GROUP related information together in informative writing? (So the reader can follow along easily; so facts about the same subtopic are together instead of mixed up.)

**Mastery = Student correctly explains the goal of informative writing AND explains why grouping information matters.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice the opinion vs. informative distinction with more examples: read short paragraphs and have the student identify which type each one is
- Use physical index cards: write facts about a topic on cards, then have the student physically sort them into groups
- Practice writing topic sentences for simple, familiar topics: "Write a topic sentence about your family," "Write a topic sentence about your school"
- Before the next lesson, have the student create a planning web for a topic they love (favorite game, hobby, animal) — just the web, no writing yet

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 4 (Informative Writing with Facts) next session. Preview: "Today you learned to plan and organize informative writing. Next time, we are going to make your informative writing even stronger by adding facts, definitions, and specific details. You will also learn how illustrations and diagrams can help explain your topic."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Expert Page:** The student becomes an "expert" on a topic they love and creates a one-page informative piece complete with a title, organized paragraphs, and a drawing or diagram. This piece can be shared with a family member.
- **Informative vs. Opinion Challenge:** Give the student a topic (e.g., "pizza"). Have them write TWO paragraphs about the same topic: one informative ("Pizza is made by...") and one opinion ("I think pizza is the best food because..."). Compare the two.
- **Planning Web Gallery:** Create planning webs for 3-4 different topics (fast — just the webs, not the full paragraphs). Display them and talk about which one would make the most interesting informative piece and why.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson introduces a new writing type, so allow time for the student to absorb the distinction between opinion and informative writing. If the student seems confused about the difference, spend extra time on the Warm-Up and the comparison chart before moving to the planning web.

**Connection to reading:** If the student has completed Grade 3 Unit 2 (Reading Informational Text), they have already encountered informative text structures as a reader. Connect explicitly: "Remember when you read informational texts and found the main idea and supporting details? Now YOU are going to be the author who creates that structure."

**The planning web is the key skill.** More than the writing itself, the ability to ORGANIZE information before writing is what separates Grade 3 informative writing from earlier grades. Students who plan before writing produce more coherent, complete, and well-organized pieces. Invest time in this skill — it pays off in every lesson that follows.

**Do not worry about perfect facts.** At this stage, the student may not know many facts about a given topic. That is fine. The goal of this lesson is to learn to ORGANIZE information, not to be an encyclopedia. If the student says "I think penguins live in cold places," that is close enough for now. Lesson 8 (Research) will teach how to find and verify information.

**Common issue — starting too broad:** Students sometimes choose topics that are too big ("animals" or "the world"). Help them narrow: "Animals is a huge topic. What ONE animal do you want to write about? Let's focus on that."

**Growth mindset note:** Some students who enjoy opinion writing may be less excited about informative writing. Say: "Opinion writing is about what YOU think. Informative writing is about what you KNOW. Both are powerful. When you write informative text, you get to be the expert — the one who teaches other people."
