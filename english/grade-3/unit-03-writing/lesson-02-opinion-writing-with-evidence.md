# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 2

## Topic
Opinion Writing with Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between a reason and evidence that supports a reason
- Use the words "because" and "for example" to connect an opinion to supporting evidence
- Write a complete opinion paragraph that includes an opinion, reasons, AND evidence from texts or personal experience

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a five-part opinion piece with introduction, three reasons, and conclusion (Lesson 1)
- Understanding of transition words: First, Also/Another reason, Finally (Lesson 1)
- Ability to write complete sentences with correct capitalization and end punctuation

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- The student's opinion piece from Lesson 1 (for revision)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Colored pencils (two colors: one for reasons, one for evidence)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the five-part opinion structure from Lesson 1 and activate thinking about what makes reasons convincing.

**Activity: Strong Reason or Weak Reason?**

Say to the student: "Last time, we learned how to organize an opinion piece with five parts. Today, we are going to make those reasons STRONGER by adding evidence. But first, let's practice telling the difference between a strong reason and a weak one."

Read each pair. Ask: "Which reason is stronger? Why?"

**Pair 1 — Opinion: Dogs are great pets.**
- A: "Dogs are cute." (Weak — vague, just a feeling)
- B: "Dogs are loyal companions because they greet you at the door every day and always want to be near you." (Strong — specific, with an example)

**Pair 2 — Opinion: Summer is the best season.**
- A: "In summer, you can swim in pools and lakes, go to the beach, and have cookouts with your family." (Strong — specific examples)
- B: "Summer is fun." (Weak — no explanation of WHY)

**Pair 3 — Opinion: Reading is important.**
- A: "Reading helps you learn new words, which makes you a better writer and speaker." (Strong — explains the connection)
- B: "Everyone says reading is good." (Weak — no real reason, just repeating what others say)

Say: "The strong reasons all have something in common — they include specific EVIDENCE. Evidence is like proof. It is the details, examples, and facts that show your reason is true. Today we are going to learn how to add evidence to every reason we write."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Evidence:** Specific details, examples, or facts that support a reason — the "proof" that shows your reason is true
- **Because:** A connecting word that links an opinion or reason to its evidence ("I like reading BECAUSE it takes me to new places")
- **For example:** A phrase that introduces a specific example as evidence ("Pets teach responsibility. FOR EXAMPLE, you have to feed them every day.")
- **Personal experience:** Something that has happened to you — your own life can be a source of evidence
- **Text evidence:** Information that comes from a book, article, or other text you have read

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain the Difference Between a Reason and Evidence**

Say: "In Lesson 1, we learned to support our opinions with reasons. A reason answers the question 'Why do you think that?' Today, we are adding another layer: EVIDENCE. Evidence answers the question 'How do you know that is true?' or 'Can you give me an example?'"

Write this chart:

| | What It Does | Question It Answers | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opinion** | States what you think | What do you believe? | "I think dogs are the best pets." |
| **Reason** | Explains why you think it | Why do you believe that? | "Dogs are loyal." |
| **Evidence** | Proves or shows the reason is true | How do you know? Can you show me? | "For example, my dog Max waits by the door every day until I come home." |

Say: "See the three layers? Opinion is what you THINK. Reason is WHY you think it. Evidence is HOW YOU KNOW or proof that your reason is true."

**Step 2: Teach the "Because" and "For Example" Connectors**

Say: "There are two magic words that help you attach evidence to your reasons."

Write:

**Connector 1: BECAUSE**
"I think ___ BECAUSE ___."

**Connector 2: FOR EXAMPLE**
"___. FOR EXAMPLE, ___."

Say: "'Because' works inside a sentence. 'For example' usually starts a new sentence. Let me show you both."

**Demonstration:**
- Without evidence: "First, recess helps kids stay healthy."
- With "because": "First, recess helps kids stay healthy **because** running and playing gives our bodies the exercise they need."
- With "for example": "First, recess helps kids stay healthy. **For example,** when we play tag or jump rope, our hearts beat faster and our muscles get stronger."

Say: "Both versions add evidence. 'Because' connects the reason and evidence in one sentence. 'For example' gives a specific example in a new sentence. Both are correct. Good writers use both."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Modeling an Opinion Paragraph with Evidence**

Say: "Watch me write an opinion paragraph. This time, I am going to add evidence to EVERY reason."

Write this in front of the student, thinking aloud:

**Topic: Should third graders be allowed to choose their own books for reading time?**

*Writing the introduction:*
Say: "I need to state my opinion clearly."
Write: **"I believe third graders should be allowed to choose their own books during reading time."**

*Writing Reason 1 with evidence:*
Say: "My first reason is about motivation. I will use 'because' to connect my evidence."
Write: **"First, students read more when they get to pick their own books because they choose topics they are excited about. For example, a student who loves animals will read a whole book about sharks, but might not finish a book about something they find boring."**
Say: "See? My reason is 'students read more when they choose.' My evidence is the example about the student who loves animals."

*Writing Reason 2 with evidence:*
Say: "My second reason is about learning to make choices."
Write: **"Also, choosing books teaches students to make good decisions. For example, a student might pick up a book, realize it is too easy, and trade it for one that is more challenging. That is a skill they will use all their life."**

*Writing Reason 3 with evidence:*
Say: "My final reason is my strongest."
Write: **"Finally, students are all different, and one book does not work for everyone because each student reads at a different level and has different interests. A book that is perfect for one reader might be too hard or too easy for another."**

*Writing the conclusion:*
Write: **"For all these reasons, letting third graders choose their own reading books makes reading time more fun, more useful, and more fair."**

Read the complete paragraph aloud. Then say: "Let's find the evidence." Use one color to underline reasons and a second color to underline evidence. Count the evidence: there should be at least one piece of evidence per reason.

**Worked Example 2: Upgrading a Weak Opinion Paragraph**

Say: "Now let's look at a paragraph that has reasons but NO evidence. Our job is to add evidence."

Write or read aloud:

> I think recess should be longer. First, it is good for our health. Also, it helps us focus in class. Finally, it is fun. That is why recess should be longer.

Say: "This has the five-part structure from Lesson 1. But the reasons are thin — they have no evidence. Let's add evidence to each one."

Work with the student to add evidence:

- "First, it is good for our health" → ADD: "**because running and playing for at least 20 minutes gives our bodies the exercise they need to stay strong.**"
- "Also, it helps us focus in class" → ADD: "**For example, after recess, I always feel like my brain is ready to think again, and I can pay attention better during math.**"
- "Finally, it is fun" → ADD: "**because we get to play with our friends, make up games, and use our imaginations. For example, my friends and I play a game we invented called Dragon Tag.**"

Read the upgraded version aloud. Ask: "Which version is more convincing — the one with evidence or the one without?"

**Worked Example 3: Using Text Evidence vs. Personal Experience**

Say: "Evidence can come from two places: your own life (personal experience) or something you have read (text evidence). Both are valuable."

Write two examples:

**Personal experience evidence:**
"I think pets teach kids responsibility because I have to feed my cat every morning and clean her litter box every evening. If I forget, she meows loudly to remind me!"

**Text evidence:**
"I think pets teach kids responsibility because I read an article that said children who care for pets learn to follow routines and think about someone else's needs."

Say: "In this unit, you can use either kind of evidence. As you get older, you will use more and more text evidence. For now, your own experiences and things you have read are both great sources."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice adding evidence to reasons collaboratively.

**Activity 1: Add Evidence to These Reasons (6 minutes)**

Say: "I am going to give you some opinion statements with reasons. Your job is to add evidence using 'because' or 'for example.' We will do the first two together, then you try the third one on your own."

**Statement 1:**
"I think all kids should learn to cook. First, cooking is an important life skill."
Facilitator and student together: Add evidence. Example: "...because when you grow up, you need to be able to make your own meals instead of eating out every day."

**Statement 2:**
"Also, cooking helps you learn math."
Together: Add evidence. Example: "For example, when you follow a recipe, you have to measure cups, tablespoons, and teaspoons, and sometimes you have to double or halve the amounts."

**Statement 3:**
"Finally, cooking is a way to show love to your family."
Student adds evidence independently. Example: "For example, I made pancakes for my mom's birthday and she said it was the best present she ever received."

*Facilitator prompt: If the student's evidence is vague ("because it just is"), ask: "Can you give me a specific example? Something that really happened to you, or something you can picture happening?"*

**Activity 2: Revise the Lesson 1 Piece (6 minutes)**

Say: "Now take out the opinion piece you wrote in Lesson 1. Read through it. For each reason, ask yourself: 'Did I include evidence? Did I use because or for example?' If not, add evidence to at least TWO of your three reasons."

Have the student reread their Lesson 1 piece and revise it by adding evidence. Help as needed.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student's Lesson 1 piece is not available, have them write a new opinion paragraph on the topic "What is the best game to play at recess?" using the five-part structure WITH evidence.*

---

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes a complete opinion paragraph with reasons and evidence independently.

**Directions:** "Choose ONE topic below. Write a complete opinion paragraph that includes: a clear introduction stating your opinion, three reasons with evidence (use 'because' or 'for example' for each), and a conclusion."

**Topic Choices:**
- Should kids have their own phone?
- Is it better to have a big family or a small family?
- What is the best subject in school?
- A topic of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a complete opinion paragraph with all five parts. Include at least one piece of evidence (using "because" or "for example") for each of the three reasons. Evidence may come from personal experience.

**Stretch:** Write a complete opinion paragraph with all five parts. Include at least TWO pieces of evidence for one of the three reasons — one from personal experience and one from something you have read or learned. Try to make each reason 2-3 sentences long.

**Challenge:** Write a complete opinion paragraph, then write a short paragraph (3-4 sentences) from the OPPOSITE point of view. What would someone who DISAGREES say? What evidence might they use? (This introduces counterargument thinking.)

### Interleaved Review

After completing the opinion paragraph, answer these review questions:

5. **Lesson 1 Review:** What are the five parts of an opinion piece? (Introduction, Reason 1, Reason 2, Reason 3, Conclusion)

6. **Unit 1 Reading Connection:** "Characters' actions can tell us about their personality. If a character in a story shares their lunch with a friend who forgot theirs, what does that tell us about the character?" (The character is kind, generous, caring, thoughtful — accept any reasonable character trait.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. What is the difference between a reason and evidence? (A reason explains WHY you hold your opinion. Evidence is proof or an example that shows the reason is true.)
2. Write one sentence using the word "because" to connect a reason to evidence. (Example: "I think we should have a class pet because taking care of an animal teaches us to be responsible." Accept any sentence that correctly uses "because" to link a reason to supporting evidence.)
3. Write one sentence using the phrase "for example" to give evidence. (Example: "Recess helps us stay healthy. For example, we play tag, jump rope, and climb on the monkey bars." Accept any sentence that gives a specific example after "for example.")

**Mastery = Student correctly explains the difference between a reason and evidence AND writes at least one correct sentence using "because" or "for example."**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the chart showing Opinion → Reason → Evidence and practice with more examples
- Give the student a reason and have them brainstorm 2-3 pieces of evidence for it orally before writing
- Practice the sentence frames: "___ because ___" and "For example, ___" with everyday topics (favorite food, favorite game, etc.)
- Before the next lesson, have the student add evidence to a simple opinion statement each day as a quick warm-up

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 3 (Informative Writing Introduction) next session. Preview: "You have learned to write strong opinions with evidence. Next, we are going to learn a different type of writing: informative writing. Instead of telling the reader what you THINK, you will teach the reader about a topic using FACTS."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Evidence Scavenger Hunt:** Choose a topic the student has an opinion about. Together, look for evidence in books, articles, or trustworthy websites. The student collects 3-5 pieces of text evidence on index cards, then uses them to write a stronger opinion piece.
- **Convince Me Challenge:** The facilitator names a position they hold (e.g., "I think bedtime should be at 8:00"). The student must present three reasons with evidence to change the facilitator's mind. The facilitator plays "hard to convince" and asks follow-up questions: "But how do you know that? Give me an example."
- **Opinion Book Review:** The student writes a short opinion paragraph about a book they recently read. State the opinion (e.g., "I think everyone should read Charlotte's Web"), give three reasons with evidence FROM THE BOOK, and write a conclusion.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson builds directly on Lesson 1. If the student mastered the five-part structure in Lesson 1, this lesson should be manageable in one session. If the student is still shaky on structure, spend extra time on the Guided Practice revision activity, which reinforces both structure AND evidence.

**The key distinction:** Many students confuse reasons and evidence because they feel similar. The simplest way to explain it: a reason answers "WHY?" and evidence answers "PROVE IT" or "SHOW ME." If you keep asking "But how do you know?" after the student gives a reason, the answer to that question is the evidence.

**Personal experience is valid evidence at this age.** While academic writing relies more heavily on text evidence, Grade 3 students are still building the bridge from personal to textual evidence. Validate personal experience ("Your own life is a great source of evidence!") while also introducing the idea that "things you have read" can support your points too. Lesson 8 (Research) will develop text evidence skills further.

**Model imperfection.** When writing in front of the student, occasionally say things like: "Hmm, let me think about what evidence I have for this reason... I am not sure yet. Let me come back to it." This normalizes the struggle of finding evidence and shows that writing is a thinking process, not a performance.

**Writing quality levels:** Here is what student work might look like at different levels for this lesson:

*Developing:* "I think cats are good pets because they are nice. Also they are soft. Finally they are fun. Cats are the best."
(Has structure from Lesson 1, but reasons lack evidence. Each reason is just a single adjective.)

*Approaching:* "I think cats are good pets. First, they are easy to take care of because you just have to feed them and clean their litter box. Also, they are cuddly. Finally, they are fun to play with because they chase string."
(Has some evidence but it is uneven — reason 2 has no evidence.)

*Mastery:* "I think cats are wonderful pets. First, cats are easy to take care of because they clean themselves and do not need to be walked like dogs. Also, cats are great companions because they curl up next to you when you are reading or watching TV. For example, my cat Luna always sits on my lap when I do homework. Finally, cats are playful and entertaining because they chase toys, pounce on shadows, and do funny things that make the whole family laugh. For all these reasons, cats are the best pets you could have."
(Clear opinion, three reasons, evidence for each using "because" and "for example," strong conclusion.)
