# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 10

## Topic
Writing Celebration and Publishing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Select their strongest piece of writing from the unit and explain why they chose it
- Produce a polished final copy ("published" version) with neat handwriting or typed text and an illustration
- Share their writing aloud with an audience and reflect on their growth as a writer

## Prerequisites
- Completion of at least 3-4 writing pieces from this unit (opinion, informative, narrative, and/or research)
- Experience with revision and editing (Lesson 9)
- A writing folder containing drafts from the unit

## Materials Needed
- The student's writing folder with all drafts from Lessons 1-9
- Fresh lined paper or blank paper for the final copy (consider special paper or a small blank book if available)
- Pencil, colored pencils, and/or markers for illustrations and decorative elements
- Self-editing checklist from Lesson 9
- Access to a computer and printer (optional, if the student wants to type their piece)
- A small audience: the facilitator and ideally 1-2 additional family members, friends, or stuffed-animal "audience members"

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build excitement for the publishing celebration and review the writing types learned in this unit.

**Activity: Unit Writing Review**

Say to the student: "Today is a special day. Today, you are going to become a PUBLISHED AUTHOR. But first, let's look back at everything you have accomplished in this unit."

Ask: "Can you name the four types of writing we have learned in this unit?" (Opinion, informative, narrative, and research.)

Say: "You have written SO much during this unit. Let's look through your writing folder together."

Spread out the student's writing pieces. For each one, briefly discuss:
- "What type of writing is this?"
- "What do you remember about writing this piece?"
- "What are you proud of in this piece?"

Say: "Today, you are going to choose your BEST piece — the one you are most proud of — and turn it into a published work. Published means polished, beautiful, and ready to share with the world."

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Publish:** To prepare a piece of writing for sharing — making it clean, polished, and presentable
- **Final copy:** The finished version of a piece of writing after all revisions and edits have been made
- **Author's choice:** The writer's decision about which piece represents their best work
- **Reflection:** Thinking about your own learning — what you have improved at, what was hard, and what you want to work on next

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Explain What Publishing Means**

Say: "When a real author publishes a book, it goes through many steps: drafting, revising, editing, designing, and printing. Today, we are going to do our own version of that process."

Write the publishing steps:

**Our Publishing Process:**
1. **Choose** your best piece from the unit
2. **Give it one final revision and edit** using the self-editing checklist
3. **Create a final copy** — neat handwriting or typed, with a title and your name
4. **Add an illustration** — a picture that goes with your writing
5. **Share** your writing aloud with your audience
6. **Reflect** on what you learned as a writer

**Step 2: Guide the Selection Process**

Say: "Choosing your best piece does not just mean picking the longest one or the one that was easiest to write. Think about these questions:"

Write:
- Which piece am I most PROUD of?
- Which piece did I put the most EFFORT into?
- Which piece best shows what I have LEARNED as a writer?
- Which piece would I most want someone else to READ?

Say: "There is no wrong answer. This is YOUR choice as an author."

**Step 3: Model Final Revision**

Say: "Even though you already revised in Lesson 9, published work gets ONE MORE look. This is like a final quality check."

Model a quick final check on a short sample:

Write a nearly-finished piece with one small error and one opportunity for improvement:

> My favrite animal is the octopus. First, octopuses are incredibly smart. For example, they can open jars, solve puzzles, and even escape from aquariums. Also, octopuses can change their color and texture to blend in with their surroundings, which helps them hide from predators. Finally, octopuses have three hearts. For all these reasons, octopuses are the most amazing animals in the ocean.

Say: "Let me do my final check."
- Spelling: "favrite" should be "favorite."
- Content: Could I add a definition? "Predators — animals that hunt other animals for food." Yes, that helps.
- Everything else looks good.

Write the corrected version:

> My favorite animal is the octopus. First, octopuses are incredibly smart. For example, they can open jars, solve puzzles, and even escape from aquariums. Also, octopuses can change their color and texture to blend in with their surroundings, which helps them hide from predators, or animals that hunt them. Finally, octopuses have three hearts — two for pumping blood to the gills and one for the rest of the body. For all these reasons, octopuses are the most amazing animals in the ocean.

Say: "One spelling fix and two small additions made this even better. That is the power of one final look."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example: What a Published Piece Looks Like**

Say: "Let me show you what a published piece looks like."

Describe (or create) a model:
- **Title** centered at the top in large, neat letters: "Why Recess Should Be Longer"
- **Author line** underneath: "By [Student Name]"
- **The text** written neatly in the student's best handwriting, or typed and printed
- **An illustration** at the bottom or on a separate page: a colorful drawing of kids playing at recess
- **Optional:** a decorative border, a "published on [date]" line, or an "About the Author" sentence at the end

Say: "Your published piece is something you can be proud of. You can display it, give it to someone, or keep it in a special place."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Guide the student through selecting a piece and doing a final revision and edit.

**Activity 1: Choose Your Best Piece (4 minutes)**

Say: "Look through your writing folder. Read through each piece you have written. Then choose the one you want to publish."

Give the student time to review their work. If they are stuck between two pieces, help by asking:
- "Which one makes you feel more proud when you read it?"
- "Which one did you learn the most from writing?"
- "Which one would you most want to read aloud to someone?"

Once the student chooses, ask: "Why did you choose this one?" Have them explain their reasoning. This is the beginning of their reflection.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student chooses a piece that has significant errors or is very short, do not redirect them. Honor their choice. The final revision process will help strengthen it.*

**Activity 2: Final Revision and Edit (8 minutes)**

Say: "Now let's give your piece one final revision and edit. Use your self-editing checklist from Lesson 9."

Guide the student through:

1. **Read it aloud.** The student reads the piece aloud to the facilitator. Listening to the words often reveals awkward phrasing, missing words, or unclear sentences.

2. **Revision check:** Ask:
   - "Is there a place where you could add one more detail or sensory word?"
   - "Is there anything that is repeated or unnecessary?"
   - "Does the order make sense from beginning to end?"

3. **Editing check:** Go through the self-editing checklist:
   - Capitalization
   - Punctuation (including dialogue punctuation if applicable)
   - Spelling (circle anything uncertain and check)
   - Paragraph structure

4. **Make final changes** on the draft before creating the final copy.

*Facilitator prompt: This is the student's piece. Make suggestions, but let THEM decide what to change. Say: "I noticed something here — what do you think?" rather than "Change this."*

## Independent Practice (15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student creates the final published version and an illustration.

**Directions:** "Create your published piece. Write (or type) the final copy of your chosen writing piece. Use your best handwriting. Include a title and your name. Add an illustration or decorative element that connects to your writing."

**Publishing Steps:**

1. **Write the title** centered at the top in large, clear letters
2. **Write "By [Your Name]"** underneath the title
3. **Copy the revised and edited text** in your best handwriting (or type it if a computer is available)
4. **Add an illustration:** Draw a picture that relates to your writing. If you wrote an opinion piece, illustrate the topic. If you wrote a narrative, illustrate your favorite scene. If you wrote an informative piece or research report, draw a labeled diagram.
5. **Optional additions:** An "About the Author" sentence at the bottom (e.g., "The author is a third grader who loves writing stories about adventures"), a decorative border, or a dedication ("For my family")

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Create a polished final copy with title, author name, neat text, and one illustration. The piece should be fully revised and edited with no significant errors remaining.

**Stretch:** Create a polished final copy with all elements above, plus: an "About the Author" section (2-3 sentences about yourself as a writer), a table of contents or section headings if the piece has multiple parts, and multiple illustrations or a labeled diagram.

**Challenge:** Create a polished final copy, plus write a "Writer's Note" on the back or on a separate page explaining: (1) what type of writing this is, (2) what writing skills you used (e.g., "I used temporal words to organize events and dialogue to show character"), (3) what you would change if you wrote it again. This is advanced metacognitive reflection.

### Interleaved Review

This lesson does not include a traditional interleaved review section. Instead, the publishing and sharing process itself serves as a comprehensive review of all skills learned in the unit.

## Sharing and Celebration (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student shares their published writing with an audience and reflects on their growth.

**This section replaces the standard Check for Understanding for this lesson.**

**Step 1: Share Writing Aloud (5 minutes)**

Gather the audience (the facilitator and any available family members, friends, or even stuffed animals as stand-ins). Create a warm, celebratory atmosphere.

Say: "Ladies and gentlemen, I would like to introduce our published author: [Student Name]! [Student Name] has been working hard on writing this unit, and today they are going to share their published piece with us."

The student reads their piece aloud. Encourage the student to:
- Read slowly and clearly
- Look up from the page occasionally
- Show the illustration

After the reading, the audience responds:
- One specific thing they liked about the writing
- One question about the writing or the topic
- Applause!

**Step 2: Writer's Reflection (5 minutes)**

Say: "Every author reflects on their writing journey. Let's reflect on yours."

Ask the student to answer these questions orally or in writing:

1. "What type of writing do you feel MOST confident about now — opinion, informative, narrative, or research? Why?"
2. "What is ONE skill you learned in this unit that made your writing better?" (Examples: using evidence, writing dialogue, adding sensory details, organizing with transitions, paraphrasing for research)
3. "What was the HARDEST part of writing in this unit? How did you handle it?"
4. "If you could give advice to a younger student who is learning to write, what would you say?"
5. "What do you want to work on next as a writer?"

*Facilitator prompt: Listen actively and affirm specific growth you have observed. For example: "I noticed that your dialogue punctuation really improved from Lesson 6 to now. In your first try, the quotation marks were tricky, but in your published piece, they are perfect. That shows real growth."*

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

This lesson's assessment is the published piece itself and the oral reflection. Use the following checklist to evaluate:

**Published Piece Checklist:**
- [ ] Student selected a piece and explained their choice
- [ ] Final revision was completed (evidence of adding, deleting, or rearranging)
- [ ] Final editing was completed (most errors in capitalization, punctuation, and spelling are corrected)
- [ ] Final copy is neat and legible with a title and author name
- [ ] An illustration or decorative element is included
- [ ] Student read the piece aloud to an audience
- [ ] Student reflected on their growth as a writer

**Mastery = Student completes the published piece with all checklist items AND articulates at least one specific skill they learned in the unit.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student could not select a piece: help them narrow down by looking for the piece with the most revision marks (showing the most investment) or the piece on their favorite topic
- If the final copy still has many errors: treat it as a teaching moment, not a failure. Choose 2-3 of the most important errors and fix them together. Perfection is not the goal; growth is.
- If the student is reluctant to share aloud: allow them to read just the first paragraph, or read it to just the facilitator rather than a larger audience. Some students need time to build confidence as public speakers.
- If the student cannot articulate growth: prompt with specifics: "Remember when dialogue punctuation was really tricky? How do you feel about it now?" or "Look at your first opinion piece and compare it to your last one. What is different?"

### If Mastery Is Met
Celebrate! The student has completed Unit 3. Next steps:
- Administer the Unit Assessment in a separate session
- Move to Unit 4 (Grammar & Vocabulary) after the assessment
- Encourage continued daily writing practice between units

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate enthusiasm and want to extend the celebration:**
- **Author Reading Series:** The student publishes and shares 2-3 pieces instead of just one, giving a mini "author reading" to the audience
- **Writing Portfolio Cover:** Create a cover page for the writing folder with the student's name, a self-portrait, a list of pieces inside, and a favorite quote about writing
- **Letter to a Future Student:** Write a letter to the next student who will study this unit, sharing tips, favorite lessons, and encouragement
- **Writing Goals:** Write 3 specific goals for future writing (e.g., "I want to learn to write longer narratives with more characters," "I want to get faster at revision," "I want to learn more about research with more sources")

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson is designed for 1-2 sessions. Session one: selection, final revision/editing, and beginning the final copy. Session two: completing the final copy, illustration, sharing, and reflection. If the student can complete everything in one session, combine them.

**The celebration matters.** This is not just a fun add-on — it is a pedagogically important moment. Sharing writing with an audience gives the student a real PURPOSE for their work. When students know someone will read or hear their writing, they care more about quality. The celebration also builds writing identity: the student thinks of themselves as a WRITER, not just a student doing a writing assignment.

**Make the audience real.** Even if the only audience is you (the facilitator), make it feel special. Put on a "formal" voice when introducing the author. Clap after they finish. Ask a genuine question about their writing. If you can involve a second person — a parent, sibling, grandparent, neighbor, or friend (even via video call) — the student's sense of accomplishment multiplies.

**Illustrations are not just decoration.** For students who think visually, the illustration may be the part of publishing they enjoy most. Encourage detailed illustrations that truly relate to the content. An illustration of a labeled diagram for an informative piece reinforces the informative writing skill. An illustration of a key scene in a narrative reinforces story comprehension.

**The reflection is formative assessment.** The student's responses to the reflection questions tell you a great deal about their self-awareness as learners. A student who can articulate what they learned and what they want to improve on is demonstrating metacognition — a powerful predictor of future academic success. Listen carefully and record their responses.

**Typing option.** If a computer is available and the student can type, allowing them to type their final copy is a valuable skill. However, do not require typing if it will slow the student down significantly or create frustration. Handwriting is perfectly appropriate at this age. The focus is on the WRITING, not the medium.

**Connect to the assessment.** After the celebration, let the student know that the Unit Assessment will happen in a separate session. Frame it positively: "The assessment is your chance to show everything you have learned. You have been practicing all of these skills for weeks — you are ready."

**Growth mindset final note:** Say to the student: "You started this unit knowing how to write simple opinions, informative pieces, and narratives. Now you can organize opinions with evidence, write informative text with facts and definitions, create narratives with dialogue and sequence, and even do research. That is an incredible amount of growth. You should be really proud of yourself."

## Answer Key

**This lesson does not have a traditional answer key.** Assessment is based on the published piece checklist and the quality of the student's reflection.

**Evaluation criteria for the published piece:**

| Element | Mastery | Approaching | Developing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Selection and reasoning | Student chose a piece and clearly explained why | Student chose a piece but could not explain why | Student could not choose without significant help |
| Revision evidence | Evidence of meaningful revision (adding, deleting, or rearranging) | Some revision attempted but minimal changes | No revision beyond Lesson 9 |
| Editing accuracy | Most errors in capitalization, punctuation, and spelling are corrected | Some errors corrected but several remain | Many errors remain uncorrected |
| Final copy presentation | Neat, legible, with title, author name, and illustration | Legible but missing title, name, or illustration | Difficult to read or missing multiple elements |
| Sharing | Read aloud with reasonable fluency and expression | Read aloud but with difficulty or reluctance | Could not or would not share aloud |
| Reflection | Articulated specific skills learned and areas for growth | General statements about the unit without specifics | Could not articulate any learning or growth |
