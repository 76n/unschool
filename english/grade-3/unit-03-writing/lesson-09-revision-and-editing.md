# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 9

## Topic
Revision and Editing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Use a self-editing checklist to review their own writing for capitalization, punctuation, spelling, paragraph structure, topic sentences, supporting details, and dialogue punctuation
- Apply three revision strategies: adding, deleting, and rearranging
- Give and receive peer feedback using a structured feedback process (facilitator acts as "peer")

## Prerequisites
- At least two completed writing pieces from this unit (opinion, informative, narrative, or research)
- Understanding of dialogue punctuation (Lesson 6)
- Understanding of paragraph structure with topic sentences and supporting details (Lessons 1-4)
- Basic spelling strategies (Grade 2 skills)

## Materials Needed
- The student's writing folder with drafts from Lessons 1-8
- Lined paper and pencil
- Colored pencils or pens (for marking revisions — use a color different from the original writing)
- Self-editing checklist (template provided in this lesson — make a copy the student can keep)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling
- Eraser (or the student can cross out neatly and write above)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the difference between revision and editing, and address the common resistance to going back and changing writing.

**Activity: The Author's Secret**

Say to the student: "I want to tell you a secret about professional authors — the people who write the books you love to read. Their first drafts are NOT perfect. Not even close. Every book you have ever read has been revised and edited many, many times before it was published."

Ask: "What do you think 'revision' means?" (Accept any answer.)

Say: "Revision means 're-seeing' your writing — looking at it with fresh eyes and making it BETTER. It is not about fixing mistakes — that is editing. Revision is about making your writing clearer, more interesting, and more powerful."

Write:

| | What It Means | What You Do |
|---|---|---|
| **Revision** | Making the writing BETTER | Add details, delete unnecessary parts, rearrange sections, strengthen word choices |
| **Editing** | Making the writing CORRECT | Fix spelling, capitalization, punctuation, grammar |

Say: "Today we are going to do BOTH. First, we will learn to revise — to make our writing stronger. Then, we will learn to edit — to catch and fix errors. Both are essential skills that every good writer uses."

**Important framing note for the facilitator:** Say something like: "Going back to change your writing is not a punishment. It does not mean your writing was bad. It means you are doing what REAL writers do. Even the best writers in the world revise and edit. This is a sign that you are becoming a serious writer."

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Revision:** The process of improving writing by changing the content — adding, deleting, rearranging, or strengthening ideas
- **Editing:** The process of correcting errors in writing — fixing spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and grammar
- **Adding:** Putting in new words, sentences, or details that make the writing clearer or more interesting
- **Deleting:** Removing words, sentences, or sections that are unnecessary, repetitive, or off-topic
- **Rearranging:** Moving words, sentences, or sections to a different place so the writing flows better
- **Peer feedback:** When another person reads your writing and tells you what is strong and what could be improved
- **Self-editing checklist:** A list of things to check in your own writing before you call it "done"

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Teach the Three Revision Strategies**

Say: "There are three main things you can do when you revise."

**Strategy 1: ADDING**

Say: "Adding means putting in something new that makes your writing better."

Write a "before" sentence:
> "The dog ran."

Say: "This is true, but it does not paint a picture. Let me ADD details."

Write the "after" version:
> "The golden retriever bolted across the muddy yard, ears flapping and tongue hanging out."

Say: "I added: what kind of dog (golden retriever), where it ran (across the muddy yard), and what it looked like (ears flapping, tongue hanging out). The reader can now PICTURE this."

**What to add:**
- Sensory details (Lesson 5)
- Evidence for reasons (Lesson 2)
- Definitions for unfamiliar words (Lesson 4)
- Dialogue (Lesson 6)
- Temporal words (Lesson 7)

**Strategy 2: DELETING**

Say: "Deleting means taking out something that does not belong or is repeated."

Write a "before" paragraph:
> "I think pizza is the best food. Pizza is really good. I love pizza so much. My favorite pizza has pepperoni. Pizza is made with dough, sauce, and cheese. I really think pizza is great."

Say: "Some of these sentences say the same thing: 'pizza is the best,' 'pizza is really good,' 'I love pizza,' 'pizza is great.' That is repetitive. Also, 'pizza is made with dough, sauce, and cheese' is an informative fact that does not belong in an opinion piece. Let me delete the unnecessary parts."

Write the "after" version:
> "I think pizza is the best food. My favorite pizza has pepperoni, warm melty cheese, and tangy tomato sauce. It is the perfect meal for any occasion."

Say: "Shorter, stronger, no repetition. Deleting is not about making your writing shorter for the sake of being short — it is about cutting the parts that do not add value."

**Strategy 3: REARRANGING**

Say: "Rearranging means moving things to a place where they make more sense."

Write a "before" version:
> "Maya really wanted a puppy. She promised to walk it every day. Maya's birthday was next week. She had been asking for a puppy for two years."

Say: "The ideas are in a strange order. The birthday fact is stuck between her wanting a puppy and how long she has been asking. Let me rearrange."

Write the "after" version:
> "Maya had been asking for a puppy for two years. She really wanted one and promised to walk it every day. Her birthday was next week, and she crossed her fingers that this would finally be the year."

Say: "Same information, but now it flows logically: background (been asking for two years) → current desire (really wants one) → upcoming event (birthday). Rearranging helps your writing flow."

**Step 2: Introduce the Self-Editing Checklist**

Say: "After you revise your writing to make it BETTER, you edit it to make it CORRECT. Here is a checklist you will use every time you edit your writing from now on."

Write the checklist (have the student copy it onto a card they can keep):

**Grade 3 Self-Editing Checklist**

*Capitalization:*
- [ ] Every sentence begins with a capital letter
- [ ] Proper nouns (names, places) are capitalized
- [ ] The word "I" is always capitalized
- [ ] The first word inside quotation marks is capitalized

*Punctuation:*
- [ ] Every sentence ends with a period, question mark, or exclamation mark
- [ ] Commas are used in dialogue before or after the tag
- [ ] Quotation marks go around the EXACT words characters say
- [ ] Question marks and exclamation marks go INSIDE quotation marks

*Spelling:*
- [ ] I have checked words I am unsure about (used a dictionary or word wall)
- [ ] Common words are spelled correctly (because, they, said, where, their/there/they're)

*Paragraph Structure:*
- [ ] Each paragraph has a clear topic sentence
- [ ] Supporting details relate to the topic sentence
- [ ] Each paragraph has a concluding or transition sentence
- [ ] New speaker in dialogue starts a new paragraph

*Content:*
- [ ] My writing makes sense from beginning to end
- [ ] I have not repeated the same idea unnecessarily
- [ ] I used specific details, not vague language
- [ ] My ending wraps up the writing (not just "The End")

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Modeling Revision and Editing on a Sample Piece**

Say: "Watch me revise and edit a piece of writing. I am going to use the three revision strategies AND the self-editing checklist."

Write this draft on the board or paper:

> i think sumer is the best season. First its hot outside. Also you can go swimming. Finally you dont have to go to school. Summer is great. the End.

Say: "Let me go through this step by step."

**Revision first (making it BETTER):**

Say: "Let me check for adding. 'First, it is hot outside' — that is a reason, but it has no evidence. Let me ADD evidence." Revise to: **"First, the warm weather means you can play outside all day long — riding bikes, running through sprinklers, and catching fireflies at night."**

Say: "'Also, you can go swimming' — thin. Let me add." Revise to: **"Also, summer is the perfect time for swimming. For example, my family goes to the lake every weekend, and there is nothing better than jumping off the dock into the cool water."**

Say: "'Finally, you don't have to go to school' — this needs development." Revise to: **"Finally, summer break gives you time to do things you love, like reading for fun, visiting grandparents, and sleeping in on rainy mornings."**

Say: "'Summer is great. The End' — weak conclusion. Let me strengthen it." Revise to: **"For all these reasons, summer will always be my favorite time of year. From the long sunny days to the cool summer nights, every part of summer feels like an adventure."**

**Now editing (making it CORRECT):**

Say: "Now let me use the self-editing checklist."
- Capitalization: "i" should be "I." "sumer" aside from spelling, is fine. "the End" — "The" needs a capital. Actually, I revised away "The End."
- Punctuation: "its" needs an apostrophe for "it's" (it is). "dont" needs an apostrophe for "don't."
- Spelling: "sumer" should be "summer."

Write the final version:

> I think summer is the best season. First, the warm weather means you can play outside all day long — riding bikes, running through sprinklers, and catching fireflies at night. Also, summer is the perfect time for swimming. For example, my family goes to the lake every weekend, and there is nothing better than jumping off the dock into the cool water. Finally, summer break gives you time to do things you love, like reading for fun, visiting grandparents, and sleeping in on rainy mornings. For all these reasons, summer will always be my favorite time of year. From the long sunny days to the cool summer nights, every part of summer feels like an adventure.

Say: "Look at the difference! The revised and edited version is specific, organized, interesting, and correct."

**Worked Example 2: Modeling Peer Feedback**

Say: "After you revise and edit your own writing, it helps to have someone ELSE read it. Fresh eyes catch things you might miss. I am going to show you how to give helpful feedback."

Write this rule:

**Feedback Sandwich:**
1. **Top bread (positive):** "Something I really liked about your writing is ___."
2. **Filling (suggestion):** "One thing that could make it even better is ___."
3. **Bottom bread (positive):** "Overall, I think your writing is ___ because ___."

Say: "The feedback sandwich starts and ends with something positive. The suggestion goes in the middle. This way, the writer feels encouraged AND knows how to improve."

**Demonstrate** by reading a sample piece aloud and modeling the feedback sandwich:

Sample: "I like dogs. Dogs are fun. They play with you. Dogs are the best."

Feedback: "Something I really liked is that your opinion is clear — I know you like dogs. One thing that could make it even better is adding specific reasons with evidence, like WHAT games you play with dogs or WHY they are fun. Overall, I think your writing has a great start because your opinion is strong."

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice revision and editing together on the student's own writing.

**Activity 1: Revise Together (6 minutes)**

Say: "Take out your writing folder and choose one piece you have written during this unit — opinion, informative, narrative, or research. We are going to revise it together."

Have the student choose a piece. Read it aloud together. Then:

1. **Check for ADDING:** Ask: "Is there a place where you could add a sensory detail? More evidence? A definition? Dialogue?" Help the student add at least one thing.

2. **Check for DELETING:** Ask: "Is anything repeated? Is there a sentence that does not really add to your writing?" Help the student delete at least one thing (or acknowledge that nothing needs deleting).

3. **Check for REARRANGING:** Ask: "Does everything flow in a logical order? Is there a sentence that would make more sense in a different place?" Help the student rearrange if needed.

*Facilitator prompt: Use a different colored pencil for revisions so the student can see the changes clearly. Arrows can show rearrangements. Carets (^) can show insertions. Strikethrough can show deletions.*

**Activity 2: Edit Together Using the Checklist (6 minutes)**

Using the same piece (now revised), go through the self-editing checklist together:

- Read through once for capitalization. Fix any errors.
- Read through once for punctuation (especially dialogue punctuation). Fix any errors.
- Read through once for spelling. Circle any words that look wrong. Check with a dictionary or have the student try the word again.
- Read through once for paragraph structure. Does each paragraph have a topic sentence? Are details relevant?

*Facilitator prompt: Do NOT find and fix errors for the student. Point to the line and say: "I see something on this line that needs fixing. Can you find it?" Give the student time to find and correct the error themselves.*

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student revises and edits a second piece of writing independently, then gives/receives peer feedback.

**Directions:** "Choose a DIFFERENT piece from your writing folder (not the one we just worked on). Revise it using the three strategies (add, delete, rearrange), then edit it using the self-editing checklist. Mark all your changes in a different color so we can see what you revised."

After the student finishes:

**Peer Feedback Activity:** The facilitator acts as the "peer." Read the student's revised piece and give feedback using the feedback sandwich:
1. One specific thing that is strong
2. One specific suggestion for improvement
3. An overall positive comment

Then ask the student to give YOU feedback on a short piece. Write a quick 4-5 sentence paragraph with 2-3 deliberate errors. Have the student use the feedback sandwich to tell you what is strong, what could improve, and give an overall comment. Then have them edit your piece.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Revise and edit one piece from the writing folder. Make at least two revisions (adding, deleting, or rearranging) and fix all errors found on the self-editing checklist. Participate in the peer feedback activity.

**Stretch:** Revise and edit one piece, making at least four revisions. For each revision, write a brief note in the margin explaining WHY you made the change (e.g., "Added a sensory detail to help the reader picture the scene" or "Deleted this sentence because it was repetitive"). Participate in peer feedback.

**Challenge:** Revise and edit one piece, then compare the BEFORE and AFTER versions. Write a short reflection (3-4 sentences): "What changes did I make? Why? How did those changes improve my writing?" This practices metacognition — thinking about your own thinking.

### Interleaved Review

After completing the revision and editing, answer these review questions:

5. **Lesson 8 Review:** What does "paraphrase" mean? (To write information in your own words instead of copying the exact words from a source.)

6. **Lesson 5 Review:** What are sensory details? Name the five senses you can use. (Sensory details are descriptions that appeal to the senses. The five senses are sight, hearing, touch, smell, and taste.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. Name the three revision strategies. (Adding, deleting, and rearranging.)
2. What is the difference between revision and editing? (Revision makes writing BETTER by improving content — adding details, removing repetition, reordering. Editing makes writing CORRECT by fixing spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and grammar.)
3. Edit this sentence — find and fix all errors: "me and my frend whent to the park becuz it was sunny said emma" (Corrected: "My friend and I went to the park because it was sunny," said Emma. OR Emma said, "My friend and I went to the park because it was sunny." — Fixes: "Me and my frend" → "My friend and I," "whent" → "went," "becuz" → "because," missing quotation marks and punctuation for dialogue, "emma" → "Emma.")

**Mastery = Student names all three revision strategies, explains the revision/editing difference, AND corrects at least 4 of 5 errors in item 3.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice the three revision strategies one at a time with short pieces — one day focus on adding, the next on deleting, the next on rearranging
- Use the self-editing checklist on very short pieces (2-3 sentences) before applying it to longer writing
- Do daily "error hunt" practice: write a sentence with one error and have the student find and fix it
- Pair reading (read aloud) is the most effective editing strategy — errors that are invisible on the page become audible when read aloud

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 10 (Writing Celebration and Publishing) next session. Preview: "Tomorrow is the big day! You are going to choose your BEST piece of writing from this entire unit, give it one final revision and polish, and PUBLISH it — that means making a beautiful final copy that you can share with others."

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Before and After Gallery:** The student selects their most-revised piece and creates a side-by-side display: the original draft on the left and the revised/edited version on the right. Use arrows and labels to show what changed and why.
- **Edit the Facilitator's Writing:** The facilitator writes a full paragraph with 5-6 deliberate errors (spelling, punctuation, capitalization, missing details, weak word choices). The student revises AND edits it, then explains each change.
- **Revision Letter:** The student writes a short letter to a future student explaining the three revision strategies and why revision matters. "Dear Future Writer, here is what I learned about making writing better..."

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson covers both revision and editing, which are two distinct skills. If time is limited, focus on revision in one session and editing in a separate session. The peer feedback activity can be done in either session.

**Revision before editing — always.** There is no point in perfecting the spelling in a sentence you are going to delete. Teach the student to revise FIRST (big changes to content and organization), then edit (fix surface errors). This order saves time and prevents frustration.

**Resistance to revision is normal.** Many students — especially those who find writing effortful — resist going back to change their work. They feel "done" and do not want to revisit something that took effort. Strategies to reduce resistance:
- Keep revision sessions SHORT (10-15 minutes)
- Start with positive feedback: "This part is really strong. Now let's make this other part match."
- Let the student CHOOSE what to revise: "Which piece do you want to work on?"
- Frame revision as power: "Revision is YOUR chance to make your writing say exactly what you want it to say."

**Read aloud is the best editing tool.** When students read their writing silently, their brain auto-corrects errors. When they read ALOUD, they hear the mistakes. Make "read it aloud" the first step of every editing session. Some students benefit from pointing to each word as they read, which slows them down enough to catch errors.

**The facilitator as "peer."** In a home learning setting, the facilitator is the only available "peer" for feedback. This is fine. Model respectful, specific feedback. Avoid vague comments like "Good job" or "This needs work." Instead: "I love how you used the word 'gleaming' to describe the ice — it helped me picture it. One suggestion: your second reason could use more evidence. Can you add a specific example?"

**Common errors at this age:**
- Sentence fragments ("Because it was cold.")
- Run-on sentences ("We went to the park and we played and then we went home and ate dinner.")
- Homophones (their/there/they're, to/too/two, your/you're)
- Missing apostrophes in contractions (dont, cant, wont)
- Forgetting to capitalize "I"
- Dialogue punctuation errors (see Lesson 6 reference card)

**Growth mindset note:** Revision is one of the best places to model a growth mindset. Say: "Your first draft is like a rough sketch. Revision is when you turn it into the real painting. Professional authors revise their books 5, 10, even 20 times. The more you revise, the better your writing becomes."
