# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 3, Lesson 7

## Topic
Narrative Writing: Sequence and Closure

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Use temporal words and phrases (first, next, then, meanwhile, later, after that, finally) to sequence events logically
- Write a satisfying ending/closure that resolves the story and shows how the character feels or what they learned
- Write a complete narrative with a beginning, middle, and end that includes characters, setting, dialogue, sequence, and closure

## Prerequisites
- Ability to write a narrative opening with characters and setting (Lesson 5)
- Ability to write dialogue with correct punctuation (Lesson 6)
- Familiarity with temporal words from Grade 2 (first, next, then, last)

## Materials Needed
- Lined paper and pencil
- Temporal words reference list (created during the lesson)
- Story mountain graphic organizer (template provided below)
- The student's narrative opening from Lesson 5 and/or scene from Lesson 6 (if available)
- Chart paper or whiteboard for modeling

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review temporal words from Grade 2 and introduce the concept of sequence in storytelling.

**Activity: Put It in Order**

Say to the student: "In Grade 2, you learned to use words like first, next, then, and last to tell events in order. Today we are going to expand that list and learn how to use sequence words to keep our stories organized. But first, let's warm up."

Read these story events out of order. Ask the student to put them in the correct sequence:

**Story events (mixed up):**
- Finally, Jake ate the best pancakes he had ever made.
- First, Jake got out a mixing bowl and all the ingredients.
- Then, he poured the batter onto the hot pan and waited for bubbles to form.
- Next, he mixed the flour, eggs, milk, and sugar together until the batter was smooth.
- After that, he carefully flipped each pancake with a spatula.

Ask: "What is the correct order?" (First → Next → Then → After that → Finally)

Say: "The temporal words — first, next, then, after that, finally — are like signposts on a road. They tell the reader, 'Here is what happened, and here is what happened NEXT.' Without them, the reader gets lost."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Sequence:** The order in which events happen — first, second, third, and so on
- **Temporal words:** Words and phrases that show the order of events or the passing of time
- **Meanwhile:** A word that shows two things are happening at the SAME time ("Meanwhile, back at the house...")
- **Closure:** The ending of a narrative — it wraps up the story and gives the reader a sense of completeness
- **Resolution:** How the problem or situation in the story is solved or settled
- **Story mountain:** A graphic organizer shaped like a mountain that shows how a story builds to a high point and then comes back down to the ending

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Expand the Temporal Words List**

Say: "In Grade 2, you used first, next, then, and last. In Grade 3, we are going to add more temporal words and phrases so your writing sounds more mature and interesting."

Write the expanded list where the student can see it (they will keep this as a reference):

**Grade 3 Temporal Words and Phrases:**

| To show sequence | To show time passing | To show things happening at the same time |
|---|---|---|
| First | Later that day | Meanwhile |
| Next | The next morning | At the same time |
| Then | After a while | While [this was happening] |
| After that | Soon | During |
| Finally | Eventually | |
| Before long | Suddenly | |
| At last | By the time | |

Say: "Using a variety of these words makes your writing sound polished. If you write 'then... then... then... then...' your reader's brain gets tired. But if you write 'first... next... after a while... meanwhile... finally...' the reader stays engaged and can follow the sequence."

**Step 2: Introduce the Story Mountain**

Say: "Every good story follows a shape — like a mountain. It starts at the bottom, builds up to the most exciting part, and then comes back down to the ending."

Draw a story mountain:

```
                    /\
                   /  \  <-- CLIMAX (most exciting part)
                  /    \
                 /      \
   RISING      /        \     FALLING
   ACTION     /          \    ACTION
             /            \
            /              \
BEGINNING  /                \  ENDING
(characters,                (resolution,
 setting,                    closure,
 situation)                  feelings)
```

Label each part:
1. **Beginning** — Introduce characters, setting, and the situation
2. **Rising Action** — Events build; the problem or adventure gets more intense
3. **Climax** — The most exciting, important, or turning-point moment
4. **Falling Action** — Events after the climax; things start to settle
5. **Ending/Closure** — The problem is resolved; the character's feelings are shown; the reader feels the story is complete

Say: "You already learned how to write the beginning in Lesson 5. Today we are going to focus on the MIDDLE (how to sequence events) and the ENDING (how to write closure)."

**Step 3: Teach Three Strategies for Satisfying Endings**

Say: "The ending is the last thing the reader reads. If it is weak, the whole story feels unfinished. Here are three strategies for writing a great ending."

Write:

**Strategy 1: Resolve the problem.**
"The problem or challenge in the story gets solved." Example: "At last, Maya found her lost dog hiding under the porch. She scooped him up and held him tight."

**Strategy 2: Show the character's feelings.**
"The reader learns how the character feels at the end." Example: "Marcus sat on the back steps, smiling. He had never felt so proud of himself."

**Strategy 3: Share what the character learned.**
"The character learns a lesson or realizes something new." Example: "Rosa understood now that being brave did not mean not being scared — it meant doing the hard thing even when you were scared."

Say: "The BEST endings use more than one strategy. You can resolve the problem AND show feelings AND share what the character learned — all in your final paragraph."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Planning and Writing a Complete Narrative**

Say: "Watch me plan and write a complete short narrative using a story mountain and temporal words."

**Step 1: Plan with a story mountain.**

Draw and fill in:

```
BEGINNING: Mia and her dad go hiking on a new trail.
           The trail goes through a forest.

RISING ACTION: They hear a strange sound.
               They follow the sound deeper into the woods.
               They find a baby bird that has fallen from its nest.

CLIMAX: The nest is too high to reach.
        Mia has an idea — she climbs on her dad's shoulders.

FALLING ACTION: Mia carefully places the bird back in the nest.
                The mama bird returns.

ENDING: Mia and her dad hike home, feeling happy.
        Mia learns that helping others — even small creatures — matters.
```

**Step 2: Write the narrative, using temporal words.**

Write in front of the student:

> Mia and her dad set off on the Pine Creek Trail early Saturday morning. The forest was cool and quiet, and sunlight filtered through the tall trees like golden ribbons. Birds sang from every branch.
>
> **After a while**, they heard a tiny, high-pitched chirping sound coming from somewhere off the trail.
>
> "Do you hear that?" Mia asked, stopping in her tracks.
>
> **"Let's go find out what it is,"** her dad said.
>
> They followed the sound **until** they found a baby bird sitting in a pile of leaves at the base of an old oak tree. Its feathers were fluffy and gray, and it opened its beak and chirped again.
>
> "It must have fallen from its nest," Mia whispered. She looked up. **High above**, a nest sat in the crook of a thick branch — too high for Mia to reach, even on her tiptoes.
>
> **Then** Mia had an idea. "Dad, what if I climb on your shoulders?"
>
> Her dad knelt down, and Mia carefully climbed onto his shoulders. He stood up slowly. **Now** she could just reach the nest. She cupped the baby bird gently in her hands and placed it back in the soft nest.
>
> **A moment later**, a larger bird swooped down and landed on the branch. It was the mama bird. She looked at her baby, ruffled her feathers, and began to sing.
>
> Mia grinned so wide her cheeks hurt. **On the walk home**, she held her dad's hand and looked up at the canopy of trees. The forest felt different now — like it was full of small, important lives, each one worth protecting.
>
> "Dad," she said quietly, "I am really glad we went hiking today."
>
> "Me too, Mia," he replied. "Me too."

Say: "Let's find the temporal words." Circle or underline each one: After a while, until, High above, Then, Now, A moment later, On the walk home.

Say: "Let's check the ending. Does it resolve the problem? (Yes — the bird is back in the nest.) Does it show feelings? (Yes — Mia grins, feels glad.) Does it share what the character learned? (Yes — the forest feels full of small, important lives worth protecting.) This ending uses ALL THREE strategies."

**Worked Example 2: Weak Ending vs. Strong Ending**

Say: "Let me show you a weak ending and a strong ending for the same story."

**Weak ending:**
> And then they went home. The End.

**Strong ending:**
> On the walk home, Mia held her dad's hand and looked up at the canopy of trees. The forest felt different now — like it was full of small, important lives, each one worth protecting. "Dad," she said quietly, "I am really glad we went hiking today."

Ask: "What is wrong with the weak ending?" (It does not resolve anything, it does not show feelings, and "The End" is not a real closure.)
Ask: "What makes the strong ending satisfying?" (It shows Mia's feelings, it shares what she learned, and the dialogue adds a personal, warm touch.)

## Guided Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice planning sequence and writing closure together.

**Activity 1: Plan a Story with the Story Mountain (6 minutes)**

Say: "Let's plan a story together using the story mountain."

Give the student a story starter: "A kid enters a cooking contest at the community center." (Or let the student suggest a scenario.)

Together, fill in the story mountain:
- **Beginning:** Who is the character? Where is the contest? What does the setting look like?
- **Rising Action:** What events happen? (Getting ingredients, starting to cook, something goes wrong)
- **Climax:** What is the most exciting or tense moment? (The dish burns? They run out of time? They try a creative fix?)
- **Falling Action:** What happens after the climax? (The judges taste the food.)
- **Ending:** How does it resolve? How does the character feel? What did they learn?

*Facilitator prompt: If the student gets stuck, offer choices: "Does the character's dish turn out great, or does something go wrong? If something goes wrong, how do they solve it?"*

**Activity 2: Write the Ending Together (6 minutes)**

Using the story mountain from Activity 1, skip ahead and write JUST the ending together (4-6 sentences).

Guide the student to:
1. Resolve the situation (what happens with the cooking contest)
2. Show the character's feelings (happy? proud? surprised? relieved?)
3. Share what the character learned (trying your best matters; cooking is about creativity; it is okay to make mistakes)
4. Use at least one temporal word (finally, at last, after everything)

Read the ending aloud. Ask: "Does this feel like a satisfying ending? Does the reader feel like the story is complete?"

*Facilitator prompt: If the ending feels flat, ask: "How does the character FEEL right now? What would they be thinking? Can you add one more sentence that shows their feeling or what they learned?"*

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student writes a complete narrative with sequence and closure independently.

**Directions:** "Write a complete short narrative (12-18 sentences) that includes ALL of these elements: characters introduced with detail, a setting described with sensory details, at least TWO lines of dialogue with correct punctuation, events in sequence using at least FOUR temporal words, and a satisfying ending with closure. Plan with a story mountain first."

**Story Choices:**
- A child and their friend have an adventure in a place they have never been before
- A child has to overcome a fear (the dark, speaking in front of people, trying something new)
- A child's pet gets loose and they have to find it
- A story of your own choice (approved by facilitator)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Write a complete narrative with all required elements. Use at least four temporal words from the reference list. The ending should use at least one closure strategy (resolve the problem, show feelings, OR share a lesson).

**Stretch:** Write a complete narrative with all required elements. Use at least six temporal words, including at least one "meanwhile" or "at the same time" to show simultaneous events. The ending should use at least TWO closure strategies. Include at least four lines of dialogue.

**Challenge:** Write a complete narrative, then go back and write an ALTERNATIVE ending — a different way the story could have ended. Compare: which ending is more satisfying? Write 2-3 sentences explaining why you think so. (This practices critical thinking about narrative craft.)

### Interleaved Review

After completing the narrative, answer these review questions:

5. **Lesson 5 Review:** Name two ways to describe a setting using sensory details. (Use details that appeal to sight, sound, touch, smell, or taste — accept any two specific senses.)

6. **Lesson 6 Review:** Where do quotation marks go in a line of dialogue? (Around ONLY the exact words the character says.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Ask the student to answer orally or in writing:

1. Name four temporal words or phrases you can use to sequence events in a narrative. (Accept any four from the reference list: first, next, then, after that, meanwhile, later, finally, eventually, before long, at last, etc.)
2. Name two strategies for writing a satisfying ending. (Any two of: resolve the problem, show the character's feelings, share what the character learned.)
3. Why should you NOT just write "The End" as your ending? (Because "The End" does not resolve anything, show feelings, or give the reader a sense of completeness. A real ending wraps up the story.)

**Mastery = Student names at least four temporal words AND two ending strategies.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Post the temporal words reference list where the student can see it during all writing sessions
- Practice sequencing with everyday activities: "Tell me how to make a sandwich. Use temporal words." / "Tell me about your morning routine using at least five temporal words."
- Practice writing endings: give the student story summaries (beginning and middle only) and have them write just the ending using the three strategies
- Read endings of familiar books together and identify which strategies the author used

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 8 (Short Research Projects) next session. Preview: "You have now learned all three types of writing: opinion, informative, and narrative. Next, we are going to combine your informative writing skills with something new: RESEARCH. You are going to choose a topic, find information from real sources, take notes, and write a short research report."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**For students who demonstrate quick mastery:**
- **Dual Narrator:** Write the same scene from two different characters' points of view. Each version should have the same events in the same sequence, but the narrator notices different things and feels differently. (This practices perspective-taking.)
- **Expand the Story:** Take the complete narrative from Independent Practice and expand the MIDDLE. Add two more events with temporal words. How does adding more events change the pace and feel of the story?
- **Ending Analysis:** Choose two favorite books. Compare their endings. Which closure strategies did each author use? Which ending do you like better, and why?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Pacing:** This lesson covers both sequencing and closure, which are two substantial skills. If the student needs more time, split into two sessions: one for temporal words and sequencing (Direct Instruction through Activity 1 of Guided Practice), and one for closure strategies and writing the complete narrative (Activity 2 of Guided Practice through Independent Practice).

**Connection to Grade 2:** In Grade 2, students used first, next, then, and last. The Grade 3 additions are: (1) a much larger set of temporal words, (2) the concept of "meanwhile" for simultaneous events, (3) more sophisticated closure strategies beyond just "the end," and (4) the ability to write a longer, more complex narrative with multiple events. Acknowledge growth: "You already know first, next, then, and last. Now you have a whole toolkit of sequence words."

**"The End" is not an ending.** This is the most important concept in the closure portion of this lesson. Many students have been trained to write "The End" since kindergarten. Gently break this habit. Say: "When you see 'The End' in a published book, it is just a design choice — the author already wrote a real ending. YOUR real ending needs to resolve the story and show feelings."

**The story mountain is a powerful planning tool.** If the student resists planning ("I just want to start writing!"), explain: "Even professional authors plan. The story mountain takes two minutes and saves you from getting stuck in the middle or writing an ending that does not make sense. Try it this one time, and see if it helps."

**Temporal word overuse.** Some students go overboard: "First I woke up. Next I ate breakfast. Then I brushed my teeth. After that I got dressed. Finally I went to school." Every sentence starting with a temporal word sounds robotic. Model variety: some sentences use temporal words, others just flow naturally. "I woke up to the smell of bacon. After breakfast, I brushed my teeth and grabbed my backpack. Mom was already waiting in the car."

**Writing quality levels for this lesson:**

*Developing:* Story has events but no temporal words or clear sequence. Ending is "The End" or just stops abruptly. No dialogue. Characters and setting are thin.

*Approaching:* Story uses some temporal words (first, then, finally) and has a recognizable beginning, middle, and end. Ending resolves the situation but does not show feelings or a lesson. Dialogue present but may have punctuation errors.

*Mastery:* Story uses a variety of temporal words, events flow logically, dialogue is correctly punctuated and reveals character, setting uses sensory details, and the ending resolves the problem, shows the character's feelings, and/or shares a lesson learned. The reader feels the story is complete and satisfying.
