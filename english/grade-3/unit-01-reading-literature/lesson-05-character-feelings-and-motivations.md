# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 1, Lesson 5

## Topic
Character Feelings and Motivations

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify how a character feels at different points in a story and explain how those feelings change
- Explain WHY a character acts the way they do (character motivation)
- Connect character emotions to character actions and story events using the frame "The character felt ___ so [he/she] ___"

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-4 (text evidence, character traits, character actions and cause-effect)
- Character trait word bank from Lesson 3

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Reading passages provided in this lesson (print or display)
- Character trait word bank from Lesson 3
- Feelings word list (created during this lesson)
- Crayons or colored pencils (for feelings timeline activity)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review character actions and cause-effect from Lesson 4 and introduce the concept of feelings driving actions.

**Activity: Why Did They Do That?**

Say: "I am going to describe a character's action. You tell me WHY you think they did it — what were they FEELING that made them act that way?"

1. "A boy hid behind the couch when the doorbell rang." Why? (He was scared, shy, or nervous)
2. "A girl gave her last cookie to her little brother." Why? (She was kind, generous, or loved her brother)
3. "A boy stomped his foot and threw his pencil on the floor." Why? (He was frustrated, angry, or upset)
4. "A girl ran to the front door and opened it before anyone else could." Why? (She was excited or eager)

Say: "Notice what you just did — you figured out the character's FEELINGS by looking at their ACTIONS. And the feelings EXPLAIN the actions. Today we are going to explore this idea: feelings drive actions, and actions drive events. It all connects."

*Facilitator note: Accept all reasonable interpretations. There can be more than one reason someone acts a certain way. If the student gives an unexpected but logical answer, affirm it: "That is another possibility! In stories, the text usually gives us clues about which feeling is the right one."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to identify character feelings, track how they change, and connect feelings to motivations and actions.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Motivation:** The reason a character acts the way they do. The "why" behind their actions. Motivations are often driven by feelings.
- **Feelings (Emotions):** What a character feels inside — happy, sad, scared, excited, frustrated, proud, jealous, lonely, relieved, etc.
- **Feelings change:** Characters do not feel the same way through the whole story. Their feelings change as events happen. Tracking how feelings change helps us understand the story.
- **Inner thoughts:** What a character is thinking inside their mind. Authors sometimes tell us a character's thoughts directly.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Build a Feelings Word List (3 minutes)**

Say: "In Lesson 3, we built a word bank of character TRAITS — words that describe what a character is LIKE. Now let's build a list of FEELINGS — words that describe how a character feels in a moment. Traits are permanent; feelings change."

Create the list together:

| Positive Feelings | Negative Feelings | In-Between Feelings |
|------------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| happy | sad | nervous |
| excited | angry | confused |
| proud | scared | surprised |
| relieved | frustrated | embarrassed |
| grateful | jealous | hopeful |
| joyful | lonely | worried |
| confident | guilty | uncertain |
| amused | disappointed | curious |

Say: "Keep this list next to your character trait word bank. Traits tell us what a character is LIKE. Feelings tell us what a character FEELS in a particular moment. A character who is usually brave (trait) might feel scared (feeling) in one scene."

**Step 2: Feelings Drive Actions — The Motivation Chain (3 minutes)**

Write on paper:

**Feeling --> Motivation --> Action --> Event**

Say: "Here is how it works: A character FEELS something. That feeling gives them a MOTIVATION — a reason to act. The motivation leads to an ACTION. And the action causes an EVENT. Let me show you a quick example."

Write:
- **Feeling:** Sam feels worried about his dog being outside in the storm.
- **Motivation:** He wants to make sure his dog is safe.
- **Action:** Sam runs outside in the rain to bring the dog inside.
- **Event:** Sam gets soaking wet, but the dog is safe.

Say: "See the chain? Sam's WORRY led him to ACT, and that action caused the event. If Sam did not feel worried, he would not have run outside, and the event would not have happened."

### Worked Examples

**Step 3: Model with a Passage (5 minutes)**

Read this passage aloud:

> **The Solo**
>
> Zara loved to sing. She sang in the shower, she sang while walking to school, and she sang along with every song on the radio. Her music teacher, Ms. Delgado, said Zara had "a voice like sunshine."
>
> When Ms. Delgado announced that the school concert would include a solo, Zara's heart leaped. She wanted that solo more than anything. She practiced every day after school, singing the same song over and over until she knew every note by heart.
>
> On the day of auditions, Zara walked into the music room and saw twelve other students waiting to try out. Her stomach dropped. She had not expected so many kids.
>
> When her turn came, Zara stood in front of Ms. Delgado and opened her mouth — but nothing came out. Her throat felt tight. Her hands were shaking. The room was too quiet.
>
> "Take your time, Zara," said Ms. Delgado gently.
>
> Zara closed her eyes. She thought about singing in the shower, where nobody was listening. She imagined she was back in her bathroom, warm water running, singing just for herself.
>
> Slowly, her voice came. It was quiet at first, then stronger. By the second verse, she was singing with all her heart. When she finished, the room was silent for a moment, and then Ms. Delgado smiled. "Beautiful," she said.

**Model — Tracking Zara's Feelings:**

Draw a simple timeline on paper:

**Zara's Feelings Timeline:**

| Story Moment | Feeling | Evidence |
|-------------|---------|----------|
| Start — singing everywhere | Happy, confident | "She sang in the shower... and every song on the radio" |
| Solo announced | Excited, eager | "Her heart leaped. She wanted that solo more than anything." |
| Sees 12 other students | Nervous, anxious | "Her stomach dropped." |
| Tries to sing | Scared, frozen | "Nothing came out. Her throat felt tight. Her hands were shaking." |
| Closes her eyes | Calming down | "She imagined she was back in her bathroom" |
| Sings | Confident again, brave | "Her voice came... By the second verse, she was singing with all her heart." |

Say: "Look at how Zara's feelings CHANGED. She went from confident to excited to nervous to scared to calm to confident again. That is a feeling journey."

**Model — Connecting Feelings to Motivations and Actions:**

Say: "Now let me trace the motivation chain."

Write:
- **Feeling:** Zara felt SCARED when she could not sing. --> **Motivation:** She wanted to get past her fear. --> **Action:** She closed her eyes and imagined singing in the shower. --> **Event:** Her voice came back, and she sang beautifully.

Say: "Zara's fear was the problem, and her strategy for overcoming it (imagining the shower) was the solution. Her feelings drove her actions."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice with support — tracking character feelings and connecting them to motivations and actions.

**Activity 1: Feelings Timeline (6 minutes)**

Read this passage together:

> **The Last Piece**
>
> Eli had been working on a 500-piece puzzle for two weeks. It was a picture of a pirate ship on a stormy ocean, and it was the hardest puzzle he had ever tried. He loved the challenge. Every evening, he fit a few more pieces together, and the picture slowly took shape.
>
> On Friday evening, Eli counted the remaining pieces. There were only three left. He felt a rush of excitement. He was almost done!
>
> He fit the first piece in. Then the second. But when he reached for the third piece — the very last one, the piece that would complete the whole ship — it was not there. He checked the box. Empty. He looked under the table, behind the couch, and in the cushions. Nothing.
>
> Eli's excitement turned to frustration. Two weeks of work, and the puzzle would never be finished. He sat down on the floor and stared at the almost-complete picture with the one missing hole in the middle.
>
> His little sister, Ana, walked in. She was holding something behind her back.
>
> "I found this under my bed," she said quietly. She held out the missing puzzle piece.
>
> Eli stared at it. "Did you take it?" he asked.
>
> Ana nodded, her eyes on the floor. "I wanted to be the one to put in the last piece," she said. "I am sorry."
>
> Eli felt a flash of anger. But then he looked at Ana's face. She was really sorry. He took a deep breath.
>
> "Here," he said, handing the piece back to her. "You put it in."
>
> Ana's eyes went wide. She carefully pressed the piece into place. The pirate ship was complete.
>
> Eli smiled. Watching Ana's face light up was even better than finishing it himself.

Have the student fill in a Feelings Timeline:

| Story Moment | Eli's Feeling | Evidence from the Text |
|-------------|---------------|----------------------|
| Working on puzzle for two weeks | | |
| Counts — only 3 pieces left | | |
| Last piece is missing | | |
| Ana gives back the piece | | |
| Eli gives the piece to Ana | | |

*Facilitator prompt: Help the student find the exact text evidence for each feeling. For example, "excitement turned to frustration" is directly stated. Other feelings require inference: when Eli gives the piece to Ana, the text says he "smiled" and "Watching Ana's face light up was even better than finishing it himself" — this shows he felt happy and generous.*

**Activity 2: Motivation Questions (6 minutes)**

Using the same passage, discuss these questions together:

1. **Why did Ana take the puzzle piece?** (She wanted to be the one to put in the last piece — her motivation was wanting to be part of the accomplishment.)
2. **Why did Eli feel angry at first?** (He had worked on the puzzle for two weeks and was frustrated that Ana had taken the piece.)
3. **Why did Eli give the piece back to Ana instead of keeping it?** (He saw that she was really sorry. He chose to be kind rather than angry. His motivation changed from wanting to finish it himself to wanting to make Ana happy.)

Have the student write one "feeling to action" sentence:
"Eli felt ___ so he ___."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices independently — tracking feelings and connecting them to motivations and actions.

Have the student read the following passage independently, then complete the activities below.

> **The Tryouts**
>
> When the poster for basketball tryouts went up in the hallway, Layla stopped and stared at it. She had always wanted to play on a team, but she was the shortest kid in her grade. She was not sure she was good enough.
>
> That evening, Layla dribbled a basketball in her driveway while her older cousin, Marcus, watched. "You are fast," he said. "And you can dribble really well. You should try out."
>
> "What if they laugh at me?" Layla asked.
>
> Marcus thought about that. "They might," he said honestly. "But you will never know if you are good enough unless you try."
>
> Layla thought about it all night. She almost talked herself out of it three times. But the next morning, she put on her sneakers and walked to the gymnasium.
>
> At tryouts, the coach had everyone run drills. Layla was the fastest sprinter. She weaved through the cones without knocking a single one over. When the coach tossed her the ball, she dribbled the full length of the court and made a layup.
>
> "Nice work," the coach said, writing something on her clipboard.
>
> Layla did not make the starting lineup. But she made the team. When she saw her name on the roster, she pressed her hand against the paper and grinned.
>
> "I almost did not try out," she whispered to Marcus later.
>
> "But you did," he said. "And that is the whole point."

**Activity 1: Feelings Timeline**

Fill in the chart:

| Story Moment | Layla's Feeling | Evidence from the Text |
|-------------|----------------|----------------------|
| Sees the tryout poster | | |
| Marcus encourages her | | |
| That night / next morning | | |
| During tryouts | | |
| Sees her name on the roster | | |

**Activity 2: Motivation Chain**

Complete the chain:
- **Feeling:** Layla felt ___ about trying out.
- **Motivation:** She wanted ___ but was afraid of ___.
- **Action:** She ___.
- **Event:** ___.

**Activity 3: Evidence-Based Response**

Answer this question in 2-3 sentences using text evidence: Why did Layla almost NOT try out, and what motivated her to try out anyway?

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete the Feelings Timeline (Activity 1) and answer Activity 3 orally with facilitator support.

**Stretch:** Complete all three activities in writing. For Activity 3, include at least two pieces of text evidence.

**Challenge:** Complete all three activities. Then write a paragraph comparing Layla's feeling journey to Zara's (from the Direct Instruction passage). How are they similar? How are they different? Use text evidence from both stories.

### Interleaved Review

4. **Character traits (Lesson 3):** Choose two character traits from the word bank that describe Layla. Write a trait sentence for each: "Layla is [trait] because [evidence]."
5. **Cause and effect (Lesson 4):** Write one "Because..." sentence about this story: "Because Layla ___, ___."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short passage:

> Mika wanted to win the spelling bee more than anything. She studied for hours every night. On the day of the contest, she misspelled her word in the third round. She walked back to her seat with tears in her eyes. But when her best friend won the final round, Mika jumped up and cheered louder than anyone.

1. How did Mika feel when she misspelled her word? What is the evidence? (Sad/disappointed — "tears in her eyes")
2. How did Mika's feelings CHANGE at the end? (She went from sad to happy — she cheered for her friend)
3. Why did Mika cheer even though she lost? What motivated her? (She was happy for her best friend; her friendship mattered more than winning)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the feelings, the change, and provides a reasonable motivation with text evidence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice identifying feelings with very simple scenarios (one feeling at a time)
- Use the feelings word list to give the student language options
- Focus on "How does the character feel? How do you know?" before adding motivation
- Repeat this lesson with additional passages before moving to Lesson 6

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 6 (Words in Context: Context Clues) next session
- During everyday reading, ask: "How is the character feeling? What makes you think that?"

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Feelings Emoji Timeline:** The student draws a series of emoji faces (or simple expressions) along a timeline to show how a character's feelings changed. Under each face, they write the feeling word and the text evidence. This is a fun visual way to track emotional arcs.

2. **Character Interview:** The student pretends to interview a character from one of the stories. They write 3-4 questions about the character's feelings and motivations, then write the character's answers using text evidence. Example: "Zara, why did you close your eyes before singing?" "I was so nervous that my voice would not come out. I imagined I was singing in my shower at home because that is where I feel safe."

3. **Feelings Journal:** The student keeps a one-week "feelings journal" where they write about their own emotions each day. Then they compare their real feelings to how a character in a story felt. This builds empathy and emotional literacy.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Feelings vs. traits — an important distinction:** Students sometimes confuse feelings and traits. A trait is a consistent part of who a character is (brave, kind, curious). A feeling is a temporary emotional state (scared, happy, frustrated). A brave character can feel scared. A kind character can feel angry. Help students see that feelings come and go, while traits are more stable. Use the analogy: "The weather changes every day (feelings), but the climate stays mostly the same (traits)."

**Not all feelings are stated directly:** In some passages, the author directly states a character's feelings ("She felt scared"). In others, feelings are implied through actions, dialogue, or physical descriptions ("Her hands were shaking" implies fear, "She grinned" implies happiness). Teach students to look for both direct statements and indirect clues. The indirect clues connect back to the S.D.T.F. analysis from Lesson 3.

**Motivation is sophisticated for Grade 3:** Understanding why a character acts a certain way requires inferential thinking, which is a developing skill at this age. Be patient. Many third graders can identify what a character did but struggle to articulate why. The "feeling to action" frame ("The character felt ___ so [he/she] ___") gives them a scaffold for this kind of thinking. It is okay if their explanations are simple at first — depth will come with practice.

**The emotional arc matters:** The stories in this lesson are deliberately designed to show characters whose feelings change. Zara goes from confident to scared to confident again. Eli goes from excited to frustrated to generous. Layla goes from doubtful to brave. Recognizing that characters (and people) experience a range of emotions in response to events is a key social-emotional learning outcome as well as a reading comprehension skill.
