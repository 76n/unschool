# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Comparing Characters

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare and contrast two characters from the same story or from different stories
- Use a Venn diagram to organize similarities and differences
- Support comparisons with specific text evidence from the stories

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-8 (text evidence, character traits, character actions/feelings/motivations, story elements)
- Character trait word bank from Lesson 3

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Reading passages provided in this lesson (print or display)
- Character trait word bank from Lesson 3
- Crayons or colored pencils (for Venn diagram)
- Large sheet of paper (for drawing a full-size Venn diagram)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate the skill of comparing and introduce the concept of comparing characters.

**Activity: Compare Two Things**

Say: "Before we compare characters in stories, let's practice comparing two everyday things."

Ask: "How are a cat and a dog ALIKE? How are they DIFFERENT?"

Give the student 2 minutes to brainstorm. Write responses in a quick list:

**Alike:**
- Both are pets
- Both have four legs
- Both have fur
- Both need food and water

**Different:**
- Cats purr; dogs bark
- Cats are usually independent; dogs often want attention
- Cats climb trees; dogs do not (usually)

Say: "Great comparing! When we compare, we look for what is the SAME and what is DIFFERENT. Today we are going to compare CHARACTERS in stories the same way — but we will use text evidence to support every similarity and difference we find."

*Facilitator note: This warm-up makes the concept of comparing concrete and accessible before applying it to literature. If the student struggles, use an even simpler pair: an apple and an orange, or summer and winter.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to compare and contrast characters using a Venn diagram and text evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Compare:** To find how two things are ALIKE — what they have in common.
- **Contrast:** To find how two things are DIFFERENT — what makes them unique.
- **Venn diagram:** Two overlapping circles used to organize similarities (in the middle, where the circles overlap) and differences (in the outer parts of each circle).
- **Similarities:** The things two characters have in common.
- **Differences:** The things that make two characters unique or distinct from each other.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the Venn Diagram (3 minutes)**

Draw a Venn diagram on paper — two large overlapping circles. Label the left circle "Character A," the right circle "Character B," and the overlapping middle section "Both."

Say: "A Venn diagram is a tool for organizing comparisons. The things that are DIFFERENT about Character A go in the left part. The things that are DIFFERENT about Character B go in the right part. The things that are the SAME about both characters go in the overlapping middle. Let me show you how it works."

**Step 2: What to Compare (2 minutes)**

Say: "When we compare characters, we can look at many things. Here are the big categories:"

Write:
- **Traits:** What are they like? (brave, shy, kind, stubborn...)
- **Actions:** What do they do?
- **Feelings:** How do they feel?
- **Motivations:** Why do they act the way they do?
- **Problems:** What challenges do they face?
- **How they change:** Do they grow or learn something?

Say: "When we fill in our Venn diagram, we will use these categories. And for each point, we will include TEXT EVIDENCE."

### Worked Examples

**Step 3: Model a Character Comparison (7 minutes)**

Read this passage aloud:

> **Two Sisters**
>
> Elena and Rosa were sisters, but they could not have been more different.
>
> Elena was the older sister. She loved order and routine. Every morning, she made her bed, organized her desk, and checked her to-do list before breakfast. She was a planner — she thought everything through before she acted. When their family went camping, Elena packed three days before the trip and made a checklist of everything they needed.
>
> Rosa was the younger sister. She was a whirlwind of energy and ideas. She rarely made her bed, her desk was covered in art supplies and half-finished projects, and she never knew what she wanted for breakfast until she opened the refrigerator. Rosa did not plan — she leaped. When the family went camping, Rosa threw things into a bag ten minutes before they left and forgot her toothbrush.
>
> But for all their differences, the sisters had something important in common: they both loved their family fiercely. When their little brother, Marco, fell off his bike and scraped his knee, Elena ran to get the first-aid kit while Rosa sat beside him and told him jokes to keep him from crying. They worked as a team.
>
> And every Friday night, different as they were, Elena and Rosa sat on the same couch, shared the same bowl of popcorn, and watched the same movie together. Neither one would have traded that for anything.

**Model Venn Diagram:**

Draw and fill in:

**Elena (left):**
- Organized, plans ahead
- Made her bed every morning
- Packed 3 days before camping
- Ran to get the first-aid kit (practical response)

**Both (middle):**
- Love their family fiercely
- Helped their brother Marco when he was hurt
- Enjoy Friday movie night together
- Sisters

**Rosa (right):**
- Energetic, spontaneous
- Never made her bed, messy desk
- Packed 10 minutes before leaving, forgot toothbrush
- Told Marco jokes (emotional response)

Say: "Notice how I used text evidence for every point. I did not just say 'Elena is organized' — I showed HOW: she made her bed, organized her desk, packed three days early. Evidence makes the comparison strong."

Say: "Also notice something interesting: when Marco got hurt, BOTH sisters helped — but they helped in DIFFERENT ways. Elena was practical (first-aid kit) and Rosa was emotional (jokes). Their different traits led to different actions, but they both showed the same motivation: love for their brother."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice with support — comparing two characters using a Venn diagram and evidence.

**Activity: Compare Characters from a Story (12 minutes)**

Read this passage together:

> **The Science Partners**
>
> When Mr. Kim assigned science partners, Leo ended up with Anya. Leo groaned inside. He liked working alone.
>
> Leo was a careful, quiet worker. He read every instruction twice before starting. He measured precisely. He wrote his observations in neat, tiny handwriting. He did not like surprises — he liked knowing exactly what would happen before it happened.
>
> Anya was the opposite. She dove straight into experiments without reading the directions. "Let's just try it and see what happens!" she said constantly. She mixed chemicals with enthusiasm, spilled things regularly, and once accidentally launched a baking soda volcano across the table.
>
> For their project — building a bridge out of spaghetti and marshmallows — they clashed immediately. Leo wanted to draw a blueprint first. Anya wanted to start building.
>
> "We need a plan," Leo said firmly.
>
> "We need to experiment," Anya said, already stacking marshmallows.
>
> They argued for ten minutes. Then Anya's tower of marshmallows collapsed, scattering sticky pieces everywhere.
>
> Anya stared at the mess. Then she looked at Leo. "Okay," she said. "Maybe a blueprint would help."
>
> Leo smiled. "And maybe after we have a plan, we can experiment a little too."
>
> They worked together for the rest of the hour. Leo drew the blueprint. Anya tested different designs. When one design failed, Leo adjusted the plan and Anya tried again. By the end of class, their bridge held more weight than any other group's.
>
> "Not bad for opposite partners," Anya said.
>
> Leo grinned. "Not bad at all."

Draw a Venn diagram together and fill it in. Guide the student with questions:

- "What is Leo like? What evidence shows his traits?" (Left circle)
- "What is Anya like? What evidence shows her traits?" (Right circle)
- "What do they have in common?" (Middle)

| Leo | Both | Anya |
|-----|------|------|
| Careful, quiet | Both want to build a good bridge | Energetic, spontaneous |
| Reads instructions twice | Both willing to compromise | Dives in without directions |
| Likes planning and blueprints | Both contributed unique strengths | Likes experimenting |
| Neat handwriting | Both ended up respecting each other | Spills things, messy |
| Does not like surprises | Science partners | "Let's try it and see!" |

After completing the Venn diagram, discuss:

1. "How are Leo and Anya DIFFERENT in how they approach work?" (Leo plans first; Anya experiments first.)
2. "How are they ALIKE?" (Both want to succeed; both are willing to listen to the other; both contributed to the bridge.)
3. "Why was their partnership successful?" (They combined their different strengths — Leo's planning and Anya's willingness to experiment.)

*Facilitator prompt: Ask: "Is one approach better than the other — planning or experimenting?" Help the student see that both have value, and the combination of the two produced the best result. This models respectful comparison — comparing does not mean declaring a winner.*

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices independently — comparing characters using a Venn diagram with text evidence.

Have the student read the following two short passages independently, then complete the comparison activities.

> **Passage 1: Kai the Builder**
>
> Kai spent every afternoon in his backyard, building things. He had built a birdhouse, a go-kart, and a fort out of old wooden pallets. His hands were always dirty and his clothes were always wrinkled, but he did not care. What mattered was creating something out of nothing.
>
> Kai was patient. When a project did not work the first time, he took it apart and started over. He never asked for help — he liked figuring things out on his own. "Mistakes are just clues," he said. "They tell you what to try next."
>
> One day, his neighbor's mailbox post snapped in a storm. Kai walked over with his toolbox. "I can fix that," he said. Three hours later, the mailbox stood straighter than it had in years.

> **Passage 2: Zoe the Performer**
>
> Zoe spent every afternoon on the stage in her living room — which was actually just a taped-off rectangle of carpet. She practiced speeches, acted out scenes from her favorite movies, and gave imaginary award speeches in front of her cat, Mr. Whiskers.
>
> Zoe was bold. She volunteered for every school play, every presentation, and every public speaking event. She loved being in front of an audience. When she forgot her lines during the school play, she did not freeze — she made up new ones on the spot, and the audience laughed.
>
> One day, a shy classmate named Ivy needed to give a presentation but was too scared to speak. "Come to my living room stage," Zoe said. "I will teach you some tricks." By the end of the week, Ivy gave her presentation with confidence.

**Activity 1: Venn Diagram**

Draw a Venn diagram. Compare Kai and Zoe:

| Kai | Both | Zoe |
|-----|------|-----|
| | | |

Include at least three items in each section (Kai, Both, Zoe).

**Activity 2: Comparison Sentences**

Write three comparison sentences using text evidence:
1. One sentence about how Kai and Zoe are DIFFERENT.
2. One sentence about how Kai and Zoe are ALIKE.
3. One sentence comparing how they each helped someone.

Use the frames:
- "Kai is ___ while Zoe is ___, as shown by ___."
- "Both Kai and Zoe ___, as shown by ___."

**Activity 3: Reflection**

Answer: Which character are YOU more like — Kai or Zoe? Explain using at least one piece of evidence from the text and one example from your own life.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete the Venn diagram (Activity 1) with at least two items in each section. Write one comparison sentence (Activity 2).

**Stretch:** Complete Activities 1 and 2 in full. For Activity 2, write all three comparison sentences with text evidence.

**Challenge:** Complete all three activities. For Activity 3, write a paragraph (4-5 sentences). Additionally, compare Kai or Zoe to a character from an earlier lesson in this unit. Use text evidence from both stories.

### Interleaved Review

4. **Character traits (Lesson 3):** Using the S.D.T.F. chart, list what Kai SAYS, DOES, THINKS, and FEELS. Choose one trait that best describes him and support it with evidence.
5. **Cause and effect (Lesson 4):** Write a "Because..." sentence about Zoe: "Because Zoe ___, ___."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Think about Kai and Zoe.

1. Name one way Kai and Zoe are ALIKE. (Both are skilled at something, both help others, both are dedicated to their passions)
2. Name one way they are DIFFERENT. (Kai builds things / Zoe performs; Kai works alone / Zoe loves audiences; Kai is patient / Zoe is bold)
3. For either answer, give the TEXT EVIDENCE that supports it.

**Mastery = Student identifies one similarity AND one difference, with text evidence for at least one.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the model example (Elena and Rosa) and practice the Venn diagram with more support
- Start with very obvious comparisons: "One character is a boy, the other is a girl. One likes building, the other likes performing." Then move to deeper comparisons.
- Practice comparing characters from stories the student already knows well
- Repeat this lesson before moving to Lesson 10

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 10 (Reading Literature Celebration) next session
- Encourage the student to compare characters whenever they read multiple stories

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Character Trading Cards:** The student creates trading cards for characters from this unit. Each card has the character's name, a drawing, 3 traits, a key quote, and a "stats" section (bravery level, kindness level, etc.). Then they compare cards side by side.

2. **Same Problem, Different Characters:** What if Kai had to give a presentation (Zoe's strength)? What if Zoe had to fix a mailbox (Kai's strength)? Write or tell a short story imagining how each character would handle the other's situation.

3. **Compare Across the Unit:** Make a chart comparing three or more characters from different lessons in this unit. Which ones are most alike? Which are most different? Use the chart to discuss what makes each character unique.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Comparing is a higher-order thinking skill:** This lesson asks students to analyze two separate sets of information (two characters), identify patterns within and across those sets, and articulate the similarities and differences. This is significantly more cognitively demanding than describing a single character. If the student finds this challenging, that is normal and expected. The Venn diagram provides essential scaffolding by making the comparison visual.

**Comparing does not mean judging:** Help students understand that comparing characters does not mean deciding which one is "better." Elena is not better than Rosa. Leo is not better than Anya. Kai is not better than Zoe. Both characters have strengths. This models a respectful, nuanced way of thinking about people — an important social-emotional learning outcome.

**Start with differences, then find similarities:** Most students find it easier to identify differences (because differences are obvious) than similarities (which require looking beneath the surface). If your student fills in the outer circles of the Venn diagram easily but struggles with the middle, ask: "Okay, they are different in many ways. But is there ANYTHING they have in common? Think about what they both value, or what they both want, or how they both treat others."

**Connecting to the unit assessment:** The unit assessment will include a character comparison task. This lesson directly prepares students for that. If the student creates a strong Venn diagram here, they will have a model to reference during the assessment. Consider keeping this lesson's work in the binder for later reference.

2. "Both Kai and Zoe use their skills to help others, as shown by Kai fixing his neighbor's mailbox and Zoe teaching Ivy how to give a presentation with confidence."
3. "Kai helped his neighbor by physically fixing the broken mailbox, while Zoe helped Ivy by teaching her tricks for public speaking. Both helped someone in need, but Kai used his building skills and Zoe used her performing skills."

**Activity 3:** (Answers will vary. Award credit for a reasonable self-comparison that references text evidence and personal experience.)

**Interleaved Review:**
