# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 1 Assessment

## Unit: Reading Literature

**Assessment Type:** Summative (end-of-unit)
**Standards:** RL.3.1, RL.3.3, RL.3.4
**Estimated Time:** 40-50 minutes
**Total Points:** 25
**Facilitation:** The student reads all passages and answers all questions independently. The facilitator may read the directions aloud but should NOT read the passages or questions for the student (unless the student has a documented reading accommodation). The facilitator should not provide hints or help with answers.

---

This assessment measures the student's mastery of all skills taught in Unit 1: Reading Literature.

## Reading Passage 1: The Brave Whistle

Read this passage carefully. Then answer the questions in Section A and Section B.

> The night before the big swim meet, Hazel could not sleep. She lay in bed, staring at the ceiling, listening to the clock tick. Her stomach felt like it was full of bouncing rubber balls.
>
> Hazel was the youngest swimmer on the team. The other swimmers were ten and eleven years old, and they had been competing for years. Hazel was only eight. She had joined the team just two months ago, and tomorrow would be her first real race.
>
> "What if I come in last?" she whispered to herself. "What if everyone laughs?"
>
> The next morning, Hazel's grandfather drove her to the pool. He had been a swimmer when he was young — not a champion, he always said, but someone who loved the water.
>
> At the pool, the air was thick and warm, and the smell of chlorine stung Hazel's nose. She stood at the edge of the pool, watching the older swimmers warm up. They moved through the water like dolphins — smooth, fast, and confident.
>
> Hazel's grandfather sat in the bleachers. Before she walked to the starting block, he gave her a small silver whistle on a chain.
>
> "This was mine when I swam," he said. "I was nervous before every single race. But I learned something important: being nervous means you care. And caring is what makes you try your hardest."
>
> Hazel held the whistle tight. It was warm from her grandfather's hand.
>
> When the starting buzzer sounded, Hazel dove into the water. The cold hit her like a wall, but she pushed through it. She kicked hard, pulled her arms through the water, and kept her eyes on the lane line. She did not think about the other swimmers. She thought about her grandfather's words: *caring is what makes you try your hardest*.
>
> Hazel did not come in first. She came in fourth out of six swimmers. But when she touched the wall and looked up at the clock, she saw that her time was faster than she had ever swum before — a personal best.
>
> Her grandfather was standing in the bleachers, clapping. When she climbed out of the pool, he wrapped a towel around her shoulders.
>
> "How do you feel?" he asked.
>
> Hazel looked at the silver whistle still clenched in her fist. She smiled. "Like a real swimmer," she said.

## Reading Passage 2: The Word Collector

Read this passage carefully. Then answer the questions in Section C.

> Marco had an unusual hobby — he collected words. Not in a dictionary or on flashcards, but in a small green notebook that he carried everywhere. Whenever he heard or read a word that sounded interesting, he wrote it down.
>
> His collection was **eclectic** — it included all sorts of different words from many places. Some came from books, like "luminous" and "treacherous." Some came from his grandmother, who spoke Spanish and English and used words like "mariposa," which means butterfly. Some came from science class, like "metamorphosis," the process of a caterpillar changing into a butterfly.
>
> Marco's classmates thought his hobby was **peculiar** — strange and a little odd. "Why do you collect words?" they asked. "You can't play with words."
>
> "Sure you can," Marco said. He opened his notebook to a random page and pointed to the word "effervescent." "This word means bubbly and fizzy, like soda. But I think it also describes my little sister — she is bubbly and full of energy."
>
> His classmate Priya laughed. "That is actually really cool."
>
> By the end of the school year, Marco's notebook was **brimming** with words — the pages were so full that he had to start a second notebook. He had collected over three hundred words, and he could use every single one in a sentence.
>
> His teacher, Ms. Nguyen, was so impressed that she asked Marco to share his favorite words with the class. Marco stood up and read ten words, explaining each one with a story or example.
>
> When he finished, the classroom was quiet for a moment. Then Priya raised her hand. "Can I start a word notebook too?" she asked.
>
> Soon, half the class had green notebooks of their own. Marco's **solitary** hobby — something he had always done alone — had become something the whole class shared.

## Reading Passage 3: Two Friends and the Garden

Read this passage carefully. Then answer the questions in Section D.

> When the empty lot on Cedar Street was turned into a community garden, two friends signed up for plots on the very same day.
>
> Ava chose a plot in the sunny corner and planted rows of bright flowers — sunflowers, zinnias, and marigolds. She watered them every morning and pulled weeds every afternoon. She wanted her plot to be the most beautiful one in the garden.
>
> Leo chose the plot next to Ava's, but he planted vegetables — tomatoes, peppers, and cucumbers. He built a small trellis out of sticks for the cucumber vines to climb. He did not care what his garden looked like. He cared about growing food his family could eat.
>
> By midsummer, both gardens were thriving. Ava's flowers were a riot of color — reds, oranges, and yellows bursting in every direction. Leo's vegetables hung heavy on the vines, ripe and ready to pick.
>
> One day, Ava noticed something: bees were buzzing all around her flowers, but they were also flying over to Leo's garden. "Your vegetables are doing well," she said.
>
> Leo nodded. "I think it is because of your flowers," he said. "The bees come for your flowers and then they pollinate my vegetables. Your garden is helping mine."
>
> Ava smiled. She had not thought of that. "And your trellis is giving my sunflowers shade in the afternoon so they do not wilt," she said. "Your garden is helping mine too."
>
> They looked at each other and laughed. Two very different gardens, growing better because they were side by side.

## Scoring Guide

### Section A: Text Evidence (6 points)

**Question 1 (2 points):**
- 2 points: States that Hazel was nervous/anxious about the swim meet AND provides accurate text evidence (e.g., "staring at the ceiling," "stomach felt like it was full of bouncing rubber balls," "What if I come in last?")
- 1 point: Correct answer but vague or no text evidence
- 0 points: Incorrect or no response

**Question 2 (2 points):**
- 2 points: States that grandfather gave her a small silver whistle AND explains what he said about nervousness (being nervous means you care, and caring makes you try your hardest) with text evidence
- 1 point: Identifies the whistle OR the advice but not both, or lacks text evidence
- 0 points: Incorrect or no response

**Question 3 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Provides TWO or more pieces of evidence that Hazel felt good: (a) her time was a personal best, (b) her grandfather was clapping, (c) she smiled, (d) she said "Like a real swimmer." Must cite from more than one part of the text.
- 1 point: Provides only one piece of evidence or evidence from only one part of the text
- 0 points: Cannot identify evidence or incorrect

### Section B: Character Analysis (8 points)

**Question 4 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Names two appropriate traits (brave, determined, nervous/anxious but courageous, young but persistent, dedicated) with specific text evidence for each
- 1 point: Names one trait with evidence, or names two traits without evidence
- 0 points: Cannot identify traits or no evidence

**Question 5 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Accurately describes feelings at all three points — beginning (nervous, scared, could not sleep), during (focused, determined, pushed through the cold), end (proud, happy, felt like a real swimmer)
- 1 point: Describes feelings at one or two points but not all three, or descriptions are vague
- 0 points: Cannot track feelings or incorrect

**Question 6 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Clearly states a cause-and-effect relationship between grandfather's actions and Hazel's experience. Example: "Because her grandfather gave her the whistle and told her that being nervous means you care, Hazel was able to find the courage to race and achieve her personal best."
- 1 point: Identifies an action or an effect but does not clearly connect them
- 0 points: Cannot identify cause-and-effect or incorrect

**Question 7 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Explains that Hazel thought about grandfather's words because they helped her stay focused and motivated. Her motivation was to try her hardest because she cared about swimming. Uses text evidence.
- 1 point: Partial explanation without text evidence
- 0 points: Cannot explain motivation or incorrect

### Section C: Vocabulary in Context (5 points)

**Question 8 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Correct meaning (including many different types/kinds, varied, diverse) AND correct clue type (Definition or Synonym — "it included all sorts of different words from many places") AND quotes the clue
- 1 point: Correct meaning but wrong clue type, or correct clue type but unclear meaning
- 0 points: Incorrect meaning and clue type

**Question 9 (1 point):**
- 1 point: Correct meaning (strange, unusual, odd) AND correct clue type (Synonym — "strange and a little odd")
- 0 points: Incorrect

**Question 10 (1 point):**
- 1 point: Correct meaning (completely full, overflowing) AND correct clue type (Definition or Example — "the pages were so full that he had to start a second notebook")
- 0 points: Incorrect

**Question 11 (1 point):**
- 1 point: Correctly identifies a piece of figurative language, correctly labels it as simile or idiom, and explains the meaning.
  - From Passage 1: "stomach felt like it was full of bouncing rubber balls" (simile — she was very nervous), "They moved through the water like dolphins" (simile — they swam smoothly and gracefully), "The cold hit her like a wall" (simile — the cold was sudden and intense)
  - From Passage 2: "a riot of color" (figurative — the flowers were extremely colorful and wild-looking)
- 0 points: Cannot identify figurative language, mislabels it, or cannot explain it

### Section D: Applied Reading (6 points)

**Question 12 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Accurately identifies differences (Ava plants flowers for beauty; Leo plants vegetables for food) AND identifies at least one similarity (both signed up on the same day, both are dedicated gardeners, both help each other's gardens, both are friends)
- 1 point: Identifies differences OR similarities but not both
- 0 points: Cannot compare or incorrect

**Question 13 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Correctly identifies all four elements — Characters: Ava and Leo; Setting: community garden on Cedar Street; Problem/Situation: they have very different approaches to gardening; Solution/Outcome: they discover their gardens help each other, showing that different approaches can work together
- 1 point: Correctly identifies 2-3 elements
- 0 points: Identifies 0-1 elements

**Question 14 (2 points):**
- 2 points: Explains that "a riot of color" means the flowers were extremely colorful, vivid, and abundant — like the color was wild and everywhere. Identifies it as figurative language (not literal — the colors were not actually rioting).
- 1 point: Partially explains the meaning or identifies it as figurative but cannot explain
- 0 points: Takes it literally or cannot explain

## Reteaching Recommendations by Skill Area

**If Section A (Text Evidence) is below 4/6:**
- The student is not yet consistently going back to the text for evidence
- Return to Lessons 1-2 and practice with short passages
- Use the "Show me where it says that" prompt in every reading interaction
- Practice the sentence frame: "I know ___ because the text says ___"
- Emphasize the difference between answering from memory/imagination and answering from the text

**If Section B (Character Analysis) is below 5/8:**
- Identify which aspect is weakest:
  - Traits (Question 4): Return to Lesson 3; practice with the S.D.T.F. chart and trait word bank
  - Feelings/change (Question 5): Return to Lesson 5; practice feelings timelines with simpler passages
  - Cause-and-effect (Question 6): Return to Lesson 4; practice the "Because..." frame
  - Motivation (Question 7): Return to Lesson 5; practice the "feeling to action" chain
- Character analysis is the most complex skill area in this unit — if the student scored well on Section A but struggled here, that is a normal pattern. More practice and exposure will strengthen these skills.

**If Section C (Vocabulary) is below 3/5:**
- Return to Lesson 6 and review the four context clue types with explicit examples
- Practice identifying signal words (or, but, unlike, such as, which means)
- Start with definition clues (most explicit) and build to contrast clues (most inferential)
- For figurative language: return to Lesson 7 and practice with simile and idiom collections
- Consider creating a vocabulary journal for daily practice

**If Section D (Applied Reading) is below 4/6:**
- This section synthesizes all skills. Weakness here often reflects weakness in one of the other sections.
- Identify which question(s) the student missed and connect them back to the relevant skill area
- Practice with additional short passages that require applying multiple skills at once
- Use story maps as a consistent tool for organizing reading comprehension

## Answer Key

### Section A: Text Evidence

**Question 1:**
Hazel could not sleep because she was nervous about the swim meet. She was the youngest swimmer on the team, it was her first real race, and she was worried about coming in last or being laughed at.
- Text evidence: "She lay in bed, staring at the ceiling" / "Her stomach felt like it was full of bouncing rubber balls" / "What if I come in last? What if everyone laughs?"

**Question 2:**
Her grandfather gave her a small silver whistle on a chain that had been his when he swam. He told her that being nervous means you care, and caring is what makes you try your hardest.
- Text evidence: "This was mine when I swam" / "being nervous means you care. And caring is what makes you try your hardest."

**Question 3:**
Even though Hazel came in fourth, she felt good because: (1) her time was a personal best — "her time was faster than she had ever swum before," and (2) she felt like a real swimmer — "she smiled" and said "Like a real swimmer." Her grandfather was also "standing in the bleachers, clapping," which shows her achievement was recognized.

### Section B: Character Analysis

**Question 4:**
- Trait 1: Brave / Courageous. Evidence: Hazel dove into the water even though she was the youngest and most nervous. She "pushed through" the cold and raced against older swimmers.
- Trait 2: Determined / Hardworking. Evidence: She kept going during the race — "She kicked hard, pulled her arms through the water, and kept her eyes on the lane line." She achieved a personal best.
- Other acceptable traits: Nervous/anxious (but overcame it), young but resilient, dedicated.

**Question 5:**
- Beginning: Nervous, scared, anxious — she could not sleep, her stomach was upset, she worried about coming in last.
- During the race: Focused, determined — she pushed through the cold, kicked hard, and thought about her grandfather's words instead of the other swimmers.
- End: Proud, happy, confident — she smiled and said she felt "like a real swimmer." She had achieved a personal best.

**Question 6:**
"Because her grandfather gave her the whistle and told her that being nervous means you care, Hazel was able to stay motivated during the race and achieve her personal best." OR "Because her grandfather shared his experience and wisdom, Hazel had the courage to race and the focus to try her hardest."

**Question 7:**
Hazel thought about her grandfather's words because they helped her stay focused when she was scared. His words reframed her nervousness as something positive (caring, not weakness). What motivated her was the idea that being nervous meant she cared about swimming, and caring would help her try her hardest. The text says she "thought about her grandfather's words: caring is what makes you try your hardest" — this was her motivation during the race.

### Section C: Vocabulary

**Question 8:**
- Meaning: Including many different types/kinds; varied and diverse
- Clue type: D (Definition) or S (Synonym) — "it included all sorts of different words from many places"
- Clue: The sentence right after the word explains it: "it included all sorts of different words from many places. Some came from books... Some came from his grandmother... Some came from science class."

**Question 9:**
- Meaning: Strange, unusual, odd
- Clue type: S (Synonym) — "strange and a little odd" appears right after the dash

**Question 10:**
- Meaning: Completely full, overflowing
- Clue type: D (Definition) or E (Example) — "the pages were so full that he had to start a second notebook"

**Question 11:**
Any of these are correct:
- "stomach felt like it was full of bouncing rubber balls" — Simile (using "like"). Means she was very nervous; her stomach felt unsettled and jumpy.
- "They moved through the water like dolphins" — Simile (using "like"). Means the swimmers moved smoothly, gracefully, and fast.
- "The cold hit her like a wall" — Simile (using "like"). Means the cold was sudden, shocking, and intense.
- "a riot of color" — Figurative language. Means the flowers were extremely colorful and vivid, as if the colors were wild and everywhere.

### Section D: Applied Reading

**Question 12:**
- Different: Ava plants flowers because she wants beauty ("She wanted her plot to be the most beautiful one"). Leo plants vegetables because he wants food for his family ("He cared about growing food his family could eat").
- Alike: Both are dedicated gardeners who take care of their plots (Ava waters and weeds; Leo builds a trellis). Both signed up on the same day. Both end up helping each other's gardens without meaning to.

**Question 13:**
- Characters: Ava and Leo
- Setting: Community garden on Cedar Street, during the growing season (spring through midsummer)
- Problem/Situation: Ava and Leo have very different approaches to gardening (flowers for beauty vs. vegetables for food)
- Solution/Outcome: They discover their gardens actually help each other — Ava's flowers attract bees that pollinate Leo's vegetables, and Leo's trellis shades Ava's sunflowers. Different approaches work better together.

**Question 14:**
"A riot of color" means the flowers were so bright and plentiful that the colors seemed wild and chaotic in a beautiful way — reds, oranges, and yellows were everywhere. This is figurative language because colors cannot literally riot (rioting means acting wildly or out of control). The author uses this phrase to paint a vivid picture of how vibrant and abundant the flowers were.
