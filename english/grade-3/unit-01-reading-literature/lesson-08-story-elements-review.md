# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 1, Lesson 8

## Topic
Story Elements Review

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify and describe the four main story elements: character, setting, problem, and solution
- Explain how all four elements work together to create a complete story
- Complete a story map graphic organizer for a grade-level passage

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-7 (text evidence, character analysis, character feelings/motivations, vocabulary/figurative language)
- Grade 2 skill: identifying characters and settings

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Reading passages provided in this lesson (print or display)
- Crayons or colored pencils (for story map illustration)
- Character trait word bank from Lesson 3
- Large sheet of paper or poster board (optional, for a larger story map)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all skills from Lessons 1-7 with a quick multi-skill check and activate prior knowledge about story elements.

**Activity: Rapid Review Round**

Say: "We have learned a lot in this unit! Let's do a quick review of our key skills. I will give you a challenge, and you answer as fast as you can."

1. **Text Evidence (Lessons 1-2):** "If I ask you a question about a story, what should you always do?" (Go back to the text and find the evidence / show where it says that)
2. **Character Traits (Lesson 3):** "Name three character traits." (Any three from the word bank: brave, kind, curious, etc.)
3. **Cause and Effect (Lesson 4):** "Finish this frame: Because the character did ___, ___." (Student completes with any logical cause-and-effect)
4. **Feelings and Motivation (Lesson 5):** "What drives a character to act?" (Feelings / motivations / what they want or need)
5. **Context Clues (Lesson 6):** "Name the four types of context clues." (Definition, synonym, example, contrast)
6. **Figurative Language (Lesson 7):** "What is a simile?" (A comparison using "like" or "as")

Say: "Excellent! Today we are going to bring all of those skills together and review the four big story elements — the building blocks every story is made of."

*Facilitator note: If the student cannot recall some of these, do not worry. The purpose of this lesson is to consolidate and review. Any gaps will become visible and can be addressed.*

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the four story elements and teach how they work together as a system.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Story elements:** The building blocks of a story — character, setting, problem, and solution. Every story has these four parts.
- **Character:** The people or animals in the story. We already know how to describe them using traits, actions, feelings, and motivations.
- **Setting:** Where and when the story takes place. Setting can affect how characters feel and what they do.
- **Problem (Conflict):** The challenge, difficulty, or question the character faces. The problem is what makes the story interesting.
- **Solution (Resolution):** How the problem is solved or how the character responds to the challenge. The solution usually comes from the character's actions.
- **Story map:** A graphic organizer that helps you record and see all the story elements in one place.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: The Four Story Elements (3 minutes)**

Say: "You have known about story elements since Grade 2. But now we are going to think about them in a deeper way — how they CONNECT to each other."

Write on paper:

**The Four Story Elements:**
1. **CHARACTER** — Who is in the story? What are they like? (Traits, feelings, motivations)
2. **SETTING** — Where and when does the story take place? How does the setting affect the characters?
3. **PROBLEM** — What challenge does the character face? What do they want or need?
4. **SOLUTION** — How does the character solve the problem? What actions do they take?

Say: "Here is the key idea: these four elements do not exist separately. They work TOGETHER. The SETTING creates the situation. The CHARACTER faces a PROBLEM because of who they are and where they are. The CHARACTER's actions lead to the SOLUTION. If you change any one element, the whole story changes."

**Step 2: How Elements Connect (3 minutes)**

Draw this diagram on paper:

```
SETTING creates the situation
    |
    v
CHARACTER faces a PROBLEM
    |
    v
CHARACTER's traits, feelings, and actions
    |
    v
SOLUTION (driven by what the character does)
```

Say: "Let me show you what I mean with a quick example. Imagine a story about a shy girl at a new school. The SETTING is a new school (unfamiliar place). The CHARACTER is shy. The PROBLEM is that she has no friends. Her SHYNESS (trait) makes the problem harder. But if she is also kind, her KINDNESS (trait) might help her make a friend — and that would be the SOLUTION."

Say: "See how the character's traits are connected to both the problem and the solution? The character is the ENGINE of the story."

### Worked Examples

**Step 3: Model a Story Map (5 minutes)**

Read this passage aloud:

> **The Missing Key**
>
> On a freezing December afternoon, Sophie walked home from school to find the front door locked. She reached into her coat pocket for the house key, but it was not there. She checked her backpack, her other pockets, even her boots. Gone.
>
> Sophie shivered on the porch. Her mom would not be home from work for two more hours. The temperature was dropping, and dark clouds were gathering. She could see her breath in the air.
>
> Sophie thought about what to do. She could walk to the neighbor's house, but the Hendersons were on vacation. She could wait on the porch, but it was too cold. Then she remembered — her mom had once told her there was a spare key hidden somewhere in the backyard.
>
> Sophie hurried to the backyard. She checked under the doormat. Nothing. She looked under a flower pot. Nothing. She was about to give up when she noticed a small rock near the garden fence that looked different from the others. She picked it up — it was hollow. Inside was a silver key.
>
> Sophie's hands were so cold that she fumbled with the key twice before getting it into the lock. But when the door finally opened and warm air rushed out, she nearly cried with relief.
>
> She stepped inside, locked the door behind her, and immediately called her mom. "I found the spare key," she said. "And I am NEVER losing my key again."

**Model Story Map:**

Draw a story map on paper and fill it in:

| Story Element | Details |
|--------------|---------|
| **CHARACTER** | Sophie — determined, resourceful, problem-solver. She does not give up even when frustrated. |
| **SETTING** | A freezing December afternoon, outside Sophie's locked house. The cold weather makes the problem urgent. |
| **PROBLEM** | Sophie is locked out of her house, it is freezing, and her mom will not be home for two hours. |
| **SOLUTION** | Sophie remembers her mom mentioned a spare key. She searches the backyard and finds it hidden in a hollow rock. |

Say: "Now let me show how the elements CONNECT."
- The SETTING (freezing cold) makes the PROBLEM urgent — she cannot just wait.
- Sophie's CHARACTER (determined, resourceful) drives the SOLUTION — she does not give up; she remembers the spare key and searches until she finds it.
- If Sophie were a different character (say, someone who gives up easily), the story would end differently.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice with support — completing a story map and explaining how elements connect.

**Activity: Story Map Together (12 minutes)**

Read this passage together:

> **The Bridge of Sticks**
>
> The creek behind Mara's house had flooded after three days of rain. The water was brown and swift, rushing over the rocks where Mara usually crossed to get to her best friend Ava's house on the other side.
>
> "I promised Ava I would come over today," Mara said, staring at the swollen creek. "She is moving away next week, and this is one of our last days together."
>
> Mara's dad shook his head. "It is too dangerous to cross," he said. "The water is too fast."
>
> Mara knew he was right, but she could not bear the thought of missing time with Ava. She sat on the bank and thought. Then she pulled out her phone and called Ava.
>
> "I cannot cross the creek," she said. "But what if we both go to the creek? We can sit on opposite sides and have a picnic together — apart but together."
>
> Ava laughed. "That is the best idea ever!"
>
> Thirty minutes later, Mara sat on the east bank with a peanut butter sandwich, and Ava sat on the west bank with a bag of grapes. They shouted stories and jokes across the water. They played "I Spy" using things they could both see. Ava held up a drawing she had made of the two of them, and Mara pretended to catch it from across the creek.
>
> When it was time to go, Ava called out: "This was the best day!"
>
> Mara smiled. Even a flooded creek could not keep best friends apart.

Work with the student to complete a story map:

| Story Element | Details |
|--------------|---------|
| **CHARACTER** | Who is in the story? What are their traits? |
| **SETTING** | Where and when? How does the setting affect the story? |
| **PROBLEM** | What challenge does the main character face? |
| **SOLUTION** | How does the character solve the problem? |

Then discuss the connections:

1. "How does the SETTING create the PROBLEM?" (The creek flooded because of rain, blocking Mara from crossing.)
2. "How do Mara's CHARACTER TRAITS help her find the SOLUTION?" (She is creative and determined — she thinks of an alternative instead of giving up.)
3. "How do Mara's FEELINGS connect to her actions?" (She loves Ava and cannot bear missing time with her, which motivates her to find a creative solution.)
4. "What would have happened if Mara had just given up?" (She would have missed her last chance to spend time with Ava before she moved.)

*Facilitator prompt: Let the student lead as much as possible. Ask the guiding questions, then give them space to think and respond. If they need help, point to a specific paragraph: "What does this part tell us about the problem?"*

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices independently — completing a full story map and connecting elements.

Have the student read the following passage independently, then complete the activities below.

> **The Talent Nobody Wanted**
>
> Every student at Lincoln Elementary had a talent. Reiko's classmate Jay could juggle. DeShawn could do a backflip. Mei played piano beautifully. Even quiet Tomoko could whistle any song perfectly.
>
> Reiko's talent was drawing. She could draw anything — animals, buildings, people — and make them look real. But when the class planned a talent show, drawing did not seem exciting enough. Nobody clapped for someone sitting at a desk with a pencil.
>
> "Maybe I should not be in the talent show," Reiko told her mom.
>
> Her mom sat down next to her. "Tell me — what do you love about drawing?"
>
> Reiko thought. "I love that I can create a whole world on a piece of paper," she said. "I love that I can show people something they have never seen before."
>
> "Then show them," her mom said simply.
>
> Reiko got an idea. On the night of the talent show, she walked onto the stage with a large easel, a pad of paper, and a set of thick markers. A camera pointed at the easel displayed her drawing on a big screen behind her.
>
> "Tell me something to draw!" she called to the audience.
>
> "A dragon!" someone shouted.
>
> In sixty seconds, Reiko drew a dragon with smoke curling from its nostrils and scales that seemed to shimmer. The audience gasped. "A castle!" someone else yelled. Thirty seconds later, a castle appeared beside the dragon. "A spaceship!" In twenty seconds, a spaceship hovered above the castle.
>
> By the time Reiko was done, she had created an entire fantasy world on stage while the audience cheered with every new addition. The drawing covered the entire sheet, and it was magnificent.
>
> Reiko did not win the talent show. But she did not care. She had shown everyone what she loved — and they had loved it too.

**Activity 1: Complete Story Map**

| Story Element | Details |
|--------------|---------|
| **CHARACTER** | Name, traits (at least 2), feelings, motivation |
| **SETTING** | Where and when? How does it affect the story? |
| **PROBLEM** | What challenge does Reiko face? |
| **SOLUTION** | How does she solve it? What actions does she take? |

**Activity 2: Connections**

Answer in complete sentences with text evidence:
1. How do Reiko's CHARACTER TRAITS help her find the SOLUTION?
2. How does the SETTING (a talent show) create the PROBLEM?
3. How is the SOLUTION connected to what Reiko's mom said?

**Activity 3: Story Elements Illustration**

Draw a four-panel illustration: one panel for character, one for setting, one for problem, one for solution. Include a caption under each panel.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete the story map (Activity 1) and answer one connection question from Activity 2 orally or in writing.

**Stretch:** Complete Activities 1 and 2 in writing. Use at least one piece of text evidence per answer.

**Challenge:** Complete all three activities. For Activity 2, answer all three questions with text evidence. Also write a paragraph comparing Reiko's story to one other story from this unit (Lessons 1-7). How are the characters' problems and solutions similar or different?

### Interleaved Review

4. **Figurative language (Lesson 7):** Find one example of descriptive or figurative language in the passage. Explain what it means. (Example: "scales that seemed to shimmer" — vivid descriptive language; "magnificent" — context clues: the drawing covered the entire sheet and the audience cheered.)
5. **Text evidence (Lessons 1-2):** Using the frame "I know ___ because the text says ___," answer this question: How do we know the audience enjoyed Reiko's performance?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Think about the story of Reiko. Answer these questions quickly:

1. Name the CHARACTER and one trait. (Reiko — creative, talented, brave, determined)
2. What is the SETTING? (Lincoln Elementary School, talent show night)
3. What is the PROBLEM? (Reiko thought drawing was not exciting enough for a talent show)
4. What is the SOLUTION? (She drew live on stage while the audience shouted ideas, displaying her work on a big screen)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies all four story elements with reasonable detail.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice with a very simple, familiar story (a fairy tale or a story from an earlier lesson)
- Focus on one element at a time: "Let's just find the characters first. Now the setting. Now the problem."
- Use the story map as a visual scaffold
- Repeat this lesson with a simpler passage before moving to Lesson 9

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 9 (Comparing Characters) next session
- Encourage the student to identify story elements in every story they read

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Create Your Own Story Map First:** The student plans a short original story by filling in a blank story map FIRST (character, setting, problem, solution) and then writing the story. This reverses the process and builds writing skills.

2. **Story Element Remix:** Take the Reiko story and change ONE element. "What if Reiko's talent were singing instead of drawing? How would the solution change?" "What if the setting were a campfire instead of a school talent show?" Changing one element shows how all elements are interconnected.

3. **Story Element Sort:** Write details from a story on index cards (mix up characters, settings, problems, and solutions from different stories in this unit). The student sorts them into the correct categories. This builds categorization skills and reviews multiple stories.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This is a consolidation lesson:** Lesson 8 is deliberately designed to pull together everything from Lessons 1-7. If the student has been progressing well, this lesson should feel like a review and confidence-builder. If the student has gaps, this lesson reveals them — and that is valuable information. Use the story map as a diagnostic tool: which elements can the student identify easily, and which ones need more work?

**Problem and solution are where students often struggle:** Most third graders can identify characters and settings, but some struggle to clearly articulate the problem (especially if it is internal — like Reiko's self-doubt) and the solution (especially if it is nuanced — Reiko did not "win," but she achieved something more important). Help students see that not all problems are physical obstacles, and not all solutions involve winning. Sometimes the solution is a change in perspective, a creative idea, or an act of courage.

**The story map is a tool they will use repeatedly:** The story map graphic organizer introduced in this lesson will be used again in Lesson 9 (comparing characters), in the unit assessment, and throughout the year. Make sure the student understands how to use it. If they create a neat, complete story map in this lesson, keep it as a model they can reference later.

**Connecting elements is higher-order thinking:** Asking "How does the setting create the problem?" or "How do the character's traits lead to the solution?" requires students to think about relationships between ideas, not just identify individual facts. This is the kind of thinking that distinguishes Grade 3 comprehension from Grade 2. Be patient — some students will make these connections naturally, while others will need explicit modeling and guided questions.

**Independent Practice:**

**Activity 1 — Story Map (Reiko):**

| Element | Details |
|---------|---------|
| CHARACTER | Reiko — creative (can draw anything), talented, initially insecure about her talent, ultimately brave and determined. She is motivated by her love of drawing. |
| SETTING | Lincoln Elementary School; the school talent show. The talent show creates pressure to perform something exciting. |
| PROBLEM | Reiko loves drawing but does not think it is exciting enough for a talent show. "Nobody clapped for someone sitting at a desk with a pencil." |
| SOLUTION | Inspired by her mom's advice to "show them," Reiko sets up an easel with a camera and draws live on stage while the audience shouts ideas. She creates an entire fantasy world in real time and the audience loves it. |

**Activity 2 — Connections:**
1. Reiko is creative and talented, which allows her to think of a unique way to present drawing (live drawing with audience participation) rather than just sitting at a desk. Her determination means she does not give up after initially wanting to drop out.
2. The talent show creates the problem because it is a performance setting where students show off exciting talents like juggling, backflips, and piano. Drawing seems boring by comparison in that setting.
3. Reiko's mom told her to "show them" what she loves about drawing. Reiko said she loves creating worlds and showing people something they have never seen. Her solution — live drawing with audience interaction — does exactly that: she creates a world on stage and shows everyone something they have never seen.

**Interleaved Review:**
4. "Scales that seemed to shimmer" is vivid descriptive language that creates a picture of shiny, moving dragon scales. "Magnificent" is used to describe the final drawing — context clues (audience cheered, the drawing covered the entire sheet) tell us it means amazing, impressive.
5. "I know the audience enjoyed Reiko's performance because the text says 'the audience gasped,' they 'cheered with every new addition,' and they kept shouting ideas for her to draw."

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Reiko — creative/talented/brave/determined
2. Lincoln Elementary School, talent show night
3. Drawing did not seem exciting enough for the talent show
4. She drew live on stage with a camera/big screen while the audience shouted ideas
