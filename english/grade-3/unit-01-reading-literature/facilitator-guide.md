# Facilitator Guide — Grade 3 English Language Arts, Unit 1: Reading Literature

**Grade:** 3
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 1 — Reading Literature
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 5 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Standards:** RL.3.1, RL.3.3, RL.3.4
**Facilitation Mode:** Partially facilitated (adult introduces the lesson and supports guided practice, then monitors independent work; student can handle warm-ups and independent practice more autonomously)

---

## Unit Overview

This is the opening unit for Grade 3 English Language Arts. It represents a significant milestone: students are transitioning from "learning to read" to "reading to learn." In Grade 2, students built fluency and learned basic comprehension strategies — asking questions, retelling, and identifying characters and settings. Now in Grade 3, students deepen those skills by learning to refer explicitly to the text as evidence, analyze characters in more sophisticated ways, and use context clues to unlock the meaning of unfamiliar words and phrases.

The unit is organized in three strands that build on each other:

- **Lessons 1-2: Text Evidence (RL.3.1)** — Students learn to ask and answer questions about stories by pointing to specific words, sentences, and details in the text. The emphasis shifts from "What do you think?" to "How do you know? Show me where the text says that."
- **Lessons 3-5: Character Analysis (RL.3.3)** — Students describe characters using traits, analyze how characters' actions drive the events of a story, and explore how characters' feelings and motivations shape their behavior. This strand moves beyond surface-level identification ("Who is in the story?") to genuine literary analysis ("Why does this character act this way, and what happens because of it?").
- **Lessons 6-7: Vocabulary in Context (RL.3.4)** — Students learn strategies for determining the meaning of unfamiliar words and phrases as they are used in a text, including four types of context clues and an introduction to figurative language.
- **Lessons 8-10: Integration and Celebration** — Students review all story elements, compare characters across texts, and celebrate their growth as readers with a culminating presentation.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Ask and answer questions about a story by referring explicitly to the text as the basis for their answers
- Distinguish between "right there" questions (answer stated directly) and "think and search" questions (answer found by combining information from different parts of the text)
- Describe characters using specific traits (brave, kind, selfish, curious, determined, etc.) and support those descriptions with evidence from the text
- Explain how a character's actions contribute to the sequence of events in a story
- Identify what motivates a character and how a character's feelings connect to their actions
- Use four types of context clues (definition, synonym, example, contrast) to determine the meaning of unfamiliar words
- Recognize basic figurative language (similes and common idioms) and understand that words do not always mean exactly what they say
- Compare and contrast two characters using a Venn diagram and text evidence

**Why this matters:** Third grade is often called the "reading watershed." Students who build strong comprehension and vocabulary skills now are positioned to succeed across all subjects, because every subject increasingly requires reading complex text. The ability to find evidence in a text, analyze characters and their motivations, and figure out unfamiliar words are foundational skills that will grow in sophistication through the rest of elementary school, middle school, and beyond. More importantly, these skills help students become thoughtful, engaged readers who enjoy stories and think deeply about the people and ideas they encounter in books.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, informally check that the student can:

- [ ] Read a grade-level passage aloud with reasonable fluency (accuracy, appropriate rate, some expression)
- [ ] Ask and answer basic who, what, where, when, why, and how questions about a short text
- [ ] Retell a story using beginning, middle, and end
- [ ] Identify the main characters and setting of a story
- [ ] Describe how events in a story connect (basic cause and effect)
- [ ] Write 2-3 sentences in response to a reading prompt

These are skills covered in the Grade 2 Reading Fluency & Comprehension unit. If the student struggles significantly with any of these, consider spending 1-2 sessions reviewing the relevant Grade 2 skills before beginning Lesson 1. In particular, if the student has difficulty reading grade-level text with reasonable fluency, focus on fluency building first — comprehension work is much harder when the student is still laboring over individual words.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

All materials are common household items or are provided within the lesson plans. Gather these before starting:

- **Pencils and paper** (lined paper is helpful for written responses)
- **Crayons, colored pencils, or markers** (for graphic organizers, character illustrations, and Venn diagrams)
- **Index cards or sticky notes** (for vocabulary cards, character trait cards, and annotation practice)
- **Reading passages** (provided in each lesson — print or display them so the student can read, underline, and annotate)
- **A folder or binder** (to collect completed work and track progress throughout the unit)
- **Highlighters or colored pencils for underlining** (for marking text evidence — two colors are ideal)
- **Scissors and glue or tape** (optional, for cut-and-sort activities)

**Suggested Read-Aloud Books (Optional):** You do not need any published books for this unit — all reading passages are original and included in the lessons. However, if you want to extend the learning, look for grade-appropriate fiction books such as:
- A fiction book about a character who overcomes a challenge (builds character analysis skills)
- A fiction book about friendship or teamwork (great for discussing character traits and motivations)
- A fiction book with rich descriptive language (supports vocabulary and figurative language lessons)
- A collection of short stories or fables with clear character traits (useful for comparing characters)

Any fiction book your student enjoys will work. The specific title matters less than the conversation you have about it.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Recommended Sessions | Notes |
|--------|-------|---------------------|-------|
| 1 | Asking Questions About Stories | 1 session | Foundation for all comprehension work |
| 2 | Finding Evidence in the Text | 1 session | Pairs with Lesson 1; may need an extra session |
| 3 | Describing Characters | 1 session | Character trait word bank introduced |
| 4 | Character Actions and Events | 1 session | Builds directly on Lesson 3 |
| 5 | Character Feelings and Motivations | 1 session | Completes the character analysis strand |
| 6 | Words in Context: Context Clues | 1 session | New skill set; take your time |
| 7 | Figurative Language Introduction | 1 session | Fun and engaging; may need extra session |
| 8 | Story Elements Review | 1 session | Consolidation lesson |
| 9 | Comparing Characters | 1 session | Synthesis of character skills |
| 10 | Reading Literature Celebration | 1 session | Culminating activity |
| — | Unit Assessment | 1 separate session | Allow 40-50 minutes |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- If the student needs extra time on Lessons 1-2 (text evidence), add an additional session. The habit of going back to the text to support answers is the single most important skill in this unit, and it will be used in every subsequent lesson.
- Lessons 3-5 (character analysis) form a connected strand. If the student grasps character traits quickly (Lesson 3), you can move briskly into Lesson 4. If character traits are challenging, spend an extra session on Lesson 3 before tackling actions and events.
- Lessons 6-7 (vocabulary) introduce a new skill set. Some students pick up context clues intuitively; others need more practice. Adjust accordingly.
- Lesson 8 is a review and consolidation lesson. If your student is performing well, you can move through it quickly. If there are gaps, use Lesson 8 as a reteaching opportunity.
- Lesson 10 is a celebration and demonstration of learning. Do not skip it — it gives the student a chance to show what they have learned, which builds confidence and ownership.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student answers comprehension questions from memory or imagination rather than referring to the text | Has not yet developed the habit of going back to the text for evidence; may be drawing on personal experience rather than what the author wrote | This is the most common issue at this grade level. Gently redirect: "That is a great idea, but let's check — does the text actually say that? Let's go back and find the words that give us the answer." Make "show me where it says that" a regular phrase in your discussions. |
| Student can identify character traits but cannot explain WHY a character has that trait | Can label a trait but has not connected it to text evidence | Prompt: "You said the character is brave. What did the character DO that shows us they are brave? Can you find a sentence in the story that proves it?" The goal is always trait + evidence. |
| Student confuses a character's actions with a character's traits | Does not yet distinguish between what a character DOES (action) and what a character IS LIKE (trait) | Explain: "An action is something the character does — like 'she climbed the tree.' A trait is what that action tells us about the character — like 'she is adventurous.' The action is the proof; the trait is the description." Use the sentence frame: "[Character] is [trait] because [he/she] [action]." |
| Student struggles with "think and search" questions that require combining information from multiple parts of the text | Can answer "right there" questions but has difficulty with inferential comprehension | This is developmentally normal for Grade 3. Start with questions where the answer is in two consecutive sentences. Gradually increase the distance between relevant pieces of information. Model the search process: "The first clue is here in paragraph 1... and the second clue is here in paragraph 3... When I put them together, I can answer the question." |
| Student guesses at unfamiliar words or skips them entirely | Has not yet developed the strategy of using context clues | Do not criticize guessing — it shows the student is trying. Redirect: "Let's read the sentence around that word. Are there any clues that help us figure out what it means?" Teach the four context clue types (Lesson 6) and practice them repeatedly. |
| Student takes figurative language literally ("He was a chicken" — "No, he was a person!") | Has not yet learned that words can have non-literal meanings; this is a normal developmental stage | Explain with enthusiasm: "In stories, authors sometimes use fun language that means something different from what the words usually mean. When we say someone 'was a chicken,' we do not mean they turned into a bird — we mean they were scared! Authors do this to make their writing more colorful and interesting." Collect examples together and make it a game. |
| Student can describe one character well but struggles to compare two characters | Comparison is a higher-order thinking skill that requires holding two sets of information simultaneously | Start with very concrete comparisons: "Let's make a list of what Character A is like. Now let's make a list of what Character B is like. Now let's look at our lists — what is the same? What is different?" The Venn diagram in Lesson 9 provides a visual scaffold for this. |
| Student writes vague or one-word answers to comprehension questions | May lack writing stamina, may not know how to construct an evidence-based answer, or may need sentence frames | Provide the frame: "I think ___ because the text says ___." Practice oral responses before written ones. Emphasize that the quality of their thinking matters more than perfect spelling or handwriting. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or during everyday reading moments to build comprehension habits:

- "What question do you have about this story so far? Let's see if the text answers it."
- "How do you know that? Can you show me where the text tells us?"
- "If you had to describe [character name] to someone who has never read this story, what would you say? What evidence supports that?"
- "Why do you think [character name] did that? What was the character feeling or thinking?"
- "What happened BECAUSE of what [character name] did? How did that action change the story?"
- "There is an interesting word here — [word]. What do you think it means? What clues in the sentence help you figure it out?"
- "Does the author mean that literally, or is it a way of saying something? What does the author really mean?"
- "How are [Character A] and [Character B] alike? How are they different?"
- "What was the most important thing that happened in this story? Why was it important?"
- "If you could give [character name] advice, what would you say? Why?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support than these materials provide. They are NOT cause for alarm — they are signals to investigate further:

- The student cannot read grade-level text with reasonable fluency after consistent practice — this suggests decoding or fluency gaps that need systematic attention before comprehension work can be effective
- The student consistently cannot answer even basic "right there" questions after reading or being read to, even with shorter texts
- The student shows no improvement in going back to the text for evidence after several lessons of explicit instruction and practice
- The student demonstrates strong oral comprehension when listening to a story but very poor comprehension when reading independently — this may indicate a decoding issue rather than a comprehension issue
- The student shows significant frustration, anxiety, or avoidance around reading despite encouragement and supportive pacing

If you see these signs, consider:
- Reviewing Grade 2 fluency and comprehension skills (Unit 1 of the Grade 2 ELA materials)
- Consulting with a reading specialist or literacy coach
- Requesting a screening for reading difficulties through the student's school
- Adjusting the pace: spending more time on fewer skills is better than rushing through all of them

Remember: third graders develop at different rates. Some students will find text evidence and character analysis intuitive; others will need many repetitions. The goal is steady progress with consistent, supportive practice.

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students will be prepared for **Unit 2: Reading Informational Text**, where they will apply many of the same skills — asking questions, finding evidence, and determining word meanings — to nonfiction texts. They will also learn new skills specific to informational text, including identifying the main idea and supporting details, using text features to locate information, and understanding text structure.

The character analysis and text evidence skills from this unit will continue to be reinforced in every subsequent unit. In Unit 3 (Writing), students will use evidence-based reasoning in their own writing. In Unit 4 (Grammar & Vocabulary), students will formalize their vocabulary strategies using dictionaries and glossaries.

Between units, encourage daily reading practice. Even 15-20 minutes a day of reading fiction builds vocabulary, comprehension, and a love of stories. Let the student choose books that interest them. After reading, ask one or two conversation starters from the list above — this keeps comprehension strategies active without feeling like a formal lesson.
