# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 1, Lesson 7

## Topic
Figurative Language Introduction

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Recognize that words and phrases do not always mean exactly what they say (literal vs. figurative language)
- Identify similes in a text and explain what they mean (comparisons using "like" or "as")
- Understand common idioms and explain their figurative meanings
- Use context clues (from Lesson 6) to figure out the meaning of figurative language in stories

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 6 (context clues, determining word meaning)
- Lessons 1-5 (text evidence, character analysis)

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Reading passages provided in this lesson (print or display)
- Index cards (for idiom/simile collection)
- Crayons or colored pencils (for illustration activity)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the idea that language can mean something different from what it literally says, and review context clues from Lesson 6.

**Activity: What Does It REALLY Mean?**

Say: "I am going to say some sentences. Tell me — does the person LITERALLY mean what they are saying, or do they mean something else?"

1. "My backpack weighs a ton!" Does the backpack really weigh a ton? (No — it means the backpack is very heavy.)
2. "I am so hungry I could eat a horse!" Could the person really eat a whole horse? (No — it means they are extremely hungry.)
3. "She has a heart of gold." Is her heart actually made of gold? (No — it means she is very kind and generous.)
4. "It is raining cats and dogs!" Are animals really falling from the sky? (No — it means it is raining very hard.)

Say: "You already know this, even if you did not realize it! When people say things like 'my backpack weighs a ton,' they do not mean it literally. They are using FIGURATIVE language — words that paint a picture or exaggerate to make a point. Authors use figurative language in stories too, and today we are going to learn how to recognize it and figure out what it really means."

*Facilitator note: Third graders usually find figurative language fun and silly. Lean into that energy! The silliness of the literal interpretation ("cats and dogs falling from the sky?!") helps students remember the difference between literal and figurative meaning.*

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach similes, introduce common idioms, and connect figurative language to context clues.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Literal meaning:** The exact, word-for-word meaning. "The cat sat on the mat" literally means a cat was sitting on a mat.
- **Figurative meaning:** A meaning that is different from the exact words. The words paint a picture, make a comparison, or exaggerate. "She had butterflies in her stomach" does not mean actual butterflies — it means she felt nervous.
- **Simile:** A comparison between two different things using the words "like" or "as." Example: "He was as brave as a lion." (He was very brave — the simile compares him to a lion.)
- **Idiom:** A common expression that means something different from the literal words. Example: "Break a leg!" does not mean actually break your leg — it means "Good luck!"
- **Descriptive language:** Words that create vivid pictures in the reader's mind. Figurative language is one type of descriptive language.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Similes (4 minutes)**

Say: "A simile is when an author compares two different things using 'like' or 'as.' Similes help us picture what something is like by comparing it to something we already know."

Write these examples:

| Simile | What It Compares | What It Means |
|--------|-----------------|---------------|
| Her smile was as bright as the sun. | smile and the sun | She had a very big, happy smile |
| He ran like the wind. | his running and the wind | He ran very fast |
| The water was as cold as ice. | water and ice | The water was extremely cold |
| She was as quiet as a mouse. | her quietness and a mouse | She was very quiet, barely making a sound |
| His voice was like thunder. | his voice and thunder | He spoke very loudly |

Say: "See the pattern? 'Like' or 'as' is the signal word. When you see 'like' or 'as' comparing two things, you have found a simile!"

Have the student try creating two similes:
- "The pillow was as soft as ___." (a cloud, cotton, marshmallows)
- "She laughed like ___." (a hyena, bells ringing, a bubbling brook)

**Step 2: Idioms (4 minutes)**

Say: "An idiom is a phrase that people use all the time, but the words do NOT mean what they literally say. You have to learn what each idiom means because you cannot figure it out from the words alone — although sometimes context clues help."

Write these common idioms:

| Idiom | Literal Meaning (Silly!) | Real (Figurative) Meaning |
|-------|-------------------------|--------------------------|
| It is raining cats and dogs | Animals are falling from the sky | It is raining very hard |
| Break a leg | Actually break your leg bone | Good luck (said before a performance) |
| Piece of cake | A slice of cake | Something is very easy |
| Hit the books | Smack books with your hand | Start studying hard |
| Under the weather | Standing beneath weather | Feeling sick or unwell |
| Cost an arm and a leg | Pay with body parts | Very expensive |
| Butterflies in my stomach | Insects inside you | Feeling nervous or excited |
| Let the cat out of the bag | Release a cat from a bag | Reveal a secret |

Say: "Some idioms are used so often that you probably already know what they mean. Others you have to figure out from context — just like we did with new vocabulary words in Lesson 6."

### Worked Examples

**Step 3: Figurative Language in a Story (4 minutes)**

Read this passage aloud:

> **Race Day**
>
> Kaia stood at the starting line, her heart pounding like a drum. The other runners looked as tall as trees next to her. She took a deep breath.
>
> "You have got this," her coach whispered. "Go out there and show them what you are made of."
>
> The starting whistle blew, and Kaia shot off the line like a rocket. Her legs moved so fast they were a blur. By the second turn, she had passed three runners. The crowd went wild.
>
> When she crossed the finish line in second place, she was on cloud nine. "That was a piece of cake!" she panted, grinning — even though her legs felt like jelly.

Say: "Let me find the figurative language in this passage."

Point to each one and explain:

1. **"heart pounding like a drum"** — Simile. Her heart was beating fast and hard, like a drum being hit. (Comparison using "like")
2. **"as tall as trees"** — Simile. The runners were very tall compared to her. (Comparison using "as")
3. **"show them what you are made of"** — Idiom. It means show them how talented/strong you are. (Not literally what material you are made from.)
4. **"shot off the line like a rocket"** — Simile. She started running very fast, like a rocket launching. (Comparison using "like")
5. **"on cloud nine"** — Idiom. She was extremely happy. (Not literally on a cloud.)
6. **"piece of cake"** — Idiom. She thought it was easy. (Not literally a piece of cake.)
7. **"legs felt like jelly"** — Simile. Her legs felt weak and wobbly. (Comparison using "like")

Say: "See how the figurative language makes the writing more exciting and vivid? Instead of saying 'She ran fast,' the author says 'she shot off like a rocket.' It creates a picture in your mind!"

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice with support — identifying figurative language and explaining what it means.

**Activity 1: Simile or Idiom? (5 minutes)**

Read each sentence. The student decides if it contains a simile or an idiom, and then explains the figurative meaning.

1. "The homework was a breeze." (Idiom — it means the homework was easy)
2. "Her eyes sparkled like diamonds." (Simile — her eyes were very bright and shiny)
3. "He let the cat out of the bag about the surprise party." (Idiom — he accidentally revealed the secret)
4. "The baby's skin was as smooth as silk." (Simile — the baby's skin was very smooth)
5. "I am feeling under the weather today." (Idiom — I am feeling sick)
6. "The old house creaked like a tired giant." (Simile — the house made loud, heavy creaking sounds)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student is unsure whether something is a simile or idiom, ask: "Is it comparing two things using 'like' or 'as'?" If yes, it is a simile. "Is it a common phrase where the words mean something different from what they say?" If yes, it is likely an idiom.*

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices independently — finding figurative language and explaining it in a story context.

Have the student read the following passage independently, then complete the activities below.

> **The Music Room**
>
> When Jaylen walked into the music room for the first time, his jaw dropped. Instruments lined every wall — guitars, violins, trumpets, and drums. The room hummed with energy, as alive as a beehive.
>
> "Welcome," said Ms. Patel, the music teacher. She had a voice like honey — warm and smooth. "Today, you will each pick an instrument to try."
>
> Jaylen's classmates rushed to the popular instruments. Within seconds, every guitar and drum was taken. Jaylen stood in the corner, feeling like a fish out of water. He did not know anything about music.
>
> Then he noticed a small silver flute sitting alone on a shelf. Nobody else had picked it up. He lifted it carefully. It was as light as a feather.
>
> Ms. Patel walked over. "Ah, the flute," she said. "Most students think it is hard, but do not judge a book by its cover. Try blowing across the opening — gently."
>
> Jaylen blew softly across the flute. A clear, high note floated through the room. Everyone stopped and turned.
>
> "Beautiful," Ms. Patel whispered. "You are a natural."
>
> Jaylen held the flute to his chest. He had found his instrument. He was walking on air.

**Activity 2: Draw It Literally vs. Figuratively**

Choose one simile or idiom from the passage. On one half of a piece of paper, draw what it would look like if the words were literal (silly picture). On the other half, draw what it really means (figurative meaning).

**Activity 3: Write Your Own**

Write two original similes and one idiom in a sentence. Example: "The sunset was as orange as a pumpkin. My sister runs like a cheetah. After getting an A on the test, I was on top of the world."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete Activity 1 (find at least four pieces of figurative language). Complete Activity 2.

**Stretch:** Complete Activity 1 (find all six). Complete Activities 2 and 3.

**Challenge:** Complete all activities. Then write a short paragraph (4-5 sentences) about an everyday activity (eating breakfast, playing outside, doing homework) using at least three similes or idioms. Make the writing vivid and fun.

### Interleaved Review

4. **Context clues (Lesson 6):** The word "natural" in the last paragraph does not mean "from nature." Using context clues, what does "You are a natural" mean? What clue type helps you figure it out?
5. **Character feelings (Lesson 5):** Track Jaylen's feelings through the story. How did he feel at the beginning, in the middle, and at the end? Use text evidence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read these sentences and answer the questions:

1. "The blanket was as warm as a hug." Is this a simile or an idiom? What does it mean?
   (Simile — the blanket was comforting and warm, like being hugged)

2. "Stop beating around the bush and tell me the truth." Is this a simile or an idiom? What does it mean?
   (Idiom — stop avoiding the topic and speak directly)

3. Write your own simile to describe a loud sound: "The thunder was as loud as ___."
   (Accept any reasonable comparison: a cannon, a drum, an explosion, etc.)

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies both types (simile and idiom), explains both meanings, AND writes a reasonable simile.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on similes first (they are more transparent because of the "like/as" signal words)
- Practice with a simile matching game: give the student the first half of a simile and have them complete it
- For idioms, create a visual "idiom dictionary" with pictures of the literal and figurative meanings
- Repeat this lesson with additional examples before moving to Lesson 8

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 8 (Story Elements Review) next session
- Start an ongoing "Figurative Language Collection" — the student keeps a list of similes and idioms they encounter in reading

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Idiom Illustrated Dictionary:** The student creates a mini-booklet of idioms. Each page has the idiom, a funny picture of the literal meaning, and the real figurative meaning. This is a great ongoing project.

2. **Simile Poem:** The student writes a poem about an animal, a season, or a family member using at least five similes. Example: "My dog is as fluffy as a cloud. His bark is like a thunderclap. He runs like the wind across the yard..."

3. **Figurative Language Detective:** During everyday conversation, the student listens for similes and idioms. When they hear one, they write it down. At the end of the week, review the list together. This builds awareness that figurative language is everywhere, not just in books.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Figurative language is fun — keep it light:** This is one of the most enjoyable lessons in the unit because the gap between literal and figurative meaning is often hilarious. Lean into the humor. When a student imagines cats and dogs actually falling from the sky, that is a learning moment AND a fun moment. Laughter helps memory.

**Similes are easier than idioms for most students:** Similes are more transparent because the comparison words ("like" and "as") signal that a comparison is happening, and students can usually figure out what is being compared. Idioms are harder because the meaning is not predictable from the words. Expect students to learn similes faster and need more exposure to idioms. The idiom collection (Extension 1) is particularly valuable because idioms are learned through repeated exposure.

**Figurative language connects to context clues:** Students may notice that the strategies from Lesson 6 apply here. When they encounter figurative language, they can use the surrounding text to figure out what it means — just like they use context clues for unfamiliar vocabulary. Reinforce this connection: "You are using context clues! The words around the figurative language help you understand the author's meaning."

**Not all figurative language is similes and idioms:** In this lesson, we focus on similes and idioms because they are the most common and accessible forms of figurative language for Grade 3 students. Metaphors ("He IS a lion"), personification ("The wind whispered"), and hyperbole ("I have told you a million times") will be covered in later grades. If a student naturally identifies one of these, celebrate it and briefly explain, but do not expect mastery of these types yet.
