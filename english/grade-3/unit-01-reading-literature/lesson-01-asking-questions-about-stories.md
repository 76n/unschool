# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 1, Lesson 1

## Topic
Asking Questions About Stories

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Ask questions about a story using who, what, where, when, why, and how
- Answer questions by referring explicitly to the text (pointing to specific words and sentences)
- Distinguish between "right there" questions and "think and search" questions

## Prerequisites
- Grade 2 comprehension skills: asking and answering who/what/where/when/why/how questions
- Ability to read a grade-level passage with reasonable fluency
- Ability to retell a story using beginning, middle, and end

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Reading passages provided in this lesson (print or display so the student can point to text)
- Two colors of highlighter or colored pencil (for marking different types of evidence)
- Index cards or sticky notes (for writing questions)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about asking questions and review Grade 2 comprehension skills.

**Activity: Question Word Quick Review**

Say: "Last year in Grade 2, you learned about question words. Let's see how many you remember. I am going to say a question, and you tell me which question word it starts with."

Read each question aloud and have the student identify the question word:

1. "Where did the family go on vacation?" (Where)
2. "Why was the dog barking at the door?" (Why)
3. "Who found the lost kitten?" (Who)
4. "When did the rain finally stop?" (When)
5. "How did the girl solve the puzzle?" (How)
6. "What happened at the end of the story?" (What)

Say: "Great! You already know your question words. This year in Grade 3, we are going to take those questioning skills to the next level. We are going to learn that when we answer a question about a story, the answer needs to come FROM the story — not from our imagination. The answer is IN the book."

*Facilitator note: If the student struggles to recall the question words, write them on a card (Who, What, Where, When, Why, How) and keep it visible throughout the lesson. This is a reference tool, not a sign of trouble.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept of text evidence and the two types of questions: "right there" and "think and search."

### Key Vocabulary
- **Text evidence:** The specific words, phrases, or sentences in a story that prove your answer is correct. When someone asks "How do you know?" you point to the text evidence.
- **Refer to the text:** To go back to the story and find the exact part that supports your answer. This is what strong readers do — they do not just guess.
- **Right there question:** A question whose answer is stated directly in one place in the text. You can put your finger right on it.
- **Think and search question:** A question whose answer requires you to find information in more than one part of the text and put the pieces together.
- **Explicitly:** Clearly and directly. When we refer "explicitly" to the text, we point to the exact words.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the Big Idea (3 minutes)**

Say: "In Grade 2, you learned how to ask and answer questions about stories. You are already good at that. Now in Grade 3, we are adding something important: PROOF. When you answer a question about a story, you need to PROVE your answer by showing where the story says it."

Write this on paper where the student can see it:

**The Reader's Rule: The answer is IN the text. Show me where.**

Say: "From now on, whenever I ask you a question about a story, I am going to ask you to show me WHERE in the text you found your answer. And whenever you answer a question, you should be able to point to the words that prove it. This is called 'referring explicitly to the text.' It means pointing to the exact words."

**Step 2: Explain the Two Types of Questions (4 minutes)**

Say: "There are two kinds of questions we can ask about stories. Let me show you."

Write on paper:

**Type 1: Right There Questions**
- The answer is stated directly in ONE place in the text.
- You can put your finger right on the answer.
- Example: "What color was the dog?" — The text says "The brown dog ran across the yard." Right there!

**Type 2: Think and Search Questions**
- The answer is NOT in just one place.
- You have to find clues in DIFFERENT parts of the text and put them together.
- Example: "How did the character change during the story?" — You need to look at the beginning AND the end and compare them.

Say: "Both types of questions require you to go back to the text. The difference is that 'right there' questions have one clear spot where you find the answer, and 'think and search' questions require you to be a detective — collecting clues from different places."

### Worked Examples

**Step 3: Model with a Passage (5 minutes)**

Say: "Let me show you exactly how this works. I am going to read a short passage, and then I will answer some questions — and I will show you how I find my proof in the text."

Read this passage aloud (or have the student read it):

> **The Lost Marble**
>
> Every morning before school, Jonas played marbles on the sidewalk outside his apartment building. His favorite marble was a bright blue one with a white swirl inside. He called it "Ocean" because it looked like a tiny sea.
>
> One Tuesday, Jonas was playing too close to the storm drain. Ocean rolled off the sidewalk and disappeared through the metal grate. Jonas dropped to his knees and peered through the narrow slits, but he could not see anything in the darkness below.
>
> "No!" he whispered. He sat on the curb for a long time, staring at the drain.
>
> After school that day, Jonas brought a flashlight and a long stick with tape on the end. He shined the light through the grate and spotted something glinting on a ledge just below the opening. Carefully, he lowered the stick through the slits and pressed the tape against the marble. When he lifted it back up, Ocean was stuck to the tape, shining in the afternoon sun.
>
> Jonas held the marble tight. "I am never playing near the drain again," he said.

Say: "Now let me answer a question and show you how I find my evidence."

**Example 1 — Right There Question:**

Say: "Question: What was Jonas's favorite marble called? Let me go back to the text and find the answer..."

Point to the third sentence of paragraph 1. Say: "Here it is: 'He called it Ocean because it looked like a tiny sea.' The answer is RIGHT THERE. I can put my finger on it. Jonas's favorite marble was called Ocean."

**Example 2 — Think and Search Question:**

Say: "Question: How did Jonas get his marble back? This time I need to search through more of the text..."

Point to paragraph 4. Say: "First clue: 'Jonas brought a flashlight and a long stick with tape on the end.' Second clue: 'He lowered the stick through the slits and pressed the tape against the marble.' Third clue: 'Ocean was stuck to the tape.' I had to put these three pieces together. Jonas used a flashlight to find the marble, then used a stick with tape to pull it out. That is a 'think and search' answer — I had to combine information from several sentences."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice with support — asking questions, finding evidence, and identifying question types.

**Activity 1: Read and Find Evidence Together (6 minutes)**

Say: "Now we are going to read a new passage together. After we read, I will ask some questions, and YOUR job is to show me where in the text you found the answer."

Read this passage together (choral reading or taking turns by paragraph):

> **The Hiding Spot**
>
> Maya and her younger brother, Sam, were playing hide-and-seek in their grandmother's big backyard. It was Sam's turn to hide. Maya covered her eyes, counted to twenty, and then started looking.
>
> She checked behind the toolshed. No Sam. She looked under the porch. No Sam. She peeked inside the old wooden playhouse. Still no Sam.
>
> "Where could he be?" Maya wondered. She was about to give up when she heard a giggle coming from the big oak tree.
>
> Maya looked up. Sam was sitting on a thick branch about five feet off the ground, grinning down at her.
>
> "How did you get up there?" Maya asked, surprised.
>
> "Grandma showed me how to climb it yesterday," Sam said proudly. "She said this tree is the best hiding spot in the whole yard."
>
> Maya laughed. "Well, next time I will look UP first!"

Now ask these questions one at a time. After each question, ask: "Can you show me where the text says that?"

1. **What game were Maya and Sam playing?** (Right there — paragraph 1: "playing hide-and-seek")
   - Ask: "Is this a 'right there' question or a 'think and search' question?" (Right there — the answer is in one spot.)

2. **Where did Maya look for Sam before she found him?** (Think and search — paragraph 2: behind the toolshed, under the porch, inside the playhouse)
   - Ask: "Is this a 'right there' or 'think and search' question?" (Think and search — I had to find three different places mentioned in the text.)

3. **How did Sam learn to climb the tree?** (Right there — "Grandma showed me how to climb it yesterday")
   - Ask: "Show me the exact sentence." (Student points to Sam's dialogue in the second-to-last paragraph.)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student answers from memory without pointing to the text, say: "That is a great answer! Now show me the proof. Where does the text say that? Let's find the exact words." Be warm but consistent about requiring text evidence.*

**Activity 2: Student Asks the Questions (6 minutes)**

Say: "Now it is YOUR turn to be the question-asker. Look at the story about Maya and Sam. Can you write two questions about it — one 'right there' question and one 'think and search' question?"

Give the student index cards or paper. Have them write:
- One question where the answer is in one specific place (right there)
- One question where the answer requires looking at more than one part (think and search)

After the student writes their questions, answer them together. For each one, find the text evidence and discuss whether it is truly a "right there" or "think and search" question.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles to create questions, offer sentence starters: "Who ___?" "Why did ___?" "What happened when ___?" "How did ___ feel about ___?"*

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices independently — reading, answering questions with text evidence, and identifying question types.

Have the student read the following passage independently, then answer the questions below.

> **The Science Fair Surprise**
>
> Amara had been working on her science fair project for three weeks. She wanted to find out which type of soil helped bean plants grow the tallest. She planted three bean seeds — one in sandy soil, one in clay soil, and one in potting soil from the garden store.
>
> Every day after school, Amara measured her plants and recorded the height in a notebook. The plant in potting soil grew the fastest. By the end of the second week, it was already eight inches tall. The plant in sandy soil grew slowly, and the one in clay soil barely grew at all.
>
> On the morning of the science fair, Amara carried her three plants and her notebook to the school gymnasium. She set up her display on a long table and waited nervously for the judges.
>
> When the judges came to her table, they looked at her plants and read her notebook carefully. One judge smiled. "This is excellent record-keeping," she said. "You measured every single day. That takes real dedication."
>
> Amara did not win first place. A fifth grader with a robot project won that. But Amara did win the "Best Scientific Method" ribbon, and she felt proud. "I learned something real," she told her mom on the way home. "And I want to do it again next year."

**Questions — Answer in complete sentences and tell where you found your evidence:**

1. What was Amara's science fair project about? (Find the evidence and write the answer.) [Right there]
2. Which type of soil helped the bean plant grow the tallest? How tall was it? [Right there]
3. Why did the judge praise Amara? What exact words did the judge use? [Right there]
4. How do you know Amara was nervous at the science fair? [Right there]
5. Even though Amara did not win first place, how do we know she felt good about her project? Use evidence from more than one part of the story. [Think and search]

**Label each question:** Write "RT" for right there or "TS" for think and search next to each question number.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 in complete sentences. Point to where you found each answer. Label each question RT or TS.

**Stretch:** Answer all 5 questions in complete sentences. For each answer, copy the exact words from the text that prove your answer. Label each question RT or TS.

**Challenge:** Answer all 5 questions. Then write two original questions about the passage — one RT and one TS — and answer them with text evidence.

### Interleaved Review
After completing the questions above, answer these review items:

6. **Retelling practice (Grade 2 skill):** In 2-3 sentences, retell the story of Amara's science fair project using beginning, middle, and end.
7. **Question word review (Grade 2 skill):** What question word would you use to ask about WHERE Amara set up her display? Write the question.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this very short passage:

> Desmond ran all the way to the library after school because today was the day the new adventure book came out. When he got there, breathless and red-faced, the librarian, Mrs. Choi, smiled and held up a copy. "I saved one for you," she said.

Answer these questions and point to the text evidence:

1. Where did Desmond go after school? (the library)
2. Why did he run there? (because the new adventure book came out today)
3. How do you know Desmond ran fast? (he was "breathless and red-faced" when he got there)
4. What kind of question is #3 — right there or think and search? (Right there — the evidence is in one sentence)

**Mastery = Student answers at least 3 out of 4 correctly AND can point to text evidence for at least 2 answers.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice with very short passages (2-3 sentences) and one question at a time
- Have the student physically underline or highlight the answer in the text before stating it aloud
- Use the prompt "Show me where it says that" consistently after every answer
- Return to this lesson for an additional session before moving to Lesson 2

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 2 (Finding Evidence in the Text) next session
- Continue using "Show me where it says that" during all reading activities

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Question Detective Game:** The student reads a page from any book and writes 3 questions on index cards (without answers). The facilitator tries to answer the questions using text evidence while the student checks whether the evidence is correct.

2. **Right There vs. Think and Search Sort:** Write 6-8 questions about a familiar story on cards. The student sorts them into two piles: "right there" and "think and search." Discuss any that are tricky to categorize.

3. **Real-World Evidence:** When watching a movie or TV show together, pause and ask: "How do you know that character is [happy/scared/angry]? What evidence did you see or hear?" This extends the text evidence concept beyond reading.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**The most important habit to build:** The single most valuable thing you can do in this lesson is establish the expectation that answers must be supported with text evidence. Every time the student answers a question, ask: "How do you know? Show me where the text says that." This may feel repetitive, but repetition is exactly what builds the habit. By the end of this unit, the student should automatically look back at the text when answering questions, without being prompted.

**If the student answers from personal experience:** Third graders often bring their own experiences into their answers, which is wonderful for making connections but can lead them away from what the text actually says. When this happens, affirm the connection ("That is a great connection to your own life!") and then redirect: "Now let's see what the TEXT says. Is the answer you gave supported by the words in the story?" The goal is not to stop personal connections but to teach the student to distinguish between personal experience and text evidence.

**Right there vs. think and search is a spectrum:** Some questions fall clearly into one category, but others are on the border. Do not worry about perfect categorization. The point is to help the student understand that some answers can be found in one spot, while others require searching through the text and combining information. If the student debates the categorization of a question, that is actually excellent thinking.

**Grade 3 session length:** The methodology framework recommends 40-50 minute sessions for Grades 3-5. If your student has a shorter attention span, split the lesson across two sessions — do the warm-up, direct instruction, and guided practice in session one, and the independent practice and exit ticket in session two.
