# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 1, Lesson 3

## Topic
Describing Characters

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Describe a character using specific character traits (brave, kind, selfish, curious, etc.)
- Identify what a character says, does, thinks, and feels as evidence for character traits
- Support character descriptions with text evidence using the frame "[Character] is [trait] because [he/she] [evidence]"

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-2 (asking questions, finding and citing text evidence)
- Grade 2 skill: identifying characters in a story

## Materials Needed
- Pencil and paper
- Reading passages provided in this lesson (print or display)
- Index cards (for creating a character trait word bank)
- Crayons or colored pencils (optional, for character illustration)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about characters and review text evidence skills from Lessons 1-2.

**Activity: Who Am I?**

Say: "I am going to describe a person using clues. You tell me what kind of person they are — what word describes them."

Read each description:

1. "This person always shares their snacks, helps others carry heavy things, and writes nice notes to their friends." (Kind / Generous / Helpful)
2. "This person went into a dark cave to rescue a lost kitten, even though they were shaking with fear." (Brave / Courageous)
3. "This person always asks 'Why?' and 'How does that work?' and reads books about everything from volcanoes to outer space." (Curious)
4. "This person took the biggest piece of cake, would not let anyone play with their toys, and always wanted to go first." (Selfish / Greedy)

Say: "The words you just used — kind, brave, curious, selfish — those are called character traits. A character trait is a word that describes what a person (or character in a story) is LIKE, based on what they say and do. Today we are going to learn how to use character traits to describe the people in stories."

*Facilitator note: Accept any reasonable trait word the student offers. There is no single "right" answer — the point is that the student can match a description of behavior to a trait label. If they say "nice" instead of "kind," that is fine. You can introduce more precise vocabulary during the lesson.*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach character traits and the four sources of evidence for character descriptions (says, does, thinks, feels).

### Key Vocabulary
- **Character trait:** A word that describes what a character is LIKE — their personality. Examples: brave, kind, selfish, curious, determined, shy, honest, stubborn.
- **Character:** A person or animal in a story.
- **Personality:** The combination of traits that makes a person who they are. Every character has a personality that we can describe using trait words.
- **Evidence for character traits:** What a character says, does, thinks, and feels. These four things tell us what the character is like.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Build the Character Trait Word Bank (4 minutes)**

Say: "Let's build a word bank of character traits we can use to describe characters. I will give you some categories, and we will brainstorm words together."

Create the word bank on paper or index cards. Start with these, and add any the student suggests:

| Positive Traits | Negative Traits | Other Useful Traits |
|----------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| brave | selfish | shy |
| kind | greedy | stubborn |
| honest | mean | impatient |
| generous | lazy | proud |
| curious | dishonest | nervous |
| determined | rude | lonely |
| patient | bossy | independent |
| helpful | careless | creative |
| loyal | jealous | adventurous |

Say: "These are words we can use to describe characters. Keep this word bank nearby — we will use it throughout this unit. You can add new words whenever you find them."

*Facilitator note: Write the word bank on index cards or a sheet of paper the student can keep in their binder. They will reference it in Lessons 4, 5, 8, and 9.*

**Step 2: Four Ways to Learn About a Character (3 minutes)**

Say: "How do we figure out what a character is like? There are four things we look at."

Write on paper:

**S.D.T.F. — How to Learn About a Character:**
- **S = Says:** What does the character SAY? Their words reveal their personality.
- **D = Does:** What does the character DO? Actions speak louder than words.
- **T = Thinks:** What does the character THINK? Their inner thoughts show us who they really are.
- **F = Feels:** What does the character FEEL? Emotions tell us what matters to the character.

Say: "When I want to describe a character, I look for what they say, do, think, and feel. Then I choose a trait word that fits. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

**Step 3: Model Character Analysis (5 minutes)**

Read this passage aloud (or have the student read it):

> **The New Student**
>
> On the first day of school, a new girl named Keiko stood alone by the classroom door, holding her backpack straps tightly. She stared at the floor and did not talk to anyone.
>
> During lunch, Aiden noticed Keiko sitting by herself at the end of a long table. He picked up his tray and walked over.
>
> "Hi, I'm Aiden," he said, sitting down across from her. "Do you want to sit with me and my friends?"
>
> Keiko looked up, surprised. "Really?" she said in a quiet voice.
>
> "Of course!" said Aiden. "We are trading snacks today. Do you want to join?"
>
> Keiko smiled for the first time that day. "I would like that," she said. She followed Aiden to a table full of laughing kids, and by the end of lunch, she was laughing too.

**Model — Describing Aiden:**

Say: "Let me use S.D.T.F. to describe Aiden."

- **Says:** He said "Do you want to sit with me and my friends?" and "Of course!"
- **Does:** He picked up his tray and walked over to Keiko. He invited her to join his friends.
- **Thinks:** (Not directly stated, but his actions show his thinking.)
- **Feels:** He seems to feel empathy — he noticed Keiko was alone and wanted to help.

Say: "Based on this evidence, I would describe Aiden as KIND and INCLUSIVE. Here is my evidence sentence:"

Write: **"Aiden is kind because he noticed Keiko sitting alone and invited her to join him and his friends."**

**Model — Describing Keiko:**

Say: "Now let me describe Keiko."

- **Says:** "Really?" in a quiet voice and "I would like that."
- **Does:** Stood alone, stared at the floor, held her backpack straps tightly.
- **Feels:** Surprised when Aiden invited her. She smiled for the first time.

Write: **"Keiko is shy because she stood alone, stared at the floor, and did not talk to anyone. But she is also friendly because when Aiden invited her, she said 'I would like that' and was laughing with the group by the end of lunch."**

Say: "Notice that a character can have MORE than one trait. Keiko is shy AND friendly. People are complex, and so are characters."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice with support — describing characters using traits and evidence.

**Activity 1: S.D.T.F. Chart (6 minutes)**

Read this passage together:

> **The Shortcut**
>
> Marcus and his little sister, Jade, were walking home from school. Their usual route went along Main Street, which was safe and well-lit.
>
> "Let's take the shortcut through the woods," said Marcus, pointing to a narrow trail. "It will save us ten minutes."
>
> Jade looked at the shadowy trail. "Mom said we should always walk on Main Street," she said.
>
> "Come on, it will be fine," Marcus said, already stepping onto the trail.
>
> Jade did not move. "No," she said firmly. "Mom made a rule, and I am going to follow it. I will meet you at home."
>
> She turned and walked down Main Street, her chin held high. Marcus watched her go, then looked at the dark trail. After a moment, he jogged to catch up with her.
>
> "You are right," he said quietly. "Main Street is better."

Draw a chart on paper and fill it in together:

**Character: Jade**

| S.D.T.F. | Evidence from the Text | What It Tells Us |
|-----------|----------------------|------------------|
| Says | "Mom made a rule, and I am going to follow it." | She respects rules and her mom |
| Does | Did not move. Turned and walked down Main Street. | She stood her ground. |
| Thinks | (Implied: she thought about her mom's rule) | She considers what is right |
| Feels | Confident — "her chin held high" | She felt sure of her decision |

Ask: "What trait words describe Jade?" Guide the student to choose from the word bank: **responsible, honest, determined, brave** (brave because she stood up to her older brother).

Have the student write: "Jade is ___ because ___."

**Activity 2: Your Turn — Describe Marcus (6 minutes)**

Say: "Now describe Marcus. Use the S.D.T.F. chart."

Have the student fill in a chart for Marcus independently (with support as needed):

| S.D.T.F. | Evidence from the Text | What It Tells Us |
|-----------|----------------------|------------------|
| Says | | |
| Does | | |
| Thinks | | |
| Feels | | |

Then have the student choose traits for Marcus and write an evidence sentence.

*Facilitator prompt: Marcus is more complex — he starts out as impulsive or careless (wanting the shortcut) but shows growth (he catches up to Jade and admits she was right). Help the student see that characters can change or show more than one trait. Ask: "Was Marcus the same at the beginning and the end? What changed?"*

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices independently — describing characters with traits and text evidence.

Have the student read the following passage independently, then complete the activities below.

> **The Best Seat**
>
> Every day at lunch, Mila raced to the cafeteria to claim the seat next to the window. It was the best seat because you could see the playground and watch the birds in the old maple tree.
>
> One Tuesday, Mila was late because she had been helping her teacher clean up after art class. When she walked into the cafeteria, someone else was sitting in her seat — a boy named Oliver, who was new to the school.
>
> Mila felt her face get hot. She wanted to tell Oliver to move. That was HER seat. She had sat there every single day since September.
>
> But then she noticed something. Oliver was eating alone. He was looking out the window with a small, sad smile. He did not know anyone yet.
>
> Mila took a deep breath. She walked over and sat down in the seat across from Oliver.
>
> "Hi," she said. "I'm Mila. That is a great seat — you can see the birds from there."
>
> Oliver's face lit up. "I love birds," he said. "I have been watching a robin build a nest in that tree."
>
> "Really?" said Mila, leaning forward. "Show me!"
>
> They spent the rest of lunch talking about birds, nests, and the maple tree. Mila forgot all about being upset. She had found something better than the best seat — a new friend.

**Activity 1: S.D.T.F. Chart for Mila**

Fill in the chart:

| S.D.T.F. | Evidence from the Text | What It Tells Us |
|-----------|----------------------|------------------|
| Says | | |
| Does | | |
| Thinks | | |
| Feels | | |

**Activity 2: Character Trait Sentences**

Write two sentences describing Mila using different character traits. Use the frame: "Mila is [trait] because [evidence from the text]."

**Activity 3: Describe Oliver**

Write one character trait sentence about Oliver. Use evidence from the text.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete the S.D.T.F. chart for Mila (Activity 1) and write one trait sentence for Mila (Activity 2). Describe Oliver orally.

**Stretch:** Complete all three activities in writing. For Activity 2, write two different trait sentences for Mila.

**Challenge:** Complete all three activities. Then write a short paragraph (3-4 sentences) describing how Mila's feelings CHANGED during the story. Use at least two pieces of text evidence.

### Interleaved Review

4. **Text evidence practice (Lessons 1-2):** Find the sentence in the story that tells us why Mila was late to lunch. Copy it exactly.
5. **Question types (Lesson 1):** Is this question "right there" or "think and search"? — "Is Mila a kind person?" Explain your answer.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short passage:

> Ben saw a small kitten stuck on a tree branch. The kitten was meowing loudly. Ben's heart pounded, but he climbed up the tree anyway. He gently picked up the kitten and climbed back down.

1. Name one character trait that describes Ben. (brave, caring, kind, helpful — accept any reasonable trait)
2. What evidence from the text supports that trait? (He climbed the tree even though his heart pounded / He gently picked up the kitten)
3. Write a complete sentence: "Ben is ___ because the text says ___."

**Mastery = Student names an appropriate trait AND supports it with specific text evidence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the S.D.T.F. chart and practice with the model passage (Aiden and Keiko)
- Focus on "Does" evidence first, since actions are the most concrete
- Provide the trait and ask the student to find the evidence (instead of asking them to generate both)
- Practice with additional short passages before moving to Lesson 4

### If Mastery Is Met
- Move to Lesson 4 (Character Actions and Events) next session
- Encourage the student to notice character traits in everyday reading

---

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Character Portrait:** The student draws a picture of Mila (or any character from this lesson) and writes 3-4 trait words around the drawing. Next to each trait, they write a piece of evidence from the story.

2. **Trait Charades:** Write character traits on index cards. Take turns acting out a trait (without saying the word) while the other person guesses. This builds understanding of what traits look like in action.

3. **Real-Life Traits:** The student describes a family member or friend using 2-3 character traits, supported by "evidence" (specific things the person says or does). This connects literary analysis to real-world observation.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**The word bank is essential:** Third graders often have a limited vocabulary for describing personality. Without the word bank, they tend to use vague words like "nice," "mean," or "good." The word bank gives them precise language. Keep it visible during this lesson and all subsequent character analysis lessons. Add new words whenever the student encounters them in reading.

**Trait vs. action is a key distinction:** Students often confuse what a character DOES with what a character IS LIKE. If a student says "Mila is sitting down across from Oliver," that is an action, not a trait. Help them bridge the gap: "Yes, she sits across from Oliver. What does that ACTION tell us about what Mila is LIKE?" (She is friendly, kind, inclusive.) The action is the evidence; the trait is the conclusion.

**Characters can have multiple traits:** Resist the temptation to find "the one right trait" for a character. Mila is kind AND generous AND brave (it took courage to approach Oliver instead of being upset). Oliver might be shy AND passionate about birds. Encourage the student to see characters as multidimensional, just like real people.

**Characters can also change:** The passage about Mila is deliberately designed to show a character whose feelings change. She starts angry and possessive ("That was HER seat") and ends happy and open ("She had found something better than the best seat — a new friend"). Noticing character change is a higher-order skill that prepares students for Lesson 5 (character feelings and motivations) and later work on character development.

|-----------|----------|------------------|

Traits: At first — impulsive, careless. At the end — honest (admits he was wrong), open-minded. Sample sentence: "Marcus is honest because he told Jade 'You are right' after he realized the shortcut was not a good idea."
