## Administration Instructions

This assessment uses two original informational passages. The student reads both passages and then answers questions organized by skill area.

**Before beginning, say:** *"Today you are going to read two passages and answer questions about them. You have learned all the skills you need for this in our lessons. Take your time, and remember — when a question asks about the text, go back to the passage and find the answer. Use your highlighter if it helps. Do your best!"*

**Reading the passages:**
- The student should read each passage independently. If the student's reading fluency is significantly below grade level, you may read the passages aloud. Note this on the observation form.
- The student may refer back to the passages at any time during the assessment.
- Allow the student to use a highlighter to mark text features, main ideas, evidence, and signal words.

**Answering questions:**
- Questions may be answered in writing or orally, depending on the student's writing stamina. If answered orally, the facilitator records the student's responses.
- For written responses, accept phonetic spelling — focus on comprehension, not conventions.
- There is no time limit on individual items. If the student is stuck on a question for more than 2 minutes, say: *"Let's come back to that one. Try the next question."*

**Life Inside the Hive**

A honeybee hive is one of the most organized communities in the animal world. Up to 60,000 bees can live in a single hive, and every bee has a specific role. The hive itself is made of **beeswax**, a waxy material that the bees produce from their own bodies. Inside the hive, bees build rows of six-sided cells called a **honeycomb**. These tiny cells are used to store honey, hold pollen, and raise baby bees. The temperature inside the hive stays at about 95 degrees Fahrenheit all year long — bees fan their wings to cool the hive in summer and cluster together to warm it in winter.

*Caption: Inside a honeybee hive, different areas of the honeycomb serve different purposes.*

---

**The Jobs of a Honeybee**

There are three types of bees in a hive: the **queen**, the **drones**, and the **worker bees**. The queen is the largest bee and the only one that lays eggs — she can lay up to 2,000 eggs in a single day. Drones are male bees whose only job is to mate with the queen. Worker bees are all female, and they do almost everything else.

A worker bee's job changes as she gets older. **First**, a young worker bee cleans the cells inside the hive. **Then**, she feeds the baby bees (called **larvae**). **Next**, she begins building new honeycomb cells from beeswax. **After** about three weeks, she becomes a forager — flying out of the hive to collect nectar and pollen from flowers. A worker bee lives for only about six weeks during the busy summer season.

---

**How Bees Make Honey**

Honey is made through a process that involves teamwork between many bees. **First**, forager bees visit flowers and use their long tongues to suck up **nectar**, a sweet liquid inside the flower. The nectar is stored in a special "honey stomach" inside the bee. **Then**, the forager flies back to the hive and passes the nectar to a house bee by mouth. **Next**, the house bees chew the nectar for about 30 minutes, mixing it with **enzymes** that begin breaking down the sugars. **After** that, the bees spread the nectar into honeycomb cells and fan it with their wings to evaporate the water. **Finally**, when the honey is thick enough, the bees seal the cell with a wax cap. A single bee produces only about one-twelfth of a teaspoon of honey in its entire lifetime. **Because** it takes so many bees to make even a small amount, a hive of 60,000 bees working together can produce 60 to 100 pounds of honey per year.

---

**Bees and Flowers: A Partnership**

Bees and flowers help each other in an important way. **When** a bee lands on a flower to drink nectar, tiny grains of **pollen** stick to the bee's fuzzy body. **As** the bee flies to the next flower, some of the pollen falls off onto that flower. This transfer of pollen — called **pollination** — is what allows flowers to make seeds and grow new plants. Without bees, many of the fruits and vegetables we eat every day could not grow. **Scientists estimate** that bees are responsible for pollinating about 75% of the fruits, nuts, and vegetables grown in the United States. **Both** bees and flowers benefit from this partnership — bees get the food they need (nectar and pollen), and flowers get the pollination they need to reproduce.

**Glossary**

- **Beeswax** — A waxy substance produced by honeybees, used to build honeycomb
- **Drones** — Male honeybees whose job is to mate with the queen
- **Enzymes** — Natural chemicals that help break down or change substances
- **Honeycomb** — A structure of six-sided wax cells built by bees to store honey and raise young
- **Larvae** — Baby bees in the early stage of development, before they become adult bees
- **Nectar** — A sweet liquid produced by flowers, collected by bees to make honey
- **Pollen** — Tiny grains produced by flowers that are needed for plant reproduction
- **Pollination** — The transfer of pollen from one flower to another, allowing the flower to make seeds
- **Queen** — The largest bee in the hive; the only one that lays eggs
- **Worker bees** — Female bees that do most of the work in the hive (cleaning, feeding, building, foraging)

## Assessment Passage 2: "Why We Should Protect Honeybees"

Honeybees are in trouble, and **everyone should care about protecting them**. Over the past 15 years, beekeepers have reported losing about 30% of their bee colonies each year. Scientists call this **Colony Collapse Disorder**, and it is a serious problem.

**One reason** we should protect bees is that they pollinate the food we eat. **According to scientists**, without bees, we would lose many of the fruits and vegetables in our grocery stores — including apples, almonds, blueberries, and cucumbers. **Because** bees pollinate so many crops, losing them would make food more expensive and harder to find.

**Another reason** is that bees support the entire ecosystem. Many wild plants depend on bees for pollination. **If** those plants disappear, the animals that eat them would also be affected. The loss of bees could cause a chain reaction that harms many other species.

**There are things we can do to help.** Planting bee-friendly flowers in gardens gives bees more places to find food. Avoiding pesticides — chemicals used to kill insects — protects bees from being poisoned. Supporting local beekeepers by buying local honey helps keep bee populations healthy. Bee stingers are barbed and get stuck in skin. **By taking these simple steps**, we can all help protect the honeybees that do so much for our world.

## Section-Specific Reteaching Guide

| If the student struggles in... | What it tells you | Reteach with... |
|-------------------------------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Section A (Text Features)** | Student cannot identify or use nonfiction navigation tools | Return to Lesson 2. Do a "text feature walk" before every reading. Use physical nonfiction books and have the student label features with sticky notes. Practice purpose-driven searches: "Use the glossary to find X. Use the chart to find Y." Focus on USING features, not just naming them. |
| **Section B (Main Idea & Details)** | Cannot determine main idea or distinguish supporting from tangential details | Return to Lessons 3-4. Use the three-step strategy (topic, what the author repeats, one-sentence summary). Practice with very short paragraphs where the main idea is clearly the first sentence. Use the umbrella analogy — physically sort details into "under the umbrella" (supporting) and "outside" (tangential). Build up to multi-paragraph texts gradually. |
| **Section C (Relationships)** | Cannot identify sequence, cause-effect, or text structure | Return to Lessons 6-7. Start with everyday cause-effect examples before returning to text ("You fell because the floor was wet"). Use the signal words anchor chart actively — have the student highlight signal words BEFORE identifying the structure. Practice one structure at a time before mixing them. |
| **Section D (Evidence / Evaluation)** | Cannot cite evidence or identify the author's point and reasons | Return to Lessons 8-9. Practice the "go back and find it" strategy with very short passages. Provide sentence starter cards as a physical reminder. For author's point, start with oral arguments: "I think you should eat breakfast because..." and have the student identify the point and reason. Build up to written text gradually. |

### Section C: Relationships Between Events/Concepts

**C1.** Steps of honey-making in order (2 points — 0.5 per correct step):
1. Forager bees visit flowers and suck up nectar with their tongues.
2. Forager flies back to the hive and passes nectar to a house bee.
3. House bees chew the nectar for about 30 minutes, mixing it with enzymes.
4. Bees spread the nectar into honeycomb cells and fan it to evaporate water.
5. When thick enough, bees seal the cell with a wax cap.

(Accept 4 or 5 steps. Award 0.5 per correctly ordered step up to 2 points.)

**C2.** Cause-effect relationship (1 point). Accept any of:
- "A hive can produce 60-100 pounds of honey per year BECAUSE it takes so many bees working together (each bee makes only 1/12 teaspoon)."
- "The honey becomes thick enough to seal BECAUSE the bees fan it with their wings to evaporate the water."
- "Bees produce a lot of honey BECAUSE 60,000 bees work together."

**C3.** Best answer: **(b) Cause and Effect** — the bee landing on the flower CAUSES pollen to stick; pollen transferring to the next flower CAUSES pollination; pollination CAUSES seeds to grow. (Also accept comparison/contrast for the "both bees and flowers benefit" element, with explanation.) Explanation should mention that the section shows how one action (bee visiting flower) causes a chain of results (pollen transfer, plant reproduction). (1 point)

**C4.** Signal word: "Both." (1 point)

**C5.** FIRST (when young): A young worker bee cleans the cells inside the hive. LAST (when older): After about three weeks, she becomes a forager, flying out of the hive to collect nectar and pollen. (1 point — must include both first and last with evidence.)

### Section D: Using Evidence / Evaluating Reasons

**D1.** Author's point: Everyone should care about protecting honeybees. / We should protect honeybees. (Accept any version that captures the persuasive point.) (1 point)

**D2.** Two reasons (2 points — 1 per reason):
- Reason 1: Bees pollinate the food we eat — without them, we would lose many fruits and vegetables, and food would become more expensive and harder to find.
- Reason 2: Bees support the entire ecosystem — wild plants depend on bees for pollination, and without those plants, animals that eat them would be affected (chain reaction).
(Also accept: Beekeepers lose 30% of colonies yearly / Colony Collapse Disorder is a serious problem — though these are more "evidence of the problem" than "reasons to protect.")

**D3.** "According to the text, beekeepers lose about 30% of their bee colonies each year." (Or: "The text states that beekeepers have reported losing about 30% of their bee colonies each year over the past 15 years.") Must include a sentence starter. (1 point)

**D4.** Off-topic sentence: "Bee stingers are barbed and get stuck in skin."
Why it does not support the point: This fact is about bee stingers, not about why we should protect bees or how to help them. It does not explain why bees are important or what we can do to save them. (1 point — 0.5 for finding the sentence, 0.5 for explaining why)

**D5.** Accept any thoughtful evaluation. Strong answer example: "Yes, this reason is very convincing because 75% is a huge number — it means that three out of every four fruits, nuts, and vegetables depend on bees. When you hear a specific number like that, it makes you realize how much we need bees. Without them, most of our food would disappear. This is more convincing than just saying 'bees help flowers grow' because the percentage shows exactly how important bees are." (1 point)

Acceptable answer: "Yes, it is convincing because 75% is a lot" or "Yes, because without bees we would not have most of our food." (1 point — award if the student engages with the reason and provides any logical explanation.)
