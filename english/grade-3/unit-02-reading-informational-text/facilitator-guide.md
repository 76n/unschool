# Facilitator Guide — Grade 3 ELA, Unit 2: Reading Informational Text

**Grade:** 3
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit:** 2 — Reading Informational Text
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4 weeks at 2-3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Partially facilitated (adult introduces the lesson and supports guided practice; student works more independently during warm-up and independent practice)

---

## Unit Overview

In Unit 1, your student explored reading literature — asking and answering questions with explicit text reference, describing characters and their contributions to story events, and determining word meanings in context. This unit shifts the lens from fiction to nonfiction. Students learn to read informational text with purpose and precision: navigating text features, determining main ideas and supporting details, describing relationships between events and concepts, and beginning to evaluate an author's reasons and evidence.

By the end of this unit, your student will be able to:

- Distinguish informational text from fiction and explain the purpose of informational writing
- Identify and use text features (headings, subheadings, table of contents, glossary, index, photographs with captions, diagrams, maps, and charts) to locate information efficiently
- Determine the main idea of an informational passage and identify the supporting details that develop it
- Distinguish between the topic, the main idea, and individual details
- Describe relationships between events, procedures, or concepts in informational text (cause-effect, sequence, comparison, problem-solution)
- Recognize signal words that indicate text structure
- Answer questions about informational text by citing explicit evidence from the text
- Identify an author's main point or claim and the reasons the author provides to support it
- Begin to evaluate whether an author's reasons are convincing

**Why this matters:** Third grade is a pivotal year in the shift from "learning to read" to "reading to learn." Informational text is the primary vehicle for learning in science, social studies, and beyond. A student who can navigate text features, extract main ideas, trace relationships, and evaluate evidence is equipped for independent learning across every subject. These skills also build the critical thinking habits that grow into formal argumentation and analysis in later grades. The reading standards addressed in this unit — RI.3.2, RI.3.3, RI.3.5, and RI.3.8 — form the backbone of nonfiction comprehension for the upper elementary years.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, the student should be able to:

- [ ] Read grade-level text fluently with accuracy and appropriate expression
- [ ] Ask and answer questions about a text, referring to the text for answers (Unit 1 literature skills)
- [ ] Identify basic informational text features from Grade 2 (headings, bold words, captions, table of contents, glossary)
- [ ] Identify the main topic and key details in a short informational passage (Grade 2 skill)
- [ ] Describe how reasons support points an author makes (Grade 2, RI.2.8)
- [ ] Write complete sentences with correct capitalization and end punctuation

**If prerequisite gaps exist:**
- If the student struggles with basic text features, spend an extra session on Lesson 2 using hands-on exploration of nonfiction books before moving to main idea work.
- If the student cannot distinguish fiction from nonfiction, begin Lesson 1 with additional sorting activities (3-4 book pairs) before using the lesson passage.
- If reading fluency is below grade level, read passages aloud to the student during direct instruction and guided practice, then have the student attempt independent reading with support. The comprehension skills in this unit can develop even while fluency is still strengthening.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Printed copies of lesson passages** — Each lesson includes original informational passages. Print or display these so the student can mark them up, highlight, and refer back during activities.
- **Highlighters or colored pencils** (at least 4 colors) — Used for color-coding text features, main ideas, supporting details, and signal words throughout the unit
- **Sticky notes** (small and medium sizes) — For marking text, annotating passages, and building graphic organizers
- **Lined paper and pencils** for written responses
- **Blank paper** for creating graphic organizers (main idea webs, T-charts, cause-effect diagrams)
- **A folder or notebook** to collect completed work across the unit
- **Optional: 3-5 grade-appropriate nonfiction books, magazines, or articles** — National Geographic Kids, Scholastic News, DK Readers Level 3, or "all about" nonfiction books about animals, weather, space, or community. These supplement the passages provided in lessons.
- **Optional: Whiteboard or large paper** for building anchor charts together

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Recommended Sessions | Notes |
|--------|-------|---------------------|-------|
| 1 | What Is Informational Text? | 1 session | Foundation-setting; lighter content load |
| 2 | Text Features Deep Dive | 1-2 sessions | Many features to cover; split if needed |
| 3 | Main Idea | 1-2 sessions | Critical concept; take time here |
| 4 | Supporting Details | 1 session | Builds directly on Lesson 3 |
| 5 | Main Idea & Details Practice | 1-2 sessions | Extended practice with longer passages |
| 6 | Relationships Between Events | 1-2 sessions | New text structures; may need extra time |
| 7 | Relationships Between Concepts | 1-2 sessions | Builds on Lesson 6 structures |
| 8 | Using Evidence to Answer Questions | 1 session | Applies all prior skills |
| 9 | Evaluating Reasons and Evidence | 1-2 sessions | Most advanced skill in the unit |
| 10 | Cumulative Review | 1 session | Review and synthesis |
| -- | Unit Assessment | Separate session (40-50 minutes) | |

**Total: 10-16 sessions across approximately 4 weeks**

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lessons 3-5 (main idea and supporting details) are the heart of this unit. If you need to slow down anywhere, slow down here. A student who deeply understands main idea and supporting details will find Lessons 6-9 much more manageable.
- Lessons 6-7 (relationships and text structures) introduce new vocabulary and thinking patterns. If the student seems overwhelmed by the number of text structures in Lesson 7, split it across two sessions — cover comparison/contrast in one session and problem/solution in another.
- Lesson 9 (evaluating reasons and evidence) is the most cognitively demanding lesson. The student does not need to master formal argument evaluation — the goal is to begin recognizing that authors make points and support them with reasons, and that some reasons are stronger than others.
- Each lesson is designed for a 40-50 minute session (appropriate for the 3-5 grade band). If sessions run shorter, extend the unit timeline accordingly.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student confuses the **topic** with the **main idea** — says "this is about dolphins" when asked for the main idea | The student can identify the subject but not the author's most important point about that subject | Use the frame: "Yes, this is about dolphins — that is the topic. But what is the MOST IMPORTANT THING the author wants you to know about dolphins?" Practice with short paragraphs where the topic is the same but the main idea differs. |
| Student lists **every detail** as a "supporting detail" rather than identifying which details actually support the main idea | The student does not yet distinguish between key supporting details and interesting-but-tangential information | Use the "umbrella" analogy: the main idea is the umbrella, and supporting details are what fits under it. If a detail does not hold up the main idea, it is extra information, not a supporting detail. Practice sorting details into "supports the main idea" and "interesting but not key." |
| Student reads informational text **straight through** without using headings, captions, or other text features | The student is applying fiction reading habits (linear, start-to-finish) to nonfiction | Before each reading, do a "text feature walk": preview the entire passage by looking at headings, pictures, captions, bold words, and diagrams. Ask: "Based on these features, what do you think this text will be about? What sections does it have?" Build the habit of previewing before reading. |
| Student **struggles to find evidence** in the text and instead answers from personal knowledge or opinion | The student understands the content but has not developed the habit of returning to the text | Model the "go back and find it" strategy. Say: "That might be true, but can you show me WHERE in the text it says that? Let's look together." Use a finger or highlighter to track evidence in the passage. Practice the phrases "According to the text..." and "The text states that..." |
| Student says **everything is "cause and effect"** even when the text uses a different structure (like comparison or description) | The student has learned one text structure and is over-applying it | Teach signal words as clues. Create an anchor chart: "because, so, as a result = cause and effect" vs. "like, unlike, both, different = compare and contrast" vs. "first, then, next, finally = sequence." Practice identifying signal words before naming the structure. |
| Student confuses the **author's point** with the **topic** or a **detail** when asked to identify what the author is arguing | Identifying claims is a new and abstract skill for third graders | Start with everyday examples: "I think recess should be longer because kids need exercise." What is my point? (Recess should be longer.) What is my reason? (Kids need exercise.) Practice with oral arguments before moving to written text. |
| Student **skips diagrams, charts, or maps** and only reads the prose portions of the text | The student treats visual elements as decoration rather than information sources | Explicitly teach that diagrams, charts, and maps contain information you cannot get from the words alone. Ask: "What does this diagram tell you that the sentences do not?" Make visual elements a required part of reading. |
| Student can identify text features **by name** but cannot use them to **locate specific information** | Recognition is developing, but functional use has not yet clicked | Give purpose-driven tasks: "Use the table of contents to find the section about habitats. Use the glossary to find the meaning of 'migrate.' Use the diagram to find how many legs an insect has." Make text features tools, not just labels. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these during lessons or throughout the day to reinforce informational text skills:

- "We just read that article — what was the most important thing the author wanted us to know?"
- "That's an interesting detail — but is it the MAIN idea, or is it a detail that supports the main idea?"
- "Let's look at this heading. What do you predict this section will be about?"
- "Can you find a bold word in this text? What do you think it means? Let's check the glossary."
- "Why do you think the author included this diagram? What information does it give us?"
- "What happened first? What happened next? What caused that to happen?"
- "The author says ___. What reason does the author give for that? Do you think that's a good reason?"
- "If someone asked you a question about this text, where would you look to find the answer?"
- "How is this text organized? Is the author comparing things, explaining causes, or describing a sequence?"
- "You said ___. Can you show me where in the text it says that?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

These signs may indicate the student needs more targeted support:

- The student can decode and read aloud fluently but consistently cannot recall or summarize what was just read, even with short passages (comprehension gap that may need specialized assessment)
- After explicit instruction and multiple practice opportunities, the student cannot distinguish between fiction and nonfiction text
- The student cannot identify the topic of a passage even after reading it twice — struggles go beyond main idea to basic comprehension
- The student shows significant frustration, avoidance, or anxiety around reading nonfiction text despite encouragement and scaffolding
- After Lessons 3-5, the student cannot identify a main idea even in a single, clearly stated paragraph with facilitator support
- The student cannot produce any written response about a text, even with sentence starters and graphic organizers

If you see these signs, consider:
- Reducing text complexity — use shorter passages with simpler vocabulary so the student can focus on comprehension strategies without decoding barriers
- Separating skills — work on text features separately from main idea, and main idea separately from text structure, rather than layering multiple skills at once
- Increasing oral discussion — have the student talk through answers before writing them, removing the writing barrier
- Providing audio support — read passages aloud so the student can focus on comprehension rather than decoding
- Consulting a reading specialist if comprehension difficulties persist across multiple sessions with reduced complexity

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 3: Writing**, where they write opinion pieces with organized reasons, informative texts with facts and definitions grouped logically, narratives with dialogue and sequence, and conduct short research projects. The informational text comprehension skills from this unit transfer directly to writing — a student who can identify main ideas, supporting details, and text structures in others' writing is better equipped to use those same structures in their own.

Between units, continue reading both fiction and informational text regularly. Encourage the student to apply the skills from this unit whenever they encounter nonfiction — previewing text features, identifying main ideas, noticing how the text is structured, and looking for evidence. The more natural these habits become, the more prepared the student will be for the increasingly complex informational text they will encounter in Grades 4 and 5.
