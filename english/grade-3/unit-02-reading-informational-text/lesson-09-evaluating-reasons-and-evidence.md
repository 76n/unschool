# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Evaluating Reasons and Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the author's main point or claim in an informational text
- Find the reasons the author gives to support their point
- Begin to evaluate whether the author's reasons are convincing by asking "Does this reason make sense?" and "Does it support the point?"

## Prerequisites
- Ability to find and cite evidence in informational text (Lesson 8)
- Ability to determine main idea and supporting details (Lessons 3-5)
- Understanding of cause-and-effect and text structures (Lessons 6-7)
- Familiarity with the concept that informational text is written to teach or explain (Lesson 1)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "Should Every School Have a Garden?" (included below)
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "Why Helmets Matter" (included below)
- Point-and-Reasons graphic organizer (one circle at top for the author's point, 3-4 boxes below for reasons, connected by arrows)
- Paper and pencil
- Highlighters (2 colors — one for the author's point, one for reasons)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review evidence skills from Lesson 8 and introduce the idea that some texts try to convince the reader of a point.

**Activity: Opinion or Information?**

Say to the student:

*"I am going to read you three sentences. Tell me if each one is sharing a fact or trying to convince you of something."*

1. "Dogs have four legs and a tail." (Sharing a fact — just information.)
2. "Everyone should adopt a dog because dogs make life better." (Trying to convince you — the author has a point and gives a reason.)
3. "The Earth is the third planet from the sun." (Sharing a fact.)

Say: *"Sentences 1 and 3 are just sharing information. But sentence 2 is different — the author is making a POINT ('everyone should adopt a dog') and giving a REASON ('because dogs make life better'). Today we are going to learn how to find the author's point and the reasons they give, and we will start thinking about whether those reasons are actually convincing."*

**Spaced Review — Sentence Starters (Lesson 8):**

*"Quick review: Give me two sentence starters you can use to show that your answer comes from the text."* (According to the text..., The text states that..., The author explains that..., In the passage it says...)

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify an author's point/claim, find the supporting reasons, and begin evaluating whether the reasons are convincing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Author's point** — The main argument or claim the author is making — what the author is trying to convince the reader to believe or understand
- **Reasons** — The explanations the author gives for WHY their point is true or WHY the reader should agree
- **Convincing** — Makes you believe something is true or makes a strong argument
- **Evaluate** — To think carefully about something and decide if it is strong, weak, good, or bad

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: What is an author's point? (3-4 minutes)**

Say: *"You already know that informational text teaches and explains. But sometimes, informational text does something more — it tries to CONVINCE you. The author has a point — an argument or claim — and uses reasons to support that point."*

*"An author's point is different from the topic and different from the main idea, although they are related. The topic is WHAT the text is about. The main idea is the MOST IMPORTANT THING the author says. The author's point is what the author is trying to CONVINCE you to believe or understand."*

Write on the board:

**Topic:** School gardens
**Main Idea:** School gardens help students learn and grow.
**Author's Point:** Every school should have a garden.

Say: *"See the difference? The point goes further — it takes a position. The author is not just sharing information; they are making an argument."*

**Step 2: Finding reasons (3-4 minutes)**

Say: *"Once you find the author's point, you need to find the REASONS the author gives to support it. Reasons answer the question 'WHY should I believe this?' or 'WHY is this true?'"*

Write on the board:

**Author's Point:** Every school should have a garden.
**Reasons:**
- Students learn about science by growing plants.
- Gardening teaches responsibility.
- School gardens provide fresh, healthy food.

Say: *"Each reason supports the point. They give evidence for WHY the author thinks every school should have a garden. The reasons are like the supporting details, but here they are supporting an ARGUMENT, not just a main idea."*

**Step 3: Beginning to evaluate (2-3 minutes)**

Say: *"Here is where it gets really interesting. Once you find the author's point and reasons, you can start to EVALUATE them. That means you think carefully and ask yourself:"*

Write on the board:

**Questions to Ask When Evaluating:**
1. Does this reason make sense?
2. Does this reason actually support the point?
3. Would this reason convince me? Why or why not?

Say: *"You are not just accepting everything the author says — you are thinking for yourself. A strong reason makes sense and clearly supports the point. A weak reason might be off-topic, not make sense, or not really prove anything."*

Give a quick example:

**Author's Point:** Everyone should drink water.
- **Strong reason:** "Water keeps your body hydrated and helps your organs work properly." (Makes sense, supports the point.)
- **Weak reason:** "Water is clear and does not have a color." (True, but it does not explain WHY you should drink water.)

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Finding the author's point and reasons**

Display this paragraph:

> Recess is important for students and should not be cut from the school day. When students play during recess, they get exercise that keeps their bodies healthy. Recess also gives students' brains a break from learning, which helps them focus better when they return to the classroom. Studies have shown that students who have recess are more creative and better at solving problems than students who do not have a break during the day.

Say: *"Step 1: What is the author's point — what is the author trying to convince me? The first sentence tells me: 'Recess is important and should not be cut from the school day.' That is the point."*

*"Step 2: What reasons does the author give?"*

Fill in the organizer:
- **Author's Point:** Recess is important and should not be cut.
- **Reason 1:** Exercise keeps students' bodies healthy.
- **Reason 2:** A brain break helps students focus better afterward.
- **Reason 3:** Students with recess are more creative and better at problem-solving.

*"Step 3: Are these reasons convincing? Let me evaluate. Reason 1 — exercise keeping bodies healthy makes sense and supports the point. Reason 2 — brain breaks helping focus makes sense and supports the point. Reason 3 — studies show creativity and problem-solving improve. That is very convincing because it is based on research, not just opinion. I think these are strong reasons."*

**Worked Example 2: Spotting a weak reason**

Display this paragraph:

> Students should be allowed to bring their pets to school. Pets make people happy, so students would be happier at school. The school hallways are very long, which means there would be plenty of room. Also, students could learn about animals by spending time with them during the day.

Say: *"The author's point is: students should bring pets to school. Let me find the reasons."*

- Reason 1: Pets make people happy, so students would be happier. (Makes sense, supports the point.)
- Reason 2: The hallways are very long with plenty of room. (Hmm — does having long hallways really support WHY students should bring pets? Not really. This is about the building, not about why pets would be good for students.)
- Reason 3: Students could learn about animals. (Makes sense, supports the point.)

Say: *"Reason 2 is a WEAK reason. It is true that hallways are long, but that does not explain WHY pets would be good for students. It does not support the point. Reasons 1 and 3 are stronger because they actually explain WHY bringing pets is a good idea."*

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student practices identifying the author's point, finding reasons, and evaluating those reasons with facilitator support.

**Activity: Point-and-Reasons Detective — "Should Every School Have a Garden?" and "Why Helmets Matter"**

---

### Reading Passage 1: "Should Every School Have a Garden?"

School gardens are growing in popularity, and for good reason — every school should have one. **First**, gardens teach students about science in a hands-on way. Students learn about plant life cycles, soil, insects, and weather by working directly with nature instead of just reading about it in a textbook. **Second**, caring for a garden teaches responsibility. Students must water the plants, pull weeds, and check on their garden regularly, or the plants will not survive. **Third**, school gardens provide fresh fruits and vegetables that students can eat, which promotes healthy eating habits. Many students try vegetables for the first time when they grow them themselves. Garden tools come in many different colors and sizes. **Finally**, working in a garden together helps students build teamwork skills. They learn to cooperate, share tasks, and solve problems as a group.

---

### Reading Passage 2: "Why Helmets Matter"

Everyone who rides a bicycle should wear a helmet. Bicycle accidents cause thousands of head injuries every year, and **research shows** that wearing a helmet reduces the risk of a serious head injury by up to 85%. Helmets work by absorbing the impact when a rider falls or crashes, protecting the brain from damage. **In addition**, many states and cities have laws requiring children to wear helmets when cycling, which shows that safety experts and lawmakers agree that helmets are important. Bicycles can be red, blue, green, or many other colors. Some people say helmets are uncomfortable, **but** modern helmets are lightweight and well-ventilated, so they are much more comfortable than helmets used to be. **The evidence is clear**: wearing a helmet while riding a bicycle is one of the simplest ways to prevent a life-changing injury.

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

**Passage 1: "Should Every School Have a Garden?"**

Say: *"Read this passage. First, find the author's point — what is the author trying to convince you of?"*

Guide: Every school should have a garden.

Say: *"Now find the reasons the author gives. Highlight each one."*

Work together to fill in the organizer:
- **Author's Point:** Every school should have a garden.
- **Reason 1:** Gardens teach science in a hands-on way.
- **Reason 2:** Caring for a garden teaches responsibility.
- **Reason 3:** Gardens provide fresh fruits and vegetables / promote healthy eating.
- **Reason 4:** Working in a garden builds teamwork skills.

Say: *"Did you notice any sentence that does NOT support the author's point?"*

Guide: "Garden tools come in many different colors and sizes." This is true but does not support the point about why schools should have gardens. It is a tangential detail — like the sneaky details we found in Lesson 4.

Say: *"Now let's evaluate. Are these reasons convincing? For each reason, ask: Does it make sense? Does it support the point?"*

Guide through evaluation:
- Reason 1 (hands-on science): Convincing — learning by doing is better than just reading, which supports the idea that gardens are good for schools.
- Reason 2 (responsibility): Convincing — taking care of plants is a real responsibility.
- Reason 3 (healthy eating): Convincing — fresh food is good for students, and trying new vegetables matters.
- Reason 4 (teamwork): Convincing — working together on a shared project builds cooperation.

Say: *"Overall, are you convinced? Why or why not?"*

Allow the student to respond. There is no wrong answer here — the goal is to practice THINKING about the reasons.

**Passage 2: "Why Helmets Matter" — More independent**

Say: *"Now try this passage with less help. Find the author's point, the reasons, and any weak or off-topic detail. Then evaluate the reasons."*

Let the student work, checking in as needed.

Guide toward:
- **Author's Point:** Everyone who rides a bicycle should wear a helmet.
- **Reason 1:** Helmets reduce serious head injury risk by up to 85% (based on research).
- **Reason 2:** Helmets absorb impact and protect the brain.
- **Reason 3:** Laws require children to wear helmets — experts and lawmakers agree they are important.
- **Reason 4:** Modern helmets are comfortable (addresses the "uncomfortable" objection).
- **Off-topic detail:** "Bicycles can be red, blue, green, or many other colors."

Say: *"Which reason do you think is the STRONGEST? Why?"*

Guide: Reason 1 is very strong because it is based on research — a specific number (85%) from studies. This is more convincing than just saying "helmets are good."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student independently identifies an author's point, finds reasons, and evaluates their strength.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**

Read this passage and answer the questions below.

> **Why Recycling Matters**
>
> Everyone should recycle. Recycling reduces the amount of trash that goes to landfills, which are filling up all over the country. When we recycle paper, we save trees — it takes about 17 trees to make one ton of paper, so recycling that paper means those trees can keep growing. Recycling bins are usually blue. Recycling aluminum cans saves energy — making a new can from recycled aluminum uses 95% less energy than making one from scratch. Recycling also reduces pollution because factories that use recycled materials produce less harmful waste than factories that start with brand-new materials.

1. What is the author's point? (1 point)
2. List three reasons the author gives to support the point. (3 points)
3. Which sentence is off-topic and does NOT support the author's point? (1 point)
4. Pick one reason and explain whether you think it is convincing or not convincing. Why? (1 point)

**Stretch:**

Complete the On-level tasks, plus:

5. Which reason in the recycling passage do you think is the STRONGEST? Explain why it is more convincing than the others. (2 points)
6. Can you think of a reason the author did NOT include that would make the argument even stronger? Write one additional reason that supports the point. (1 point)

**Challenge:**

Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:

7. Write your own short persuasive passage (4-5 sentences) about a topic you care about. Your passage must include: (a) a clear author's point in the first sentence, (b) at least two strong reasons that support the point, and (c) one sentence that uses evidence (a fact or statistic, even if you make it up reasonably). (3 points)

### Interleaved Review

**Review from Lesson 7 — Text Structure:**

8. What text structure does the "Why Recycling Matters" passage mostly use — cause-effect, sequence, comparison/contrast, or problem/solution? What signal words or clues helped you decide? (1 point)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short paragraph:

> Students should read for at least 20 minutes every day. Reading improves vocabulary — the more you read, the more words you learn. Reading also strengthens your brain, just like exercise strengthens your muscles. Schools that have daily reading programs see higher test scores across all subjects.

1. What is the author's point? (1 point)
2. Name two reasons the author gives to support the point. (1 point)
3. Is the reason "schools with daily reading programs see higher test scores" convincing? Why or why not? (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the distinction between "sharing information" and "making a point." Practice with oral examples: "I think pizza is the best lunch. Here is why: ..." Have the student identify the point and reasons.
- Use the Point-and-Reasons organizer as a scaffold for every practice passage
- Focus first on finding the point and reasons; add evaluation later once the student is comfortable identifying them
- Re-assess before moving to Lesson 10

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 10: Cumulative Review
- Encourage the student to notice when authors are making a point and giving reasons in any text they read — news articles, advertisements, even everyday conversations

## Extension (Optional)
- **Ad Detective:** Find a print advertisement or commercial and identify: What is the author/advertiser's point? What reasons do they give? Are the reasons convincing, or are they using tricks (like bright colors and fun music) instead of real reasons?
- **Convince Me:** The student picks a topic they feel strongly about and writes 3 reasons to support their point. Then the facilitator plays "devil's advocate" — pointing out possible weaknesses in each reason. The student revises their reasons to make them stronger.
- **Reason Ranker:** Give the student a point (e.g., "Students should have longer summer breaks") and 5 reasons — some strong, some weak. The student ranks them from most convincing to least convincing and explains their ranking.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This is the most advanced lesson in the unit.** Evaluating reasons and evidence is a cognitive step beyond identifying them. Be patient — third graders are just beginning to develop critical evaluation skills. The goal is awareness, not mastery of formal argumentation.
- **The off-topic detail pattern continues:** Both passages include a tangential detail (garden tool colors; bicycle colors). By now, the student should be developing the habit of spotting details that do not support the author's point. This connects back to Lesson 4 (supporting vs. tangential details).
- **Common misconception — the author's point is the same as the topic:** Students may say "the author's point is recycling" when the point is actually "everyone should recycle." The point includes a position or argument, not just a subject.
- **Common misconception — all reasons are equally strong:** Students may accept every reason without evaluation. Guide them to think about which reasons have evidence (numbers, research, facts) vs. which are just statements. Reasons backed by data are generally stronger.
- **Do not expect sophisticated evaluation:** At Grade 3, it is enough for the student to say "I think this reason is convincing because it makes sense" or "I do not think this reason is convincing because it does not really explain why." The evaluation will deepen in Grades 4-5.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson directly addresses RI.3.8 (describe the logical connection between particular sentences and paragraphs in a text; compare and contrast the most important points and key details presented in two texts on the same topic). Identifying an author's point and evaluating reasons builds the foundation for the more rigorous argument evaluation required in Grades 6-8.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice

**Passage 1: "Should Every School Have a Garden?"**
- Author's Point: Every school should have a garden.
- Reason 1: Gardens teach science in a hands-on way.
- Reason 2: Caring for a garden teaches responsibility.
- Reason 3: Gardens provide fresh fruits and vegetables / promote healthy eating.
- Reason 4: Working in a garden builds teamwork.
- Off-topic: "Garden tools come in many different colors and sizes."
- Evaluation: All four reasons are convincing because each one clearly explains a benefit of school gardens.

**Passage 2: "Why Helmets Matter"**
- Author's Point: Everyone who rides a bicycle should wear a helmet.
- Reason 1: Helmets reduce serious head injury risk by up to 85% (research).
- Reason 2: Helmets absorb impact and protect the brain.
- Reason 3: Laws require helmets — experts and lawmakers agree.
- Reason 4: Modern helmets are comfortable (counters the objection).
- Off-topic: "Bicycles can be red, blue, green, or many other colors."
- Strongest reason: Reason 1 — the specific statistic (85%) from research makes it very convincing because it is measurable evidence, not just an opinion.

### Independent Practice

**On-level:**
1. Author's point: Everyone should recycle. (1 point)
2. Any three: (a) Recycling reduces trash going to landfills; (b) Recycling paper saves trees (17 trees per ton); (c) Recycling aluminum saves 95% of the energy; (d) Recycling reduces pollution from factories. (3 points)
3. "Recycling bins are usually blue." This does not explain WHY people should recycle. (1 point)
4. Accept any evaluation with reasoning. Example: "The reason about saving trees is convincing because 17 trees per ton of paper is a big number, and I can picture how many trees that is. It makes recycling feel really important." OR "The energy reason is convincing because 95% is almost all the energy, which is a huge savings." (1 point)

**Stretch:**
5. Accept any well-reasoned answer. Example: "I think the reason about aluminum cans is strongest because the 95% statistic is very specific and easy to understand. When you hear that recycling uses almost NO energy compared to making new cans, it is hard to argue against recycling." (2 points: 1 for identifying a reason, 1 for explaining why)
6. Accept any reasonable additional reason. Examples: "Recycling creates jobs for people who work at recycling plants." / "Recycling helps protect animals whose habitats are destroyed by landfills or pollution." / "Recycling saves money for cities that would otherwise have to build new landfills." (1 point)

**Challenge:**
7. Accept any persuasive passage with (a) a clear point in the first sentence, (b) at least two reasons, and (c) one piece of evidence. Example: "Every student should learn to play a musical instrument. Playing music improves memory — studies show that musicians remember 20% more information than non-musicians. Learning an instrument also teaches patience, because it takes weeks of practice to learn even a simple song. Music brings people together, making schools happier and more connected places." (3 points: 1 for clear point, 1 for strong reasons, 1 for evidence)

**Interleaved Review:**
8. The passage mostly uses cause-effect (recycling causes less trash in landfills, saves trees, saves energy, reduces pollution). The reasons explain the EFFECTS of recycling. It could also be seen as problem/solution (problem: too much waste/pollution; solution: recycling). (1 point)

### Exit Ticket
1. Author's point: Students should read for at least 20 minutes every day. (1 point)
2. Any two: (a) Reading improves vocabulary; (b) Reading strengthens your brain; (c) Schools with daily reading programs see higher test scores. (1 point)
3. Accept any thoughtful evaluation. Example: "Yes, this reason is convincing because it shows real results — test scores going up means reading actually helps students do better in school. This is not just an opinion; it is something that has been measured." (1 point)
