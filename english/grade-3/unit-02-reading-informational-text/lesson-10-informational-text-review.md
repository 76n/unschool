By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply all Unit 2 skills to a new informational passage: identify text features, determine main idea and supporting details, describe relationships between events and concepts, cite text evidence, and identify the author's point and reasons
- Demonstrate readiness for the unit assessment by successfully using multiple skills in combination

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick review of key concepts from the entire unit using a rapid-fire format.

**Activity: Unit Skills Speed Round**

Say to the student:

*"We have covered a LOT in this unit. Let's do a speed round to warm up all those skills before we use them together on a new passage. I will ask questions, and you answer as quickly as you can."*

1. "What is informational text written to do?" (Teach, explain, inform, describe real things)
2. "Name three text features." (Any three: heading, subheading, table of contents, glossary, index, caption, diagram, map, chart, bold words)
3. "What is the difference between the topic and the main idea?" (Topic = what it is about; main idea = the most important point the author makes about the topic)
4. "What is a supporting detail?" (A fact, example, or reason that supports the main idea)
5. "Name two signal words for cause and effect." (because, so, as a result, therefore, since)
6. "Name two signal words for comparison/contrast." (both, however, unlike, while, on the other hand)
7. "What phrase can you use to show your answer comes from the text?" (According to the text..., The text states that..., etc.)
8. "What is an author's point?" (The argument or claim the author is trying to convince you to believe)

Say: *"Excellent. Today you are going to use ALL of these skills together on one passage. Think of this as a practice run for your unit assessment."*

**What Are Monarch Butterflies?**

Monarch butterflies are one of the most recognizable insects in North America. They have bright orange wings with black lines and white spots along the edges. Monarchs are not just beautiful — they are also one of the few insects that make a long-distance **migration**, traveling thousands of miles between their summer and winter homes. An adult monarch butterfly weighs less than a paperclip, yet it can fly distances that would challenge much larger animals.

*[Diagram: Life cycle of a monarch butterfly showing four stages — egg, caterpillar (larva), chrysalis (pupa), and adult butterfly, arranged in a circle with arrows connecting each stage]*
*Caption: The four stages of a monarch butterfly's life cycle.*

---

**The Great Migration**

Every fall, millions of monarch butterflies in the eastern United States and Canada begin an incredible journey. They fly south for up to 3,000 miles to reach the mountains of central Mexico, where they spend the winter clustered together on fir trees. **First**, the butterflies gather in large groups as the days grow shorter and cooler in late summer. **Then**, they begin flying south, traveling up to 100 miles per day. **Next**, they stop along the way to rest and drink **nectar** from flowers, refueling for the next leg of the journey. **Finally**, after about two months of flying, the monarchs arrive at their winter home in the oyamel fir forests of Mexico.

In spring, the butterflies begin the return journey north. **However**, no single butterfly completes the entire round trip. The monarchs that flew south lay eggs along the way, and it takes three or four **generations** of butterflies to make it all the way back to Canada. **Because** of this, the butterflies that arrive in Canada in summer are the great-grandchildren of the ones that left the previous fall.

*[Map: Shows eastern North America with arrows marking the monarch migration route — from southern Canada and the eastern US south through Texas to central Mexico, and a return route heading north in spring]*
*Caption: The migration route of eastern monarch butterflies.*

---

**How Monarchs Navigate**

Scientists have long wondered how monarch butterflies know where to go, especially **since** the butterflies making the journey have never been to Mexico before. Research has revealed that monarchs use a combination of two tools. **First**, they use the position of the sun to determine which direction is south. **Second**, they have a built-in **internal compass** that responds to the Earth's magnetic field, helping them stay on course even on cloudy days. **As a result** of these two navigation systems working together, monarchs can find their way to the exact same forests in Mexico that their great-grandparents left months earlier.

---

**Dangers Along the Way**

Monarch butterflies face many dangers during their migration. **One major problem** is the loss of **milkweed** plants. Milkweed is the only plant where monarch caterpillars can eat and grow. **Because** farmers use chemicals to kill weeds in their fields, milkweed is disappearing from many areas. **As a result**, there are fewer places for monarchs to lay their eggs along the migration route.

**Another problem** is the destruction of the oyamel fir forests in Mexico where monarchs spend the winter. Logging and climate change are reducing the size of these forests. Extreme weather events, such as unexpected freezes and storms, can also kill large numbers of butterflies. Scientists estimate that the monarch population has declined by about 80% over the past 20 years.

---

**Monarch Migration Data**

| Fact | Detail |
|---|---|
| Migration distance | Up to 3,000 miles (one way) |
| Daily travel distance | Up to 100 miles per day |
| Migration duration | About 2 months (fall journey) |
| Winter location | Oyamel fir forests, central Mexico |
| Generations for return trip | 3-4 generations |
| Population decline | ~80% over the past 20 years |

---

**Protecting Monarchs**

Everyone should help protect monarch butterflies. Planting milkweed in gardens and along roadsides gives monarchs places to lay eggs and gives caterpillars food to eat. Avoiding pesticides near milkweed plants keeps the plants safe for monarchs. **In addition**, supporting organizations that protect the forests in Mexico helps preserve the monarchs' winter home. Scientists, farmers, gardeners, and governments are all working together **to solve** the problem of declining monarch populations. Monarch butterflies are orange and black. By taking these steps, we can help ensure that the incredible monarch migration continues for generations to come.

**Glossary**

- **Generations** — Groups of offspring born around the same time; parents and children are different generations
- **Internal compass** — A natural ability in some animals to sense the Earth's magnetic field and determine direction
- **Migration** — The regular, long-distance movement of animals from one place to another, usually with the seasons
- **Milkweed** — A type of plant that monarch caterpillars eat; essential for monarch reproduction
- **Nectar** — A sweet liquid produced by flowers that butterflies and bees drink for energy

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student independently applies all unit skills to the passage.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**

Answer each question using the "Monarch Butterflies" passage. Use evidence sentence starters where indicated.

1. **Text Features:** Use the chart to answer: How far do monarchs migrate (one way)? What text feature did you use? (1 point)
2. **Main Idea:** What is the main idea of the section "How Monarchs Navigate"? (1 point)
3. **Supporting Details:** Name two dangers monarchs face during migration. (2 points)
4. **Cause and Effect:** Complete this sentence: "Milkweed is disappearing because ___. As a result, ___." (2 points)
5. **Evidence:** Using a sentence starter, answer: How many generations does it take to complete the return trip north? (1 point)
6. **Author's Point:** What is the author's point in "Protecting Monarchs"? Name one reason the author gives. (2 points)

**Stretch:**

Complete the On-level tasks, plus:

7. **Text Structure:** The section "Dangers Along the Way" uses which text structure(s)? Give at least two signal words as proof. (2 points)
8. **Summary:** Write a one-sentence summary of the entire passage (all sections combined). (1 point)
9. **Evaluation:** The author says the monarch population has declined by 80%. Does including this specific number make the argument for protecting monarchs more convincing? Why or why not? (1 point)

**Challenge:**

Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:

10. **Compare Structures:** Section 2 ("The Great Migration") uses sequence, and Section 4 ("Dangers Along the Way") uses problem/cause-effect. Why do you think the author chose different structures for different sections? Write 2-3 sentences explaining your thinking. (2 points)
11. **Write a New Section:** Write a short section (3-4 sentences) that could be added to this passage. It should have a heading, at least one bold word, and a clear main idea with supporting details. Choose your own topic related to monarchs (e.g., "Monarchs and Milkweed," "Monarch Butterflies in Your Backyard," or "How You Can Help"). (2 points)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Answer these three questions about the "Monarch Butterflies" passage:

1. What is the main idea of the section "What Are Monarch Butterflies?" (1 point)
2. According to the text, what two tools do monarchs use to navigate? Use a sentence starter. (1 point)
3. The author says "everyone should help protect monarch butterflies." Name one reason the author gives to support this point. (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Identify which specific skill(s) the student missed and review that lesson's anchor chart and strategies
- Practice the weak skill(s) in isolation before combining skills again
- Provide an additional practice session with a shorter, simpler passage before the unit assessment
- Consider splitting the assessment over two sessions if the student needs more processing time

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for the Unit Assessment
- Review the assessment format so the student knows what to expect
- Remind the student to use their full toolkit: preview text features, find main ideas, look for signal words, cite evidence, and evaluate reasons

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This lesson is intentionally lighter on new content:** The purpose is review and consolidation, not new learning. Let the student do the heavy lifting. Your role shifts from instructor to coach — ask questions, prompt thinking, and confirm answers rather than teaching new concepts.
- **The passage is longer than previous lessons:** This is deliberate preparation for the unit assessment, which will also require reading longer passages. If the student seems fatigued, take a short break between reading and answering questions.
- **Use the anchor charts:** Encourage the student to refer to the anchor charts built throughout the unit. The charts are tools, not crutches — using them is a sign of good reading strategy, not weakness.
- **Common issue — forgetting evidence starters:** After nine lessons, some students may revert to answering without citing evidence. Keep prompting: "Show me WHERE in the text it says that."
- **Diagnostic opportunity:** This lesson gives you a clear picture of which skills the student has mastered and which need reinforcement before the assessment. If the student struggles with a particular skill, note it and plan a brief reteaching session before administering the assessment.
- **The tangential detail pattern appears one final time:** "Monarch butterflies are orange and black" in the Protecting Monarchs section. By now, the student should catch it quickly. If they do, acknowledge the growth.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson integrates all unit standards: RI.3.2 (main idea and details), RI.3.3 (relationships between events/concepts), RI.3.5 (text features), and RI.3.8 (author's point and reasons).
- **All passages are original:** Written specifically for this curriculum.
