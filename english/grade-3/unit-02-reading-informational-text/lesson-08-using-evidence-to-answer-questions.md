# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 8

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Using Evidence to Answer Questions

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Answer questions about informational text by finding and citing explicit evidence from the text
- Use the phrases "According to the text..." and "The text states that..." when answering questions
- Distinguish between answers based on the text and answers based on personal opinion or outside knowledge

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine main idea and supporting details (Lessons 3-5)
- Ability to identify text structure and relationships (Lessons 6-7)
- Ability to use text features to locate information (Lesson 2)
- Ability to read Grade 3 level text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "The Amazing Octopus" (included below)
- Evidence sentence starter cards (printed or written on sticky notes): "According to the text...", "The text states that...", "The author explains that...", "In the passage, it says..."
- Highlighters (for marking evidence in the text)
- Paper and pencil

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review text structure from Lesson 7 and introduce the concept of answering questions with evidence.

**Activity: Quick Structure ID + Evidence Preview**

Say to the student:

*"Quick review: What text structure uses the signal words 'both,' 'however,' and 'on the other hand'?"* (Comparison/contrast)

*"What text structure describes a challenge and how people fix it?"* (Problem/solution)

*"Good. Now I have a question for you: Which is bigger — the sun or the moon?"*

Allow the student to answer. Then ask: *"How do you know? Where did you learn that?"*

The student will likely answer from personal knowledge. Say: *"You are right, but you answered from what you ALREADY KNOW. Today, we are going to practice a very important skill: answering questions using evidence from the TEXT — the actual words the author wrote. This is different from answering with your own opinion or your own knowledge. When someone asks you a question about a text, you need to SHOW them where in the text you found the answer."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the skill of finding and citing text evidence, including specific sentence starters for evidence-based responses.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Evidence** — Information from the text that proves or supports your answer
- **Explicit** — Clearly and directly stated in the text (not hidden or implied)
- **Cite** — To point to or refer to a specific part of the text as proof of your answer
- **Text-based answer** — An answer that comes directly from what the text says

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Why evidence matters (2-3 minutes)**

Say: *"When you read informational text and someone asks you a question about it, they want to know what the TEXT says — not what you guess or what you think. This is a skill called 'citing evidence.' It means you find the specific words or sentences in the text that answer the question and you POINT to them."*

*"Think of it like being a detective. A detective does not just say 'I think the suspect did it.' A detective says 'The fingerprints at the scene match the suspect.' The fingerprints are the evidence. In reading, the words in the text are your evidence."*

Write on the board:

**Answering with Evidence:**
1. Read the question carefully.
2. Go BACK to the text and FIND the answer.
3. Highlight or underline the evidence.
4. Use a sentence starter to show where your answer comes from.

**Step 2: Teach evidence sentence starters (3-4 minutes)**

Say: *"When you answer a question with evidence, you use special phrases to show that your answer comes from the text. Here are four you can use:"*

Write on the board (or display the sentence starter cards):

- **"According to the text, ..."**
- **"The text states that ..."**
- **"The author explains that ..."**
- **"In the passage, it says ..."**

Say: *"These phrases are like flags that tell the reader: 'I am not making this up — the text actually says this.' Let me show you the difference."*

**Without evidence:** "Octopuses are smart."
**With evidence:** "According to the text, octopuses are one of the most intelligent animals in the ocean."

Say: *"See the difference? The second answer tells you exactly where the information came from. It is stronger and more convincing."*

**Step 3: Text-based vs. opinion-based answers (2-3 minutes)**

Say: *"It is very important to know the difference between two types of answers:"*

Write on the board:

| Text-Based Answer | Opinion-Based Answer |
|---|---|
| Comes from the text | Comes from your own thinking |
| Can be proved by pointing to a sentence | Cannot be proved by the text |
| Uses evidence starters | Uses phrases like "I think" or "I believe" |

Say: *"Both types of answers have their place. But when someone asks 'What does the text say?' or 'According to the passage...?', they want a text-based answer with evidence."*

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Finding evidence to answer a question**

Display this paragraph:

> The cheetah is the fastest land animal on Earth. It can run at speeds of up to 70 miles per hour. However, a cheetah can only maintain this top speed for about 30 seconds before it needs to rest. Cheetahs hunt by sprinting in short, explosive bursts rather than running for long distances.

Question: "How fast can a cheetah run?"

Say: *"Let me find the answer. I re-read the paragraph and look for information about speed. I find it: 'It can run at speeds of up to 70 miles per hour.' Now I use a sentence starter:"*

*"According to the text, a cheetah can run at speeds of up to 70 miles per hour."*

Say: *"Notice that I did not just say '70 miles per hour.' I used 'According to the text' to show that my answer comes from the passage."*

**Worked Example 2: Distinguishing text evidence from personal knowledge**

Using the same paragraph, ask this question: "Why do cheetahs need to rest after sprinting?"

Say: *"Let me check: does the text tell me WHY they rest? The text says they 'can only maintain this top speed for about 30 seconds before it needs to rest.' It tells me they can only run fast for a short time, but it does NOT explain the scientific reason why."*

*"If I said 'Cheetahs rest because their muscles get tired and their body temperature rises,' that might be TRUE, but I would be using my own knowledge — the text does not say that. A text-based answer would be: 'The text states that a cheetah can only maintain its top speed for about 30 seconds before it needs to rest.' I stick to what the text actually says."*

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student practices finding evidence and using sentence starters to answer questions about an informational passage.

**Activity: Evidence Hunt — "The Amazing Octopus"**

Provide the student with the following passage. Read it together, then practice answering questions with evidence.

---

### Reading Passage: "The Amazing Octopus"

**A Body Built for Survival**

The octopus is one of the most remarkable creatures in the ocean. It has eight flexible **arms** covered with powerful **suction cups** that can grip almost anything. Unlike most animals, the octopus has no bones at all — its entire body is soft. **Because** its body is boneless, an octopus can squeeze through incredibly tiny spaces. Scientists have observed octopuses fitting through openings no bigger than a quarter, as long as their hard beak (the only rigid part of their body) can fit through.

**Masters of Disguise**

Octopuses are masters of camouflage. They can change the color, pattern, and even the texture of their skin in less than one second. Special cells in their skin called **chromatophores** contain different colored pigments that expand or shrink to create different colors. An octopus sitting on a sandy ocean floor can make itself look like sand. One sitting on coral can make itself look like coral. This ability helps octopuses hide from predators and sneak up on prey.

**Surprising Intelligence**

Octopuses are considered one of the most intelligent invertebrates — animals without a backbone. In laboratory experiments, octopuses have learned to open jars, solve mazes, and even recognize individual human faces. Some octopuses have been observed using tools, such as carrying coconut shells and using them as portable shelters. In aquariums, octopuses have figured out how to unscrew lids, escape from their tanks, and even squirt water at bright lights they find annoying.

**Defense Strategies**

When an octopus is threatened, it has several defense strategies. **First**, it can squirt a cloud of dark **ink** into the water, which confuses the attacker and gives the octopus time to escape. **If** the ink does not work, the octopus can use its camouflage to blend into the surroundings and become nearly invisible. **As a last resort**, an octopus can detach one of its arms. The detached arm continues to wiggle, distracting the predator while the octopus swims away. Amazingly, the octopus can grow the arm back over time.

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

Work through these questions with decreasing levels of support.

**Question 1 (Full modeling):** "How does an octopus change its color?"

Say: *"Let me show you how to find the answer. The question asks HOW it changes color. I look in the section about camouflage. I find: 'Special cells in their skin called chromatophores contain different colored pigments that expand or shrink to create different colors.' Let me put it in a sentence: 'According to the text, octopuses change their color using special cells called chromatophores that contain different colored pigments that expand or shrink.'"*

**Question 2 (Guided):** "What is the only hard part of an octopus's body?"

Say: *"Read the first section again. Can you find the answer? Highlight it."*

If the student struggles, say: *"Look for the sentence that talks about bones and rigid parts."*

Guide the student to answer: *"The text states that the only rigid part of an octopus's body is its hard beak."*

**Question 3 (Supported independence):** "What are two examples of octopus intelligence mentioned in the passage?"

Say: *"Find the section about intelligence. Look for specific examples. Write your answer using an evidence sentence starter."*

Guide as needed toward: *"According to the text, octopuses have learned to open jars and solve mazes. The author also explains that some octopuses carry coconut shells and use them as portable shelters."*

**Question 4 (Independent):** "What does an octopus do 'as a last resort' when it is threatened?"

Say: *"Find the answer on your own. Highlight the evidence and write your answer with a sentence starter."*

Target: *"The text states that as a last resort, an octopus can detach one of its arms, which wiggles to distract the predator while the octopus swims away."*

**Question 5 (Distinguishing text evidence from opinion):** "Do you think octopuses are the most interesting animal in the ocean? Is this a question you can answer from the text?"

Guide the student to recognize: This is an OPINION question — the text does not say whether octopuses are the "most interesting." The student can share their opinion, but it would not be a text-based answer. A text-based question would be something like "What evidence does the text give that octopuses are intelligent?"

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student independently answers questions about informational text using evidence and sentence starters.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**

Use the "Amazing Octopus" passage to answer each question. You MUST include evidence from the text using a sentence starter.

1. How small of a space can an octopus squeeze through? (1 point)
2. How does an octopus's camouflage help it? Give two reasons from the text. (2 points)
3. What happens when an octopus squirts ink? (1 point)
4. Can an octopus grow back a lost arm? Use evidence from the text to support your answer. (1 point)

**Stretch:**

Complete the On-level tasks, plus:

5. A friend says: "Octopuses are just like jellyfish because they are both sea creatures with no bones." Using evidence from the passage, explain whether the text supports this comparison. (Hint: The text describes octopus features that are very different from jellyfish.) (2 points)

6. Write a question about the passage that CAN be answered with text evidence, and a question that CANNOT be answered from the text. Explain the difference. (2 points)

**Challenge:**

Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:

7. Using evidence from the passage, write a short paragraph (3-4 sentences) explaining why octopuses are considered highly intelligent. Each sentence must include evidence from the text. Use at least two different sentence starters. (3 points)

### Interleaved Review

**Review from Lesson 6 — Text Structure:**

8. Look at the "Defense Strategies" section of the octopus passage. What text structure does this section use? What signal words help you identify it? (1 point)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this paragraph:

> The Amazon rainforest produces about 20% of the world's oxygen. It is home to more than 10% of all animal species on Earth, including jaguars, macaws, and poison dart frogs. The Amazon River, which runs through the forest, is the second longest river in the world.

1. According to the passage, how much of the world's oxygen does the Amazon rainforest produce? Write your answer using a sentence starter. (1 point)
2. Name one animal species the text says lives in the Amazon. (1 point)
3. A student says: "The Amazon rainforest is the biggest forest in the world." Can this answer be supported by evidence from this paragraph? Why or why not? (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice the "go back and find it" strategy with very short passages (2-3 sentences)
- Use physical highlighting — have the student highlight the answer in the text BEFORE writing their response
- Provide the sentence starters on cards that the student keeps next to them while writing
- Practice distinguishing "the text says" from "I think" with everyday examples
- Re-assess before moving to Lesson 9

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 9: Evaluating Reasons and Evidence
- Challenge the student to practice the evidence habit with any text — "show me WHERE it says that"

## Extension (Optional)
- **Evidence Highlight Challenge:** Give the student a nonfiction article and 5 questions. For each question, the student highlights the evidence in the text before writing their answer. Award bonus points for using different sentence starters for each answer.
- **Question Writer:** The student writes 5 questions about the "Amazing Octopus" passage that CAN be answered with text evidence, plus the answers (with evidence cited). Then the student writes 2 questions that CANNOT be answered from the text.
- **Evidence Battle:** The facilitator and student each answer the same question about a text. They compare answers to see who cited the most specific evidence from the text.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This is a keystone skill:** The ability to cite text evidence is foundational for all academic reading from Grade 3 onward. Every state assessment requires students to support answers with evidence from the text. Invest time here.
- **The sentence starters are training wheels:** Over time, the student should incorporate evidence naturally without always starting with "According to the text..." But for now, the starters build the habit of returning to the text.
- **Common misconception — answering from memory or background knowledge:** Students often answer correctly but from their own knowledge rather than the text. Even if the answer is right, redirect: "That IS true, but can you show me where in the text it says that?" This builds the discipline of evidence-based reasoning.
- **Common misconception — paraphrasing means changing every word:** Students may think they need to completely rewrite the text's language. Clarify: they can use the text's exact words (that is quoting) or they can put it in their own words (that is paraphrasing). Both are valid ways to cite evidence at this grade level.
- **Question 5 in guided practice is critical:** The distinction between text-based and opinion-based questions is essential for test-taking and for developing analytical thinking. Students need to know WHEN to look in the text and when a question is asking for their own thinking.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson addresses RI.3.1 (ask and answer questions to demonstrate understanding, referring explicitly to the text as the basis for the answers). While RI.3.1 is not one of the unit's four focus standards, it underpins all the others — you cannot determine main ideas, describe relationships, or evaluate evidence without the habit of referring to the text.
- **All passages are original:** Written specifically for this curriculum.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice

1. "According to the text, octopuses change their color using special cells called chromatophores that contain pigments. These pigments expand or shrink to create different colors."
2. "The text states that the only rigid part of an octopus's body is its hard beak."
3. "According to the text, octopuses have learned to open jars, solve mazes, and recognize human faces. The author also explains that some octopuses use coconut shells as portable shelters." (Accept any two examples.)
4. "The text states that as a last resort, an octopus can detach one of its arms. The arm wiggles to distract the predator while the octopus swims away. The octopus can grow the arm back over time."
5. This is an opinion question — it cannot be answered from the text. The text describes octopuses as "remarkable" and "intelligent" but does not call them the "most interesting."

### Independent Practice

**On-level:**
1. "According to the text, an octopus can squeeze through openings no bigger than a quarter, as long as its hard beak can fit through." (1 point)
2. "The text states that camouflage helps octopuses hide from predators and sneak up on prey." (2 points for both reasons; 1 point for one reason)
3. "According to the text, when an octopus squirts ink, it creates a dark cloud in the water that confuses the attacker and gives the octopus time to escape." (1 point)
4. "The text states that an octopus can grow back a lost arm over time." / "In the passage, it says 'Amazingly, the octopus can grow the arm back over time.'" (1 point)

**Stretch:**
5. The text does not compare octopuses to jellyfish. However, the text does say octopuses have no bones, but it also describes many features that are unique to octopuses: eight arms with suction cups, chromatophores for color change, high intelligence (opening jars, solving mazes), and the ability to detach and regrow arms. According to the text, octopuses are far more complex than the comparison suggests. (2 points: 1 for using evidence, 1 for reasoning)
6. Can be answered: "What are chromatophores?" (Answer: According to the text, chromatophores are special cells in the octopus's skin that contain different colored pigments.) Cannot be answered: "How long do octopuses live?" (The text does not mention lifespan.) The difference is that the first question has an answer stated in the text, while the second asks for information the text does not provide. (2 points)

**Challenge:**
7. Accept a 3-4 sentence paragraph about octopus intelligence using evidence from the text with at least two different sentence starters. Example: "According to the text, octopuses are considered one of the most intelligent invertebrates. The author explains that in laboratory experiments, they have learned to open jars, solve mazes, and even recognize individual human faces. The text also states that some octopuses use coconut shells as portable shelters, which is an example of tool use. In the passage, it says that aquarium octopuses have figured out how to unscrew lids and escape from their tanks." (3 points)

**Interleaved Review:**
8. The "Defense Strategies" section uses SEQUENCE (first, if..., as a last resort — the defenses are described in an escalating order). It also contains some cause-effect (ink confuses predator, giving octopus time to escape). Signal words: first, if, as a last resort. (1 point)

### Exit Ticket
1. "According to the text, the Amazon rainforest produces about 20% of the world's oxygen." (1 point — must use a sentence starter)
2. Jaguars, macaws, or poison dart frogs (any one). (1 point)
3. No, this cannot be supported by this paragraph. The text says the Amazon produces 20% of the world's oxygen and is home to many species, but it does not say it is the biggest forest in the world. That information is not in this paragraph. (1 point)
