# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 4

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Supporting Details

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define "supporting details" as facts, examples, or pieces of information that explain, prove, or develop the main idea
- Distinguish between details that support the main idea and details that are interesting but not key
- Identify at least three supporting details for a given main idea in an informational paragraph

## Prerequisites
- Ability to distinguish between topic and main idea (Lesson 3)
- Ability to identify the main idea of a single paragraph (Lesson 3)
- Familiarity with text features and how to use them (Lesson 2)
- Ability to read Grade 3 level text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "The Life of a Firefighter" (included below)
- Paper and pencil
- Highlighters (2 colors — one for main idea, one for supporting details)
- Sticky notes
- Prepared "umbrella" graphic organizer template (or blank paper to create one): the umbrella top has space for the main idea and below it are 3-4 lines for details

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review main idea (Lesson 3) and activate thinking about which details matter most.

**Activity: Main Idea Quick Check + Detail Sort**

Say to the student:

*"Let's review. Listen to this paragraph and tell me the main idea."*

> Dolphins are incredibly fast swimmers. They can swim up to 20 miles per hour. Some dolphins have been seen leaping as high as 20 feet out of the water. Their smooth, streamlined bodies help them glide through the water with very little effort.

Ask: *"What is the topic?"* (Dolphins / dolphin swimming)
Ask: *"What is the main idea?"* (Dolphins are incredibly fast swimmers.)

*"Great. Now here is the new challenge for today. I am going to read you four sentences. ALL of them are true. But only some of them SUPPORT the main idea that dolphins are fast swimmers. Listen carefully."*

- A. "Dolphins can swim up to 20 miles per hour." (Supports — shows how fast)
- B. "Dolphins are gray in color." (True but does NOT support the main idea about speed)
- C. "Their streamlined bodies help them glide through water." (Supports — explains WHY they are fast)
- D. "Dolphins live in groups called pods." (True but does NOT support the main idea about speed)

Ask for each: *"Does this detail support the main idea that dolphins are fast swimmers? Or is it interesting but about something else?"*

Say: *"Today we are going to learn how to find the details that SUPPORT the main idea — and how to tell them apart from details that are interesting but not directly connected."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach what supporting details are, how they connect to the main idea, and how to distinguish supporting details from tangential information.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Supporting details** — Facts, examples, reasons, or descriptions that explain, prove, or tell more about the main idea
- **Main idea** — The most important point the author makes about the topic (review from Lesson 3)
- **Relevant** — Connected to and important for the main idea
- **Tangential** — Interesting but not directly connected to the main idea (also called "off-topic" or "extra" details)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Define supporting details (3 minutes)**

Say: *"In Lesson 3, we learned that the main idea is the most important point. But the main idea does not stand alone — it needs SUPPORT. Supporting details are the facts, examples, and explanations that hold up the main idea. Without supporting details, the main idea is just a claim with no proof."*

Write on the anchor chart or whiteboard:

**Supporting Details:**
- Tell more about the main idea
- Explain HOW or WHY the main idea is true
- Give examples, facts, or reasons
- Answer the questions: "How do you know?" "Can you prove it?" "What is an example?"

Say: *"Remember our umbrella from last lesson? The main idea is the umbrella. The supporting details are the poles that hold it up. If you take away the poles, the umbrella falls. The details keep the main idea standing."*

**Step 2: Not every detail is a supporting detail (3-4 minutes)**

Say: *"Here is something important: not every detail in a paragraph supports the main idea. Sometimes authors include extra information that is interesting or true but does not directly connect to the main point. Your job as a reader is to figure out WHICH details support the main idea and which ones are just extra."*

Write on the anchor chart:

**Is it a supporting detail? Ask yourself:**
- Does this detail tell more about the main idea?
- Does it help explain, prove, or give an example of the main idea?
- If I removed this detail, would the main idea be weaker?

If YES to these questions = supporting detail.
If NO = interesting but not a key supporting detail.

**Step 3: Model the thinking process (2-3 minutes)**

Say: *"Let me show you what I mean with a quick example."*

Write this main idea on the board: **"Recycling helps protect the environment."**

Then list these details:
- A. "Recycling paper saves trees because fewer trees need to be cut down."
- B. "The recycling symbol has three arrows that form a triangle."
- C. "Recycling plastic keeps it out of the ocean, where it can harm sea animals."
- D. "Recycling aluminum cans uses 95% less energy than making new aluminum."
- E. "The color of recycling bins is usually blue."

Say: *"Let me check each one. Detail A — does recycling paper saving trees tell me HOW recycling helps the environment? Yes! That is a supporting detail. Detail B — does the shape of the recycling symbol help explain how recycling protects the environment? No, it is interesting but it does not support the main idea. Detail C — does keeping plastic out of the ocean explain how recycling helps? Yes! Supporting detail. Detail D — does using less energy support the idea that recycling helps the environment? Yes! Detail E — does the color of the bin explain how recycling protects the environment? No, that is extra information."*

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Identifying supporting details with the umbrella organizer**

Display this paragraph:

> Owls are expert hunters. They have excellent eyesight that lets them see small animals in near-total darkness. Their ears are specially designed — one ear is higher than the other, which helps them pinpoint the exact location of sounds. Owls can also fly almost silently because of the soft edges on their feathers. This means their prey does not hear them coming.

Say: *"Step 1: The main idea is 'Owls are expert hunters' — that is the first sentence. Step 2: Now I need to find the details that SUPPORT this main idea. Each detail should explain HOW or WHY owls are expert hunters."*

Fill in the umbrella organizer:
- **Main Idea (umbrella):** Owls are expert hunters.
- **Supporting Detail 1:** They have excellent eyesight for seeing in darkness.
- **Supporting Detail 2:** Their ears help them pinpoint the exact location of sounds.
- **Supporting Detail 3:** They fly almost silently so prey does not hear them.

Say: *"Each detail explains one reason WHY owls are expert hunters. Together, they prove the main idea."*

**Worked Example 2: Sorting supporting details from extra details**

Display this paragraph:

> Rainforests are home to an enormous variety of living things. More than half of all plant and animal species on Earth live in rainforests. Scientists discover new species in the rainforest every year. Rainforests are found near the equator, where the weather is warm and wet all year long. Some rainforest trees grow taller than 200 feet. Colorful birds, tiny frogs, large cats, and millions of insects all share the rainforest habitat.

Say: *"The main idea is 'Rainforests are home to an enormous variety of living things.' Now let me sort the details."*

- "More than half of all species live in rainforests" — SUPPORTS (shows how many different things live there)
- "Scientists discover new species every year" — SUPPORTS (shows there are so many species we keep finding more)
- "Rainforests are found near the equator" — INTERESTING but does NOT support the main idea about variety of living things; it is about location
- "Some trees grow taller than 200 feet" — INTERESTING but about tree height, not variety of living things
- "Colorful birds, tiny frogs, large cats, and millions of insects share the habitat" — SUPPORTS (gives examples of the variety)

Say: *"The details about location and tree height are true, but they do not help prove that rainforests have enormous variety. The other three details DO support the main idea."*

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student practices identifying supporting details in a multi-section passage with facilitator guidance.

**Activity: Supporting Details Highlighter — "The Life of a Firefighter"**

Provide the student with the following passage. Read each section together, identify the main idea, then highlight the supporting details.

---

### Reading Passage: "The Life of a Firefighter"

**Training**

Becoming a firefighter requires serious training. New firefighters spend weeks at a fire academy, where they learn how to use hoses, ladders, and breathing equipment. They practice rescuing people from burning buildings using life-size practice structures. Firefighters also train in physical fitness because they need to carry heavy equipment and climb stairs while wearing gear that weighs more than 60 pounds. Fire stations are usually painted red or have a large number on the front. The training never really stops — even experienced firefighters practice their skills regularly to stay sharp.

**A Day at the Station**

Firefighters at a fire station stay busy even when there is no fire. In the morning, they check all the equipment on the fire trucks to make sure everything works. They test the hoses, inspect the breathing tanks, and count the medical supplies. Firefighters also clean the station, cook meals together, and exercise to stay in shape. Some fire trucks can pump more than 1,000 gallons of water per minute. When the alarm sounds, firefighters must be dressed in their gear and on the truck in less than two minutes.

**Helping the Community**

Firefighters do much more than fight fires. They respond to car accidents, help people during floods and storms, and rescue animals stuck in dangerous places. Many firefighters are also trained as emergency medical technicians, or **EMTs**, which means they can give medical care to injured people at the scene of an emergency. Firefighters visit schools to teach children about fire safety. Fire hydrants are usually painted yellow or red. By helping in so many ways, firefighters are some of the most important helpers in a community.

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

**Section 1: "Training"**

Say: *"Read this section. First, what is the main idea?"*

Guide: Becoming a firefighter requires serious training.

Say: *"Now, use your highlighter to highlight the details that SUPPORT this main idea — the details that explain what the training involves or why it is serious. If you find a detail that is interesting but does NOT support the main idea, do not highlight it."*

After the student highlights, review together:
- SUPPORTS: weeks at fire academy, learning to use equipment, practicing rescues, physical fitness training, training never stops
- DOES NOT SUPPORT: "Fire stations are usually painted red or have a large number on the front." (This is about what stations look like, not about training.)

Ask: *"Did you catch the sneaky detail? Why does the sentence about fire stations being painted red NOT support the main idea about training?"*

**Section 2: "A Day at the Station"**

Say: *"Find the main idea and then highlight the supporting details."*

Guide toward main idea: Firefighters at a station stay busy even when there is no fire.

After the student highlights, review:
- SUPPORTS: check equipment, test hoses, inspect tanks, count supplies, clean station, cook meals, exercise, ready in less than two minutes
- DOES NOT SUPPORT: "Some fire trucks can pump more than 1,000 gallons of water per minute." (This is about the truck's capacity, not about what firefighters do during the day.)

**Section 3: "Helping the Community"**

Say: *"This one is your turn. Find the main idea and the supporting details. Then find the detail that does NOT support the main idea."*

Let the student work more independently. Check their work.

Guide toward main idea: Firefighters do much more than fight fires.

- SUPPORTS: respond to car accidents, help during floods/storms, rescue animals, trained as EMTs, visit schools for fire safety
- DOES NOT SUPPORT: "Fire hydrants are usually painted yellow or red." (About hydrant color, not about how firefighters help the community.)

Say: *"Great work! You noticed that each section had one detail that was interesting but did not fit under the main idea umbrella."*

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student identifies main ideas and supporting details independently, including sorting relevant from tangential details.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**

**Paragraph 1:**

> The Arctic fox is perfectly adapted to survive in cold weather. Its thick fur changes color from brown in summer to white in winter, helping it blend in with the snow. The Arctic fox has small, rounded ears that lose less body heat than large ears would. It even has fur on the bottoms of its feet, which keeps its paws warm and helps it walk on ice without slipping.

1. What is the main idea? (1 point)
2. List two supporting details that explain HOW the Arctic fox is adapted to cold weather. (2 points)

**Paragraph 2:**

> Community gardens bring many benefits to neighborhoods. People who garden together often become friends, which makes the neighborhood feel more connected. Gardens provide fresh fruits and vegetables for families who might not be able to buy them at a store. Children who help in community gardens learn where food comes from and how plants grow. Garden tools can be expensive to buy. Community gardens also make empty lots look beautiful instead of overgrown and messy.

3. What is the main idea? (1 point)
4. List three supporting details. (3 points)
5. Which detail in the paragraph does NOT directly support the main idea? Explain why. (1 point)

**Stretch:**

Complete the On-level tasks, plus:

6. Look back at the "Wonderful Wolves" passage from Lesson 3 (the "Pack Life" section). The main idea is "Wolves are social animals that live and work together in packs." List two supporting details from that section. (2 points)

7. Explain in your own words: Why is it important to tell the difference between supporting details and extra details? Write 2-3 sentences. (1 point)

**Challenge:**

Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:

8. Write a paragraph (4-5 sentences) with a clear main idea and three supporting details. Then write one more sentence that is an "extra" detail — true but not supporting the main idea. Label which sentence is the main idea, which are supporting details, and which is the extra detail. (2 points)

### Interleaved Review

**Review from Lesson 3 — Topic vs. Main Idea:**

9. Read this paragraph: "The Amazon River is the second longest river in the world. It flows through several South American countries, including Brazil, Peru, and Colombia. The river is home to thousands of species of fish, including piranhas and electric eels. During the rainy season, parts of the Amazon can become several miles wide." What is the TOPIC and what is the MAIN IDEA? (1 point)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this paragraph:

> Bats play an important role in the environment. Many bats eat insects — a single bat can eat more than 1,000 mosquitoes in one hour, helping to control insect populations. Other bats drink nectar from flowers and spread pollen as they fly, which helps plants grow and produce fruit. Bats are the only mammals that can truly fly. Some bats also eat fruit and scatter the seeds in their droppings, helping new plants grow in different places.

1. What is the main idea? (1 point)
2. Name two supporting details that explain HOW bats help the environment. (1 point)
3. Which sentence does NOT support the main idea? Why not? (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the umbrella analogy — physically draw the umbrella and write the main idea on top, then test each detail: "Does this fit under the umbrella?"
- Use shorter paragraphs (2-3 sentences) where every detail IS a supporting detail, then gradually add tangential details as the student builds confidence
- Practice orally first: state a main idea aloud, then give details one at a time and have the student say "supports" or "extra"
- Re-assess before moving to Lesson 5

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 5: Main Idea and Details Practice (extended application)
- Encourage the student to identify the main idea and supporting details in anything they read at home

## Extension (Optional)
- **Detail Detective Game:** The facilitator writes a main idea on a card and 6 detail cards (4 supporting, 2 extra). The student sorts the details into "supporting" and "extra" piles. Play with 3-4 different main ideas.
- **Backward Building:** Give the student three supporting details and have them figure out what the main idea must be. Example: "It has thick fur," "It hibernates in winter," "It stores body fat in the fall" — what is the main idea? (Bears are well prepared for winter.)
- **News Detective:** Find a short nonfiction article (from a kid-friendly news source or magazine) and have the student highlight the main idea in one color and supporting details in another. Discuss any details that do not fit.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **The "sneaky detail" approach is deliberate:** Each section of "The Life of a Firefighter" includes one tangential detail placed naturally in the paragraph. This teaches students that not everything in a paragraph is equally important — a critical comprehension skill.
- **Common misconception — every sentence is a supporting detail:** Students often assume that because a sentence is in the paragraph, it must support the main idea. The sorting exercises in this lesson directly address that assumption.
- **Common misconception — supporting details are the most interesting sentences:** Students may gravitate toward the most dramatic or surprising detail rather than the ones most closely connected to the main idea. Redirect: "That IS really interesting! But does it help explain the main idea, or is it extra information?"
- **Physical sorting is powerful:** If the student struggles with abstract identification, write each sentence on a strip of paper and let them physically sort into piles. The tactile experience makes the abstract concept concrete.
- **Connection between Lessons 3 and 4:** This lesson builds directly on Lesson 3. If the student still struggles with main idea identification, address that before pushing forward with supporting details. A student who cannot find the main idea cannot determine which details support it.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson deepens RI.3.2 (determine the main idea of a text; recount the key details and explain how they support the main idea). The emphasis on "explain how they support" is the new third-grade expectation beyond Grade 2.
- **All passages are original:** Written specifically for this curriculum.
