# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 7

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Relationships Between Concepts — Comparison/Contrast, Problem/Solution, Description

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify comparison/contrast text structure using signal words (like, unlike, both, different, similar, however, but, on the other hand)
- Identify problem/solution text structure using signal words (problem, solution, solved, fix, answer, as a result)
- Recognize that informational texts use different organizational structures and name at least four structures (cause-effect, sequence, comparison/contrast, problem/solution)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify cause-and-effect and sequence relationships (Lesson 6)
- Familiarity with signal words (Lesson 6)
- Ability to determine main idea and supporting details (Lessons 3-5)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "Animal Adaptations: Surviving in Extreme Places" (included below)
- Text Structure Anchor Chart (to build together — four structures with signal words)
- Venn diagram template (for comparison/contrast)
- Problem/Solution T-chart template (two columns: Problem | Solution)
- Highlighters (3 colors — one for comparison/contrast words, one for problem/solution words, one for cause-effect words)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review cause-effect and sequence signal words from Lesson 6 and introduce the idea that texts can be organized in different ways.

**Activity: Signal Word Sort**

Write or read these signal words one at a time. The student sorts each into "cause-effect" or "sequence":

1. "because" (Cause-effect)
2. "first" (Sequence)
3. "as a result" (Cause-effect)
4. "then" (Sequence)
5. "since" (Cause-effect)
6. "finally" (Sequence)

Say: *"Good. You know two ways that authors organize information — by showing cause and effect, and by putting events in order (sequence). Today you are going to learn two MORE ways authors organize text: comparison and contrast, and problem and solution. By the end of today, you will know FOUR text structures. That is a powerful toolkit for understanding any informational text."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach comparison/contrast and problem/solution text structures with signal words, and build a comprehensive text structure anchor chart.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Text structure** — The way an author organizes information in a text
- **Comparison/contrast** — A text structure that shows how two or more things are alike (comparison) and different (contrast)
- **Problem/solution** — A text structure that describes a problem and then explains how it was solved or could be solved
- **Description** — A text structure where the author describes a topic by listing its features, characteristics, or examples (the simplest structure)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Build the full text structure anchor chart (2 minutes)**

Say: *"Let's put all our text structures in one place."*

Write on the anchor chart:

| Text Structure | What It Does | Signal Words |
|---|---|---|
| **Cause and Effect** | Shows why something happens | because, so, as a result, therefore, since |
| **Sequence** | Shows the order of events or steps | first, then, next, after, finally, before |
| **Comparison/Contrast** | Shows how things are alike and different | (to be filled in) |
| **Problem/Solution** | Describes a problem and its solution | (to be filled in) |

**Step 2: Teach comparison/contrast (4-5 minutes)**

Say: *"When an author writes about two things and shows how they are alike AND different, the text structure is comparison/contrast. The 'comparison' part shows similarities — how the things are alike. The 'contrast' part shows differences — how the things are different."*

Write the signal words: **like, unlike, both, same, different, similar, however, but, on the other hand, in contrast, whereas, while, also**

Say: *"Listen to this example:"*

> "**Both** African and Asian elephants have tusks and trunks. **However**, African elephants are larger and have bigger ears. Asian elephants, **on the other hand**, have smaller ears and smoother skin."

Say: *"The signal word 'both' tells me the author is comparing — showing something the same. 'However' and 'on the other hand' tell me the author is contrasting — showing differences. I could put this in a Venn diagram — the overlaps are the comparisons, and the sides are the contrasts."*

Draw a quick Venn diagram:
- African only: larger, bigger ears
- Both: tusks, trunks
- Asian only: smaller ears, smoother skin

**Step 3: Teach problem/solution (3-4 minutes)**

Say: *"Sometimes an author describes a problem and then tells you how people solved it or could solve it. This is called problem/solution. It is like a story with a real-world challenge and a real-world answer."*

Write the signal words: **problem, solution, solve, fix, answer, challenge, issue, as a result, one way to, in order to**

Say: *"Listen to this example:"*

> "Many cities have a **problem** with too much trash going to landfills. **One way to solve** this is by recycling. When people recycle paper, plastic, and glass, those materials are reused instead of thrown away. **As a result**, less trash ends up in landfills."

Say: *"The word 'problem' tells me what the issue is: too much trash in landfills. 'One way to solve' tells me the author is giving a solution: recycling. 'As a result' shows the outcome of the solution."*

Fill in a Problem/Solution T-chart:
- Problem: Too much trash going to landfills
- Solution: Recycling paper, plastic, and glass so materials are reused

**Step 4: Quick mention of description (1 minute)**

Say: *"There is one more simple text structure you should know: description. When an author just describes a topic by listing its features, characteristics, or examples without specifically comparing, showing cause-effect, sequencing, or solving a problem, that is a description structure. Many paragraphs use this — it is the most basic structure. The other structures are more specific."*

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Identifying comparison/contrast in a paragraph**

Display this paragraph:

> Frogs and toads look **similar**, but they have important differences. **Both** are amphibians that start life in water. **However**, frogs have smooth, moist skin, **while** toads have dry, bumpy skin. Frogs are usually found near water because they need to keep their skin wet. Toads, **on the other hand**, can live farther from water because their thicker skin holds in moisture better.

Say: *"I see the signal words 'similar,' 'both,' 'however,' 'while,' and 'on the other hand.' These all point to comparison/contrast. Let me fill in a Venn diagram."*

- Frogs only: smooth, moist skin; live near water
- Both: amphibians; start life in water
- Toads only: dry, bumpy skin; can live farther from water

**Worked Example 2: Identifying problem/solution in a paragraph**

Display this paragraph:

> **One problem** facing polar bears is the melting of Arctic sea ice. Polar bears depend on sea ice to hunt seals — their main food. As temperatures rise, the ice melts earlier in the spring and forms later in the fall, leaving polar bears with less time to hunt. **To help solve** this problem, scientists study polar bear populations and governments work to reduce pollution that causes warming. Some groups have also created protected areas where polar bears can find food and shelter.

Say: *"I see 'one problem' and 'to help solve.' This is problem/solution."*

- Problem: Arctic sea ice is melting, so polar bears have less time to hunt seals.
- Solutions: Scientists study populations; governments reduce pollution; protected areas are created.

**Purpose:** The student identifies text structures in a multi-section passage with facilitator support.

**Activity: Text Structure Detective — "Animal Adaptations: Surviving in Extreme Places"**

Provide the student with the following passage. Read it together, then analyze each section for its text structure.

### Reading Passage: "Animal Adaptations: Surviving in Extreme Places"

**Desert vs. Arctic: Two Extreme Environments**

The desert and the Arctic might seem like complete opposites, but they are **both** extreme environments where animals must adapt to survive. Deserts are extremely hot and dry, with temperatures that can climb above 120 degrees Fahrenheit. The Arctic, **on the other hand**, is extremely cold, with temperatures that can drop below negative 50 degrees. **However**, **both** environments receive very little precipitation. Deserts get less than 10 inches of rain per year, and many parts of the Arctic are **similarly** dry. **Despite** these harsh conditions, animals have found remarkable ways to live in both places.

**The Camel: A Desert Survivor**

Camels have many features that help them survive the harsh desert heat. Their wide, flat feet spread their weight across the sand so they do not sink. Long eyelashes and closeable nostrils protect them from blowing sand during sandstorms. The fat stored in their humps provides energy when food is scarce. Camels can also go for days without water **because** their bodies are extremely efficient at conserving moisture. **As a result** of these adaptations, camels can thrive in some of the hottest, driest places on Earth.

**The Penguin: Problem Solvers of the Cold**

Penguins face a serious **problem**: they live in one of the coldest places on Earth but must keep their bodies warm enough to survive. **One solution** penguins have developed is a thick layer of waterproof feathers that trap warm air close to their skin. Under their feathers, a layer of fat called **blubber** adds extra insulation. **Another solution** is behavioral — emperor penguins huddle together in large groups during blizzards, taking turns standing on the outside where the wind is coldest. By rotating positions, every penguin gets a chance to warm up in the center. **Because** of these adaptations, penguins can survive temperatures that would be fatal to most other birds.

**A Growing Challenge**

**Both** desert and Arctic animals face a new **problem**: climate change. Rising temperatures are causing Arctic ice to melt faster, threatening animals like polar bears and penguins. In deserts, hotter temperatures and less rainfall make it harder for animals to find water. Scientists are working **to solve** these problems by studying how animals adapt and by pushing for laws that protect wild habitats. **However**, the challenge is enormous, and **both** environments need protection if their amazing animals are to survive.

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

**Section 1: "Desert vs. Arctic"**

Say: *"Read this section and highlight the signal words. What text structure is the author using?"*

Guide: Comparison/contrast. Signal words: both (x2), on the other hand, however, similarly, despite.

Say: *"Let's fill in a Venn diagram for this section."*

- Desert only: extremely hot, above 120 degrees
- Both: extreme environments, very little precipitation, animals must adapt
- Arctic only: extremely cold, below -50 degrees

**Section 2: "The Camel"**

Say: *"What text structure is this section? Look for signal words."*

Guide: This is primarily description (describing the camel's adaptations) with some cause-effect (because camels conserve moisture --> as a result, they thrive in hot places).

Say: *"Good — sometimes sections use more than one structure. The main structure here is description — the author lists the camel's features. But there is also a cause-effect relationship at the end."*

**Section 3: "The Penguin"**

Say: *"Read this section. What structure is it?"*

Guide: Problem/solution. Signal words: problem, one solution, another solution.

Help the student fill in a Problem/Solution chart:
- Problem: Penguins live in extremely cold places and must keep warm.
- Solution 1: Thick waterproof feathers trap warm air.
- Solution 2: Layer of blubber adds insulation.
- Solution 3: Huddling together and rotating positions.

**Section 4: "A Growing Challenge"**

Say: *"This section is interesting because it mixes structures. Can you find BOTH comparison/contrast AND problem/solution?"*

Guide:
- Comparison/contrast: "Both" desert and Arctic animals face climate change (comparing their shared problem).
- Problem/solution: Problem = climate change threatening both environments. Solution = scientists studying adaptations, pushing for protective laws.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Name the four text structures we have learned about. (1 point)
2. Read this sentence: "Cats and dogs are both popular pets, but cats are more independent while dogs need more attention from their owners." What text structure is this? Name two signal words. (1 point)
3. Read this sentence: "The playground was in bad shape, so volunteers came together and rebuilt it with new equipment." What text structure is this? (1 point)
4. An author writes a text that says: "First, water evaporates from the ocean. Then, it forms clouds. Next, the clouds release rain. Finally, the rain flows back to the ocean." What text structure is this? (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the text structure anchor chart and quiz the student on signal words for each structure
- Create a "signal word flashcard" set — write signal words on cards and have the student sort them by structure
- Practice with very short texts (2-3 sentences each) rather than full paragraphs
- Re-assess before moving to Lesson 8

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 8: Using Evidence to Answer Questions
- Encourage the student to notice text structures in any informational text they read

## Extension (Optional)
- **Text Structure Bingo:** Create a bingo card with text structures and signal words. As the student reads nonfiction passages, they mark off structures and signal words they find.
- **Structure Switcheroo:** Give the student information about a topic and have them write about it using two DIFFERENT structures. For example, write about recycling using problem/solution, then write about recycling using cause-effect. Discuss how the structure changes what the reader focuses on.
- **Real-World Structure Hunt:** The student finds examples of each text structure in real life: a recipe (sequence), a product comparison (comparison/contrast), a news article about fixing a problem (problem/solution), a science explanation (cause-effect).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This lesson introduces a lot of new vocabulary and concepts.** If the student seems overwhelmed, split it into two sessions. Session 1: Teach comparison/contrast with practice. Session 2: Teach problem/solution with practice, then do the combined analysis.
- **Texts often use multiple structures:** Real-world informational texts rarely use only one structure. The "Animal Adaptations" passage deliberately mixes structures to show this. Help the student understand that the question is "What is the MAIN structure of this section?" not "Is there only one structure?"
- **Common misconception — problem/solution and cause-effect are the same:** Students may confuse them because both involve "something happened and something else happened as a result." Clarify: in problem/solution, the focus is on a PROBLEM (something bad or challenging) and an intentional ACTION to fix it. In cause-effect, the focus is on one event naturally causing another — there may not be a problem at all.
- **Common misconception — comparison always means "the same":** Students may think comparison only shows similarities. Clarify: comparison/contrast shows BOTH similarities AND differences. The comparison part shows what is alike; the contrast part shows what is different.
- **Description is the default:** When a text does not clearly fit one of the other three structures, it is likely using description — simply describing a topic with facts and examples. This is the most basic structure and does not have distinctive signal words.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson deepens RI.3.3 (describe the relationship between a series of historical events, scientific ideas or concepts, or steps in technical procedures) and supports RI.3.5 (understanding how texts are organized). Recognizing text structure helps students comprehend and remember information.
