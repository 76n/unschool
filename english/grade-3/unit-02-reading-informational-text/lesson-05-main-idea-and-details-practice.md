# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 5

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Main Idea and Supporting Details — Extended Practice

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Determine the main idea and supporting details in a multi-paragraph informational passage
- Complete a main idea and details graphic organizer for each section of a longer text
- Summarize a section of informational text in one sentence using the main idea and key details

## Prerequisites
- Ability to define and identify main idea in a single paragraph (Lesson 3)
- Ability to distinguish supporting details from tangential details (Lesson 4)
- Familiarity with the umbrella analogy and three-step main idea strategy (Lessons 3-4)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "Weather Wonders" (included below)
- Main Idea Web graphic organizer (one per section — 4 total; template: circle in center for main idea, 3-4 lines branching out for supporting details)
- Highlighters (2 colors)
- Paper and pencil for written summaries
- Sticky notes

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review main idea and supporting details skills from Lessons 3-4 with a quick application, plus spaced review of earlier skills.

**Activity: Quick Paragraph Analysis**

Say to the student:

*"Let's warm up. Read this paragraph and tell me the main idea and two supporting details."*

> Ladybugs are helpful insects for gardeners. They eat aphids, which are tiny bugs that damage plants. A single ladybug can eat up to 50 aphids in one day. Gardeners sometimes buy ladybugs and release them in their gardens to protect their plants.

Ask: *"What is the main idea?"* (Ladybugs are helpful insects for gardeners.)
Ask: *"Give me two supporting details."* (They eat aphids that damage plants; a single ladybug can eat 50 aphids per day; gardeners buy ladybugs to protect plants — any two.)

**Spaced Review — Text Features (Lesson 2):**
Ask: *"Quick question from an earlier lesson: If you were reading a book and wanted to find out what 'aphid' means, which text feature would you use?"* (Glossary.)

Say: *"Today we are going to take everything you have learned about main ideas and supporting details and apply it to a LONGER passage — one with multiple sections. You will find the main idea of each section, identify the supporting details, and practice summarizing. This is the real work of reading informational text."*

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to work with main ideas and details across a multi-section text and introduce the skill of one-sentence summarizing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Summary** — A short retelling of the most important information in a text, using your own words
- **Graphic organizer** — A visual tool that helps you organize information (such as a main idea web or umbrella chart)
- **Section** — A part of a text, usually with its own heading

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: From single paragraphs to multiple sections (3 minutes)**

Say: *"In Lessons 3 and 4, you practiced finding the main idea of one paragraph at a time. Real informational texts have MANY sections, and each section has its own main idea and supporting details. Today you will read a whole passage with four sections and find the main idea and details of EACH section."*

Say: *"Here is the strategy: treat each section like its own mini-text. Use the same three steps for each section."*

Review the three steps:
1. What is the topic of this section?
2. What does the author say about the topic over and over?
3. What is the most important point in one sentence?

Then ask: What details support that main idea?

**Step 2: Introducing the one-sentence summary (4-5 minutes)**

Say: *"After you find the main idea and supporting details, I am going to teach you a new skill: summarizing a section in ONE SENTENCE. A summary is a short retelling of the most important information. When you summarize a whole section in one sentence, you combine the main idea with the most important details."*

Write on the board:

**One-Sentence Summary Formula:**
[Main idea] + [most important detail or two] = Summary

Say: *"A summary is NOT a copy of the first sentence. It is YOUR words — you take the main idea and the key details and put them together in your own sentence."*

**Step 3: Model a one-sentence summary (2-3 minutes)**

Display this paragraph:

> Hummingbirds are the smallest birds in the world. The bee hummingbird, found in Cuba, is only 2 inches long and weighs less than a penny. Despite their tiny size, hummingbirds are powerful fliers. They can beat their wings up to 80 times per second, which allows them to hover in one spot and even fly backward.

Say: *"Main idea: Hummingbirds are the smallest birds in the world but are powerful fliers. Key details: the bee hummingbird is only 2 inches long; they beat their wings up to 80 times per second and can hover or fly backward."*

*"Now I will write a one-sentence summary in my own words: 'Even though hummingbirds are the smallest birds in the world — some only 2 inches long — they are powerful fliers that can beat their wings 80 times per second.' See how I combined the main idea and the most important details into one sentence? I did not copy the paragraph word for word. I used my own words."*

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Main idea web for a paragraph**

Display this paragraph:

> Sea otters use tools to help them eat. When a sea otter finds a clam or a mussel, it picks up a rock from the ocean floor. Then the otter floats on its back, places the shellfish on its belly, and uses the rock to crack open the hard shell. Sea otters are one of the few animals in the world that use tools.

Say: *"Let me fill in the main idea web."*

Center circle: **Sea otters use tools to help them eat.**

Branches:
- Detail 1: They pick up rocks from the ocean floor.
- Detail 2: They float on their backs and place shellfish on their bellies.
- Detail 3: They crack open shells with the rock.
- Detail 4: They are one of the few animals that use tools.

One-sentence summary: *"Sea otters are one of the few animals that use tools — they crack open shellfish by hitting them with rocks while floating on their backs."*

**Worked Example 2: Finding the main idea when it is at the END of the paragraph**

Display this paragraph:

> Some plants grow in soil. Others grow in water. A few plants even grow on rocks or on the sides of trees, getting their nutrients from the air and rain. There are even plants that survive in the desert with almost no water at all. Plants are able to grow in many different environments around the world.

Say: *"The main idea here is at the END: 'Plants are able to grow in many different environments around the world.' The rest of the sentences before it are examples of different environments. When the main idea is at the end, the details come first and build up to the main point."*

One-sentence summary: *"Plants can grow in many different environments, including soil, water, rocks, trees, and even deserts."*

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student reads a multi-section passage and completes main idea webs and one-sentence summaries for each section, with facilitator support.

**Activity: Main Idea Webs — "Weather Wonders"**

Provide the student with the following passage. Read the entire passage together first, then work through each section.

---

### Reading Passage: "Weather Wonders"

**What Causes Weather?**

All weather on Earth is caused by the **sun**. The sun heats the Earth's surface, but it does not heat every place equally. Areas near the equator get more direct sunlight and are warmer, while areas near the poles get less sunlight and are colder. These differences in temperature cause air to move. Warm air rises and cool air sinks, creating wind. When warm, moist air rises high into the sky, it cools and forms **clouds**. Weather is really just the sun's energy moving air and water around the Earth.

**Clouds and Rain**

Clouds are made of tiny water droplets or ice crystals that float in the air. When warm air rises and cools, the water vapor in the air **condenses** — it changes from an invisible gas into tiny droplets that we can see as a cloud. As more and more droplets collect inside a cloud, they stick together and get heavier. When the droplets become too heavy to float, they fall to the ground as **precipitation** — rain, snow, sleet, or hail. Fluffy white clouds are usually fair-weather clouds, while dark gray clouds often mean rain is on the way.

**Thunderstorms**

Thunderstorms are powerful storms with lightning, thunder, and heavy rain. They form when warm, moist air rises very quickly into the atmosphere. As the air rises, it creates tall, towering clouds called **cumulonimbus** clouds. Inside these clouds, water droplets and ice crystals bump into each other, building up electrical energy. When enough energy builds up, it is released as a bolt of **lightning**. The lightning heats the air around it so quickly that the air expands with a loud BOOM — that is **thunder**. Thunderstorms usually last about 30 minutes, but they can produce dangerous conditions including heavy rain, strong winds, and hail.

**Wind**

Wind is air that moves from one place to another. It is caused by differences in air pressure — air always moves from areas of high pressure to areas of low pressure. The greater the difference in pressure, the stronger the wind. A gentle **breeze** might blow at just 5 miles per hour, while hurricane winds can reach speeds of more than 150 miles per hour. Wind is important because it helps spread seeds, moves clouds across the sky, and can be used to generate electricity with **wind turbines**.

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

Work through each section, having the student complete a main idea web and one-sentence summary. Provide decreasing support as you move through the sections.

**Section 1: "What Causes Weather?" — Heavy support**

Say: *"Let's start together. Read the section again. What is the topic?"* (Weather / what causes weather)

Say: *"What does the author keep saying? What is the most important point?"*

Guide: All weather is caused by the sun.

Say: *"Now find the supporting details. What facts tell us HOW the sun causes weather?"*

Help the student fill in the web:
- Center: All weather on Earth is caused by the sun.
- Detail 1: The sun heats the Earth unevenly (equator vs. poles).
- Detail 2: Temperature differences cause air to move (warm air rises, cool air sinks = wind).
- Detail 3: When warm, moist air rises, it cools and forms clouds.

Say: *"Now write a one-sentence summary in your own words."*

Model if needed: "The sun causes all weather by heating the Earth unevenly, which makes air move and creates wind and clouds."

**Section 2: "Clouds and Rain" — Moderate support**

Say: *"Your turn to try with a little less help. Read the section. Find the main idea and supporting details."*

If the student identifies the main idea, say: *"Good. Now fill in the web."*

Guide toward:
- Center: Clouds form when water vapor condenses, and rain falls when droplets get too heavy.
- Detail 1: Clouds are made of tiny water droplets or ice crystals.
- Detail 2: Water vapor condenses (changes from gas to droplets) when air cools.
- Detail 3: Droplets stick together, get heavy, and fall as precipitation (rain, snow, sleet, hail).

Say: *"Now summarize this section in one sentence."*

Target: "Clouds form when rising warm air cools and water vapor condenses into droplets, which eventually fall as rain, snow, sleet, or hail."

**Section 3: "Thunderstorms" — Light support**

Say: *"Now do this section mostly on your own. Fill in the web, then write a summary. I am here if you need help."*

Guide toward:
- Center: Thunderstorms are powerful storms that form when warm, moist air rises quickly.
- Detail 1: They create tall cumulonimbus clouds.
- Detail 2: Water droplets and ice crystals bump together, building electrical energy.
- Detail 3: The energy releases as lightning; lightning heats air causing thunder.
- Detail 4: They can produce dangerous conditions (heavy rain, strong winds, hail).

Summary: "Thunderstorms form when warm air rises quickly, creating tall clouds where electrical energy builds up and is released as lightning and thunder."

**Section 4: "Wind" — Independent**

Say: *"This one is all you. Complete the web and the summary, then show me."*

Guide toward:
- Center: Wind is caused by differences in air pressure.
- Detail 1: Air moves from high pressure to low pressure.
- Detail 2: Greater pressure differences = stronger wind.
- Detail 3: Wind speeds range from 5 mph (breeze) to 150+ mph (hurricane).
- Detail 4: Wind is important — spreads seeds, moves clouds, generates electricity.

Summary: "Wind is moving air caused by differences in air pressure, and it can range from a gentle breeze to powerful hurricane-force gusts."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student applies main idea, supporting details, and summarizing skills independently with new passages.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**

Read each passage. For each one, write: (a) the main idea, (b) two supporting details, and (c) a one-sentence summary.

**Passage A: "Coral Reefs"**

> Coral reefs are often called the "rainforests of the sea" because they are home to so many living things. Although coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor, they support about 25% of all ocean species. Reefs provide food and shelter for thousands of kinds of fish, sea turtles, sharks, and other marine animals. They also protect coastlines from large waves and storms by acting as natural barriers.

1a. Main idea: (1 point)
1b. Two supporting details: (2 points)
1c. One-sentence summary: (1 point)

**Passage B: "Honeybee Communication"**

> Honeybees have a remarkable way of telling other bees where to find food. When a bee discovers flowers with nectar, it flies back to the hive and performs a special movement called a "waggle dance." The direction the bee dances tells the other bees which way to fly. The length of the dance tells them how far away the food is. By watching the dance, other bees know exactly where to go to find the nectar.

2a. Main idea: (1 point)
2b. Two supporting details: (2 points)
2c. One-sentence summary: (1 point)

**Stretch:**

Complete the On-level tasks, plus:

3. Look back at the "Weather Wonders" passage. Write a whole-passage summary (2-3 sentences) that covers ALL four sections. Use your one-sentence summaries to help you. (2 points)

4. Compare the main idea webs you created for two different sections of "Weather Wonders." How are the sections connected to each other? Write 1-2 sentences explaining the connection. (1 point)

**Challenge:**

Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:

5. Read this passage and identify the main idea. Then explain why the LAST sentence is the main idea, not the first sentence.

> Squirrels bury nuts in the fall to save them for winter. Blue jays store acorns in tree bark. Bears eat large amounts of food to build up fat before hibernating. Chipmunks fill their underground burrows with seeds and nuts. Many animals have clever ways of preparing for the cold winter months.

Write the main idea and explain where it appears and why. (2 points)

**Review from Lesson 1 — Informational Text Purpose:**

6. What is the purpose of the "Weather Wonders" passage? Is it written to entertain, to inform, or to persuade? How do you know? (1 point)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this paragraph:

> Deserts are not always hot. While many people picture deserts as scorching sandy places, some deserts are actually very cold. Antarctica is considered a desert because it gets very little precipitation — less than 10 inches per year. The Gobi Desert in Asia has winter temperatures that drop below negative 40 degrees. What makes a place a desert is not its temperature, but how little rain or snow it receives.

1. What is the main idea? (1 point)
2. Name one supporting detail. (1 point)
3. Write a one-sentence summary of this paragraph in your own words. (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to single-paragraph practice from Lessons 3-4 before attempting multi-section passages again
- Provide partially completed graphic organizers (main idea given, student fills in details — or details given, student determines main idea)
- Practice the one-sentence summary with oral rehearsal: have the student SAY the summary before writing it
- Re-assess before moving to Lesson 6

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 6: Relationships Between Events
- Encourage the student to practice summarizing paragraphs in any nonfiction text they read

## Extension (Optional)
- **Summary Chain:** The student writes a one-sentence summary for each section of "Weather Wonders," then combines all four summaries into a complete passage summary (4-5 sentences). This practices both section-level and whole-text summarizing.
- **Teach It Back:** The student uses their main idea webs to "teach" the Weather Wonders content to a family member or stuffed animal, explaining the main idea and key details of each section without looking at the text.
- **Create Your Own:** The student writes a short two-section informational text about a topic they know well. Each section should have a clear main idea and at least three supporting details. Then they create a main idea web for each section.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This lesson is intentionally a practice and consolidation lesson:** Lessons 3 and 4 introduced the concepts; this lesson gives extended time to apply them in a realistic reading context. Do not rush through the guided practice — the multi-section analysis is where the real learning happens.
- **Gradual release is built in:** The guided practice deliberately reduces facilitator support from section to section (heavy support for Section 1, moderate for Section 2, light for Section 3, independent for Section 4). Follow this pattern. If the student is not ready for independence by Section 4, provide more support and plan a second session for this lesson.
- **One-sentence summaries are hard:** This is a new skill that combines main idea identification, detail selection, and paraphrasing. Do not expect perfection. A summary that captures the main idea in the student's own words, even if awkward, is a success. Polish will come with practice.
- **Common misconception — summaries are copies:** Students often want to copy the first sentence of a paragraph as their summary. Emphasize: "A summary is in YOUR words. It should sound like you are telling a friend what you just read."
- **Common misconception — summaries include everything:** Students may try to include every detail. Redirect: "A summary is SHORT — just the most important information. If someone had one minute and you could only tell them the main point, what would you say?"
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson reinforces RI.3.2 (determine the main idea; recount key details and explain how they support the main idea). The summarizing skill is a step toward the "recount" component of the standard.
- **All passages are original:** Written specifically for this curriculum.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice — Main Idea Webs

**Section 1: "What Causes Weather?"**
- Main Idea: All weather on Earth is caused by the sun.
- Details: (1) Sun heats Earth unevenly — equator warmer, poles colder. (2) Temperature differences cause air movement — warm air rises, cool air sinks, creating wind. (3) Rising warm, moist air cools and forms clouds.
- Summary: "The sun causes all weather by heating the Earth unevenly, which creates wind and clouds."

**Section 2: "Clouds and Rain"**
- Main Idea: Clouds form when water vapor condenses, and precipitation falls when droplets get too heavy.
- Details: (1) Clouds are tiny water droplets or ice crystals. (2) Water vapor condenses when warm air rises and cools. (3) Droplets stick together and fall as rain, snow, sleet, or hail.
- Summary: "Clouds form when warm air cools and water vapor condenses into droplets, which fall as precipitation when they get too heavy."

**Section 3: "Thunderstorms"**
- Main Idea: Thunderstorms are powerful storms that form when warm, moist air rises very quickly.
- Details: (1) Create tall cumulonimbus clouds. (2) Droplets and ice crystals build electrical energy. (3) Energy released as lightning; air heated by lightning causes thunder. (4) Can produce heavy rain, strong winds, hail.
- Summary: "Thunderstorms form when warm air rises quickly, building electrical energy in tall clouds that is released as lightning and thunder."

**Section 4: "Wind"**
- Main Idea: Wind is air moving from high pressure to low pressure areas.
- Details: (1) Air moves from high to low pressure. (2) Greater difference = stronger wind. (3) Ranges from 5 mph breeze to 150+ mph hurricane. (4) Wind spreads seeds, moves clouds, generates electricity.
- Summary: "Wind is moving air caused by pressure differences, ranging from gentle breezes to powerful hurricanes, and it plays important roles in nature."

### Independent Practice

**On-level:**
1a. Main idea: Coral reefs support an enormous number of living things (they are the "rainforests of the sea").
1b. Any two: Reefs cover less than 1% of ocean floor but support 25% of species; they provide food and shelter for fish, turtles, sharks; they protect coastlines from waves and storms.
1c. Summary: "Coral reefs, though small in area, are incredibly important because they support a huge variety of ocean life and protect coastlines from storms." (Accept any reasonable paraphrase.)

2a. Main idea: Honeybees use a special "waggle dance" to tell other bees where to find food.
2b. Any two: Direction of dance tells which way to fly; length of dance tells how far; other bees watch and know where to go.
2c. Summary: "Honeybees communicate the location of food sources by performing a waggle dance that shows other bees the direction and distance to fly." (Accept any reasonable paraphrase.)

**Stretch:**
3. Whole-passage summary example: "All weather on Earth is caused by the sun heating the Earth unevenly, which creates wind and clouds. When water vapor in rising air condenses, it forms clouds that can produce rain, snow, or thunderstorms. Wind is caused by differences in air pressure and plays important roles in nature." (2 points — award 1 point for covering at least 3 of 4 sections, 2 points for covering all 4.)

4. Connection example: "The sections are connected because they all explain different parts of weather that start with the sun. The first section explains the cause (the sun), and the other sections explain specific weather events (clouds, thunderstorms, wind) that result from the sun's energy." (1 point)

**Challenge:**
5. Main idea: "Many animals have clever ways of preparing for the cold winter months." This is the LAST sentence. It appears last because the author first gives several examples (squirrels, blue jays, bears, chipmunks) and then wraps up with the main point that ties all the examples together. The first sentences are supporting details — each one gives an example of an animal preparing for winter. The last sentence is the main idea because it covers ALL the examples. (2 points: 1 for correct identification, 1 for explanation.)

**Interleaved Review:**
6. The purpose is to inform. It teaches the reader real facts about weather (what causes it, how clouds form, how thunderstorms work, what wind is). It does not tell a made-up story (not entertain) and it does not try to convince the reader of an opinion (not persuade). (1 point)

### Exit Ticket
1. Main idea: Deserts are not always hot — what makes a place a desert is how little precipitation it gets, not its temperature. (Accept: "What makes a place a desert is not its temperature, but how little rain or snow it receives.") (1 point)
2. Any one: Antarctica is a desert with very little precipitation; the Gobi Desert has winter temperatures below -40 degrees; some deserts are very cold. (1 point)
3. Summary example: "Although many people think deserts are always hot, a desert is actually defined by how little rain or snow it gets — and some deserts, like Antarctica and the Gobi, are extremely cold." (Accept any reasonable paraphrase.) (1 point)
