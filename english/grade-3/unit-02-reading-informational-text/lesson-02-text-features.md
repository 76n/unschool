# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 2

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Text Features — Deep Dive

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify and name at least eight text features: headings, subheadings, table of contents, glossary, index, photographs with captions, diagrams, maps, and charts
- Explain the purpose of each text feature (what it helps the reader do)
- Use text features to locate specific information within an informational passage efficiently

## Prerequisites
- Understanding that informational text is nonfiction writing meant to teach, explain, or describe (Lesson 1)
- Basic familiarity with headings, bold words, captions, table of contents, and glossary from Grade 2
- Ability to read Grade 3 level text independently or with minimal support

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "Our Solar System" (included below)
- Text Features Reference Chart (to build together during instruction)
- Text Features Scavenger Hunt recording sheet (3 columns: Feature / Where I Found It / How It Helps)
- Highlighters or colored pencils (at least 4 colors)
- Sticky notes for labeling features
- Pencils and paper

**Purpose:** Review Lesson 1 concepts and activate prior knowledge about text features.

**Activity: Text Feature Quick Recall**

Say to the student:

*"Before we start today, let's see what you remember. I am going to give you a clue, and you tell me which text feature I am describing."*

1. "I am a list at the front of a book that shows sections and page numbers." (Table of contents)
2. "I am a word printed in thick, dark letters to show I am important." (Bold word)
3. "I am a sentence near a picture that tells you what the picture shows." (Caption)
4. "I am at the back of a book, and I list important words with their definitions in ABC order." (Glossary)
5. "I am the title at the top of a section that tells you what that section is about." (Heading)

Say: *"Great! You already know five text features from Grade 2. Today we are going to learn about even more text features and practice using them like tools — not just recognizing them, but actually using them to find information quickly."*

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Expand the student's knowledge of text features beyond what was learned in Grade 2, and shift from recognition to functional use.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Heading** — A title at the top of a section that tells what the section is about
- **Subheading** — A smaller heading within a section that breaks the section into smaller parts
- **Table of contents** — A list at the beginning of a book that shows sections/chapters and their page numbers
- **Glossary** — An alphabetical list at the back of a book that gives definitions of important words
- **Index** — An alphabetical list at the back of a book that tells you which pages mention a specific topic
- **Caption** — Text near a photograph, diagram, or illustration that explains what the image shows
- **Diagram** — A drawing with labels that shows how something works or what its parts are
- **Map** — A drawing that shows where places are located
- **Chart (or table)** — Information organized in rows and columns to make it easy to read and compare

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Review known features quickly (2 minutes)**

Say: *"You already know headings, bold words, captions, table of contents, and glossary. These are like your starter toolkit for reading informational text. Today we are adding new tools to the toolkit."*

**Step 2: Introduce new features one at a time (6-8 minutes)**

**Feature 1 — Subheading**

Say: *"You know that a heading tells you what a whole section is about. A subheading is a smaller heading INSIDE a section. It breaks a big section into smaller pieces. Think of it like this: if the heading is the name of a room in a house, the subheading is the label on a drawer in that room — it gets more specific."*

Write on the reference chart: **Subheading** — A smaller heading inside a section that tells what a specific part is about.

Example: *"A section might have the heading 'Planets in Our Solar System.' Under that, there might be subheadings like 'The Inner Planets' and 'The Outer Planets.' The subheadings divide the section into smaller, more focused parts."*

**Feature 2 — Index**

Say: *"A glossary gives you definitions of important words. An index is different. An index is also at the back of a book, also in ABC order, but instead of definitions, it gives you PAGE NUMBERS. It tells you exactly which pages talk about a certain topic. If you want to find where the book mentions Mars, you look up 'Mars' in the index, and it tells you 'page 12.' Then you flip right to page 12."*

Write on the reference chart: **Index** — Alphabetical list at the back of a book with page numbers for specific topics.

Say: *"Here is a way to remember the difference: Glossary gives DEFINITIONS. Index gives PAGE NUMBERS."*

**Feature 3 — Diagram**

Say: *"A diagram is a drawing with labels that shows how something works or what parts something has. It is not just a pretty picture — it teaches you something. A diagram of the solar system might show the sun in the middle and all the planets in order around it, with labels telling you each planet's name."*

Write on the reference chart: **Diagram** — A labeled drawing that shows parts, steps, or how something works.

**Feature 4 — Map**

Say: *"A map shows where things are located. In an informational text about animals, a map might show which continents have elephants. In a text about weather, a map might show where hurricanes happen most often. Maps help you understand WHERE something is in the world."*

Write on the reference chart: **Map** — Shows where places or things are located in the world.

**Feature 5 — Chart (or Table)**

Say: *"A chart — sometimes called a table — organizes information into rows and columns. Instead of writing everything in paragraphs, the author puts related facts in a chart so you can see them side by side and compare them easily. For example, a chart might show different planets and how far they are from the sun."*

Write on the reference chart: **Chart/Table** — Organizes facts in rows and columns for easy reading and comparing.

**Step 3: Quick verbal review (1-2 minutes)**

Say: *"Now we have a full toolkit. Let me quiz you quickly."*

- "Which feature has labels on a drawing?" (Diagram)
- "Which feature tells you what pages mention a certain topic?" (Index)
- "Which feature shows where things are in the world?" (Map)
- "Which feature organizes facts in rows and columns?" (Chart/table)
- "Which feature is a smaller heading inside a bigger section?" (Subheading)

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Using a table of contents to find information**

Display this table of contents:

> **Table of Contents**
> - The Sun ..... 2
> - The Inner Planets ..... 4
> - The Outer Planets ..... 8
> - Moons and Asteroids ..... 12

Say: *"Suppose I want to learn about Jupiter. Jupiter is one of the outer planets. I look at the table of contents and find 'The Outer Planets' on page 8. I would turn to page 8 to start reading about Jupiter. I do not need to read the whole book from the beginning — the table of contents helps me jump right to the section I need."*

**Worked Example 2: Using a diagram to get information**

Say: *"Suppose I see a diagram of a plant with labels pointing to the roots, stem, leaves, and flower. The paragraph next to it says 'Plants have different parts that each do an important job.' The diagram SHOWS me where each part is located on the plant. The paragraph tells me they have different jobs, but the diagram shows me exactly what the parts look like and where they are. The diagram gives me information that the words alone do not."*

**What Is the Solar System?**

Our **solar system** is made up of the sun and everything that moves around it. The sun is a star — a huge ball of hot, glowing gas — at the center of the solar system. Eight **planets** travel around the sun in paths called **orbits**. The planets closer to the sun are small and rocky. The planets farther from the sun are large and made mostly of gas.

*[Diagram: The sun on the left, with eight planets in order going to the right: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn (with rings), Uranus, Neptune. Each planet is labeled.]*
*Caption: The eight planets of our solar system, shown in order from closest to farthest from the sun. (Not to scale.)*

---

**The Inner Planets**

The four planets closest to the sun are called the **inner planets**: Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. They are all small and made of rock and metal.

***Mercury***

Mercury is the closest planet to the sun and the smallest planet in our solar system. It has no **atmosphere** — no air or weather. During the day, Mercury is extremely hot, and at night, it is freezing cold.

***Earth***

Earth is the third planet from the sun. It is the only planet known to have liquid water and living things. Earth's atmosphere protects us from the sun's harmful rays.

---

**The Outer Planets**

The four planets farthest from the sun are called the **outer planets**: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. They are much larger than the inner planets and are made mostly of gas, so they are sometimes called **gas giants**.

***Jupiter***

Jupiter is the largest planet in our solar system. It is so big that more than 1,300 Earths could fit inside it. Jupiter has a famous storm called the **Great Red Spot**, which has been raging for hundreds of years.

---

**Other Objects in Space**

Planets are not the only things in our solar system. **Asteroids** are chunks of rock that orbit the sun, mostly between Mars and Jupiter. **Comets** are balls of ice and dust that develop long, glowing tails as they pass near the sun.

**Facilitator Instructions for the Scavenger Hunt:**

Say: *"This passage is packed with text features. Let's go on a scavenger hunt and find as many as we can. For each one, we will write down what it is, where we found it, and how it helps us."*

**Finding 1 — Table of Contents**

Say: *"Look at the very top. What text feature do you see before the passage starts?"*

Guide the student: Table of contents — at the beginning — tells us the sections and helps us find specific topics quickly.

**Finding 2 — Headings and Subheadings**

Say: *"As you read through, you saw titles above sections. Some were bigger, and some were smaller and indented. Can you find a heading AND a subheading?"*

If the student struggles with the difference, say: *"The big titles like 'The Inner Planets' are headings. The smaller titles like 'Mercury' and 'Earth' under 'The Inner Planets' are subheadings — they divide the big section into smaller parts."*

**Finding 3 — Bold Words**

Say: *"Find three bold words. Why are they bold?"*

Guide: They are important vocabulary — key terms the reader needs to know.

**Finding 4 — Diagram with Caption**

Say: *"Find the diagram. What does it show? What does the caption tell you?"*

Guide: The diagram shows the eight planets in order from the sun. The caption explains what the diagram shows and notes it is not to scale.

**Finding 5 — Map with Caption**

Say: *"Find the map. How is a map different from a diagram?"*

Guide: The map shows real places on Earth — specifically where meteorites have been found. A diagram shows parts of something or how something works; a map shows locations.

**Finding 6 — Chart**

Say: *"Find the chart. What information does it give you? How is reading a chart different from reading a paragraph?"*

Guide: The chart shows each planet's distance from the sun, type, and number of moons. A chart organizes facts in rows and columns so you can compare information at a glance instead of reading through paragraphs.

**Finding 7 — Glossary**

Say: *"Find the glossary. How is it organized?"*

Guide: Alphabetical order; gives definitions of key terms.

**Finding 8 — Index**

Say: *"Find the index. How is it different from the glossary?"*

Guide: The glossary gives definitions; the index gives page numbers. If you want to know WHAT a word means, use the glossary. If you want to know WHERE a topic is discussed, use the index.

**Using features to find information — practice:**

Say: *"Now let's practice USING these features. I am going to ask you questions, and you use the text features — not the paragraphs — to find the answer as quickly as you can."*

- "How many moons does Jupiter have?" (Use the chart — 95)
- "What page would you turn to for information about comets?" (Use the index — page 2)
- "What does the word 'orbit' mean?" (Use the glossary — the paths planets follow as they travel around the sun)
- "Which section tells you about Mercury?" (Use the table of contents — "The Inner Planets," page 1)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student uses text features independently to locate information and demonstrate understanding of each feature's function.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**

Using the "Our Solar System" passage, answer these questions:

1. Name the text feature you would use to find out what "atmosphere" means. Then write the definition. (2 points)
2. Name the text feature you would use to find which page discusses asteroids. Then write the page number. (2 points)
3. Look at the Planet Data Chart. Which planet is farthest from the sun? Which planet has the most moons? (2 points)
4. What is the difference between a heading and a subheading? Give one example of each from the passage. (2 points)

**Stretch:**

Complete the On-level tasks, plus:

5. Look at the diagram of the solar system. What information does the diagram give you that the paragraphs do NOT give you? Write 1-2 sentences. (2 points)
6. Explain the difference between the glossary and the index. When would you use each one? (2 points)

**Challenge:**

Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:

7. Imagine you are creating an informational book about the ocean. Design a table of contents with at least four sections. Then create a short chart with at least three rows and three columns about ocean animals (you can make up reasonable facts). (3 points)

### Interleaved Review

**Review from Lesson 1 — Informational vs. Fiction:**

8. The passage about our solar system is informational text. Write two sentences explaining how you know it is informational text and not fiction. (1 point)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Answer each question:

1. What is the purpose of an index? How is it different from a glossary? (1 point)
2. You are reading an informational book about birds. You want to quickly find the section about bird migration. Which text feature would you use first — the table of contents, the glossary, or a diagram? Why? (1 point)
3. Look at this chart:

   | Animal | Legs | Habitat |
   |---|---|---|
   | Spider | 8 | Gardens, forests |
   | Ant | 6 | Underground |
   | Earthworm | 0 | Soil |

   How many legs does a spider have? What text feature is this? (1 point)

4. Name one text feature that uses a picture or drawing to give information. (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the reference chart and review each feature with the student, pointing to examples in the passage
- Give the student a physical nonfiction book and ask them to find specific features — the hands-on, tactile experience often helps
- Practice "purpose-driven" searches: give the student a question and have them decide WHICH feature to use before looking for the answer
- Re-assess the following session before moving to Lesson 3

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 3: Main Idea
- Encourage the student to explore nonfiction books at home, identifying as many text features as they can

## Extension (Optional)
- **Text Feature Museum:** The student creates a poster or booklet called "My Text Feature Museum." Each section features a different text feature with (a) the name, (b) a definition in their own words, (c) an example they draw or create, and (d) one sentence explaining how it helps readers.
- **Feature Designer:** The student picks a topic they know well (a favorite sport, animal, or hobby) and creates an informational page that includes at least five different text features: a heading, a subheading, a bold word, a diagram or chart, and a caption.
- **Speed Search Challenge:** Using a nonfiction book or the lesson passage, the facilitator asks questions and times how fast the student can find the answer using text features (not reading the whole text). Try to beat your own time!

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This lesson covers many features:** If the student seems overwhelmed, split it into two sessions. Session 1: review known features (headings, bold words, captions, table of contents, glossary) plus introduce subheading and index. Session 2: introduce diagram, map, and chart, then do the scavenger hunt and independent practice.
- **The shift from recognition to use is critical:** In Grade 2, students learned to identify text features. In Grade 3, the emphasis shifts to USING features as tools. The key question is not just "What is this?" but "How would you use this to find information?"
- **Common misconception — index vs. glossary:** Students frequently confuse these. Keep reinforcing: "Glossary = definitions, Index = page numbers." You can use the mnemonic: "Glossary gives you the words (definitions); Index gives you the way (page numbers)."
- **Common misconception — subheadings vs. headings:** Students may not see the hierarchical relationship. Use a house analogy: "The heading is the name of the room. The subheadings are the labels on the drawers inside that room."
- **Charts are everywhere:** Point out that charts appear in everyday life — schedules, menus, score tables in sports. Connecting text features to real life makes them feel useful rather than academic.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson directly addresses RI.3.5 (use text features and search tools to locate information relevant to a given topic efficiently). The ability to navigate text features is foundational for all subsequent lessons in this unit.
- **All passages are original:** The passages in this lesson were written specifically for this curriculum.
