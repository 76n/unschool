# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 3

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
Main Idea

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define "main idea" as the most important point the author wants the reader to understand about a topic
- Distinguish between the topic (what the text is about) and the main idea (what the author says about the topic)
- Identify the main idea of a single paragraph in an informational text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify informational text and its purpose (Lesson 1)
- Familiarity with text features and how to use them (Lesson 2)
- Grade 2 skill: identifying the main topic and key details in informational text
- Ability to read Grade 3 level text independently

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "Wonderful Wolves" (included below)
- Paper and pencil for graphic organizer work
- Highlighters (2 colors — one for main idea, one for topic)
- Sticky notes
- Whiteboard or blank paper for anchor chart

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review text features (Lesson 2) and activate thinking about what is "most important" in a text.

**Activity: Feature Finder + What's Important?**

Say to the student:

*"Quick review: I am going to ask you two text feature questions, then we will try something new."*

1. "If you wanted to find out what page discusses 'migration' in a nonfiction book, which text feature would you use?" (Index)
2. "What is the purpose of a diagram?" (To show parts, labels, or how something works — gives visual information)

*"Good. Now here is a different kind of challenge. Listen to this list of sentences about dogs:"*

- "Dogs have four legs."
- "Dogs are loyal animals that make wonderful pets for families."
- "Some dogs have curly fur."
- "Dogs need water every day."

*"Which of those sentences do you think is the MOST IMPORTANT — the one that captures the biggest idea? The others are true, but which one is the main message about dogs?"*

Allow the student to respond. Guide toward: "Dogs are loyal animals that make wonderful pets for families" is the main idea — it tells you the most important thing the author wants you to know. The others are details.

Say: *"Today we are going to learn how to find the MAIN IDEA in informational text — the most important point the author is making."*

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the concept of main idea, distinguish it from the topic, and model finding the main idea in a paragraph.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Topic** — What the text is about, stated in a word or short phrase (example: "wolves")
- **Main idea** — The most important point the author makes about the topic — what the author wants you to understand or remember (example: "Wolves are social animals that depend on their pack to survive")
- **Details** — Facts, examples, or pieces of information in the text that tell more about the main idea

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Topic vs. Main Idea (4 minutes)**

Say: *"In Grade 2, you learned to find the main topic of an informational text. Now in Grade 3, we are going to go deeper. We need to understand the difference between the TOPIC and the MAIN IDEA, because they are NOT the same thing."*

Write on the anchor chart:

**Topic = WHAT the text is about (a word or short phrase)**
**Main Idea = WHAT THE AUTHOR SAYS about the topic (a sentence)**

Say: *"The topic is like the name tag on a box. It tells you what is inside: 'frogs.' But the main idea is what the author actually wants you to learn ABOUT frogs. Two different authors could write about the same topic — frogs — but have completely different main ideas."*

Give two examples:
- Author A writes about frogs. Main idea: "Frogs go through an amazing change called metamorphosis as they grow from tadpoles into adults."
- Author B writes about frogs. Main idea: "Many frog species around the world are in danger because their habitats are being destroyed."

Say: *"See? Same topic — frogs. But two very different main ideas. The topic tells you the subject. The main idea tells you what the author thinks is MOST IMPORTANT about that subject."*

**Step 2: How to find the main idea (3-4 minutes)**

Say: *"Finding the main idea takes some detective work. Here is a three-step strategy you can use."*

Write on the anchor chart:

**How to Find the Main Idea:**
1. **Ask: What is the topic?** (What is this paragraph about?)
2. **Ask: What does the author say about the topic OVER AND OVER?** (What point keeps coming back?)
3. **Ask: Can I say the most important point in one sentence?** (That sentence is the main idea.)

Say: *"Sometimes the author actually writes the main idea as a sentence in the paragraph — often at the beginning or the end. Other times, you have to figure it out from the details. Either way, the three questions will guide you."*

**Step 3: The Umbrella Analogy (1-2 minutes)**

Say: *"Think of the main idea like an umbrella. The umbrella covers everything underneath it. The details are the things underneath — they all fit under the umbrella. If a detail does not fit under the umbrella, it is probably not about the main idea."*

Draw a simple umbrella diagram: the umbrella top is labeled "Main Idea" and underneath are three to four lines labeled "Detail."

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Finding the main idea in a paragraph**

Display this paragraph:

> Honeybees are some of the hardest-working insects on Earth. A single worker bee visits between 50 and 1,000 flowers every day to collect nectar. Bees work together to build their hive, care for baby bees, and guard the entrance from enemies. Even in the winter, bees keep busy by clustering together and vibrating their wings to keep the hive warm.

Say: *"Let me use my three-step strategy."*

*"Step 1: What is the topic? This paragraph is about honeybees. That is the topic — one or two words."*

*"Step 2: What does the author say about honeybees over and over? I see 'hardest-working,' 'visits 50 to 1,000 flowers every day,' 'work together,' 'keep busy.' Every sentence talks about how hard bees work."*

*"Step 3: Can I say the most important point in one sentence? Yes: 'Honeybees are some of the hardest-working insects on Earth.' That IS actually the first sentence of the paragraph! The author put the main idea right at the beginning, and all the other sentences give details that show HOW hard bees work."*

Write:
- Topic: Honeybees
- Main Idea: Honeybees are some of the hardest-working insects on Earth.

**Worked Example 2: Finding the main idea when it is NOT the first sentence**

Display this paragraph:

> Baby sea turtles hatch from eggs buried in the sand on a beach. As soon as they hatch, they must crawl across the sand to reach the ocean — a dangerous journey for such tiny animals. Seagulls, crabs, and raccoons all try to eat the baby turtles before they reach the water. Even after they get to the ocean, the young turtles face sharks and large fish. Only about 1 out of every 1,000 baby sea turtles survives to become an adult.

Say: *"Step 1: What is the topic? Baby sea turtles. Step 2: What does the author keep saying? 'Dangerous journey,' 'seagulls, crabs, and raccoons try to eat them,' 'sharks and large fish,' 'only 1 out of 1,000 survives.' Every sentence talks about how dangerous life is for baby turtles. Step 3: The most important point in one sentence — 'Baby sea turtles face many dangers as they grow up.' Is that the first sentence? No — the first sentence just tells where they hatch. The main idea is something I figured out from all the details put together."*

Write:
- Topic: Baby sea turtles
- Main Idea: Baby sea turtles face many dangers as they grow up. (Not directly stated — inferred from the details.)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student practices finding the main idea in paragraphs from an informational passage with facilitator support.

**Activity: Main Idea Detective — "Wonderful Wolves"**

Provide the student with the following passage. Read each section together, then identify the main idea of each section.

---

### Reading Passage: "Wonderful Wolves"

**Pack Life**

Wolves are social animals that live in family groups called **packs**. A pack usually has 6 to 10 wolves, including a mother, a father, and their pups. Every wolf in the pack has a role. Some wolves lead the hunt. Others watch over the young pups while the hunters are away. The pack works together to find food, protect each other, and raise the young. A lone wolf — a wolf without a pack — has a much harder time surviving.

**Communication**

Wolves are excellent communicators. They use howling to talk to other wolves that are far away — a wolf's howl can be heard from up to 10 miles away. Wolves also communicate through body language. A wolf that holds its tail high is showing confidence. A wolf that crouches low with its tail between its legs is showing that it is not a threat. Wolves even use facial expressions, like baring their teeth or pulling back their ears, to send messages to other wolves.

**Hunting**

Wolves are skilled hunters that use teamwork to catch their food. They mostly hunt large animals like deer, elk, and moose — animals that are much bigger than a single wolf. To catch such large prey, wolves work together. Some wolves chase the animal from behind while others cut off its escape from the sides. A wolf pack can travel up to 30 miles in a single day while searching for food.

---

**Facilitator Instructions:**

**Paragraph 1: "Pack Life"**

Say: *"Let's find the main idea of this section. Step 1: What is the topic?"*

Guide the student to answer: The topic is wolf packs / wolves living in packs.

Say: *"Step 2: What does the author keep saying about wolf packs?"*

Guide: Every sentence talks about how wolves live together and depend on each other — they have roles, they work together, a lone wolf has a harder time.

Say: *"Step 3: Can you say the most important point in one sentence?"*

If the student struggles, offer: *"Try starting with 'Wolves...' What is the biggest idea?"*

Target answer: Wolves are social animals that live and work together in packs. (The first sentence states it directly.)

**Paragraph 2: "Communication"**

Say: *"Now let's try the next section. What is the topic?"*

Guide: How wolves communicate / wolf communication.

Say: *"What does the author keep saying about wolf communication?"*

Guide: Wolves have many ways to communicate — howling, body language, facial expressions.

Say: *"What is the main idea in one sentence?"*

Target answer: Wolves communicate in many different ways. (Or: "Wolves are excellent communicators." — the first sentence.)

**Paragraph 3: "Hunting"**

Say: *"Your turn to try with less help. Use the three steps. What is the topic, what does the author keep saying, and what is the main idea?"*

Let the student work through it. Provide hints only if needed.

Target answer:
- Topic: Wolf hunting
- Main idea: Wolves use teamwork to hunt large animals. (Or: "Wolves are skilled hunters that use teamwork to catch their food.")

If the student gives the topic instead of the main idea (says "hunting" or "wolves"), prompt: *"That is the topic — the subject. Now tell me what the AUTHOR SAYS about wolf hunting. What is the most important point?"*

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student identifies main ideas independently, distinguishing topic from main idea.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**

Read each paragraph. Write the TOPIC (a word or short phrase) and the MAIN IDEA (a complete sentence).

**Paragraph A:**

> Butterflies go through four stages of life. First, an adult butterfly lays tiny eggs on a leaf. The eggs hatch into caterpillars, which eat and grow bigger every day. Then the caterpillar forms a hard case called a chrysalis around itself. Inside the chrysalis, the caterpillar changes completely. Finally, a butterfly breaks out of the chrysalis and flies away. This four-stage process is called metamorphosis.

1. Topic: ______ (1 point)
2. Main Idea: ______ (1 point)

**Paragraph B:**

> Camels are perfectly built for life in the desert. Their wide, flat feet keep them from sinking into the sand. Their long eyelashes and closeable nostrils protect them from blowing sand during sandstorms. Most importantly, camels store fat in their humps, which their bodies can turn into energy and water when food and water are hard to find.

3. Topic: ______ (1 point)
4. Main Idea: ______ (1 point)

**Stretch:**

Complete the On-level tasks, plus:

**Paragraph C:**

> Octopuses have several tricks to escape from predators. They can squirt a cloud of dark ink into the water, which confuses the attacker and gives the octopus time to swim away. They can also change their skin color and texture in less than a second to blend in with rocks or coral. Some octopuses even squeeze their soft bodies into tiny cracks and holes to hide.

5. Topic: ______ (1 point)
6. Main Idea: ______ (1 point)
7. Is the main idea stated directly in the paragraph, or did you have to figure it out from the details? How do you know? (1 point)

**Challenge:**

Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:

8. Write your own informational paragraph (4-5 sentences) about a topic you choose. Your paragraph should have a clear main idea — either stated in the first sentence or the last sentence. Then write the topic and main idea below your paragraph. (2 points)

### Interleaved Review

**Review from Lesson 2 — Text Features:**

9. Look back at the "Wonderful Wolves" passage. Name two text features you can find in it and explain how each one helps the reader. (1 point)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read this paragraph:

> Elephants are known for their incredible memories. Scientists have found that elephants can remember other elephants they met many years ago. Mother elephants remember the locations of water sources across hundreds of miles, even if they have not visited them in years. Older elephants in a herd use their memories to lead the group to food and water during droughts.

1. What is the TOPIC of this paragraph? (1 point)
2. What is the MAIN IDEA of this paragraph? Write it as a complete sentence. (1 point)
3. Is the topic the same as the main idea? Explain why or why not. (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the three-step strategy and practice with very short paragraphs (2-3 sentences) where the main idea is clearly stated in the first sentence
- Use the "which sentence is most important?" approach — show the student all the sentences and ask them to pick the one that covers the biggest idea
- Practice the umbrella analogy with physical sticky notes: write each sentence on a sticky note, then figure out which one goes on the umbrella (main idea) and which ones go underneath (details)
- Re-assess before moving to Lesson 4

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 4: Supporting Details
- Challenge the student to find the main idea in a paragraph from any nonfiction book or article they have at home

## Extension (Optional)
- **Main Idea Mix-Up:** Write the main idea and 3 details from a paragraph on separate strips of paper. Shuffle them. The student sorts them into "Main Idea" and "Details" piles.
- **Two Paragraphs, Same Topic:** The student writes two short paragraphs about the same topic (e.g., dogs) but with different main ideas (e.g., "Dogs need a lot of exercise" vs. "Dogs are very loyal animals"). This reinforces the difference between topic and main idea.
- **Main Idea Song:** Have the student create a jingle or rhyme to remember the three-step strategy (e.g., "What's the topic? What does the author say? Put it in one sentence — you found the main idea today!").

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **This is one of the most important lessons in the unit.** Main idea is a skill that students will use in every subject area for the rest of their education. Take the time to build a strong foundation here.
- **The topic vs. main idea distinction is tricky.** Expect to revisit this many times. The most common error is stating the topic when asked for the main idea. Keep redirecting: "That is the TOPIC — what it is about. The main idea is what the author SAYS about it, in a full sentence."
- **Common misconception — the main idea is always the first sentence:** Sometimes it is, but not always. Worked Example 2 deliberately shows a paragraph where the main idea must be inferred. Over time, students need to learn that the main idea can appear at the beginning, middle, end, or not be explicitly stated at all.
- **Common misconception — the main idea is the most interesting sentence:** Students may pick the most exciting or surprising detail ("Only 1 out of 1,000 baby sea turtles survives!") instead of the overarching idea. Guide them: "That is an amazing detail, but does it cover everything in the paragraph? The main idea covers ALL the information, not just one fact."
- **The three-step strategy is a scaffold:** Over time, the student should internalize the process and no longer need to go through the steps consciously. For now, encourage explicit use of all three steps.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson addresses RI.3.2 (determine the main idea of a text; recount the key details and explain how they support the main idea). Lesson 4 will deepen the "supporting details" component.
- **All passages are original:** Written specifically for this curriculum.
