# English Language Arts — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 1

> **Methodology Reference:** [Teaching Methodology Framework](../../../methodology/teaching-methodology-framework.md)

## Topic
What Is Informational Text?

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish informational text from fiction by identifying at least three differences
- Explain the purpose of informational text (to teach, explain, or describe real things)
- Identify at least four text features commonly found in informational text (headings, photographs with captions, bold words, diagrams)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to read Grade 3 level text independently or with minimal support
- Understanding that books can be fiction (made up) or nonfiction (real) from Grade 2
- Familiarity with basic text features (headings, bold words, captions) from Grade 2 Unit 2
- Experience asking and answering questions about text (Unit 1)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or displayed copy of the passage "The Amazing World of Ants" (included below)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about the difference between fiction and nonfiction, building on Grade 2 understanding.

**Activity: Quick Sort**

Say to the student:

*"I am going to describe five books. For each one, tell me if you think it is fiction — a made-up story — or nonfiction — a book about real things. Ready?"*

1. "A book about a talking dog who becomes the president of the United States." (Fiction)
2. "A book that explains how thunderstorms form and what causes lightning." (Nonfiction)
3. "A book about a princess who discovers a secret door to a magical kingdom." (Fiction)
4. "A book with photographs that teaches you about different kinds of sharks." (Nonfiction)
5. "A book about a real astronaut and the missions she flew in space." (Nonfiction)

After each answer, ask: *"How did you decide?"*

If the student struggles, guide with: *"Is this about real things or made-up things? Could this really happen?"*

Say: *"Great thinking! Today we are going to look closely at nonfiction — specifically, a kind of nonfiction called informational text. We will learn what makes it special and how it is different from the fiction stories we read in Unit 1."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define informational text, distinguish it from fiction, and preview the text features that make informational text unique.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Informational text** — A type of nonfiction writing that gives the reader facts and information about a real topic
- **Fiction** — Writing that tells a made-up story with imaginary characters and events
- **Nonfiction** — Writing about real people, places, things, or events
- **Text features** — Special parts of an informational text that help readers find and understand information (such as headings, photographs, captions, bold words, and diagrams)
- **Purpose** — The reason an author writes a text

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Define informational text (3 minutes)**

Say: *"In Unit 1, we read fiction — stories with characters, settings, and events that the author made up. Now we are going to read a different kind of text called informational text. Informational text is nonfiction writing that teaches the reader about a real topic. The author's purpose — the reason they wrote it — is to inform you, which means to teach you, explain something to you, or describe something real."*

Write on the board or anchor chart:

**Informational Text**
- About real topics
- Written to teach, explain, or describe
- Contains facts (things that are true and can be checked)

Say: *"Here is an important idea: every text is written for a reason. Fiction is written to entertain — to tell an exciting, funny, or interesting story. Informational text is written to inform — to help you learn something new about the real world."*

**Step 2: Side-by-side comparison (5-6 minutes)**

Say: *"Let me show you how different these two kinds of text look. I have two short pieces of writing. Both are about ants. But one is fiction and one is informational text. Let's read them and see what we notice."*

Read both excerpts aloud (or have the student read them):

---

**Excerpt A: "Annie the Ant's Big Adventure"**

Annie was the smallest ant in the whole colony, but she had the biggest dreams. "Someday," she whispered to her best friend Benny the Beetle, "I am going to climb to the very top of the tallest tree in the forest."

Benny laughed. "You are too small, Annie! The wind will blow you right off."

But Annie did not listen. She packed a tiny backpack with crumbs and started climbing. Higher and higher she went, past the squirrel's nest, past the robin's eggs, past the caterpillar who was taking a nap on a leaf.

---

**Excerpt B: "All About Ants"**

**What Are Ants?**

Ants are insects that live in large groups called **colonies**. A single colony can have thousands — or even millions — of ants. Ants are found on every continent except Antarctica.

**How Ants Live**

Every ant in a colony has a job. The **queen** lays eggs. **Worker ants** find food, build tunnels, and protect the colony. Ants communicate with each other by leaving chemical trails that other ants can smell.

*[Diagram: Cross-section of an ant colony showing tunnels, chambers, the queen's room, and food storage areas]*
*Caption: Inside an ant colony, different chambers are used for different purposes.*

---

After reading, say: *"These are both about ants. But they are very different. Let's figure out how."*

Guide the student to build a T-chart on paper or whiteboard:

| Fiction (Excerpt A) | Informational Text (Excerpt B) |
|---|---|
| Characters with names (Annie, Benny) | No characters — teaches about real ants |
| Ants talk and have feelings | Describes what real ants actually do |
| Made-up events (packing a backpack, climbing a tree) | Real facts (ants live in colonies, the queen lays eggs) |
| Written to entertain — tells a fun story | Written to inform — teaches the reader about ants |
| No headings, bold words, or diagrams | Has headings, bold words, a diagram, and a caption |

Say: *"Notice how the informational text has special tools to help you find information — headings that tell you what each section is about, bold words that highlight important vocabulary, and a diagram with a caption. These are called text features. Fiction does not usually have these because fiction is meant to be read straight through as a story."*

**Step 3: Preview of text features (2-3 minutes)**

Point to the informational excerpt and briefly identify each feature:

Say: *"Let me point out the text features in this passage. The words 'What Are Ants?' and 'How Ants Live' are headings — they tell you what each section covers. The words 'colonies,' 'queen,' and 'worker ants' are in bold — they are important vocabulary. There is a diagram showing the inside of an ant colony, and underneath it there is a caption explaining what the diagram shows. We will learn much more about all of these features in our next lesson. For now, just notice that informational text looks different from fiction because of these features."*

### Worked Examples

**Worked Example 1: Identifying a text as informational or fiction**

Display this short paragraph:

> Honeybees are amazing insects. A single beehive can hold up to 60,000 bees. The bees work together to make honey, which they use as food during the winter. Each bee has a specific role: the queen lays eggs, drones mate with the queen, and worker bees collect nectar from flowers.

Say: *"Let me think about this. Is this fiction or informational text? Let me check: Are there characters with names acting out a story? No. Is this about real things? Yes — real bees, real facts about how they live. Is there a made-up plot? No. Is the purpose to entertain me with a story, or to teach me about bees? It is teaching me facts about bees. So this is informational text. The author's purpose is to inform."*

**Worked Example 2: Identifying the purpose of an informational text**

Say: *"Now, what is the purpose of this passage? What does the author want me to learn? The author wants me to know that honeybees are amazing insects that live in large groups, work together, and each have specific jobs. The author wrote this to teach me — to inform me — about bees."*

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student practices distinguishing informational text from fiction and identifying text features, with facilitator support.

**Activity: Read and Analyze "The Amazing World of Ants"**

Provide the student with the following full passage. Read it together (the student reads aloud, with your support as needed).

---

### Reading Passage: "The Amazing World of Ants"

**Table of Contents**

| Section | Page |
|---|---|
| Ant Bodies | 1 |
| Where Ants Live | 1 |
| What Ants Eat | 2 |
| Incredible Ant Facts | 2 |

---

**Ant Bodies**

Ants are **insects**, which means they have six legs and three body parts: the head, the thorax (middle), and the abdomen (back). Most ants are very small — many are only about 1 to 2 millimeters long. But even though they are tiny, ants are incredibly strong. An ant can carry an object that weighs 10 to 50 times its own body weight. That would be like a person lifting a car!

*[Diagram: Labeled diagram of an ant showing head, thorax, abdomen, six legs, and antennae]*
*Caption: The three main body parts of an ant.*

**Where Ants Live**

Ants build their homes in many places. Some ants dig tunnels underground to create huge nests called **colonies**. Other ants build nests in trees, under rocks, or even inside walls of buildings. **Army ants** do not build permanent homes at all — they are always on the move, traveling in large groups to find food.

**What Ants Eat**

Ants eat many different things. Most ants eat seeds, fruit, and other insects. **Leafcutter ants** cut pieces of leaves and carry them back to their nest. But they do not eat the leaves! Instead, they use the leaves to grow a special **fungus**, which is what they actually eat. Some ants even "farm" tiny insects called **aphids** — they protect the aphids and collect a sweet liquid the aphids produce.

**Incredible Ant Facts**

- Ants have been on Earth for more than 100 million years — they lived alongside the dinosaurs.
- There are more than 12,000 different **species** of ants in the world.
- If you added up all the ants on Earth, they would weigh about the same as all the humans on Earth.

**Glossary**

- **Aphids** — Small insects that feed on plants and produce a sweet liquid
- **Colony** — A large group of ants that live and work together in one nest
- **Fungus** — A living thing (like a mushroom) that is not a plant or animal
- **Insects** — Small animals with six legs, three body parts, and usually wings
- **Species** — A group of living things that are the same kind

---

**After reading, guide the student through these questions:**

**Question 1:** *"Is this passage fiction or informational text? How do you know?"*

If the student says "informational text" but cannot explain how they know, prompt: *"What clues tell you this is informational text and not a story?"*

Look for: It is about real ants, it gives facts, it has headings and bold words, there are no made-up characters, the purpose is to teach.

**Question 2:** *"What is the author's purpose for writing this passage?"*

If the student struggles, say: *"Did the author write this to entertain you with a funny story, or to teach you something? What did the author want you to learn?"*

Look for: To teach us about ants / to inform us about ant bodies, where they live, what they eat, and interesting facts.

**Question 3:** *"Can you find three text features in this passage? Point to each one and tell me its name."*

If the student struggles, say: *"Look at the beginning of the passage — is there a list that shows you the sections? Look at the words that are darker and thicker — what are those called? Look at the picture description — what feature is that?"*

Look for: Table of contents, headings ("Ant Bodies," "Where Ants Live," etc.), bold words (insects, colonies, army ants, leafcutter ants, fungus, aphids, species), diagram with caption, glossary.

**Question 4:** *"Pick one bold word from the passage. Can you find its definition in the glossary?"*

Guide the student to use the glossary to look up the word. Say: *"See how the glossary is organized in ABC order? That makes it easy to find the word you need."*

**Question 5:** *"How would you use the table of contents? If I wanted to learn about ant food, which section should I turn to?"*

Look for: "What Ants Eat" — the table of contents tells you which page (or section) to look at.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** The student demonstrates the ability to distinguish informational text from fiction, identify the purpose of informational text, and recognize text features.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**

1. Read the two short paragraphs below. Label each one as "fiction" or "informational text." Explain how you decided. (2 points)

   > **Paragraph A:** The octopus is one of the smartest animals in the ocean. It has eight arms, three hearts, and blue blood. Octopuses can change their skin color in less than a second to blend in with rocks, coral, and sand.

   > **Paragraph B:** Ollie the Octopus was bored. He had already rearranged his rock collection twice and counted all the fish swimming by his cave. "I need an adventure," he said, stretching all eight of his arms.

2. Look at the "Amazing World of Ants" passage. Name three text features you can find and explain what each one does. (3 points)

3. In one sentence, explain the purpose of informational text. (1 point)

**Stretch:**

Complete the On-level tasks, plus:

4. Imagine you picked up a book at the library. It has headings, photographs with captions, a glossary in the back, and it starts with: "Dolphins are mammals that live in the ocean." Would you predict this is fiction or informational text? List three clues from the description that help you decide. (2 points)

5. Why do you think informational texts include text features like headings and glossaries, while fiction books usually do not? Write 2-3 sentences explaining your thinking. (2 points)

**Challenge:**

Complete the On-level and Stretch tasks, plus:

6. Write a short informational paragraph (3-4 sentences) about an animal you know well. Your paragraph must include at least one bold vocabulary word and be written to inform — not to tell a story. Then write one sentence explaining what text feature you used and why. (3 points)

### Interleaved Review

**Review from Unit 1 — Reading Literature:**

7. Think back to the fiction stories we read in Unit 1. Name one difference between how you read a fiction story and how you might read an informational text. (1 point)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Answer each question:

1. What is informational text? (1 point)
2. Name the author's purpose for writing informational text. (1 point)
3. Name two text features you would expect to find in an informational text but NOT in a fiction story. (1 point)
4. Read this sentence: "Volcanoes are openings in the Earth's surface where hot melted rock called magma comes out." Is this from a fiction story or informational text? How do you know? (1 point)

**Mastery = 3 out of 4 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the T-chart comparing fiction and informational text
- Read one more short informational passage together and have the student point out what makes it informational (facts, text features, purpose to teach)
- Use physical nonfiction books and fiction books — let the student hold, flip through, and compare them side by side
- Re-assess the following session before moving to Lesson 2

### If Mastery Is Met
- The student is ready for Lesson 2: Text Features
- Encourage the student to find an informational text at home or in the library and bring it to the next session for the Text Features deep dive

## Extension (Optional)
- **Informational Text Hunt:** The student looks around the house or classroom for examples of informational text — instruction manuals, recipe cards, magazine articles, food labels, pamphlets. They list 3-5 examples and explain the purpose of each one.
- **Mini-Author:** The student creates a one-page informational "book" about their favorite topic (a pet, a sport, a hobby). They include at least two text features: a heading and a bold word with a definition.
- **Fiction vs. Nonfiction Flip:** Give the student a simple fact ("Penguins live in Antarctica"). Have them write two versions: one as the beginning of a fiction story, and one as the beginning of an informational paragraph. Discuss how the writing changes based on purpose.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Pacing:** This lesson introduces foundational concepts that students will build on for the entire unit. Do not rush it. If the student seems to need more time distinguishing fiction from informational text, it is worth spending an extra 10-15 minutes on this before moving forward.
- **The comparison approach is powerful:** Showing fiction and informational text side by side about the same topic (ants) is much more effective than teaching informational text in isolation. The contrast makes the differences vivid and memorable.
- **Common misconception — "nonfiction" and "informational text":** All informational text is nonfiction, but not all nonfiction is informational text. A biography or autobiography is nonfiction but has a narrative structure. For Grade 3, this distinction is not critical — treat "informational text" and "nonfiction" as largely synonymous. If the student asks, a simple explanation is: "Informational text is the kind of nonfiction that teaches you facts organized by topic, not tells a true story from beginning to end."
- **Common misconception — text features are decoration:** Students sometimes think pictures and diagrams are just there to look nice. Emphasize throughout the unit that every text feature carries information that helps the reader understand the topic better.
- **Connection to standards:** This lesson begins addressing RI.3.5 (use text features and search tools to locate information relevant to a given topic efficiently) and lays groundwork for all other RI.3 standards by establishing what informational text is and how it works.
- **All passages are original:** The passages in this lesson were written specifically for this curriculum and are not taken from any published source.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice
1. Informational text. Clues: about real ants, gives facts, has headings/bold words/diagram/glossary/table of contents, no made-up characters, purpose is to teach.
2. To teach/inform the reader about ants — their bodies, where they live, what they eat, and interesting facts.
3. Accept any three: table of contents, headings, bold words, diagram, caption, glossary. Student should name and point to each.
4. Accept any bold word with its glossary definition (e.g., "Colony — a large group of ants that live and work together in one nest").
5. "What Ants Eat" — the table of contents shows this section covers ant food.

### Independent Practice

**On-level:**
1. Paragraph A: Informational text — it gives real facts about octopuses (eight arms, three hearts, blue blood, color-changing), no characters, purpose is to teach. Paragraph B: Fiction — it has a character (Ollie), he talks, he has feelings (bored), it tells a made-up story.
2. Accept any three text features with explanations. Examples: (a) Table of contents — lists the sections and helps you find information, (b) Headings — tell you what each section is about, (c) Bold words — highlight important vocabulary, (d) Diagram with caption — shows a picture of an ant's body parts with labels, (e) Glossary — gives definitions of important words.
3. Informational text is nonfiction writing that teaches, explains, or describes real things to the reader. (Accept any version that captures the "teaches about real things" idea.)

**Stretch:**
4. Informational text. Clues: (a) It has headings, (b) It has photographs with captions, (c) It has a glossary, (d) The opening sentence states a fact about dolphins. (Accept any three clues.)
5. Accept responses showing understanding that text features help readers find and understand information, while fiction is read as a continuous story and does not need navigation tools. Example: "Informational texts have headings and glossaries because they have a lot of facts organized by topic, and readers need help finding specific information. Fiction tells one story from beginning to end, so you just read straight through."

**Challenge:**
6. Accept any informational paragraph that (a) is about a real animal, (b) includes facts rather than a story, (c) has at least one bold word, and (d) includes a sentence explaining the text feature. Example: "Dogs are loyal **mammals** that have been companions to humans for thousands of years. There are more than 300 breeds of dogs in the world. Dogs have an excellent sense of smell — they can detect scents that humans cannot. I used a bold word to show that 'mammals' is important vocabulary."

**Interleaved Review:**
7. Accept any reasonable difference. Examples: Fiction is read start to finish; informational text can be read section by section. Fiction has characters and events; informational text has facts and features. You read fiction for the story; you read informational text to learn something.

### Exit Ticket
1. Informational text is nonfiction writing that gives the reader facts and information about a real topic / writing that teaches, explains, or describes real things. (1 point)
2. To inform / to teach / to explain / to describe real things. (1 point)
3. Accept any two: headings, bold words, captions, diagrams, table of contents, glossary, index, charts, maps. (1 point)
4. Informational text. It states a real fact about volcanoes, uses a vocabulary word ("magma") with a definition, and has no characters or story events. (1 point)
