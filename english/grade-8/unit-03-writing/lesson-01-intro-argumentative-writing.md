# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Introduction to Argumentative Writing: Claims and Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write a clear, debatable claim that takes a specific position on an issue
- Select and explain at least two pieces of relevant evidence that support a claim
- Identify the four-part argument essay structure using the CCER Framework (Claim–Counterclaim–Evidence–Rebuttal)

## Prerequisites
- Grade 7 W.7.1: Experience writing structured argument essays with claims, reasons, and evidence
- Grade 7: Familiarity with acknowledging counterclaims (though not yet distinguishing/rebutting them at Grade 8 depth)
- G8 Unit 2: Ability to evaluate the quality of evidence and reasoning in informational texts
- G8 Unit 1–2: Understanding that authors make purposeful choices to persuade or inform readers

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers (to create and post the CCER Framework)
- Student writing notebooks or folders
- "Claim vs. Topic" comparison chart (described in Direct Instruction)
- CCER Graphic Organizer (described in Independent Practice)
- Timer

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge from Grade 7 argument writing; surface what students already know about claims and evidence before formal instruction.

**Activity: Claim or Topic? Quick Sort**

Display or read aloud the following five statements. Students write each as either "Claim" (C) or "Topic" (T) in their notebooks.

1. "The use of smartphones in schools"
2. "Students should be allowed to use smartphones in class for educational purposes"
3. "Homework"
4. "Nightly homework does more harm than good for middle school students"
5. "The school cafeteria menu"

**Say:** "Without looking anything up, sort each statement as either a topic — a subject someone could write about — or a claim — a debatable position someone is actually taking. Be ready to explain your thinking."

After 2 minutes of individual work, briefly call on students.

**Say:** "Notice what makes a claim different from a topic: a claim takes a position. Someone could reasonably disagree with it. Today we're going to sharpen your ability to write strong claims — and connect them to the right kind of evidence."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the CCER Framework as the organizing structure for argument essays; distinguish strong from weak claims; model the connection between claim and evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Claim:** A debatable statement that takes a clear position on an issue; the central argument an essay defends
- **Evidence:** Facts, statistics, examples, expert opinions, or logical reasoning used to support a claim
- **CCER Framework:** A four-part structure for argument writing — Claim, Counterclaim, Evidence, Rebuttal

### Instruction Notes

**Create the CCER anchor chart as you explain:**

```
CCER FRAMEWORK
C — Claim: What do I believe? What position am I defending?
C — Counterclaim: What would someone who disagrees say?
E — Evidence: What facts, examples, or reasoning support my claim?
R — Rebuttal: Why is my claim still stronger despite the counterclaim?
```

**Say:** "In Grade 7, you learned to write argument essays with a claim, evidence, and a brief acknowledgment of the other side. In Grade 8, we go further. We don't just mention the opposing view — we actively challenge it and explain why our position holds up. That's the big shift this year. The CCER Framework is our roadmap."

**Say:** "Let's start with the foundation: the claim. A strong claim has three qualities." Write on the board:
1. It is debatable — someone could reasonably disagree
2. It is specific — it says exactly what you think, not just "I think this topic is interesting"
3. It is arguable — you can actually find evidence and reasoning to support it

**Say:** "Watch me improve a weak claim."

### Worked Examples

**Weak claim → Strong claim progression:**

| Weak Claim | Problem | Strong Claim |
|------------|---------|--------------|
| "Social media affects teenagers." | Too vague — everyone agrees; nothing to argue | "Social media platforms should be required to display daily usage limits to protect adolescent mental health." |
| "Exercise is good for you." | Not debatable — too obvious | "Middle schools should replace one academic class period per week with structured physical activity." |
| "I think school uniforms are a controversial topic." | Doesn't take a position | "Mandatory school uniforms reduce social competition and should be adopted by all public middle schools." |

**Say:** "Now look at this claim: 'Middle schools should replace one academic class period per week with structured physical activity.' What kind of evidence would support this? Think — what would actually prove this is a good idea?"

Elicit from students: research on exercise and academic performance, student focus and attention data, examples of schools that have done this, expert opinions from pediatricians or educators.

**Say:** "Notice I'm looking for evidence that directly connects to my claim. If my claim is about academic outcomes, I need evidence about academic outcomes — not just general evidence that exercise is healthy. That connection is everything."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice writing and evaluating claims collaboratively; connect claims to evidence types with facilitator scaffolding.

**Activity: Claim Clinic**

Present the following scenario to students:

*"A local school board is debating whether to extend the school day by 45 minutes. You have been asked to write a letter arguing for or against this proposal."*

**Step 1 (3 min):** Each student writes two claims — one arguing FOR extending the school day, one arguing AGAINST. They don't have to believe both; they're practicing writing the structure.

**Step 2 (3 min):** Share claims aloud. Together, evaluate each using the three criteria: Is it debatable? Is it specific? Is it arguable?

**Say:** "Let's test one. [Read a student claim aloud.] Can someone reasonably disagree with this? Is it specific about what it's claiming? Could we find real evidence for it? Give me a thumbs up or thumbs down for each."

**Step 3 (4 min):** For ONE claim (student's choice — for or against), students list three pieces of evidence they would use. They don't need to find real sources now — they can name the *type* of evidence (e.g., "a study showing whether extended hours improve test scores," "a survey of student opinions on fatigue").

**Facilitator tip:** If students struggle to distinguish evidence types, model: "There's a difference between saying 'students are tired' (assertion) and 'a 2024 survey of 500 middle schoolers found 78% reported fatigue after 3 PM' (evidence). Evidence has specificity."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Draft a claim and plan supporting evidence independently using the CCER Graphic Organizer.

**Task:** Choose ONE of the following prompts. Using the CCER Graphic Organizer below, fill in the Claim row and the Evidence rows. (The Counterclaim and Rebuttal rows will be covered in Lesson 2 — leave them blank for now.)

**CCER Graphic Organizer (describe for student use):**
```
Issue: _______________________________________________

CLAIM: My position is that ___________________________
       because _______________________________________.

EVIDENCE 1: _________________________________________
  Why it supports my claim: __________________________

EVIDENCE 2: _________________________________________
  Why it supports my claim: __________________________

EVIDENCE 3 (optional): ______________________________
  Why it supports my claim: __________________________

COUNTERCLAIM: (leave blank — Lesson 2)
REBUTTAL: (leave blank — Lesson 2)
```

**Prompts:**
- Should students be required to complete at least one year of a second language in middle school?
- Should schools eliminate traditional letter grades in favor of written feedback only?
- Should all students in Grade 8 be required to complete 20 hours of community service to graduate?

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete the Claim row and at least two Evidence rows with the "Why it supports my claim" explanation for each.

**Stretch:** Complete all three Evidence rows. Then write 2–3 sentences of introductory context that would appear before your claim in a real essay (the "hook" and background context that leads the reader to your claim).

**Challenge:** Complete all Evidence rows. Write a full introductory paragraph (hook, background context, claim). Then evaluate your own claim: Is it specific enough? Revise it to be more precise. Write the revised version and explain what you changed and why.

### Interleaved Review
Complete these two tasks in your notebook after finishing the organizer:

1. **Reading Connection:** In G8 Unit 2, you read informational texts that made arguments. In 2–3 sentences, describe how one of those texts introduced its central claim. Was the claim explicit (directly stated) or implicit (implied)? How did you know?
2. **Vocabulary Review:** Define "coherence" in writing (from G8 Unit 2 vocabulary). Then write one sentence explaining why coherence matters in an argument essay.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
Write a claim on the following issue (no graphic organizer needed — write it as a single sentence):

*"Issue: Whether students should have a say in hiring decisions for new teachers at their school."*

Then identify ONE piece of evidence (just name the type — you don't need a source) that would support your claim.

**Expected Answers:**
- Claim example: "Students should have an advisory role in teacher hiring because they are the direct recipients of instruction and can identify qualities that improve their learning experience."
- Evidence type examples: student surveys on teacher effectiveness; research on student engagement and teacher relationship quality; examples of schools with student advisory panels and their outcomes

**Mastery = the claim is debatable, specific, and argable; the evidence type is logically connected to the claim (not just generic)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to the "Claim vs. Topic" distinction from Direct Instruction. Ask the student to tell you what the issue is (extended school day, uniforms, etc.) and then ask: "What do YOU think about it? Say it out loud." Often students can articulate a position verbally that they struggle to write. Scribe their verbal claim and help them see it already has the three qualities.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask the student to write a second claim on the same issue from the opposing perspective. Then ask: "Which claim do you think would be easier to support with evidence? Why?" This anticipates the counterclaim work in Lesson 2.

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. Find an opinion piece (newspaper editorial, magazine column, or online commentary) on any topic of personal interest. Identify the writer's claim and list the three strongest pieces of evidence they use. Write a one-paragraph analysis of whether the evidence actually supports the claim.
2. Write a full introductory paragraph for the claim you developed in independent practice. Include a hook (question, surprising statistic, or vivid detail), two sentences of background context, and your claim.
3. Choose a claim you personally disagree with and argue for it anyway. Write the claim and three pieces of evidence. Reflect in 2–3 sentences: Was it harder to find evidence for a position you don't hold? What does that tell you about argument writing?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Many students will confuse a strong thesis from Grade 7 informative writing with an argument claim. Clarify: an informative thesis states what you will explain; an argument claim states what you believe and will defend. Both require specificity, but only claims are inherently debatable.
- The CCER Framework anchor chart should stay posted throughout Lessons 1–4 and referenced explicitly in each lesson.
- Students who worked on persuasive writing in Grade 7 may ask about the difference between persuasive and argumentative writing. Briefly clarify: argumentative writing engages seriously with the opposing view rather than only presenting your side; it's more intellectually rigorous and academic.
- In guided practice, monitor for students who write claims that are actually topics. Intervene with the question: "Could someone reasonable disagree with this?" If not, it's not a claim yet.
- The interleaved review tasks connect to prior G8 units — take the last 2 minutes of class to briefly discuss what students wrote for the reading connection, so they see the continuity across units.

## Answer Key / Model Responses

**Warm-Up Quick Sort:**
1. "The use of smartphones in schools" — T (topic)
2. "Students should be allowed to use smartphones in class for educational purposes" — C (claim)
3. "Homework" — T (topic)
4. "Nightly homework does more harm than good for middle school students" — C (claim)
5. "The school cafeteria menu" — T (topic)

**Sample CCER Organizer (On-Level, Community Service prompt):**

*Issue: Whether all Grade 8 students should complete 20 hours of community service to graduate.*

*Claim: All Grade 8 students should be required to complete 20 hours of community service before graduation because hands-on civic engagement develops empathy and real-world skills that classroom learning alone cannot provide.*

*Evidence 1: Research by the National Youth Foundation (fictional — model type) found that students who participated in structured service learning showed measurably higher rates of civic engagement in adulthood.*
Why it supports my claim: This connects service hours directly to long-term civic outcomes, which is the core purpose of requiring it.

*Evidence 2: Many professional fields — including healthcare, law, and education — consider community experience a marker of character and preparedness.*
Why it supports my claim: This shows that the skills built in service hours have real-world professional value, making the graduation requirement practical, not just idealistic.

**Exit Ticket Model Response:**
*Claim: "Students should have a formal advisory role in teacher hiring because teachers' effectiveness depends in part on their ability to connect with students, and students are uniquely positioned to evaluate that quality."*
*Evidence type: Survey data from schools that include student advisory panels in hiring, showing teacher retention rates or student satisfaction outcomes.*
