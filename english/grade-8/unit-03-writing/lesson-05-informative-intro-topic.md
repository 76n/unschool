# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Informative Writing: Introducing and Examining a Topic

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write a clear, focused topic introduction for an informative essay using the "Topic Lens" strategy (a controlling idea that previews the essay's angle without arguing a debatable position)
- Select and organize well-chosen supporting material — including facts, extended definitions, concrete details, and quotations — appropriate for an informative purpose
- Distinguish informative writing from argumentative writing in purpose, structure, and register

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1–4: Experience with argument essay structure and formal style
- Grade 7 W.7.2: Prior experience writing informative texts with organized body paragraphs
- G8 Unit 2: Familiarity with how informational texts use facts, definitions, and quotations to develop ideas
- Grade 7: Understanding of how authors use topic sentences and supporting details

## Materials Needed
- "Topic Lens" anchor chart (described in Direct Instruction)
- Informative Organizer (described in Direct Instruction/Independent Practice)
- Student writing notebooks
- Timer

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish informative from argumentative writing by purpose and structure; surface any confusion students carry from the argument unit.

**Activity: Argument or Inform? Sort and Discuss**

**Say:** "We've spent four lessons writing arguments. Now we're shifting modes. Before we do, let's make sure we know the difference. I'll give you five writing purpose statements. Mark each as A (argument) or I (informative)."

1. "To convince readers that cell phone use should be banned in classrooms"
2. "To explain how social media algorithms determine what content users see"
3. "To inform readers about the history of the Olympic Games"
4. "To persuade the school board to extend library hours"
5. "To examine the causes and effects of plastic pollution in ocean ecosystems"

After 2 minutes, discuss briefly. Emphasize: informative writing aims to build the reader's understanding of a topic — it does not take a side in a debate. The writer guides the reader to knowledge, not to agreement.

**Say:** "This is an important shift. In argument writing, you wanted your reader to believe something. In informative writing, you want your reader to understand something. The goal changes everything about how you write."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the Topic Lens strategy for informative introductions; model selecting and organizing supporting material types; clarify the informative essay's structure.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Topic Lens:** The controlling idea of an informative essay — a specific angle or question that tells the reader what aspect of the topic will be examined; it is not a debatable claim, but it does focus and shape the essay
- **Extended definition:** A definition that goes beyond a dictionary entry to explain a concept fully, including how it works, why it matters, or what it is often confused with
- **Controlling idea:** In informative writing, the central concept the essay will develop — all body paragraphs connect back to this idea

### Instruction Notes

**Create the Topic Lens anchor chart:**

```
TOPIC LENS STRATEGY

TOPIC: What is the subject?
ANGLE: What specific aspect of the topic will I examine?
TOPIC LENS: A statement that announces the topic AND the angle

Example:
  Topic: Plastic pollution in oceans
  Angle: The life cycle of a single piece of plastic waste from land to ocean ecosystem
  Topic Lens: "From the moment plastic is discarded on a city street to its eventual
               breakdown in the deep ocean, a single piece of trash follows a
               surprisingly complex and damaging journey through ecosystems."

NOT a Topic Lens (too vague):
  "Plastic pollution is a big problem in our oceans."

NOT a Topic Lens (argument, not informative):
  "Plastic pollution must be stopped immediately through international regulation."
```

**Say:** "Notice the difference. The Topic Lens tells the reader what to expect — what angle or question the essay will explore. It doesn't argue. It guides. Think of it as a flashlight: it shows the reader which part of the room you're going to illuminate."

**Introduce the four supporting material types for informative writing (write on board):**

| Material Type | What It Does | Example |
|---------------|-------------|---------|
| Facts and statistics | Provide verifiable information | "The average piece of ocean plastic takes over 400 years to fully degrade." |
| Extended definitions | Explain a concept fully | "Microplastics — particles smaller than 5 mm — result from the breakdown of larger plastic debris and are now found in drinking water, soil, and the food chain." |
| Concrete details | Make abstract ideas specific and vivid | "On a single square mile of ocean surface, researchers have counted over 46,000 pieces of floating plastic." |
| Quotations | Add expert voice or illustrative language | "'We are essentially experimenting on marine life at a global scale,' notes one oceanographer who has spent two decades studying Pacific currents." |

**Say:** "Strong informative writing uses a mix of these types — not just one. A paragraph built entirely on facts can feel like a list. A paragraph with only an extended definition can feel like a dictionary. The art is combining them so each type does its job."

**Model Topic Lens + Introduction paragraph:**

*Topic: Sleep deprivation in adolescents*

**Say:** "Step 1: Identify the angle. I'm not going to cover everything about sleep — that would take a book. My angle is: how does sleep deprivation specifically affect the adolescent brain during a critical period of development?"

*Topic Lens: "During adolescence, the brain undergoes some of its most significant developmental changes — yet many teenagers consistently receive far less sleep than their developing minds require, with measurable consequences for learning, mood, and long-term health."*

*Introduction paragraph:*
> "Adolescence is often described as a time of growth, change, and the formation of identity — but one of the most critical processes during this period is invisible to most: the rapid development of the adolescent brain. Between the ages of 12 and 18, neural pathways are being built, pruned, and reinforced in ways that shape cognitive capacity for decades to come. Sleep is the primary time during which this work occurs. Yet the average American teenager sleeps approximately 6.5 to 7 hours per night — far below the 8 to 10 hours that developmental researchers consider optimal. During adolescence, the brain undergoes some of its most significant changes; many teenagers consistently receive far less sleep than their developing minds require, with measurable consequences for learning, mood, and long-term health."

**Point out:** hook (invisible brain development); background context (what adolescence means for the brain); Topic Lens (the controlling idea named).

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively develop a topic lens and select supporting material for a shared informative topic.

**Topic: The science of habit formation**

**Step 1 (3 min):** Together, brainstorm 4–5 possible angles for an informative essay on habit formation. (Examples: how habits are formed in the brain; why habits are hard to break; the difference between habits and routines; how schools could use habit science to improve student performance)

Vote or discuss: Which angle would produce the most focused and interesting informative essay for a student our age?

**Step 2 (4 min):** Students independently write a Topic Lens for the chosen angle. Share 3–4. Evaluate: Does it name the topic AND the angle? Is it informative (not argumentative)?

**Step 3 (4 min):** For the chosen angle, generate examples of each material type:
- One fact or statistic
- One extended definition (choose a key term from the topic)
- One concrete detail
- One potential quotation (they can describe who they would quote, even if they don't have a specific quote)

Facilitator check: "Is your fact something that could be verified? Is your extended definition going beyond a dictionary entry to explain how the concept works?"

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Draft an informative introduction and plan body paragraph material using the Informative Organizer.

**Informative Organizer:**

```
INFORMATIVE ESSAY PLANNING ORGANIZER

TOPIC: ___________________________________________________
ANGLE (specific aspect I will examine): ____________________
TOPIC LENS STATEMENT: ____________________________________

INTRODUCTION
  Hook (engaging opening that leads to the topic): ___________
  Background context (2–3 sentences): _____________________
  Topic Lens Statement: ___________________________________

BODY PARAGRAPH PLAN
  Subtopic 1 (what aspect will I cover first?): ______________
    Supporting material type: __________ Content: __________
    Supporting material type: __________ Content: __________

  Subtopic 2: ____________________________________________
    Supporting material type: __________ Content: __________
    Supporting material type: __________ Content: __________

  Subtopic 3 (optional): __________________________________

CONCLUSION PLAN (gesture toward significance): _____________
```

**Prompts (choose one):**
- Explain the causes and effects of food deserts in urban communities
- Examine how the internet has changed the way human memory works
- Explain the science behind why music affects mood and emotion
- Describe the history and cultural significance of a traditional food or practice from your family's background or a culture you've studied

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete the Informative Organizer and write the full introduction paragraph (hook, background, Topic Lens).

**Stretch:** Complete the organizer, write the introduction paragraph, and draft one body paragraph that uses at least two types of supporting material. Label the material types in the margin.

**Challenge:** Complete Stretch. Then write a second body paragraph and plan your conclusion. After drafting, compare your Topic Lens to your introduction paragraph: does the paragraph actually preview what you planned? Revise the Topic Lens if needed, and explain what you changed.

### Interleaved Review
Complete these two tasks after finishing the main task:

1. **Argument Connection:** In one sentence each, explain how a Topic Lens (informative) is similar to a claim (argument) and how they are different. Use the terms "debatable," "guiding," and "position" in your response.
2. **Reading Connection:** In G8 Unit 2, you read informational texts that used extended definitions to explain complex concepts. Identify one term from those readings that was given an extended definition. In 2–3 sentences, explain how the author extended the definition beyond a basic dictionary meaning.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
Read the following three opening statements. Identify which is: (A) a Topic Lens, (B) an argument claim, (C) too vague to be either. Explain your reasoning in one sentence for each.

1. "The water cycle affects weather patterns in many important ways."
2. "Industrial farming practices must be reformed to protect global water supplies from contamination."
3. "From a single raindrop's absorption into soil to its eventual evaporation from the ocean surface, the water cycle connects every ecosystem on Earth in a continuous exchange of energy and matter."

**Expected Answers:**
1. C — Too vague: it names the topic but gives no angle or controlling idea; "many important ways" is not a specific direction for the essay
2. B — Argument claim: "must be reformed" is a debatable position, not an informative guide
3. A — Topic Lens: it names the topic (water cycle), gives a specific angle (the journey of a single drop through the cycle), and guides the reader without arguing a position

**Mastery = student correctly identifies all three and provides accurate reasoning for each**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to the CCER Framework vs. Topic Lens comparison. Ask: "Can someone reasonably disagree with statement 3? If not, is it an argument?" Then ask: "Does statement 3 tell you what the essay will focus on?" Help the student see that the Topic Lens guides without arguing.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask the student to write their own Topic Lens on any topic of their choice that avoids being either an argument claim or a vague topic sentence. Then ask them to name what type of supporting material they would use first.

## Extension (Optional)
1. Find an informative article or non-fiction chapter on a topic you find interesting. Identify the author's Topic Lens (it may not be in the first sentence — sometimes it comes after a hook). Write one sentence explaining what angle they chose and why that angle makes the essay more focused than "covering everything about the topic."
2. Take a claim from your Lessons 1–4 argument essay and rewrite it as a Topic Lens for an informative essay. What changes in the sentence? What changes in what the essay would cover?
3. Write two full introduction paragraphs about the same topic — one argumentative (with a debatable claim), one informative (with a Topic Lens). Compare them side by side and write 3–4 sentences reflecting on how the different purpose shapes every element of the introduction.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students coming directly from four argument lessons may resist letting go of the argumentative stance. If a student keeps writing claims instead of topic lenses, try this: "Read your sentence aloud. Can I reasonably disagree with it? If yes, it's an argument claim. Good for argument writing — but we're in informative mode now. Rewrite it so it guides rather than argues."
- The "Topic Lens" anchor chart should stay posted through Lesson 6. Reference it in every informative lesson the way you referenced the CCER Framework in every argument lesson.
- Students sometimes think informative writing has no point of view. Clarify: the author has an angle — a specific lens — but they are not trying to convince the reader to agree with a debatable position. The author's job is to illuminate, not persuade.
- Quotation handling will be developed more fully in Lesson 6. In this lesson, students only need to identify what they might quote and from whom — the craft of integrating quotations smoothly comes next lesson.
- Watch for students who write a Topic Lens that is essentially a table of contents ("This essay will cover A, B, and C"). Redirect: a Topic Lens is not a preview list; it is a sentence that tells the reader the angle and controlling idea.

## Answer Key / Model Responses

**Warm-Up Sort:**
1. A (argument — "convince," taking a position on banning)
2. I (informative — "explain," building understanding of a process)
3. I (informative — "inform," historical coverage without a position)
4. A (argument — "persuade," calling for a specific action)
5. I (informative — "examine," analyzing causes and effects)

**Sample Topic Lens (habit formation — Step 2 model):**
> "While habits may feel like automatic behaviors beyond conscious control, neuroscience has revealed that habits are built through a predictable three-part loop — and that understanding this loop gives people meaningful power to build better ones and break destructive ones."

**Sample Introduction (food deserts prompt):**
> "A grocery store seems like the most ordinary thing in the world — but for millions of Americans living in low-income urban neighborhoods, a fresh vegetable can require a two-hour bus ride. These neighborhoods, often called 'food deserts,' are defined not by what they have, but by what they lack: reliable access to affordable, nutritious food. The causes of food deserts run deeper than geography; they are rooted in decades of economic disinvestment, transportation inequality, and market decisions that prioritize profit over community health. Understanding how food deserts form, persist, and affect the communities within them reveals a complex intersection of economics, public health, and urban planning that shapes daily life for millions of people."

*Topic Lens (final sentence): controls the angle (how food deserts form, persist, and affect communities); names the subject; guides without arguing.*
