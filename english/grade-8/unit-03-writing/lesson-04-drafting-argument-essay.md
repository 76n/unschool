# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 3, Lesson 4

## Topic
Drafting a Complete Argument Essay

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write a complete argument essay that moves from claim through evidence, counterclaim-rebuttal, and conclusion in a cohesive, well-organized structure
- Apply the CCER Framework, ADR pattern, appropriate transitions, and formal style in a sustained piece of writing
- Assess their own completed draft against a self-evaluation checklist tied to W.8.1 criteria

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1–3: Full CCER Framework; ADR counterclaim pattern; transitions by category; formal style; concluding statements
- Grade 7 W.7.1: Prior experience with argument essay drafts
- G8 Unit 2: Understanding of how evidence is evaluated in complex texts
- G8 Units 1–2: Reading stamina and analytical thinking to sustain independent drafting

## Materials Needed
- CCER Framework anchor chart (from Lessons 1–3)
- Formal Style Wall (from Lesson 3)
- Transition Reference Card (from Lesson 3 — students should have personal copies by now)
- Full Draft Graphic Organizer (described below)
- W.8.1 Self-Evaluation Checklist (described in Independent Practice)
- Student writing notebooks or drafting paper
- Timer

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Synthesize the three preceding lessons by reviewing the full CCER structure; build momentum for drafting by clarifying each section's job.

**Activity: The Essay Skeleton**

**Say:** "Today you're writing a full argument essay. Before we start, let's make sure you know exactly what each section is supposed to do. I'm going to name the part; you tell me its job."

Ask in rapid sequence (call on individual students or use think-pair-share):

- "What does the introduction do?" (Hook, background context, and claim — orient the reader and establish your position)
- "What does an evidence paragraph do?" (Supports the claim with specific, relevant evidence and explains the connection)
- "What does the ADR paragraph do?" (Acknowledges a strong opposing view, distinguishes your position from it, and rebuts with evidence)
- "What does the conclusion do?" (Synthesize the argument, reinforce the claim with confidence, gesture toward broader implications)
- "What's the difference between acknowledging and distinguishing a counterclaim?" (Acknowledging = naming it; distinguishing = identifying its flaw or limit and showing why your view holds up)

**Say:** "Good. You've been building each piece over three lessons. Today you put them all together. Think of it as construction day — you have all the materials; now you assemble the building."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the Full Draft Graphic Organizer; model how to plan an essay efficiently without over-planning; clarify essay length expectations.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sustained argument:** A complete argument that maintains logical coherence from opening claim to concluding statement, without drift or contradiction
- **Essay cohesion:** The quality of an essay in which each section connects clearly to the others through transitions, consistent claim focus, and logical progression
- **Self-evaluation:** A systematic, criteria-based review of one's own writing against specific standards before submitting or revising

### Instruction Notes

**Introduce the Full Draft Graphic Organizer:**

```
FULL ARGUMENT ESSAY — PLANNING ORGANIZER

INTRODUCTION
  Hook (opening sentence or two — question, fact, scenario): ________
  Background context (2–3 sentences): ___________________________
  Claim (your debatable position): _______________________________

EVIDENCE PARAGRAPH 1
  Topic sentence (connects to claim): ____________________________
  Evidence 1a: ________________________________________________
    Connection to claim: _______________________________________
  Evidence 1b (optional): ______________________________________
    Connection to claim: _______________________________________
  Transition to next section: ___________________________________

EVIDENCE PARAGRAPH 2 (optional — for stretch/challenge)
  Topic sentence: _____________________________________________
  Evidence: __________________________________________________
  Connection: ________________________________________________

ADR PARAGRAPH (Counterclaim-Rebuttal)
  Acknowledge: While some argue that ___________________________
  Distinguish: However, this overlooks / fails to account for _____
  Rebuttal (with evidence): ____________________________________
  Closing sentence: ___________________________________________

CONCLUSION
  Synthesis sentence: _________________________________________
  Claim reinforcement: ________________________________________
  Gesture outward (implications): ______________________________
```

**Say:** "You have about 20 minutes to write your draft. Don't try to make every sentence perfect as you go. Get the ideas on paper first, and then you can improve the language. In Lesson 9, you'll do a full revision pass. Today is about drafting — getting the whole argument on paper."

**Discuss essay length:**

**Say:** "A complete argument essay at this level typically runs 4–6 paragraphs: an introduction, one or two evidence paragraphs, an ADR paragraph, and a conclusion. That's about 350–500 words for a solid draft. You're not writing a 10-page paper — you're writing a focused, well-constructed argument."

**Model rapid planning (3 min):**

*Prompt: "Should all students be required to participate in at least one sport or physical activity program during middle school?"*

Walk through filling in the organizer at speed, thinking aloud:

"Hook: I'll open with a scenario — a student falling asleep in class. Background: connect it to physical inactivity and modern sedentary habits. Claim: Middle school students should be required to participate in at least one structured physical activity per semester because... Evidence paragraph 1: research on exercise and academic focus. ADR: Some argue that mandatory sports exclude students with disabilities or who prefer non-athletic activities — I'll distinguish by saying 'activity programs' should include non-competitive options. Rebuttal: structured movement in any form yields the cognitive benefits. Conclusion: synthesize — schools are more than academic factories; they are responsible for developing whole, healthy people."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Complete the planning organizer collaboratively for a shared prompt; practice the opening paragraph together.

**Shared Prompt:** *"Should middle school students be permitted to grade their own work and report their own grades in at least some assignments?"*

**Step 1 (4 min):** Together, complete the Introduction section of the Full Draft Organizer. Generate three possible hooks; vote on the strongest. Write the background context as a class. Each student writes their own claim (they may agree or disagree — but must take a clear position).

**Step 2 (4 min):** Students independently fill in the Evidence Paragraph 1 section of the organizer for their chosen position.

**Step 3 (3 min):** Pairs share their evidence paragraph plans and give each other one piece of feedback: "Does the topic sentence connect clearly to your claim?" and "Does your evidence directly support the claim, or is it only loosely related?"

**Facilitator prompt:** "If someone says 'Students are more motivated when they're trusted,' is that evidence or an assertion? What would make it evidence?" (A study, survey data, specific examples — specificity and a source or type source)

---

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Draft a complete argument essay using the Full Draft Graphic Organizer; complete a W.8.1 Self-Evaluation Checklist.

**Task:** Using your planning organizer (which you may have started in Lessons 1–3 OR which you fill in today), draft a complete argument essay. Choose from the prompts below or use your existing CCER topic from previous lessons.

**Prompts (if starting new):**
- Should all middle school students be required to keep a daily reading log for at least 20 minutes outside of school?
- Should schools make mental health education — including stress management and emotional regulation — a required part of the curriculum?

**W.8.1 Self-Evaluation Checklist (to be completed after drafting):**

```
W.8.1 SELF-EVALUATION — ARGUMENT ESSAY DRAFT

Introduction
[ ] I have a hook that engages the reader
[ ] I have background context that leads to my claim
[ ] My claim is debatable, specific, and clearly stated

Evidence
[ ] Each evidence paragraph has a topic sentence connected to the claim
[ ] I have at least two pieces of evidence
[ ] I explain how each piece of evidence supports my claim (no "dropped evidence")

Counterclaim-Rebuttal (ADR)
[ ] I have fairly acknowledged the opposing view
[ ] I have distinguished my position (identified what the opposing view overlooks)
[ ] I have rebutted with evidence, not just reassertion

Transitions and Cohesion
[ ] I use transitions from at least three different categories (elaborating, contrasting, concluding)
[ ] My paragraphs connect logically — the essay flows

Formal Style
[ ] I do not use second person (you/your) in argumentative mode
[ ] I have no slang or casual hedges (kind of, sort of, I think)
[ ] My sentences are clear and professional

Conclusion
[ ] My conclusion synthesizes rather than just summarizes
[ ] I reinforce my claim with confidence
[ ] I gesture toward broader implications

After checking each box, identify: What is the ONE strongest part of your draft? What is ONE area that most needs revision?
```

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete a 4-paragraph draft (introduction, 1 evidence paragraph, ADR paragraph, conclusion) and fill out the self-evaluation checklist.

**Stretch:** Complete a 5-paragraph draft (introduction, 2 evidence paragraphs, ADR paragraph, conclusion); label each paragraph in the margin with its CCER function; fill out the checklist.

**Challenge:** Complete the Stretch draft. Then add a second counterclaim-rebuttal in your ADR paragraph — acknowledge and rebut a second opposing argument. Evaluate: does addressing two counterclaims strengthen or complicate your essay? Write 2–3 sentences reflecting on this.

### Interleaved Review
Complete these two tasks in your notebook after the self-evaluation checklist:

1. **Reading Connection:** Recall an argument or opinion piece from G8 Unit 2. In 2–3 sentences, compare that writer's method of handling the opposing view with the ADR pattern you used in your draft. What did the published writer do that you would like to try?
2. **Vocabulary Review:** Write a sentence using the word "juxtapose" correctly in a way that connects to argument writing (e.g., juxtaposing two pieces of evidence, or juxtaposing a claim and counterclaim). Then explain what juxtaposing these elements accomplishes for the reader.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
Without looking at your essay, write one sentence that names your claim, one sentence naming the strongest piece of evidence you used, and one sentence naming what counterclaim you addressed and how you distinguished your position.

**Expected Answers:**
Students should be able to articulate all three from memory in their own words. The claim should be specific and debatable. The evidence should be clearly connected to the claim. The distinction should go beyond "I said they were wrong" to identifying what the opposing view overlooks or fails to address.

**Mastery = student can accurately name all three elements; the distinction sentence shows a specific ADR move rather than a vague restatement**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Ask the student to return to their draft and read the ADR paragraph aloud. Ask: "Where exactly did you distinguish your position? What specific word or sentence showed that?" If they cannot find it, that section needs revision in Lesson 9.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "If you could only revise one section of your essay before submitting it, which would it be and what would you change? Why?" This previews Lesson 9's revision work.

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. Exchange drafts with a peer or family member. Ask the reader to identify: (1) Where is the claim? (2) What is the counterclaim? (3) How does the writer distinguish their position? (4) What is the most convincing part? Take notes on their feedback.
2. Research one of the evidence types you listed in your essay to find a specific real-world source. Add a properly cited quotation or specific statistic to your draft. (Introduce it with the source context: "According to [who/what]...")
3. Write a "rebuttal letter" — imagine the person who holds the counterclaim position responded to your essay. Write their letter (their argument against your essay), then write your reply. This is a full argument dialogue.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This is a full drafting session. Students who have been building on their CCER Organizer from Lessons 1–2 are ahead — they can spend more time writing and less time planning. Students who are starting fresh should fill in the Full Draft Organizer in the first 5 minutes of independent practice and then draft.
- The self-evaluation checklist is designed to be completed honestly — students should be able to identify gaps in their own draft. If a student checks every box and claims perfection, ask them to read their ADR paragraph aloud and identify the exact sentence where they "distinguished" — this usually surfaces real issues.
- Encourage students not to revise during drafting. The goal today is a complete draft; revision is Lesson 9's job. Students who stop at every sentence to edit rarely finish the essay.
- Save all drafts — students will return to them in Lesson 9 for revision and again in Lesson 10 for portfolio selection.
- Watch for students who write only 2–3 long paragraphs without paragraph breaks. Remind them that organization into named sections (introduction, evidence, ADR, conclusion) is a structural requirement, not a stylistic choice — readers need to see the argument's shape.

## Answer Key / Model Responses

**Sample Complete Argument Essay Draft (Proficient Level — mental health education prompt):**

---

*Should mental health education be a required part of the middle school curriculum?*

**Introduction:**
Each year, millions of adolescents in the United States experience anxiety, depression, or significant emotional distress — yet many receive no instruction in how to recognize or manage these challenges. Middle school, a period of intense social and psychological development, is precisely when students most need this knowledge. Mental health education — including stress management strategies, emotional regulation techniques, and awareness of mental health conditions — should be a required component of every middle school curriculum.

**Evidence Paragraph:**
The case for required mental health education is supported by a growing body of research on adolescent well-being. Studies on school-based mental health programs consistently show that students who receive structured instruction in emotional regulation demonstrate lower rates of anxiety and improved academic performance. Furthermore, early identification of mental health challenges — which a well-designed curriculum facilitates — is associated with significantly better long-term outcomes. Teaching students to recognize stress responses and apply evidence-based coping strategies is not a distraction from academics; it is preparation for the sustained focus that academic work demands.

**ADR Paragraph:**
While some educators argue that mental health instruction belongs in the domain of families and therapists rather than schools, this concern, though understandable, fails to account for the reality that many students do not have access to mental health support outside of school. For students from lower-income households, or those whose families do not openly discuss mental health, school may be the only place where this knowledge is available. The critical distinction here is between therapy — which schools are not equipped to provide — and education, which is precisely their mission. Teaching students about mental health is no different from teaching them about physical health: both are essential foundations for a productive life.

**Conclusion:**
The evidence from psychological research, school-based program outcomes, and equity considerations all converge on the same conclusion: mental health education is not a luxury for wealthy districts with abundant counseling resources — it is a foundational responsibility of every school. Students who understand their own minds are better prepared to learn, to form relationships, and to navigate the demands of adolescence. Requiring mental health education is not a concession to distraction; it is an investment in the very capacity for learning that schools exist to develop.

---

*Self-evaluation notes (model):*
Strongest part: The ADR paragraph — the distinction between "therapy" and "education" is a specific and effective way to address the opposing view.
Most needs revision: The evidence paragraph could use a more specific cited example rather than general "studies show" language.
