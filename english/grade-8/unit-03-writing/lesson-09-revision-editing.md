# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 3, Lesson 9

## Topic
Revision and Editing Across All Three Genres

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply genre-specific revision strategies to an argument, informative, or narrative draft using a self-assessment protocol
- Give and receive structured peer feedback using a three-move protocol (glow, grow, question)
- Distinguish revision (improving content, organization, style, and development) from editing (correcting grammar, spelling, and conventions)

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1–8: Complete or near-complete drafts in at least two of the three genres (argument, informative, narrative)
- All prior lessons: Familiarity with CCER Framework, ADR pattern, Topic Lens, SRS strategy, transitions, formal style, and narrative techniques
- Grade 7: Experience with revision in argument and narrative writing
- Grade 7 Language: Understanding of sentence-level conventions (punctuation, subject-verb agreement, pronoun reference)

## Materials Needed
- Student drafts from Lessons 4 (argument), 5–6 (informative), and 7–8 (narrative) — at least two drafts per student
- Genre-Specific Revision Checklist (provided below)
- Peer Feedback Protocol card (described in Guided Practice)
- Editing Reference Card (described in Independent Practice)
- Colored pencils or highlighters (optional — for marking revision moves on drafts)
- Student writing notebooks
- Timer

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Establish the critical distinction between revision (big-picture content and structure) and editing (surface-level conventions); create mental readiness for revision as intellectual work.

**Activity: Revise or Edit? Sort It Out**

**Say:** "I'll read six writing tasks. For each one, decide: Is this a revision task (R) or an editing task (E)? Write your answer."

1. Adding a transition word between two paragraphs
2. Changing the claim because it isn't specific enough
3. Fixing a comma splice
4. Rewriting the counterclaim paragraph because it misrepresents the opposing view
5. Correcting the spelling of "necessary"
6. Expanding a single paragraph into a full scene using sensory details

After students respond, discuss. The key distinction:
- Revision = changing ideas, structure, development, word choice for meaning
- Editing = correcting surface errors in grammar, spelling, and mechanics

**Say:** "Most student writers edit when they think they're revising — they fix spelling while leaving the argument full of holes. Today we do the hard work first: substantive revision. Editing comes after."

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the Genre-Specific Revision Checklist; model how to apply it to a draft; distinguish surface revision from substantive revision.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Substantive revision:** Changes to the content, structure, argument, or development of a draft — as opposed to only fixing spelling or grammar
- **Peer feedback:** Structured response to a peer's writing that focuses on specific criteria and provides actionable suggestions
- **Glow-Grow-Question Protocol:** A peer feedback structure: Glow (what works well and why), Grow (one specific, actionable suggestion), Question (one genuine question the reader has after reading)

### Instruction Notes

**Introduce the Genre-Specific Revision Checklist:**

**ARGUMENT ESSAY REVISION CHECKLIST (W.8.1)**
```
CLAIM AND STRUCTURE
[ ] Is my claim debatable, specific, and clearly stated in the introduction?
[ ] Does my introduction have a hook, context, and claim?
[ ] Do my evidence paragraphs each have a topic sentence connected to the claim?
[ ] Does my evidence explain HOW it supports the claim, not just state it?

COUNTERCLAIM-REBUTTAL
[ ] Have I fairly acknowledged the opposing view?
[ ] Do I DISTINGUISH (not just acknowledge) — do I identify what the opposing view overlooks?
[ ] Do I rebut with evidence, not just reassertion?

TRANSITIONS AND STYLE
[ ] Do I use transitions from at least three different categories?
[ ] Is the style formal throughout (no second person, slang, or casual hedges)?
[ ] Does my conclusion synthesize rather than summarize?
[ ] Does my conclusion gesture toward broader implications?
```

**INFORMATIVE ESSAY REVISION CHECKLIST (W.8.2)**
```
TOPIC LENS AND STRUCTURE
[ ] Is my Topic Lens clear and specific — does it name the topic AND the angle?
[ ] Does my introduction provide a hook, context, and Topic Lens?
[ ] Do my body paragraphs connect back to the Topic Lens?
[ ] Do I use at least two different types of supporting material (fact, definition, detail, quotation)?

PRECISION AND DEVELOPMENT
[ ] Have I used domain-specific vocabulary where appropriate?
[ ] Do I introduce domain-specific terms with appositive definitions on first use?
[ ] Are my word choices precise? (No "thing," "affect," "important," without specifics)
[ ] Do I use varied transitions that match the logical relationship of each paragraph?

CONCLUSION
[ ] Does my conclusion synthesize rather than summarize?
[ ] Does it show the significance of the topic?
```

**NARRATIVE REVISION CHECKLIST (W.8.3)**
```
OPENING AND ORIENTATION
[ ] Do I use an engaging opening technique (in medias res, vivid scene, significant detail)?
[ ] Is orientation woven in rather than dumped in a block?
[ ] Is my point of view consistent throughout?

DEVELOPMENT AND TECHNIQUE
[ ] Do I use dialogue that reveals character or advances the narrative?
[ ] Do I have at least one SRS unit (scene → reflection → scene)?
[ ] Are my key moments expanded with sensory details (3+ senses)?
[ ] Do I vary sentence length — short sentences for impact, longer ones for immersion?
[ ] Do I show rather than tell emotional states?

CONCLUSION
[ ] Does my conclusion reflect on meaning rather than summarize events?
[ ] Does it use specific, resonant language (not "this changed me forever")?
```

**Model applying a checklist to a draft:**

Read a sample paragraph (written on board or distributed) with 2–3 clear issues. Work through the checklist as an annotator:

> "My essay argues that schools should teach cooking. I talked about nutrition. Some people think it's not school's job, but I disagree because cooking is important for health. In conclusion, schools should definitely teach cooking because it's a life skill."

Walk through the argument checklist:
- Claim? Present but vague ("should teach cooking" — what kind? why? for whom?). Needs specificity.
- Evidence? "I talked about nutrition" — no actual evidence presented. Dropped.
- ADR? "Some people think it's not school's job, but I disagree" — acknowledges but doesn't distinguish.
- Conclusion? Summarizes ("life skill") but doesn't synthesize or gesture outward.

**Say:** "Notice I'm not fixing grammar — I'm doing substantive revision. These are content and structure issues. They matter more than a misplaced comma."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice peer feedback using the Glow-Grow-Question Protocol; apply one checklist category to a peer's draft.

**Activity: Structured Peer Response**

Distribute the Peer Feedback Protocol card:

```
GLOW-GROW-QUESTION PROTOCOL

Before reading: Know the genre and which checklist applies.

After reading:

GLOW: "I notice that ________ works well because ________."
      Be specific — name the technique and explain its effect.
      (Not: "I liked this." Instead: "Your ADR paragraph works well because
       the distinguish move clearly names what the opposing view overlooks.")

GROW: "One revision that would strengthen this draft is ________
       because ________."
      Be actionable — the writer should be able to start the revision
      immediately. (Not: "Make it better." Instead: "The conclusion
       currently summarizes — you could strengthen it by adding a
       sentence that gestures toward why this matters beyond the essay.")

QUESTION: "After reading, I still wondered: ________."
           An authentic reader question — something the draft left unclear
           or unresolved. (Not: "I wondered if it was good." Instead:
           "I wondered what specific evidence you would use to support
           the claim in paragraph 2 — the topic sentence makes a strong
           promise, but I wanted more.")
```

**Step 1 (4 min):** Students silently read one peer's draft. They use the correct genre checklist to identify one area of strength and one priority revision area.

**Step 2 (4 min):** Students write their three-part feedback (Glow, Grow, Question) on a sticky note or feedback form.

**Step 3 (2 min):** Share feedback verbally. Both writer and reviewer discuss: "Does the Grow suggestion make sense? Is the Question one the writer can actually address?"

**Facilitator tip:** Circulate during peer feedback. If feedback is too vague ("I liked it" or "maybe add more stuff"), prompt: "Which checklist item are you addressing? Name the specific technique and explain what would strengthen it."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply revision strategies independently to one draft; complete a focused revision using checklist criteria; complete sentence-level editing pass.

**Task: Two-Stage Process**

**Stage 1 — Substantive Revision (7 min):**
Choose your argument OR your narrative draft (whichever you want to include in your portfolio). Using the appropriate genre checklist, identify the top two revision priorities. Make those two revisions in writing — not just annotations.

Use this revision documentation form:

```
REVISION DOCUMENTATION
Draft I'm revising: argument / informative / narrative (circle one)

Revision Priority 1 (from checklist):
  What needs to change: _________________________________
  My revision (write the new version): __________________

Revision Priority 2 (from checklist):
  What needs to change: _________________________________
  My revision (write the new version): __________________

After revising, write one sentence: What made these two things
the highest priorities? Why did they matter most?
```

**Stage 2 — Editing Pass (3 min):**
Using the Editing Reference Card below, complete a sentence-level editing pass on the revised sections.

**Editing Reference Card:**
```
EDITING CHECKLIST — SENTENCE LEVEL
[ ] Every sentence has a subject and a verb
[ ] No run-on sentences (two independent clauses joined without
    correct punctuation or conjunction)
[ ] No comma splices (two independent clauses joined with only a comma)
[ ] Dialogue is punctuated correctly (comma/period inside quotation marks;
    new paragraph for each new speaker)
[ ] Subject and verb agree in number
[ ] Pronoun reference is clear (avoid "this" without a clear antecedent)
[ ] Consistent verb tense within scenes and sections
```

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete Stage 1 (two substantive revisions with documentation) and Stage 2 (editing pass on revised sections).

**Stretch:** Complete Stage 1 revisions on both the argument AND the narrative draft (four revision priorities total). Document all four. Complete editing pass on both.

**Challenge:** Complete Stretch. Then write a "revision narrative" (4–5 sentences): Describe the most significant revision you made in this lesson. What did the draft say before? What does it say now? Why does the revision strengthen the piece? What did you learn about your own writing from this revision process?

### Interleaved Review
Complete these two tasks after finishing the revision stages:

1. **Cross-Genre Comparison:** In 3 sentences, explain how the revision process is different for an argument essay versus a narrative. What is the "highest priority" type of revision in each genre, and why?
2. **Grammar Review:** Find one sentence in your revised draft that uses a relative clause (a clause beginning with who, which, that, or whose). Write the sentence, underline the relative clause, and explain whether it is restrictive (no commas needed) or non-restrictive (requires commas). If you don't have a relative clause, add one to a sentence where it would improve precision.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
Read this sentence from a student's argument essay conclusion:

*"In conclusion, I argued that schools should teach coding. I talked about jobs and I talked about critical thinking. The other side said it costs money but I said that was wrong. So schools should teach coding."*

List three specific revisions this paragraph needs. For each, name the checklist category and describe exactly what change would improve it.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Conclusion does not synthesize — it summarizes each point as a list. Checklist: Conclusion. Revision: Replace the list with a sentence that shows what all the evidence adds up to: "Taken together, the evidence from employment data and cognitive research establishes that coding instruction is both an economic investment and an educational one."
2. ADR section (embedded in conclusion) is present but only acknowledges, not distinguishes. Checklist: Counterclaim-Rebuttal. Revision: "Rather than conceding that cost is prohibitive, a stronger rebuttal would distinguish: note that implementation cost projections consistently underestimate the long-term economic return of a technically literate workforce."
3. Formal style — "I argued," "I talked about," "I said" are first-person academic hedges that undermine the essay's authority. Checklist: Formal Style. Revision: Remove "I argued/talked/said" and state the content directly: "The evidence demonstrates that..." / "The cost objection fails to account for..."

**Mastery = student identifies at least two specific, accurate revision needs; names the relevant checklist category; provides an actionable, specific revision direction (not just "make it better")**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Use the checklist as a guided reading tool. Ask: "Let's look at just the Conclusion checklist item. Does this paragraph synthesize or summarize? Find the sentence where it should synthesize and tell me what you would write there." Provide one checklist category at a time.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "Now take one of your three revisions and actually write the improved version of that sentence or paragraph. You have 90 seconds." This moves the student from analysis to application.

## Extension (Optional)
1. Complete a full revision of one draft using all items on the appropriate genre checklist. Create a "before and after" document that shows the original and revised versions of your three most significant changes.
2. Write a "Revision Letter" (2–3 paragraphs) to the facilitator explaining the choices you made in revising your essay: What did you change, why did you change it, and how does the revised version better achieve the purpose of the genre?
3. Apply the narrative checklist to a piece of published writing (a short story, personal essay, or narrative non-fiction excerpt). Identify what the published writer does well and what, hypothetically, you might suggest they revise if the work were a student draft.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most common student error during revision is editing instead of revising — fixing typos and calling it done. The revision documentation form forces substantive change by requiring students to write the new version, not just annotate.
- The Glow-Grow-Question Protocol produces better peer feedback than open-ended "tell them what you think" approaches. Monitor for feedback that is too general (Grow: "make it clearer") — redirect to the checklist: "Which checklist item are you addressing? What specifically would be clearer?"
- Students who struggle to find their own revision priorities may need you to ask a guiding question based on the checklist: "Read your ADR paragraph. Point to where you distinguish. If you can't find it, that's your revision priority."
- Today's lesson sets up Lesson 10's portfolio work. Students should leave with at least one revised draft they are willing to present as a strong piece. Remind them that revision is not failure — it's professionalism.
- For students who are significantly behind on drafts, prioritize getting one complete draft of any genre into revision rather than trying to revise all three. Portfolio depth on one piece is more valuable than three half-finished drafts.

## Answer Key / Model Responses

**Warm-Up: Revise or Edit?**
1. Adding a transition — R (content/cohesion change)
2. Changing the claim — R (structural content change)
3. Fixing a comma splice — E (mechanical correction)
4. Rewriting the counterclaim — R (substantial content change)
5. Fixing spelling — E (mechanical correction)
6. Expanding a paragraph into a scene — R (development change)

**Sample Revised Argument Paragraph (from model paragraph in Direct Instruction):**

*Original:* "My essay argues that schools should teach cooking. I talked about nutrition. Some people think it's not school's job, but I disagree because cooking is important for health. In conclusion, schools should definitely teach cooking because it's a life skill."

*Revised (substantive revision applied):*
> "The evidence from nutritional science and public health research supports a clear conclusion: schools that incorporate practical cooking instruction equip students with the knowledge and skills to make informed dietary choices — choices that have measurable, lifelong consequences for health outcomes. While critics argue that cooking instruction falls outside the academic mandate of public schools, this objection misunderstands what an academic mandate requires: the development of capable, self-sufficient people. Teaching students to feed themselves well is not peripheral to that mission. It is foundational to it. Schools that leave practical nutrition and food preparation entirely to chance or family circumstance are abdicating a responsibility with consequences as significant as any academic gap."

*Checklist comparison:*
- Claim: Now implies a specific, arguable position
- Evidence: Refers to nutritional science and public health research (types named)
- ADR: "While critics argue...this objection misunderstands" — full distinguish move
- Formal style: First person eliminated; tone is authoritative without casual hedging
- Conclusion (as integrated): synthesizes and gestures outward (compares to academic gap)
