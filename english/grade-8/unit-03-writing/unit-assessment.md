# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 3: Writing

## Unit Assessment

### Overview

This assessment evaluates students' mastery of the three major writing modes studied in Unit 3: argumentative writing (W.8.1), informative/explanatory writing (W.8.2), and narrative writing (W.8.3). It includes three writing tasks — one per standard — and five short-answer questions testing revision and editing knowledge.

| Component | Points | Standard |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| Task 1: Argument Writing | 20 points | W.8.1 |
| Task 2: Informative Writing | 20 points | W.8.2 |
| Task 3: Narrative Writing | 20 points | W.8.3 |
| Short-Answer Questions (5 × 0 or 2 pts each) | Scored separately — not included in 60-pt total (see note) | W.8.1–3 |
| **Total** | **60 points** | |

**Mastery threshold:** 70% = 42 out of 60 points
**Recommended administration time:** 55–65 minutes
**Conditions:** Students may use graphic organizer planning time (5–8 minutes) before each writing task. Students may use their Transition Reference Card and domain-specific vocabulary notes. Drafts are written fresh — students may not submit portfolio pieces from lessons.
**Note on short-answer questions:** The 5 short-answer questions (10 points total) are included as diagnostic evidence for revision/editing skills but are not counted in the 60-point mastery calculation. Facilitators may count them separately for formative reporting.

## Task 1: Argument Writing (W.8.1)

### Prompt

You are a student representative writing a formal letter to your school's administration. Your letter will argue for or against the following proposal:

*The school administration is considering implementing a policy that would require all students in Grades 6–8 to complete a 20-minute daily "Digital Detox" period — a scheduled time with no phone, tablet, or computer access — during the school day.*

Write a well-organized argument essay in the form of a formal letter. Your letter should:
- Clearly state your position (for or against the Digital Detox period) in a specific, debatable claim
- Support your claim with at least two reasons supported by relevant evidence or logical reasoning
- Acknowledge and distinguish an opposing viewpoint using the ADR pattern (Acknowledge–Distinguish–Rebuttal)
- Use transitions to create cohesion and clarify the relationship between ideas
- Maintain a formal style appropriate for a letter to school administration
- End with a strong concluding statement that synthesizes your argument and gestures toward its significance

**Audience:** School administration
**Purpose:** To persuade with a reasoned argument

**Requirements checklist (student self-check before submitting):**
- [ ] My claim is specific and debatable (not just a topic)
- [ ] I have at least two pieces of supporting evidence with explanation
- [ ] I have an ADR paragraph: Acknowledge (fair), Distinguish (identifies flaw), Rebuttal (evidence-based)
- [ ] I use transitions from at least two different categories
- [ ] My style is formal throughout (no second person in argument mode, no slang)
- [ ] My conclusion synthesizes and gestures outward

---

### Task 1 Rubric — Argument Writing (W.8.1) — 20 Points

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|----------------|--------------|
| **Claim** (4 pts) | No clear claim, or claim is a topic rather than a position | Claim is present but vague or not debatable; lacks specificity | Claim is debatable, specific, and clearly stated; connected to the context of the prompt | Claim is precise, compelling, and strategically positioned; demonstrates command of the issue |
| **Evidence and Development** (4 pts) | Little or no evidence; evidence is unrelated to claim; no explanation | Some evidence present; connection to claim is unclear or undeveloped; "dropped evidence" | At least two pieces of relevant evidence; each explained in relation to the claim; no dropped evidence | Evidence is well-chosen, specific, and fully integrated; explanations demonstrate nuanced analysis |
| **Counterclaim — ADR** (4 pts) | No counterclaim addressed | Opposing view briefly mentioned but not fairly represented; no distinguish or rebuttal | Opposing view fairly acknowledged; distinguish move identifies a specific flaw; rebuttal includes evidence or reasoning | Counterclaim fully steelmanned (presented at its strongest); distinction is sharp and original; rebuttal is persuasive and evidence-based |
| **Transitions and Cohesion** (4 pts) | Transitions absent or misused; essay difficult to follow | Some transitions present; mostly from one category; some logical gaps | Transitions from at least two categories; essay flows logically; paragraphs connect clearly | Varied, precise transitions throughout; the logical relationship between every paragraph is explicitly and correctly signaled |

## Task 2: Informative Writing (W.8.2)

### Prompt

Your school's science or social studies magazine has invited Grade 8 students to submit a short informative article on a topic of their choice from the list below. Your article will be read by students in Grades 6–8 and by adult readers in the community.

**Choose one topic:**
- How misinformation spreads online and why it is difficult to correct
- The science of why music affects our emotions
- How cities are designed to manage extreme weather events
- The history and cultural significance of a traditional food or art form from a culture you have studied

Write a focused informative essay that:
- Introduces your topic with a clear Topic Lens (an angle, not a debatable position)
- Develops the topic using well-chosen facts, at least one extended definition, at least one concrete detail, and (where appropriate) a quotation or reference to an expert
- Uses domain-specific vocabulary with appropriate introduction (appositive technique)
- Uses varied transitions that match the organizational structure of your essay
- Maintains a formal style throughout
- Concludes with a statement that synthesizes the information and underscores the topic's significance

**Audience:** Middle school students and community adults
**Purpose:** To inform and deepen understanding

**Requirements checklist:**
- [ ] My Topic Lens names the topic AND a specific angle (not a claim, not a vague topic)
- [ ] I use at least three types of supporting material (fact, extended definition, concrete detail, quotation)
- [ ] I use at least one domain-specific term with an appositive introduction
- [ ] I use transitions from at least two organizational categories
- [ ] My style is formal throughout
- [ ] My conclusion synthesizes and shows significance (does not just summarize)

---

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|----------------|--------------|

## Task 3: Narrative Writing (W.8.3)

### Prompt

Write a narrative about a moment when you discovered something unexpected — about yourself, about another person, about a place, or about how something works. The experience can be real or imagined, but the narrative should feel specific and grounded in detail.

Your narrative should:
- Begin with an engaging opening that orients the reader (using at least one of the three opening techniques: in medias res, vivid scene-setting, or significant detail)
- Develop the experience using at least one scene with sensory details, dialogue (at least one line), and reflection
- Use the Scene–Reflection–Scene (SRS) structure for at least one central moment
- Use precise words, sensory language, and varied sentence lengths
- End with a conclusion that reflects on the meaning of the experience (does not summarize; uses specific, resonant language)

**Audience:** A reader who has not shared this experience
**Purpose:** To engage the reader and convey the meaning of the experience

**Requirements checklist:**
- [ ] My opening uses at least one named technique (in medias res / vivid scene / significant detail)
- [ ] Orientation is woven in (not dumped in a block)
- [ ] I have at least one piece of dialogue
- [ ] I have at least one SRS unit (scene → reflection → scene or resolution)
- [ ] I use at least three sensory details from different senses
- [ ] I vary sentence length (at least one short sentence for impact)
- [ ] My conclusion reflects on meaning (does not summarize)

---

### Task 3 Rubric — Narrative Writing (W.8.3) — 20 Points

| Criterion | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|-----------|--------------|----------------|----------------|--------------|
| **Opening and Orientation** (4 pts) | No clear opening technique; orientation is absent or presented as a flat block of background | Opening present but generic; orientation partially woven in; limited engagement | Clear opening technique used; orientation woven into action or description; reader is engaged and oriented | Opening technique is executed with skill; orientation is seamlessly embedded; first sentences create immediate curiosity or emotional connection |
| **Narrative Techniques** (4 pts) | No dialogue, sensory detail, or structure — a list of events | Some dialogue or detail; limited SRS awareness; mostly summary | Dialogue used purposefully; at least one SRS unit; sensory details from multiple senses | Dialogue reveals character through subtext; SRS structure creates genuine depth; sensory details are specific and evocative |
| **Pacing and Development** (4 pts) | No variation in pacing — everything treated equally; no expansion of key moments | Some expansion of key moments; pacing uneven; some important moments compressed when they should be scenes | Key moment(s) expanded into full scenes; transitions between scenes use compression effectively | Pacing is controlled with evident craft; the writer's choices about what to slow down and what to skip reveal genuine storytelling awareness |
| **Precise Language and Sensory Details** (4 pts) | Vague, general language throughout; no sensory engagement | Some specific language; limited sensory details; sentence length uniform | Precise word choices throughout; sensory details from at least three senses; sentence length varied (short + long for effect) | Language is vivid, precise, and distinctive; sensory details are unexpected and resonant; sentence rhythm contributes to tone and pacing |
| **Conclusion and Reflection** (4 pts) | No conclusion or conclusion only summarizes events | Conclusion present but summarizes rather than reflects; "this changed me" without specificity | Conclusion reflects on meaning; uses specific language; does not summarize; provides a sense of closure | Conclusion is resonant and earned; the reflection reveals an insight the reader could not have anticipated; specific language creates a lasting impression |

## Short-Answer Questions — Revision and Editing (diagnostic, not included in 60-pt total)

*Answer each question in 1–3 sentences. These questions test your ability to apply revision and editing knowledge.*

**Question 1:** A student writes the following claim: "Students should think about whether or not social media is something they should use." Explain what is weak about this claim and rewrite it as a strong, specific, debatable claim.

**Question 2:** A student writes this conclusion: "In conclusion, I argued about three things: evidence, the other side, and my claim. So you can see that my argument is right." Identify two specific problems with this conclusion and explain what revisions would address each.

**Question 3:** Read the following passage and identify whether the underlined transition is correct or incorrect. Explain your reasoning and, if incorrect, provide a better choice.

*"Regular exercise has been shown to improve focus and reduce anxiety. **Furthermore**, some critics argue that mandatory physical activity programs exclude students with physical disabilities."*

Underlined word: "Furthermore"

**Question 4:** A student writes: "Me and my friend went to the park and it was really fun and then we ate lunch and then we went home." Identify two editing issues in this sentence and provide a corrected version.

**Question 5:** Explain the difference between "acknowledging" and "distinguishing" a counterclaim in an argument essay. Give a one-sentence example of each.

---

### Short-Answer Answer Key

**Q1:** Weak: The claim is not debatable (it only suggests students "should think about" something — not a position) and is vague (what specifically about social media?). Strong revision example: "Middle school students should limit social media use to 30 minutes per day because unrestricted access during early adolescence is correlated with measurable declines in sleep quality and academic focus."

**Q2:** Problems: (1) Conclusion summarizes the essay's structure rather than synthesizing the argument's content ("I argued about three things: evidence, the other side, and my claim") — revision: replace the list with a sentence that shows what all the evidence adds up to. (2) "So you can see that my argument is right" is informal and makes a claim without reinforcing it with synthesis — revision: close with an authoritative statement that gestures toward broader significance ("The evidence demonstrates that...").

**Q3:** Incorrect. "Furthermore" signals that the writer is adding a similar point. But the sentence introduces a contrasting or opposing viewpoint (critics' objection). A better choice: "While" or "Although" (at the start of a combined sentence) OR "However, some critics argue..." — a contrast transition.

**Q4:** Issues: (1) Subject pronoun error: "Me and my friend" should be "My friend and I" (pronoun in subject position). (2) Run-on sentence / lack of variety — four clauses connected with "and then." Corrected version: "My friend and I went to the park. After spending the morning there, we had lunch and then headed home."

**Q5:** Acknowledging = naming the opposing view without explaining its flaw: "Some argue that social media should not be restricted for teenagers." Distinguishing = identifying what the opposing view overlooks or where its logic breaks down: "While some argue that restricting social media infringes on student autonomy, this position fails to account for the neurological vulnerability of the adolescent brain to addictive reinforcement loops — a factor that makes unconstrained access categorically different for teenagers than for adults."

## Model Responses at the Proficient Level

### Task 1 Model Response — Argument (Proficient Level)

---

*Dear Members of the School Administration,*

I am writing to express my support for the proposed Digital Detox period. While the idea of mandating technology-free time may seem counterintuitive in an era defined by digital connectivity, the evidence from attention research and classroom experience makes a compelling case that brief, structured breaks from screens can meaningfully improve students' readiness to learn.

The primary benefit of a daily Digital Detox period is its effect on attentional capacity. Research on sustained attention in adolescents consistently shows that continuous screen engagement — even when academic in purpose — reduces the brain's ability to maintain focus for extended periods. A 20-minute structured break without digital stimulation allows the default mode network of the brain to consolidate recent information and reset attentional resources. Students who return to instruction after such a break demonstrate measurably better focus during subsequent learning segments. The investment of 20 minutes, in other words, returns dividends in every class period that follows.

While some students and parents may argue that a mandatory technology-free period infringes on student autonomy and fails to respect students' ability to manage their own digital habits, this concern, though understandable, does not account for the developmental realities of early adolescence. The capacity for self-regulated attention is precisely what is still developing in the 11–14 age range — and expecting students to voluntarily disengage from platforms specifically engineered to maintain engagement is not a reasonable expectation. The Digital Detox period does not assume students lack discipline; it acknowledges that even disciplined adults benefit from structured recovery time.

The evidence from cognitive science, combined with the observed experience of educators, makes a strong case for the Digital Detox period. Schools are responsible not only for delivering content but for creating the cognitive conditions in which learning can occur. Twenty minutes of intentional quiet, built into the structure of the day, is not a retreat from learning — it is one of the most efficient investments a school can make in the quality of everything that comes before and after it.

Respectfully,
[Student Name]

### Task 2 Model Response — Informative (Proficient Level)

---

**Why Misinformation Spreads and Why It Stays**

In the seconds it takes to scroll past a news headline, the human brain has already decided whether to believe it — and that decision is made largely before any reading occurs. This rapid, largely unconscious process of credibility assessment is central to understanding why misinformation spreads so efficiently online: it exploits the shortcuts that human cognition was built to rely on, using the very mechanisms that help people process information quickly against their ability to process it accurately.

The first mechanism that makes misinformation so persistent is the illusory truth effect — a cognitive phenomenon in which repeated exposure to a statement increases its perceived credibility, regardless of whether the statement is accurate. Psychologists who study this effect have found that subjects who encountered false statements multiple times rated them as more likely to be true than novel false statements, even when subjects were initially told the statements were false. In the social media environment, where content is algorithmically amplified through sharing and engagement, this effect is dramatically accelerated: a false claim that spreads widely becomes, for many users, more familiar than any correction.

Compounding this is the challenge of correction itself. Researchers who study the "backfire effect" — the tendency of corrections to paradoxically strengthen false beliefs in some individuals — have documented that factual rebuttals can fail when the original belief is tied to identity or group membership. Specifically, when misinformation aligns with a person's existing worldview, correcting it can feel like an attack rather than information. As a result, corrections face an asymmetric battle: the original misinformation travels easily because it confirms what people already suspect; the correction must overcome both the illusory truth of the original and the defensive resistance of the audience.

Understanding how misinformation spreads is not a passive academic exercise. It is the foundation of media literacy — the skills people need to be informed participants in a world where the line between fact and fabrication is, by design, increasingly difficult to see.

### Task 3 Model Response — Narrative (Proficient Level)

---

**The Jar**

My grandmother kept a jar of buttons in the kitchen drawer, and she always told me that every button came from a story. I was nine when she said this for the first time and I thought she was being poetic in the way old people are poetic — in the way that says something general and nods at you, waiting. I nodded back. I forgot about it.

The winter I turned thirteen, I was the only one home with her on a Tuesday afternoon when she asked me to get the jar. She had been sick that fall — nothing dramatic, just a slow diminishing that I was too young to name but old enough to feel. I pulled open the drawer and the jar was heavy in my hands, surprisingly so, as though the buttons had accumulated weight over decades rather than just sitting there.

She told me about each one. The green button from a coat she wore to her first job interview, 1971, in a city she had never been to before. The small mother-of-pearl circle from a wedding dress that had belonged to her own grandmother and was cut apart when it finally became too fragile to preserve. The mismatched brown button she had never been able to explain — she had found it on a bus somewhere in her thirties and kept it because it seemed like something worth keeping.

I sat there for an hour. I didn't know that I was being handed something, exactly — that the buttons were not just stories but a kind of instruction. Pay attention. Keep things that seem worth keeping. The things that matter are often inexplicable and always small.

When I left that afternoon, the jar went back in the drawer. My grandmother died the following spring. I did not take the buttons. I wish, now, that I had — not for the objects themselves but for what it would mean to be able to open a drawer and find the whole weight of a life in glass.
