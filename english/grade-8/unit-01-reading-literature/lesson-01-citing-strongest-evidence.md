# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 1, Lesson 1

## Topic
Citing the Strongest Textual Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between citing several pieces of evidence (Grade 7) and identifying the single strongest piece of evidence (Grade 8)
- Apply three criteria — relevance, specificity, and directness — to evaluate and rank multiple pieces of textual evidence
- Explain in writing why a selected piece of evidence is stronger than alternative choices from the same text

## Prerequisites
- Can cite several pieces of textual evidence to support both explicit and inferential analysis (RL.7.1)
- Can integrate multiple pieces of evidence into a multi-evidence paragraph using claim, evidence, and explanation
- Can distinguish between evidence that supports explicit statements and evidence that supports inferences
- Understands the structure of an analytical response (claim → evidence → explanation)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Highlighters in two colors
- Index cards (4 per student, for Evidence Strength Scale sorting activity)
- Pencils

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of evidence-citing from Grade 7 and prime students for the shift to evidence ranking.

**Activity: Evidence Sort**

Write two short statements on the board:

> **Statement A:** Mia was nervous about the audition.
> **Statement B:** Mia practiced every night for three weeks, but the morning of the audition she could not finish her breakfast.

**Say:** "Last year, you practiced finding several pieces of evidence to support a claim. Today we're going to take that skill one step further. Look at these two pieces of evidence. Both of them could support the claim that a character is nervous. Turn and tell your partner: which one do you think is stronger evidence, and why?"

Allow 60 seconds for partner discussion, then take two or three responses.

**Say:** "Notice that you're already doing something important — you're not just asking 'Does this work?' You're asking 'Which one works better and why?' That's the Grade 8 shift. Today we'll learn a tool for making that judgment precise."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Introduce the Evidence Strength Scale (three criteria) and model its application with a new passage.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Relevance:** The degree to which a piece of evidence directly addresses the specific claim being made. Relevant evidence does not just touch on a related topic — it speaks directly to the exact point.
- **Specificity:** The degree to which evidence contains concrete, particular detail rather than broad or vague language. Specific evidence names, shows, or demonstrates rather than generalizes.
- **Directness:** The degree to which evidence supports the claim without requiring a long chain of inference. Direct evidence states or shows the idea clearly; indirect evidence requires many interpretive steps.

### Instruction Notes

Display the Evidence Strength Scale anchor chart (draw on board):

```
EVIDENCE STRENGTH SCALE
─────────────────────────────────
Criterion 1: RELEVANCE (0-2)
  0 = off-topic  1 = loosely related  2 = directly addresses the claim

Criterion 2: SPECIFICITY (0-2)
  0 = vague generalization  1 = some detail  2 = concrete, precise detail

Criterion 3: DIRECTNESS (0-2)
  0 = requires many inferential steps  1 = one step  2 = clearly shows the claim

Total: __ / 6
─────────────────────────────────
```

**Say:** "This is the Evidence Strength Scale. Each piece of evidence gets a score from 0 to 2 on each of three criteria. The highest possible score is 6. We're going to use this scale to evaluate evidence, not just collect it. Let me show you how."

Read aloud the following passage:

---

**Passage: "The Weight of It"**

Darius had spent the summer learning to cook from his grandmother, Nana Bea, who moved through the kitchen with unhurried confidence. She never measured anything. A handful of this, a pour of that, and somehow everything came out exactly right. Darius watched every motion, asking questions she sometimes answered and sometimes just smiled at. When school started in September, Nana Bea went back to her home two states away, and the kitchen felt different — bigger and quieter. Darius stood at the counter the Saturday after she left, a bowl of onions in front of him and a cutting board he had never used without her beside him. He picked up the knife. His hand trembled, not with fear of the blade, but with the sudden understanding that learning something from someone you love means carrying a piece of them when they're gone.

---

**Say:** "The claim I want to support is: Darius has a deep emotional connection to his grandmother. I found three possible pieces of evidence. Watch me score each one."

Write on board:

> **Evidence A:** "Darius had spent the summer learning to cook from his grandmother, Nana Bea."

**Say:** "Let me score Evidence A. Relevance: Does it directly address the emotional connection claim? It shows they spent time together, but it's about the activity of cooking, not the emotional depth. I'll give it a 1. Specificity: Is there concrete detail? It tells us who and what season, but doesn't show feeling. A 1. Directness: Does it clearly show emotional connection? Not without several inferential steps — I have to assume learning together equals deep connection. A 1. Total: 3 out of 6."

> **Evidence B:** "Nana Bea went back to her home two states away, and the kitchen felt different — bigger and quieter."

**Say:** "Evidence B. Relevance: It shows Darius notices her absence through the changed feeling of the kitchen — that's about connection. Score: 2. Specificity: 'bigger and quieter' is a specific sensory detail. Score: 2. Directness: It shows emotional impact through implication but still requires some inference. Score: 1. Total: 5 out of 6."

> **Evidence C:** "His hand trembled, not with fear of the blade, but with the sudden understanding that learning something from someone you love means carrying a piece of them when they're gone."

**Say:** "Evidence C. Relevance: This directly addresses emotional connection — it even uses the word 'love.' Score: 2. Specificity: The physical trembling and the precise internal realization are both concrete. Score: 2. Directness: This states the emotional truth outright. Score: 2. Total: 6 out of 6. Evidence C is the strongest piece of evidence, and now I can explain exactly why."

### Worked Examples

**Claim:** Darius has a deep emotional connection to his grandmother.

**Strongest Evidence:** "His hand trembled, not with fear of the blade, but with the sudden understanding that learning something from someone you love means carrying a piece of them when they're gone."

**Explanation of Strength:** This evidence scores highest on all three criteria. It is directly relevant because it explicitly names love for his grandmother as the cause of his emotional reaction. It is highly specific because it contrasts physical trembling with emotional understanding in concrete terms. It is maximally direct because the narrator states the connection outright rather than requiring the reader to infer it from behavior alone. Evidence A and B both support the claim, but neither achieves the same combination of precision and emotional directness.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Practice applying the Evidence Strength Scale collaboratively with facilitator support.

Read aloud the following passage together:

---

**Passage: "Crossing the Courtyard"**

Priya had walked across the school's central courtyard every day for two years without once stopping. The courtyard was loud at lunch — a collision of voices, music from someone's phone, the scrape of chairs on concrete — and Priya moved through it quickly, head down, earbuds in. She had learned this route the first week of sixth grade when she tripped over a backpack and everyone laughed. Not cruelly, she supposed, but they laughed, and she had not forgotten. Now, in eighth grade, Naledi stopped her in the middle of the courtyard. Naledi was someone who stopped, who laughed without caring who heard, who saved seats for people she barely knew. "You always look like you're trying to get somewhere else," Naledi said, not unkindly. "What if here was okay?" Priya did not know what to say. She stood in the middle of the courtyard and, for the first time in two years, heard the noise as noise rather than as something aimed at her.

---

**Claim:** Priya has been shaped by a past experience of social embarrassment.

**Say:** "I want you to find three pieces of evidence from this passage that could support this claim. Write each one on a separate index card. Then score each one using the Evidence Strength Scale. We'll do this together — I'll score mine out loud while you score yours, and then we'll compare."

Work through the scoring together:

- Guide students toward noticing that the most direct evidence is the explicit reference to the incident: *"She had learned this route the first week of sixth grade when she tripped over a backpack and everyone laughed."* and *"she had not forgotten."*
- Students may also identify the behavioral evidence: *"Priya moved through it quickly, head down, earbuds in."* Score this as high in specificity but slightly lower in directness since it shows avoidance without naming the cause.
- Discuss why the line *"she had not forgotten"* is especially powerful — it is brief but maximally direct.

**Say:** "When you defend your strongest evidence selection, you're not saying the other evidence is wrong. You're saying this piece does the most work, most clearly, most precisely, for this specific claim. That's what RL.8.1 is asking you to do."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Apply the Evidence Strength Scale independently to a new passage with a provided claim.

Read the following passage independently:

---

**Passage: "The Cartographer's Son"**

Emre's father had drawn maps for as long as Emre could remember — hand-drawn maps of neighborhoods, city blocks, imaginary continents. The walls of the apartment were covered in them, some framed, most just pinned up and slowly curling at the corners. Emre had grown up thinking this was normal until a classmate visited in fifth grade and said, "Your house looks like a museum." The comment settled into Emre like a stone into still water. In seventh grade, when his father asked him to help update the neighborhood map — noting which businesses had opened or closed — Emre said he was too busy. In eighth grade, his father fell ill and spent three weeks in the hospital. Each afternoon, Emre sat in the plastic chair beside his father's bed and thought about the unfinished map on the kitchen table. On the fourth day of the third week, he asked the nurse for paper and drew, from memory, the street where they lived. He slid it under his father's hand while he slept. When his father woke and found it, he held it for a long time without speaking.

---

**Claim:** Emre's relationship with his father's mapmaking changed over time.

**Questions:**
1. List three pieces of evidence from the passage that could support this claim.
2. Score each piece of evidence on the Evidence Strength Scale (0-6).
3. Identify the strongest piece of evidence and write 2-3 sentences explaining why it is stronger than the other two options. Use the terms relevance, specificity, and directness in your explanation.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete all three questions using the Evidence Strength Scale template. Your explanation in question 3 should use at least one of the three criteria terms.

**Stretch:** Complete all three questions. In your explanation for question 3, address all three criteria explicitly and explain why the evidence you chose outperforms the other options on each criterion.

**Challenge:** Complete all three questions with full criteria analysis. Then write an additional response: What would a seventh-grade student do differently with this passage, and what does the Grade 8 approach add to literary analysis? Refer to specific evidence in your answer.

### Interleaved Review

4. (RL.7.1 — Grade 7 skill) From the same passage, find two pieces of evidence that work together to support the following inference: Emre felt shame about his father's unusual hobby. Explain how the two pieces connect to each other.
5. (RL.7.2 — Grade 7 skill) In two to three sentences, write an objective summary of the passage. Remember: no personal opinion, no evaluation of the characters — just the essential events.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following passage and answer the question below:

> Marcus had tried three times to fix the leaking pipe under the kitchen sink. Each attempt left the floor wetter and his father's expression more carefully neutral. On the fourth try, Marcus pulled everything out from under the sink, laid it on the floor in order, and read the entire instruction sheet before touching anything. When he turned the water back on, nothing leaked. His father said nothing, but later Marcus heard him on the phone: "Yeah, the kid figured it out."

**Claim:** Marcus demonstrates persistence in learning a new skill.

Which is the strongest piece of evidence for this claim, and why?

a) "Marcus had tried three times to fix the leaking pipe under the kitchen sink."
b) "On the fourth try, Marcus pulled everything out from under the sink, laid it on the floor in order, and read the entire instruction sheet before touching anything."
c) "His father said nothing, but later Marcus heard him on the phone: 'Yeah, the kid figured it out.'"

Write one sentence naming your choice and two sentences explaining why it is stronger than the other options.

**Expected Answers:**
Option (b) is the strongest evidence. It is most relevant because it directly shows the methodical approach that constitutes learning a skill through persistence. It is most specific because it lists three concrete actions (pulling everything out, laying items in order, reading instructions) rather than simply noting the number of attempts. It is most direct because it shows the behavior associated with persistence without requiring the reader to infer it from others' reactions or from a count of failures.

**Mastery = Student selects option (b) and provides at least one valid criterion-based justification (relevance, specificity, or directness).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Revisit the Evidence Strength Scale with the student. Have them score each option aloud on a single criterion (start with specificity) before moving to the others. Common error: students select (a) because it explicitly mentions trying multiple times — discuss why counting attempts is less specific than showing the quality of the process.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask the student: "Could option (a) and option (b) work together as a two-evidence response? How would you connect them?" This bridges to multi-evidence writing while reinforcing single-evidence evaluation.

---

## Extension (Optional)

1. Take any book you are currently reading or have recently finished. Choose one claim a reader might make about a character and find three pieces of evidence. Score each on the Evidence Strength Scale and write a paragraph defending your strongest selection.
2. Bring in two pieces of evidence for the same claim and swap with a partner. Each person scores the other's evidence independently, then compare scores and discuss any disagreements.
3. Write a short passage (100-150 words) of your own that deliberately contains one very strong piece of evidence and two weaker ones for a claim about the main character. Challenge a partner to identify which is strongest and why.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The most important moment in this lesson is the shift from "Does this evidence work?" to "Why is this evidence the strongest?" If students are comfortable listing evidence but struggle to compare and rank, spend extra time on the scoring activity in guided practice before moving to independent work.
- Some students will initially treat the Evidence Strength Scale as a math problem, aiming to maximize scores without reading carefully. Emphasize that the scores are a thinking tool, not a test — the goal is to be able to justify the score with specific reasons from the text.
- The interleaved review questions revisit Grade 7 skills (RL.7.1 multi-evidence and RL.7.2 objective summary) intentionally. These skills are not retired at Grade 8; rather, Grade 8 builds on them. Affirm students who write strong multi-evidence responses in question 4.
- If time is short, the independent practice can be completed as homework or continued in the following session. The exit ticket should always be completed in class.
- Save student index cards from guided practice for Lesson 2, where students will revisit the same passage to practice distinguishing strong from weak evidence by deliberately evaluating options they originally rejected.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Question 1-2: Possible evidence and sample scores**

Evidence A: "Emre had grown up thinking this was normal until a classmate visited in fifth grade and said, 'Your house looks like a museum.'" — Relevance: 2 (shows the beginning of shame/distance from mapmaking), Specificity: 2 (direct quote, specific event), Directness: 1 (requires inference about long-term effect). Total: 5/6.

Evidence B: "In seventh grade, when his father asked him to help update the neighborhood map — noting which businesses had opened or closed — Emre said he was too busy." — Relevance: 2, Specificity: 2 (specific request and refusal), Directness: 2 (directly shows avoidance). Total: 6/6.

Evidence C: "He slid it under his father's hand while he slept. When his father woke and found it, he held it for a long time without speaking." — Relevance: 2 (shows reconnection), Specificity: 2 (specific action and response), Directness: 1-2 (the silence is evocative but requires interpretation). Total: 5-6/6.

**Question 3 (sample response):** The strongest evidence for the claim that Emre's relationship with his father's mapmaking changed over time is Evidence B: "In seventh grade, when his father asked him to help update the neighborhood map...Emre said he was too busy." This evidence is highly relevant because it directly shows Emre actively distancing himself from the activity — not just feeling privately embarrassed, but refusing his father's invitation. It is specific because it names a concrete moment with a specific request and refusal. It is direct because it shows the change in behavior explicitly, without requiring the reader to infer distance from a physical detail or a third party's comment. Evidence A and C are also strong, but Evidence A requires the reader to infer long-term impact from a single childhood comment, and Evidence C, while emotionally powerful, shows the relationship's end-state rather than the change itself.

### Interleaved Review

**Question 4 (sample response):** Two pieces of evidence that work together to support the inference that Emre felt shame about his hobby: (1) "The comment settled into Emre like a stone into still water" and (2) "In seventh grade, when his father asked him to help update the neighborhood map...Emre said he was too busy." The first piece shows the internal impact of the classmate's comment — a vivid metaphor for how it sank in and stayed. The second shows how that shame affected behavior two years later. Together, they build a picture of shame that is internalized and then acted upon.

**Question 5 (sample objective summary):** Emre grows up in an apartment filled with his father's hand-drawn maps and initially considers this normal. After a classmate's comment makes him self-conscious, he distances himself from the practice and declines to help his father. When his father becomes seriously ill and is hospitalized, Emre reconsiders and draws a map of their street from memory, placing it in his father's hand. His father responds with silent, extended attention to the drawing.

### Exit Ticket
Option (b) is strongest. See Expected Answers section above for full justification.
