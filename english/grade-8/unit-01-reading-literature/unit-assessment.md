# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 1: Reading Literature

## Unit Assessment

---

### Overview

This assessment evaluates mastery of the four reading literature standards addressed in Unit 1. Students demonstrate their ability to select and justify the strongest textual evidence, analyze theme development, analyze how dramatic irony creates effects, and analyze how modern fiction draws on traditional sources.

| Standard | Focus |
|----------|-------|
| RL.8.1 | Cite the textual evidence that most strongly supports an analysis of what the text says explicitly as well as inferences drawn from the text |
| RL.8.2 | Determine a theme or central idea and analyze its development; provide an objective summary |
| RL.8.6 | Analyze how differences in the points of view of characters and the audience/reader create effects such as suspense or humor |
| RL.8.9 | Analyze how a modern work of fiction draws on traditional sources, describing how the material is rendered new |

**Administration Notes:**
- Administer after Lesson 10
- Suggested time: 50-60 minutes
- Total points: 60
- Mastery threshold: 70% overall (42/60 points); at least 50% in each standard section
- Students may use their Evidence Strength Scale criteria reference (relevance, specificity, directness) during the assessment — this tool assesses application, not memorization
- All passages are original fiction; no outside knowledge is required

### SECTION 1: Citing the Strongest Evidence (RL.8.1)

**Total: 14 points**

Read the following passage and answer the questions that follow.

---

**Passage A: "The Interview"**

Miriam had been practicing her answers for the community scholarship interview for three weeks. She had researched the committee members, prepared stories that demonstrated her commitment to the neighborhood, and memorized facts about the scholarship's history. On the morning of the interview, she rode the subway to the community center with her notes folded in her jacket pocket. She arrived twenty minutes early. The receptionist told her the interview had been moved to the following Thursday due to a scheduling conflict. They had sent an email. Miriam checked her phone. The email was in her spam folder, unread for four days. She stood for a moment in the lobby. Then she asked the receptionist if she could sit and review her notes while she waited — for next Thursday, she meant. The receptionist said of course. Miriam sat down, unfolded her notes, and read them through from the beginning.

---

**1.** (Multiple Choice — 2 points) Which piece of evidence most strongly supports the claim that Miriam responds to setbacks with composure and persistence?

a) "She arrived twenty minutes early."
b) "The email was in her spam folder, unread for four days."
c) "Miriam sat down, unfolded her notes, and read them through from the beginning."
d) "She had researched the committee members, prepared stories that demonstrated her commitment to the neighborhood, and memorized facts about the scholarship's history."

**2.** (Short Answer — 3 points) A student selects option (d) from Question 1 as the strongest evidence. Identify which criterion this evidence fails for the specific claim about responding to setbacks with composure and persistence, and explain why option (c) is stronger. Use at least two criterion terms (relevance, specificity, directness) in your response.

**3.** (Multiple Choice — 2 points) Which piece of evidence most strongly supports the inference that Miriam is not defeated by the unexpected news?

a) "She rode the subway to the community center with her notes folded in her jacket pocket."
b) "She stood for a moment in the lobby."
c) "Then she asked the receptionist if she could sit and review her notes while she waited — for next Thursday, she meant."
d) "She had been practicing her answers for the community scholarship interview for three weeks."

**4.** (Short Answer — 3 points) In 2-3 sentences, explain why the evidence you selected in Question 3 is stronger than option (a) for the same claim. Use the Evidence Strength Scale criteria in your explanation.

**5.** (Short Answer — 4 points) A different claim: "Miriam's preparation is thorough and deliberate." Write a 3-4 sentence response defending the strongest evidence for this claim. Your response must (a) state the evidence you selected, (b) score it on at least two of the three criteria, and (c) briefly explain why at least one other piece of evidence is weaker for this specific claim.

### SECTION 2: Theme and Objective Summary (RL.8.2)

**Total: 18 points**

Read the following passage and answer the questions that follow.

---

**Passage B: "Unfinished Business"**

The town's oldest building was a three-story warehouse that had been empty for eleven years. Nobody owned it outright — the ownership had been disputed in a legal case that nobody had fully resolved. During those eleven years, three groups had tried to develop the building: a restaurant collective, a youth arts organization, and a real estate developer. Each had tried and withdrawn for different reasons. In the twelfth year, a retired engineer named Mr. Bassi, who lived across the street and had watched all of this, applied for temporary stewardship of the building through a little-used municipal process. The application was approved. He did not renovate the building. He opened the ground floor to the neighborhood on Saturday mornings: coffee, tables, and whatever anyone brought to work on. By the end of the third month, twelve regular participants had keys. Nothing in the building was fixed. The roof still leaked in one corner. But on Saturday mornings, people came.

---

**6.** (Multiple Choice — 2 points) Which of the following best states a complete theme for this passage?

a) "Buildings"
b) "Persistence can overcome legal obstacles if you are patient enough."
c) "Community grows not from solving problems but from gathering around them."
d) "The story is about a man who takes care of an old building that nobody else wanted."

**7.** (Short Answer — 3 points) Analyze how Mr. Bassi's character arc contributes to the theme you identified in Question 6. Apply the three-question framework: What does he want? What does he face? What does he understand or do at the end? Write 3-4 sentences.

**8.** (Short Answer — 3 points) Identify one specific way the setting (the warehouse's physical state and history) contributes to the theme. Write 2-3 sentences explaining the setting's thematic function — what it embodies, represents, or enables that directly supports the theme.

**9.** (Short Answer — 3 points) Write an objective summary of this passage in 3-4 sentences. Your summary must follow all three Summary Rules: report events (no evaluation), report only what the text says (no interpretation), include only essential events (no unnecessary detail).

**10.** (Multiple Choice — 2 points) Which of the following summaries best follows the three Summary Rules?

a) "This touching story shows how one man's patience and wisdom transformed a neglected building into a true community gathering place, proving that where others see problems, visionaries see potential."
b) "After eleven years of disputed ownership and three failed development attempts, a retired engineer named Mr. Bassi obtains temporary stewardship of an empty warehouse and opens its ground floor to the neighborhood on Saturday mornings. Twelve regular participants eventually have keys to the space."
c) "Mr. Bassi was a brilliant, persistent man who saw what others couldn't and created something beautiful out of nothing, showing how community is more important than ownership."
d) "The warehouse had legal problems and was empty for a long time. Mr. Bassi fixed this by being nicer than the other people who had tried."

**11.** (Short Answer — 5 points) Write a complete theme analysis of this passage in one paragraph (5-7 sentences). Your analysis must: (a) state a complete theme as a full sentence, (b) analyze how character contributes to the theme with specific evidence, (c) analyze how setting or plot contributes, and (d) cite the strongest evidence for your theme, scoring it on at least two criteria.

### SECTION 3: Point of View and Dramatic Irony — Perspectives Passage (RL.8.6)

**Total: 14 points**

Read the following passage and answer the questions that follow.

---

**Passage C: "The Rehearsal"**

The school's spring concert was three weeks away. The choir teacher, Ms. Ndiaye, had assigned the solo to a quiet eighth-grader named Theo. What Theo did not know was that Ms. Ndiaye had given the same assignment to a second student, Priya, as a backup — "just in case," she had told Priya, "which means I hope I never have to use you, but I want you ready." Priya had been practicing for two weeks in her room every evening, a secret she had kept from everyone, including Theo. In rehearsal, Theo sang well. He had no reason to doubt his role. After rehearsal, Ms. Ndiaye said to the full choir: "Everyone is where they should be. Let's trust the process." She meant this honestly. She did not realize that Priya had understood this to mean that Priya would be used. Theo had understood it to mean he was confirmed in the solo. They left rehearsal in opposite directions, both confident, for different reasons.

---

**12.** (Multiple Choice — 2 points) Which of the following best identifies the dramatic irony in this passage?

a) Ms. Ndiaye prefers Priya's voice but won't say so to Theo.
b) Theo and Priya have both been told different things, and neither knows the other is involved.
c) The reader knows that both Theo and Priya believe they have the solo, while each is unaware of the other's belief.
d) Ms. Ndiaye is planning to give the solo to a third student she hasn't told either of them about.

**13.** (Short Answer — 3 points) Complete the Dramatic Irony Detector for this passage: (a) What does Theo know? (b) What does Priya know? (c) What does the reader know that neither of them fully knows? (d) What effect does this knowledge gap create — is it primarily suspense, humor, or a different effect? Explain in 2-3 sentences.

**14.** (Multiple Choice — 2 points) Which of the following is the strongest evidence that the author has deliberately constructed a dramatic irony gap for the reader?

a) "Ms. Ndiaye had given the same assignment to a second student, Priya, as a backup."
b) "Theo sang well. He had no reason to doubt his role."
c) "She meant this honestly."
d) "They left rehearsal in opposite directions, both confident, for different reasons."

**15.** (Short Answer — 3 points) Explain how Ms. Ndiaye's final statement — "Everyone is where they should be. Let's trust the process" — functions as the pivot of the dramatic irony. In 2-3 sentences, explain: (a) what Ms. Ndiaye intends by this statement, (b) how Theo and Priya each interpret it, and (c) what role the statement plays in sustaining the knowledge gap.

**16.** (Short Answer — 4 points) Analyze the effect the dramatic irony in this passage creates. Is it closer to suspense, humor, or something else (pathos, unease, tension)? In 3-5 sentences, explain what specific features of the passage (stakes, tone, type of misunderstanding) determine the effect, and cite the strongest evidence for your analysis.

### SECTION 4: Modern Fiction and Traditional Sources (RL.8.9)

**Total: 9 points**

Read the following passage and then answer the questions that follow.

---

**Traditional Source Summary (provided):** In the myth of Sisyphus, King Sisyphus is condemned by the gods to roll a boulder up a hill for eternity. Every time he reaches the top, the boulder rolls back down. The story is often interpreted as a meditation on futile, repetitive labor — and, in some philosophical readings, on whether a person can find meaning within a task that has no end.

---

**Passage D: "Shift Work"**

Every weekday morning, Darius arrived at the hospital at 5:45 a.m. to mop the floors before the day staff arrived. The floors were clean by 6:30. By 8:00, they had been walked on by hundreds of people and were no longer clean. Darius knew this. He had known it for nine years. One morning, a resident physician he had never spoken to paused at the supply closet doorway and said: "Do you ever think it's pointless? You clean it and they dirty it again." Darius considered the question. He said: "The floor being clean for an hour matters to someone who walks in scared. So, no." He returned to his work. The physician stood for a moment, then went to begin rounds.

---

**17.** (Multiple Choice — 2 points) Which element of the Sisyphus myth does this passage most directly draw on?

a) The punishment inflicted by gods for hubris
b) The eternally repetitive, seemingly futile nature of the labor
c) The hero's attempt to escape from the underworld
d) The boulder as a symbol of ambition and overreach

**18.** (Short Answer — 3 points) Complete a condensed Myth/Modern Parallel chart in your notebook or answer below. Identify at least three elements (such as the central figure, the nature of the task, the question of meaning, the resolution) and explain what changed from the mythological source to the modern passage.

**19.** (Short Answer — 4 points) In 3-5 sentences, explain how "Shift Work" renders the Sisyphus material new. Your response must: (a) identify at least two specific transformations (what changed), (b) explain the thematic effect of each change, and (c) cite the strongest evidence from the passage for your analysis.

### SECTION 5: Extended Response — Integrated Literary Analysis (All Standards)

**Total: 5 points**

Read the following passage and write a response to the prompt that follows.

---

**Passage E: "The Understudy's Song"**

For as long as Cora could remember, her older sister Bea had been the singer in the family. Bea's voice was the kind that made people stop conversations. When Bea won a spot in the city youth orchestra's vocal ensemble, Cora began practicing in secret — not to compete, she told herself, but just to know what it was like. The ensemble needed a second soprano for their winter showcase. The director auditioned twelve students. Cora was selected. What the director did not know — and what the reader has been told — was that Bea had been at the same audition, in the back row, watching. She had not been selected. The director had not recognized them as sisters. On the night of the showcase, Cora sang. The audience applauded. Bea, in her seat, applauded too — genuinely, fully, without visible hesitation. Afterward, they walked home together. Neither mentioned the audition. Halfway home, Bea said: "You've been practicing." Cora said: "Yes." Bea said: "Good." They walked the rest of the way without speaking. At the door, Bea held it open.

---

**Prompt:** Write a multi-paragraph response (minimum 4 paragraphs) analyzing this passage using all four Unit 1 analytical lenses. Your response must include:

- **RL.8.1:** Identify and defend the single strongest piece of evidence for a claim about a character, using at least two criterion terms.
- **RL.8.2:** State a complete theme for the passage and analyze how at least two elements of the Theme-Character-Setting Triangle (character, setting, or plot) develop it.
- **RL.8.6:** Identify the dramatic irony, describe the knowledge gap, and analyze the effect it creates.
- **RL.8.9:** Identify the traditional pattern or archetype this passage draws on and explain how it is rendered new.
- **Synthesis:** In your final paragraph, explain how at least two of the four lenses illuminate each other — what do you see when you apply both that you would not see from either alone?

### Answer Key

---

#### SECTION 1 — Answer Key

**Question 1:** Correct answer: **(c)** — "Miriam sat down, unfolded her notes, and read them through from the beginning."
This evidence is the strongest because it directly shows Miriam's response to the setback: she continues her preparation without apparent distress. It is specific (three concrete actions), relevant to the claim about composure and persistence, and direct — her behavior is the evidence of her composure, not an inference about her character.

**Question 2 (model response):** Option (d) fails relevance for this specific claim. It shows thorough preparation before the setback but does not address how Miriam responds when the setback occurs. Relevance requires evidence that directly addresses the specific claim — preparation before the interview is not the same as composure in response to unexpected disruption. Option (c) is stronger on both relevance (it directly shows Miriam continuing preparation after discovering the date change) and directness (the sequence of concrete actions shows composure and persistence without requiring the reader to make inferential leaps about character).

**Question 3:** Correct answer: **(c)** — "Then she asked the receptionist if she could sit and review her notes while she waited — for next Thursday, she meant."
This is the most direct evidence of non-defeat: she does not leave, does not express distress, and immediately repositions toward the future date. Options (a) and (d) show preparation, not response to setback.

**Question 4 (model response):** Option (c) is stronger than option (a) on both relevance and directness. Option (a) shows Miriam carrying her notes — evidence of preparation, not of response to the unexpected news. For a claim about not being defeated, the relevant evidence must show her behavior after she learns the interview has been moved. Option (c) directly shows that behavior: she asks to stay and use the time, immediately reframing the setback as an opportunity to review. This is directly relevant to the claim and requires no inferential steps to connect to non-defeat.

**Question 5 (model response):** The strongest evidence for the claim "Miriam's preparation is thorough and deliberate" is: "She had researched the committee members, prepared stories that demonstrated her commitment to the neighborhood, and memorized facts about the scholarship's history." This evidence scores a 2 on specificity because it enumerates three distinct, concrete types of preparation rather than simply stating that she studied. It scores a 2 on relevance because it directly addresses the claim about thoroughness — three distinct categories of preparation support "thorough" as a precise description. By contrast, "She arrived twenty minutes early" is weaker for this claim: it shows timeliness (related, but different), and does not demonstrate the depth or range of her preparation — it fails relevance for the specific claim.

#### SECTION 2 — Answer Key

**Question 6:** Correct answer: **(c)** — "Community grows not from solving problems but from gathering around them."
Option (a) is a topic word, not a theme. Option (b) misreads the passage — Mr. Bassi does not overcome legal obstacles through patience; he works around them via a different legal process. Option (d) is plot summary, not theme.

**Question 7 (model response):** Mr. Bassi wants the warehouse to be used by the neighborhood. He faces eleven years of legal gridlock and three failed development attempts. He does not try to solve the dispute; instead, he takes temporary stewardship and opens the space as-is. What he understands — or demonstrates through action — is that community does not require a perfect or solved space. The character arc contributes to the theme because his refusal to wait for resolution embodies the theme: community gathers around problems, not after they are fixed.

**Question 8 (model response):** The warehouse's physical state — still unfinished, still with a leaking roof — functions as a theme engine because it embodies the setting's acceptance of imperfection. Mr. Bassi does not fix the building before opening it. The fact that people come despite the leaky roof directly supports the theme: the community's gathering is not contingent on the space being solved or finished. The setting itself models the theme's claim — that gathering around a problem is its own form of resolution.

**Question 9 (model response — objective summary):** An empty warehouse in a small town has been unoccupied for eleven years due to unresolved ownership disputes. After three separate groups fail to develop it, a retired engineer named Mr. Bassi obtains temporary stewardship through a municipal process. He opens the ground floor to the neighborhood on Saturday mornings with no amenities beyond coffee and tables. Within three months, twelve regular participants have keys, and the building remains structurally unfinished.

**Question 10:** Correct answer: **(b)** — This is the only option that reports events without evaluative language or thematic interpretation. Option (a) includes "touching," "visionaries see potential," and a thematic claim. Option (c) is evaluative throughout and adds interpretation. Option (d) misrepresents the facts ("fixed this by being nicer than the other people") and is evaluative.

**Question 11 (model response):** The passage develops the theme that community is not the product of solving a problem but of gathering in its presence. This theme emerges through Mr. Bassi's character arc: he does not attempt to resolve the warehouse's legal or structural problems — he simply opens the space, unfixed, for others. His choice of action (temporary stewardship, coffee, open tables) is the thematic statement in behavioral form. The setting reinforces the theme: the leaking roof in one corner, explicitly noted at the passage's end, signals that the space is not complete — yet people come. The strongest evidence is: "Nothing in the building was fixed. The roof still leaked in one corner. But on Saturday mornings, people came." This evidence scores 6/6: it is directly relevant to the theme (community without resolution), highly specific (the leaking corner is a precise, named imperfection), and maximally direct (the conjunction "But" explicitly contrasts imperfection with gathering, making the thematic relationship visible in a single sentence).

#### SECTION 3 — Answer Key

**Question 12:** Correct answer: **(c)** — The reader knows that both Theo and Priya believe they have the solo, while each is unaware of the other's belief. This is the precise description of the knowledge gap created for the reader. Option (b) is close but incomplete — it describes the communication problem without specifying that the reader knows both students' beliefs simultaneously.

**Question 13 (model response — Dramatic Irony Detector):**
(a) What Theo knows: He has the solo; rehearsal went well; Ms. Ndiaye's statement confirmed him.
(b) What Priya knows: She is the backup, practicing in secret; she interpreted Ms. Ndiaye's statement as confirmation that she will be needed.
(c) What the reader knows that neither of them fully knows: Both students are confident about the same role for incompatible reasons; neither knows the other is involved; Ms. Ndiaye's ambiguous statement fed both misunderstandings simultaneously.
(d) Effect: This creates a quiet, uneasy tension rather than humor or strong suspense. The stakes are real (a significant performance opportunity) but not physical. The tone is not comic because both characters are sympathetic and both have been set up to fail — the reader experiences a kind of anticipatory concern or unease about a situation neither character can resolve without pain.

**Question 14:** Correct answer: **(d)** — "They left rehearsal in opposite directions, both confident, for different reasons."
This is the strongest evidence for deliberate dramatic irony construction because the final phrase ("for different reasons") is an explicit authorial statement that names the knowledge gap directly. The image of opposite directions is a structural enactment of the irony — two characters moving confidently in different directions based on incompatible beliefs. Score: 6/6.

**Question 15 (model response):** Ms. Ndiaye intends the statement as a general encouragement — she is expressing confidence in the ensemble's readiness without referencing either student's specific role. Theo hears it as personal confirmation of his solo. Priya hears it as confirmation that she will be needed. The statement sustains the knowledge gap by being genuinely ambiguous: Ms. Ndiaye's honesty means she believes her words are true for all students, but the ambiguity allows both Theo and Priya to hear what they need to hear. The gap is sustained precisely because the statement is not deliberately deceptive — it is honest communication that generates incompatible interpretations.

**Question 16 (model response):** The dramatic irony in this passage creates an effect closer to unease or anticipatory tension than either humor or classic suspense. The stakes are real — both students will likely face disappointment — but the tone is not comic (both students are sympathetic; neither is being mocked for overconfidence). The misunderstanding is not benign (unlike "The Ambassador" or "Expert Opinion" from the unit), because someone will be hurt. But it is not physically dangerous. The strongest evidence for this analysis is: "They left rehearsal in opposite directions, both confident, for different reasons." This evidence captures both the structural irony (diverging directions from a shared starting point) and its tone: "confident" applied to both is neither funny nor terrifying, but carries a quiet dread because the reader knows one confidence is misplaced.

#### SECTION 4 — Answer Key

**Question 17:** Correct answer: **(b)** — The eternally repetitive, seemingly futile nature of the labor.
The passage is built entirely around the daily mopping cycle that is undone by foot traffic — directly parallel to the boulder that rolls back down after every ascent.

**Question 18 (condensed chart):**
| Element | In Sisyphus | In "Shift Work" | What Changed? |
|---------|------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Central figure | A king condemned by gods | A hospital worker who chose his work | No condemnation — the repetition is chosen, not punished |
| Nature of task | Rolling a boulder; purely futile | Mopping floors; briefly effective | The modern task actually helps someone, even temporarily |
| The question of meaning | Philosophical — can meaning exist in futility? | Practical — Darius answers it directly: yes | The question is posed and answered within the story |
| Outcome | Eternal, unresolved | Darius returns to work; the physician reflects | The modern version provides resolution through a single exchange |

**Question 19 (model response):** "Shift Work" renders the Sisyphus material new in two significant ways. First, it removes the element of divine punishment and condemnation: Darius was not condemned to clean floors; he chose this work for nine years. The transformation shifts the story from futility-as-punishment to futility-as-choice, which fundamentally changes the thematic question — not "how do you endure what you cannot escape?" but "how do you find meaning in work that is never done?" Second, the modern version resolves the philosophical question that Sisyphus leaves open: Darius's answer — "The floor being clean for an hour matters to someone who walks in scared" — provides a direct, concrete answer to the question of meaning in repetitive work. This is the strongest evidence in the passage (Relevance: 2, Specificity: 2, Directness: 2; Score: 6/6). The transformation changes the myth from a meditation on futility into an argument that meaning is found in the momentary, human effect of any act of care — regardless of its permanence.

**Model Response Guidance (Passage E: "The Understudy's Song"):**

**RL.8.1:** The strongest evidence for the claim "Bea demonstrates genuine generosity toward her sister despite her own loss" is: "Bea, in her seat, applauded too — genuinely, fully, without visible hesitation." This evidence scores 2 on relevance (the qualifier "without visible hesitation" directly addresses the quality of generosity — not a forced, reluctant gesture but a full one). Specificity: 2 (three adverbs — "genuinely, fully, without visible hesitation" — make the action precise). Directness: 2 (the evidence directly shows the behavior claimed, requiring no inferential steps). Score: 6/6.

**RL.8.2:** Theme: "Genuine love can hold generosity and loss simultaneously, and sometimes the most powerful things between people are the things they choose not to say." Character: Cora practices in secret (not explicitly to compete) and is selected; she does not tell Bea, and neither mentions the audition. Bea applauds without hesitation. The character arcs are parallel acts of restraint that protect the other person. Setting: The walk home — a transitional space between public performance and private relationship — is where the sisters speak in compressed, charged language ("You've been practicing." / "Yes." / "Good."). Bea holding the door at the end is a physical gesture of welcome that closes the arc. Theme evidence: "Bea said: 'Good.'" — Directly relevant (her one-word response holds approval, acceptance, and generosity simultaneously). Score: 6/6 given its specific brevity against the emotional complexity it contains.

**RL.8.6:** Dramatic irony: The reader is told that Bea was at the audition, in the back row, watching, and was not selected. The director did not recognize them as sisters. Cora does not know Bea was there. On the night of the performance, Cora sings and is applauded — by Bea, who was in the audience with this private knowledge. Knowledge gap: Cora does not know Bea witnessed her audition or was rejected for the same role. The effect is pathos and quiet poignancy — not suspense (nothing dangerous hangs in the balance) and not humor (the situation involves genuine loss for Bea). The irony creates a kind of aching tenderness: the reader watches the sister who lost be gracious to the sister who won, knowing what the winner does not.

**RL.8.9:** The passage draws on the archetype of the younger sibling who surpasses the older — found in biblical stories (Jacob and Esau, Joseph and his brothers), fairy tales (youngest child who succeeds where elders fail), and mythological traditions across cultures. The rendering new: in traditional sources, the younger sibling's success often causes jealousy, conflict, or betrayal (Cain and Abel, the jealous siblings in Cinderella). Here, the older sibling's response is entirely generous. The transformation inverts the expected resolution: instead of conflict, there is quiet acceptance. The traditional pattern's reversal is itself the thematic argument — the story says that love can transcend the expected outcomes of rivalry.

**Synthesis:** The RL.8.6 (dramatic irony) and RL.8.9 (traditional pattern) lenses interact most powerfully in this passage. The reader knows Bea witnessed the audition — this is the dramatic irony. But the reason the irony produces pathos rather than suspense or humor is that the reader already knows from the traditional pattern that this situation should produce jealousy or conflict. The traditional template primes the reader to expect betrayal; the dramatic irony is structured so that the reader watches, knowing what Bea knows (she was passed over), and waits for the expected response — which never comes. The absence of the expected response is the story's most powerful moment. Applying only RL.8.6 reveals the knowledge gap; applying only RL.8.9 reveals the traditional expectation. Applying both reveals why the gap matters so much: it is the space where the reader expects the myth's ending, and where the author delivers something entirely different.

Revisit Lessons 6-8. Check whether the student can accurately complete the Dramatic Irony Detector (identifying what the character and reader each know) before attempting effect analysis. Students who cannot name the gap specifically cannot analyze its effect.

Revisit Lesson 9. The most common difficulty is with "rendering new" — students can often identify the traditional pattern but struggle to articulate what changed and why it matters. Focus on the "What Changed? / Thematic Effect" columns of the Myth/Modern Parallel chart.

Targeted single-standard reteaching is recommended before Unit 2. These skills (especially RL.8.1 and RL.8.2) directly underpin the informational reading and writing standards in upcoming units.
