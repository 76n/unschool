# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Modern Fiction Drawing on Traditional Works

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify mythic themes, archetypal character types, and traditional narrative patterns (the quest, the test, the return) in modern fiction
- Analyze how a modern author transforms traditional source material — changing setting, character, or outcome — to address contemporary ideas or readers
- Explain specifically how the traditional material is "rendered new" rather than simply retold

## Prerequisites
- Can compare and contrast a fictional portrayal with a historical account to understand how authors use or alter sources (RL.7.9)
- Can analyze theme development through character, setting, and plot (RL.8.2, Lessons 3-4)
- Can cite the strongest textual evidence using the Evidence Strength Scale (RL.8.1)
- Has some general familiarity with classic myth or traditional story patterns (quest, transformation, trickster, hero's journey)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Myth/Modern Parallel chart (draw on board or provide as handout)
- Highlighters in two colors

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate knowledge of myth and traditional story patterns; establish that modern fiction regularly draws on these sources.

**Activity: Spot the Pattern**

Read aloud the following brief plot summary:

> "A young person of ordinary background discovers they have an unusual ability. Guided by an older mentor, they embark on a difficult journey to confront a powerful force threatening their community. Along the way, they face tests of loyalty and courage. They succeed, but the victory requires a personal sacrifice, and they return changed."

**Say:** "You have heard this story before. Many times. Not necessarily this version — but this pattern. What stories, films, books, or legends does this remind you of? Name as many as you can in 60 seconds."

Students will likely name numerous examples: hero's journey stories from mythology, fantasy novels, films. Take responses.

**Say:** "This pattern is ancient — it appears in Greek myths, Norse legends, African epics, Indigenous oral traditions, and religious narratives across cultures. When modern authors write fiction, they often reach into this deep well of traditional story patterns, not to copy them but to use them as scaffolding. Today we analyze how they do that — and what they change to make something new."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Introduce the Myth/Modern Parallel chart and model how to trace traditional elements in modern fiction.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Archetype:** A recurring character type, symbol, or pattern found across cultures and time periods. Common archetypes include: the hero, the mentor, the trickster, the threshold guardian, the shadow (antagonist who mirrors the hero's flaw). Archetypes are patterns, not rigid templates — a modern story can use an archetype while reinventing its surface features.
- **Traditional narrative pattern:** A recurring story structure found in myths, folktales, and religious texts. Common patterns include: the quest (a journey toward a significant goal), the test (a character must prove worthiness), the descent and return (going into darkness or death and coming back changed), the trickster cycle (a clever figure disrupts the established order).
- **Rendering new:** The process by which a modern author transforms traditional material — by updating setting, changing character demographics, reversing outcomes, or shifting the thematic emphasis — so that the ancient pattern speaks to a contemporary situation. "Rendering new" is the opposite of simple retelling; it is purposeful transformation.

**Say:** "The shift from RL.7.9 to RL.8.9 is important. In Grade 7, you compared fiction with history — a real account versus a fictional one. In Grade 8, you compare modern fiction with traditional stories — myths, folktales, religious narratives. The question is not 'Is this accurate to history?' but 'How did the author use this ancient pattern, and what did they change to make it speak to today?'"

**Say:** "Let me share a traditional pattern and then read a modern passage that draws on it."

**Traditional source summary (Prometheus, briefly):** In Greek mythology, Prometheus steals fire from the gods and gives it to humanity, enabling civilization. As punishment, Zeus chains Prometheus to a rock where an eagle eats his liver each day; it regenerates overnight and the cycle continues. The story explores the cost of bringing knowledge or power to those who were denied it.

Read aloud the following modern passage:

---

**Passage: "The Download"**

In the summer before ninth grade, sixteen-year-old Sasha discovered a way to access the premium tutoring database that the district only licensed for the top-performing high school. The middle school students — including Sasha's little brother Malik — had no access to it. The content inside was extraordinary: interactive lessons, practice problems, guided explanations. The kind of material that changed outcomes. Sasha copied the access credentials from a cousin who attended the high school and shared them in a private group of forty middle school students before the school year began. The district discovered the unauthorized access in October. Sasha was suspended for three days and the access was terminated. In the interview with the principal, Sasha said: "They had it and we didn't. That seemed like the only problem." Malik finished the year with the highest math score in his class. He never mentioned the database.

---

**Apply the Parallel Chart:**

| Element | In Prometheus | In "The Download" | What Changed? | Thematic Effect |
|---------|--------------|-------------------|---------------|----------------|
| Setting | Ancient Greece; gods on Olympus | Contemporary school district | Modern bureaucratic inequality replaces divine hierarchy | The "gods" are now institutions; the fire is educational access |
| Main character | Prometheus, a Titan | Sasha, a teenager | Mortal, young, not supernatural | Makes the act of defiance available to anyone |
| Key conflict | Gods withhold fire from humanity | District withholds educational resources from poorer school | Structural inequality vs. individual access | Same structural injustice, no divine law needed |
| The test/trial | Eternal punishment | Suspension and termination of access | Finite consequence, recoverable | The modern version does not destroy the hero |
| Outcome | Prometheus is punished; humanity keeps fire | Sasha is punished; Malik keeps the knowledge | Knowledge persists; punishment is temporary | Modern story suggests the system cannot fully undo what was shared |
| Theme | The cost of bringing knowledge to those denied it | The same — but now the "hero" can survive it | Less tragic, more resilient | Modern version is more hopeful about individual defiance |

**Say:** "The author of 'The Download' renders the Prometheus myth new by updating the setting (gods → institutions), humanizing the hero (Titan → teenager), and softening the consequence (eternal torture → suspension). But the core pattern — one person risks punishment to give others access to power that was being withheld — is preserved and recognizable. That is how traditional material becomes new: the pattern stays; the specific shape changes."

### Worked Examples

**Strongest evidence that "The Download" draws on a Prometheus pattern:** "They had it and we didn't. That seemed like the only problem." — Relevance: 2. This is Sasha's statement of the central ethical principle — the same moral logic as Prometheus: the withholding of knowledge is the injustice. Specificity: 2. Directness: 2. Score: 6/6.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Apply the Myth/Modern Parallel chart collaboratively to a new pairing.

**Traditional source summary (Orpheus and Eurydice, briefly):** Orpheus, a musician of extraordinary talent, loses his wife Eurydice to death. He travels to the underworld and persuades the gods to release her with his music, on the condition that he walk ahead of her and not look back. He cannot resist looking back at the last moment; she is lost again. The story explores grief, the limits of art, and the inability to fully recover what is lost.

Read aloud the following modern passage:

---

**Passage: "The Recording"**

After his grandmother died, Tomás found three years of voice memos on her old phone — rambling, hour-long recordings of her thoughts, observations, and stories, made for reasons no one understood. He spent three weeks transcribing them. He transcribed everything: the repetitions, the pauses, the moments where she hummed before finding a word. When he finished, he printed the transcriptions, bound them, and brought the book to the family gathering. His aunt said, "You shouldn't read it — it'll make the grief worse." Tomás did not read it aloud. Instead, he set it on the table and left it there. His mother picked it up at the end of the evening. She read it for two hours without stopping. Later, she told Tomás: "She's still in there. But she's still gone." Tomás already knew this. He had known it since the first page.

---

**Work through the Parallel Chart together:**

Guide students to identify:
- Setting: modern, domestic (phone, family gathering) vs. ancient underworld
- Main character: Tomás, a grieving grandson vs. Orpheus, a grieving husband — both use a form of art/craft (music/transcription) to "recover" the lost person
- The key conflict: death as permanent loss vs. the possibility of recovery through art
- The test or trial: the condition in Orpheus is "don't look back" — what is the analogous constraint in Tomás's story? (His aunt says not to read it — he does not read it aloud, but he gives it to the family; is this the "looking back"?)
- Outcome: Orpheus loses Eurydice by looking back; Tomás "succeeds" in transcribing but the grandmother is still gone. The recorded voice is present and absent simultaneously.
- Theme: What does the modern version say that the original myth also says? Both: art can approach the lost but cannot fully return them.

**Say:** "The modern story renders the myth new by replacing the supernatural (underworld, gods, conditions) with the mundane (phone recordings, a family gathering, a bound book). But the emotional core is the same: someone uses art to try to recover what death took, and discovers that recovery is partial and bittersweet. What does the author gain by removing the supernatural? The story becomes about something anyone can do — and the grief becomes more accessible, more present."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Independently identify and analyze mythic/traditional source material in a modern passage.

Read the following passage:

---

**Passage: "The Three Tasks"**

Juniper had applied for the summer internship at the engineering lab by submitting a project portfolio, passing a technical interview, and completing a written response about her motivations. These were the stated requirements. What she found, on her first day, was that there was a fourth requirement no one had mentioned: earning the approval of the lab director's assistant, a meticulous woman named Ms. Osei, who had been with the lab for twenty years and had outlasted seven directors. Ms. Osei set Juniper three additional tasks. First: reorganize the lab's filing system, which had been "temporarily reorganized" by a previous intern and never corrected. Second: locate and catalog a collection of equipment manuals that had been misfiled across three storage rooms. Third: compose a brief history of the lab's projects over the past decade, using only the materials available in the lab. Juniper completed all three without complaint. On the last day of the second week, Ms. Osei handed her a card. On it was written: "You're ready." Juniper was not sure for what, exactly. But she was ready.

---

**Questions:**
1. Identify the traditional narrative pattern this passage most clearly draws on. Briefly describe the pattern and name one traditional source in which it appears.
2. Complete the Myth/Modern Parallel chart for this passage and your identified source. You may draw the chart in your notebook or fill it in from memory. Include at least four rows.
3. Identify two specific ways the modern passage "renders new" the traditional pattern — what did the author change, and why? What is the thematic effect of each change?
4. Identify the strongest evidence that the passage draws on a traditional source. Score it on the Evidence Strength Scale and defend it.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete questions 1, 2, and 4. For question 2, complete at least three rows of the chart.

**Stretch:** Complete all four questions. In question 3, use the word "archetype" or "traditional narrative pattern" in your analysis.

**Challenge:** Complete all four questions. Then write a short paragraph: What does this story say about the relationship between formal qualifications and genuine readiness? How does the use of a traditional pattern (the three tasks/tests) strengthen this theme?

### Interleaved Review

5. (RL.8.2 — Lessons 3-4) Apply the Theme-Character-Setting Triangle to the "Three Tasks" passage. Write a complete theme statement and identify one way the setting contributes to the theme (beyond being just a backdrop).
6. (RL.8.1 — Lessons 1-2) A student wants to support this claim: "Ms. Osei is the real judge of whether Juniper belongs at the lab." Find the strongest evidence. Score it. Then identify one piece of weak evidence a student might mistakenly choose and diagnose its failure.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following short passage:

> Sofia had received the map from her great-aunt, along with the instruction: "Follow it, but choose only one path when the road divides. The other path leads back to where you started." Sofia followed the map for three days. On the third day, the road divided. She chose the left path. At the end of the left path was the community garden her great-aunt had built forty years ago, now overgrown and forgotten. Sofia spent the summer restoring it.

Name the traditional narrative pattern this passage most resembles and identify one specific way the modern author has rendered it new. Write two sentences.

**Expected Answers:** The passage most resembles a quest pattern: a character receives guidance and undertakes a journey toward a meaningful discovery. The author renders it new by making the "treasure" at the end of the journey not gold or power but a neglected community garden — replacing material reward with the work of restoration and connection to family legacy. The contemporary emphasis on community care and inherited responsibility transforms an ancient pattern of individual heroic reward into a story about collective and intergenerational meaning.

**Mastery = Student correctly names a traditional pattern and identifies at least one specific transformation (what changed and/or why).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Ask the student: "What happens in this story? A character gets a map and follows it to find something at the end. Does that remind you of any traditional story pattern?" Then ask: "What is different about what Sofia finds at the end, compared to what a hero in a traditional quest usually finds? What does the difference mean?"

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "What theme does this modern quest develop? How does the transformation of the traditional 'treasure' shape the theme?" This bridges Lesson 9 to the integrated analysis work in Lesson 10.

## Extension (Optional)

1. Choose a book, film, or story you know well and identify the traditional narrative pattern or mythic archetype it draws on most strongly. Complete the Myth/Modern Parallel chart and write a paragraph analyzing how the material is rendered new.
2. Research one traditional story from a culture other than the dominant Western one (for example: the Epic of Gilgamesh, Anansi the spider trickster, the Journey to the West, the Mahabharata). Summarize its central pattern, then identify a modern story that uses a similar pattern and analyze the transformation.
3. Write a short modern passage (150-200 words) that deliberately draws on a traditional myth or folktale you know. After writing, annotate the passage to identify which traditional elements you used and which you transformed. Write a brief explanation of your choices.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The key distinction from RL.7.9 is worth making explicit at the start and end of the lesson: RL.7.9 compared fiction to history (accuracy, alteration); RL.8.9 compares fiction to myth and traditional story (pattern, transformation, rendering new). Students who apply the wrong lens will ask "Is this accurate to the myth?" rather than "What did the author change, and why?"
- Students do not need extensive knowledge of classical mythology to succeed in this lesson. The warm-up establishes that they already know the basic patterns. The "Prometheus" and "Orpheus" summaries in the lesson provide enough context for analysis. Avoid spending too much time on mythological background; the analysis of the modern passage is the primary skill.
- The "Three Tasks" passage in independent practice deliberately uses a pattern from fairy tales (the helper who tests the hero with three challenges) without a single specific mythological source. This is intentional — students should recognize the pattern even without naming one exact origin. If a student identifies Cinderella, folklore, or another fairy tale archetype, affirm this.
- The Myth/Modern Parallel chart is the key concrete tool for this lesson. Ensure students complete it during independent practice before moving to analysis.
- This lesson prepares students for Lesson 10 (integrated analysis), where they will apply RL.8.9 alongside RL.8.1, RL.8.2, and RL.8.6 to a single complex text.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Question 1:** The traditional narrative pattern is the "three tasks" or "triple test" — a hero figure who must prove worthiness by completing a series of increasingly difficult challenges, often set by a gatekeeper figure. This appears in numerous fairy tales (Cinderella, various Grimm tales), Greek myths (Heracles' twelve labors), and folk traditions across cultures. A gatekeeper who sets tests before allowing access to an opportunity or blessing is one of the most universal patterns in traditional storytelling.

**Question 2 (sample chart):**
| Element | In the Traditional Pattern | In "Three Tasks" | What Changed? |
|---------|---------------------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Hero | A young person of special destiny | Juniper, who has already qualified formally | The formal qualifications precede the test |
| Gatekeeper | A wizard, king, or divine figure | Ms. Osei, a 20-year administrative assistant | The gatekeeper is institutional, not supernatural |
| The three tasks | Often magical or dangerous | Mundane but exacting: filing, cataloging, writing | The tasks are about care and attention, not strength |
| Reward | Marriage, kingdom, magic power | A card that says "You're ready" | Understated, internal, not material |
| Theme | Worthiness through heroic deeds | Worthiness through careful, unglamorous attention | The definition of "readiness" has changed |

**Question 3:** Two specific transformations:
(1) The gatekeeper is institutional rather than supernatural. Ms. Osei is a 20-year administrative assistant — not a wizard or divine figure. The change grounds the test in real institutional power: the person who has been there the longest, who has seen everything, who outlasts those with official authority. This transformation says that wisdom and authority come from sustained presence and attention, not from birthright or magic.
(2) The reward is unspecified and internal. Traditional three-task stories end with a concrete reward (the hand in marriage, the kingdom). Juniper receives a card that says "You're ready" — and doesn't know for what. The rendering-new consists of making the reward about capacity and readiness rather than possession. The theme shifts from "you deserve this thing" to "you are now capable of whatever comes next."

**Question 4:** Strongest evidence: "What she found, on her first day, was that there was a fourth requirement no one had mentioned: earning the approval of the lab director's assistant...who had been with the lab for twenty years and had outlasted seven directors." — Relevance: 2 (directly establishes the hidden gatekeeper figure who sets the real test, the core of the traditional pattern). Specificity: 2 (twenty years, seven directors — concrete detail establishes authority). Directness: 2 (the phrase "fourth requirement no one had mentioned" signals the traditional hidden-test structure). Score: 6/6.

### Interleaved Review

**Question 5:** Theme-Character-Setting Triangle:
Character: Juniper wants the internship; she faces a hidden, unofficial test; she understands (implicitly) that readiness is demonstrated through unglamorous, careful work.
Setting: An engineering lab — a setting associated with precision and technical competence. The lab's messy filing system (temporary reorganization, misfiled equipment) means the setting itself is the test material. The setting is not just backdrop; it is the medium through which worthiness is assessed.
Plot: The arc moves from formal qualification → hidden test → three tasks completed → minimal verbal confirmation. The quiet resolution matches the theme.
Theme: "Formal credentials prove eligibility, but genuine readiness is demonstrated through careful, unremarkable attention to what has been left undone."

**Question 6:** Strongest evidence: "Ms. Osei set Juniper three additional tasks." — Relevance: 2. Specificity: 2. Directness: 2. Score: 6/6. This directly establishes Ms. Osei as the real judge.

Weak evidence: "Juniper had applied for the summer internship at the engineering lab by submitting a project portfolio, passing a technical interview, and completing a written response about her motivations." Diagnosis: fails relevance for this specific claim. This evidence supports the idea that Juniper met the formal requirements — which is actually the opposite of the claim that Ms. Osei is the real judge. It is irrelevant to (and arguably undermines) the claim.
