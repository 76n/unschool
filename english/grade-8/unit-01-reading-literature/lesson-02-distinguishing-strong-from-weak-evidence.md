# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 1, Lesson 2

## Topic
Distinguishing Strong from Weak Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify characteristics that make a piece of evidence weak (vague, irrelevant, or requiring excessive inferential steps)
- Revise a weak evidence selection by finding or reframing a stronger alternative from the same text
- Explain in writing why one piece of evidence is insufficient and which evidence should replace it

## Prerequisites
- Can apply the Evidence Strength Scale (relevance, specificity, directness) to evaluate and rank evidence (Lesson 1, RL.8.1)
- Can cite several pieces of textual evidence to support both explicit and inferential claims (RL.7.1)
- Understands the structure of an analytical response (claim → evidence → explanation)
- Can distinguish between direct statements in the text and inferences drawn from the text

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Highlighters in two colors
- Index cards from Lesson 1 (if saved)
- Pencils

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review the Evidence Strength Scale and introduce the idea that weak evidence has identifiable, fixable problems.

**Activity: The Weak Evidence Gallery**

Write three deliberately weak pieces of evidence on the board, all supposedly supporting the claim: *"The character feels isolated from her community."*

> **Weak A:** "She was a student at the school."
> **Weak B:** "Nobody really understood her deep down inside."
> **Weak C:** "She sat down at the lunch table and opened her bag."

**Say:** "All three of these are technically from a story about this character. But none of them are strong evidence for this claim. Your challenge: for each one, identify which criterion it fails — relevance, specificity, or directness — and explain why. You have 90 seconds."

Take responses. Guide students to see:
- Weak A fails relevance — being a student doesn't address isolation.
- Weak B fails specificity — "deep down inside" is vague and generalized.
- Weak C fails directness — it shows a neutral action with no clear connection to isolation.

**Say:** "Today we're going to go beyond identifying weak evidence. We're going to practice revising it — finding or framing stronger alternatives."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Model the process of diagnosing weak evidence and locating a stronger replacement.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Vague evidence:** Evidence that uses broad or general language that could apply to almost any character or situation, offering little analytical leverage.
- **Irrelevant evidence:** Evidence that addresses a related but different aspect of the text from the specific claim being made; often a sign the reader grabbed the nearest quotation rather than the most useful one.
- **Over-inferential evidence:** Evidence that could support the claim, but only after several large interpretive steps — the connection between the quote and the claim is loose enough that a different reader might draw a completely different conclusion.

### Instruction Notes

**Say:** "In Grade 7, you practiced finding several pieces of evidence. One challenge with multi-evidence work is that students sometimes include weak evidence to pad the count — three quotes, but the second one doesn't really do much. Grade 8 asks you to cut that second quote and replace it with a better one, or to defend your single best option. That requires knowing what's wrong with the weak evidence, not just knowing that it's weak."

Write on the board: **Diagnose → Replace → Defend**

**Say:** "The process has three steps. First, diagnose the weakness: which criterion does this evidence fail, and why? Second, replace it: go back to the text and find evidence that actually meets all three criteria for this claim. Third, defend the replacement by explaining why the new evidence is stronger."

Read aloud the following passage:

---

**Passage: "Static"**

Soren had not spoken to her mother in four days. It wasn't a fight, exactly — more like a slow withdrawal, like the tide going out when you're not watching. Her mother left dinners on the counter, warm under foil, and Soren ate them alone at the kitchen island, back to the door. She carried her phone everywhere, not because anyone was calling but because it gave her hands something to do. On Tuesday, her mother set a cup of tea on the counter beside Soren's bowl and sat down three seats away without saying anything. They sat like that for eleven minutes — Soren counted — before her mother quietly said, "I'm here when you're ready." Soren did not answer, but she moved her bowl two seats closer.

---

**Claim:** Despite their distance, Soren and her mother are still connected.

**Weak evidence selected by a hypothetical student:** "Soren had not spoken to her mother in four days."

**Say:** "Let me diagnose this. On relevance — this actually works against the claim. It's about their distance, not their connection. Score: 0. This is irrelevant to the specific claim. The student grabbed the first sentence — a common error."

**Say:** "Now I replace it. I go back to the text looking for something that shows connection despite distance. I find: 'Soren did not answer, but she moved her bowl two seats closer.' This is highly relevant — the physical movement toward her mother signals connection. It's specific — two seats is a precise, observable detail. And it's direct — the action clearly symbolizes a step toward reconnection. I'll defend that choice."

**Worked Example:**

**Claim:** Despite their distance, Soren and her mother are still connected.

**Revised strongest evidence:** "Soren did not answer, but she moved her bowl two seats closer."

**Defense:** This evidence is the strongest replacement because it directly addresses connection despite distance: the non-verbal physical act of moving closer is a clear signal that Soren is responding to her mother's offer. The specificity of "two seats closer" is more analytical than a vague reference to emotional warmth because it grounds the connection in a precise, observable action. By contrast, the original evidence selection — "Soren had not spoken to her mother in four days" — is irrelevant to the claim about connection and would actually support the opposite claim.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Practice the Diagnose → Replace → Defend process collaboratively.

Read the following passage together:

---

**Passage: "Inheritance"**

Camille's grandmother had brought three things from Haiti when she immigrated: a wooden comb, a recipe for griot, and a story she told only once a year, on New Year's Eve. The comb lived on the bathroom shelf, and Camille had run it through her hair every morning since she was five without once thinking about where it came from. The griot recipe was written in her grandmother's handwriting on a folded piece of paper inside the spice cabinet — not a card, just paper, like it might not be important. Camille had started cooking it herself this year, checking the paper constantly, afraid of getting it wrong. The New Year's Eve story she had heard seven times now. Each year it changed slightly, the details shifting like sand, but the ending was always the same: a woman standing at the edge of the sea, watching something disappear and deciding to remember it anyway.

---

**Claim:** Camille's grandmother deliberately preserved her cultural identity across generations.

**Present a deliberately weak evidence selection:** "The comb lived on the bathroom shelf, and Camille had run it through her hair every morning since she was five without once thinking about where it came from."

**Say:** "Work with me. Step one: diagnose. What's wrong with this evidence for this specific claim?"

Lead students to see: the quote describes Camille's unconscious use of the comb — it's evidence of transmission, but it actually undermines the claim about the grandmother's deliberate preservation because Camille isn't even thinking about its origin.

**Say:** "Step two: replace. Look back at the text. Find the evidence that most strongly supports deliberate preservation — intentional, purposeful, thoughtful transmission of culture."

Guide students toward: *"a story she told only once a year, on New Year's Eve"* and especially *"a woman standing at the edge of the sea, watching something disappear and deciding to remember it anyway."*

Discuss: The final line of the story-within-the-story is thematically resonant with the claim — it is itself about deliberate memory as an act of cultural preservation.

**Say:** "Step three: defend. Tell me why your replacement evidence is stronger than the original selection."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)

**Purpose:** Apply the full Diagnose → Replace → Defend process independently with a new passage.

Read the following passage independently:

---

**Passage: "The Announcement"**

The day the school announced that it would close at the end of the year, Mr. Osei pulled his seventh-period class close and asked them to write one thing they wanted to remember. He did not explain why. He did not talk about the closing, or the new building across town, or what would happen to the teachers. He just put a piece of paper on each desk and waited. The room was very quiet. Some students wrote quickly; others held their pencils and stared at the ceiling. Jasmine wrote three words, crossed them out, wrote them again. Afterward, Mr. Osei collected the papers and clipped them into a folder without reading them. He set the folder on his desk, where it sat for the rest of the week, visible to anyone who walked in. On Friday, he gave the papers back, one to each student, sealed in a small envelope.

---

**Claim:** Mr. Osei valued his students' inner responses to loss more than any official explanation.

For each task below, use the Diagnose → Replace → Defend process:

1. A student selected this evidence for the above claim: *"The day the school announced that it would close at the end of the year, Mr. Osei pulled his seventh-period class close."* Diagnose the weakness of this evidence (which criterion does it fail?), replace it with the strongest evidence you can find, and defend your replacement in 2-3 sentences.

2. A second student selected this evidence: *"Some students wrote quickly; others held their pencils and stared at the ceiling."* Diagnose its weakness for this specific claim, replace it with stronger evidence, and defend your replacement.

3. Which single piece of evidence from the entire passage is strongest for this claim? Score it on all three criteria and write a full defense using all three criterion terms.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete questions 1 and 3. For question 1, your defense should reference at least one criterion term.

**Stretch:** Complete all three questions. Your defense in question 3 must explicitly compare your chosen evidence to at least one piece you rejected.

**Challenge:** Complete all three questions with full criterion analysis. Then write one additional paragraph: Identify a claim this passage does NOT support well — one where all the evidence is weak — and explain why the author did not provide strong evidence for that claim (consider: what might the author have intended by leaving that question open?).

### Interleaved Review

4. (RL.8.1 — Lesson 1) From the passage above, find two pieces of evidence that together support the inference that Mr. Osei was protecting his students from something painful. Score both on the Evidence Strength Scale and explain why these two pieces work better together than either works alone.
5. (RL.7.2 — Grade 7 skill) Write a two-sentence objective summary of the passage. Your summary should be free of personal opinion and should capture only the essential events.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following short passage:

> Tomás trained for the regional chess tournament for six months, studying openings and watching recorded matches for hours each evening. At the tournament, he lost in the second round to a player he had studied carefully. On the bus home, he opened his notebook and wrote down everything he remembered about the game, especially the moves he had not anticipated.

**Claim:** Tomás approaches failure as an opportunity to improve.

A student selected this evidence: *"Tomás trained for the regional chess tournament for six months."*

What is wrong with this evidence for this specific claim? Name the criterion it fails and identify stronger evidence from the passage.

**Expected Answers:** This evidence fails relevance — it shows preparation before failure, not a response to failure. It does not address how Tomás treats failure as an opportunity. Stronger evidence: *"On the bus home, he opened his notebook and wrote down everything he remembered about the game, especially the moves he had not anticipated."* This is directly relevant (it shows his response to losing), highly specific (he writes immediately, focusing specifically on what he didn't anticipate), and direct (writing to analyze failure is clearly an improvement-oriented behavior).

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies a relevance failure and points to stronger evidence anywhere in the passage.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Ask the student to first identify what the claim is specifically about (response to failure) and then check each piece of evidence against that specific focus. Students who are selecting any evidence that mentions Tomás — rather than evidence that matches the specific claim — need scaffolding on claim-to-evidence alignment.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "Could the training evidence ever be used in a different argument about Tomás? What claim would it be strong evidence for?" This reinforces the idea that evidence quality is always relative to a specific claim.

## Extension (Optional)

1. Find a paragraph you have written before — for any class — that includes multiple pieces of evidence. Score each piece on the Evidence Strength Scale. If any score below 4/6, revise the paragraph using the Diagnose → Replace → Defend process.
2. Create a "Weak Evidence Hall of Fame" — a list of five types of evidence that commonly appear in student writing but are typically weak (e.g., character names without context, general descriptions of setting, plot-summary quotes that don't address the claim). For each type, write one example and explain which criterion it fails.
3. Working from a book you have read recently, choose a claim about the main character and deliberately find the weakest possible evidence in the text. Write an explanation of why it is the weakest option before identifying the strongest alternative.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- Students often have an easier time identifying weak evidence than explaining what makes it weak. The diagnostic vocabulary (relevance, specificity, directness) from Lesson 1 is the key tool here. Require students to use these terms — not just "it doesn't work" — in their diagnoses.
- A common student error is selecting evidence that contradicts rather than fails to support a claim. Treat this as a separate category: counter-evidence. It is not just weak — it actively undermines. Naming this distinction helps students understand that evidence evaluation is not binary (good/bad) but nuanced.
- The independent practice passage includes a deliberately ambiguous action (collecting the papers without reading them) that can support multiple interpretations. This is intentional. Use it to show students that the quality of evidence depends on the precision of the claim — changing the claim changes which evidence is strongest.
- If using the index cards from Lesson 1, have students revisit the evidence they scored and check whether any originally high-scored options are actually weaker than they thought on closer reading.
- Keep the "Diagnose → Replace → Defend" framework visible on the board throughout the lesson. Students may want to copy it into their notes as a portable revision strategy.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Question 1:**
Diagnosis: The evidence fails relevance. It describes Mr. Osei gathering his students, which is a physical action of proximity but does not address his valuing of inner responses or his stance toward official explanations. It is essentially a scene-setting detail.
Replacement: "He did not explain why. He did not talk about the closing, or the new building across town, or what would happen to the teachers. He just put a piece of paper on each desk and waited." (or: "He collected the papers and clipped them into a folder without reading them.")
Defense: The replacement evidence is directly relevant because it shows Mr. Osei actively choosing not to offer official explanations — creating space for students' internal responses instead. "He just put a piece of paper on each desk and waited" is specific and direct in showing a deliberate action that prioritizes student agency over information delivery.

**Question 2:**
Diagnosis: This evidence fails directness for this specific claim. It describes student reactions (some writing quickly, some staring) rather than Mr. Osei's intentions or values. To connect it to the claim about Mr. Osei's priorities requires several inferential steps — we must infer from student behavior what the teacher valued, rather than seeing Mr. Osei's own choices as evidence.
Replacement: "He did not explain why" or the sealed envelope return. The sealed envelope is strong: "He gave the papers back, one to each student, sealed in a small envelope." This shows Mr. Osei treating their written responses as private and valuable — returning them without reading, preserving their inner experience.
Defense: The sealed envelope evidence is more direct because it shows Mr. Osei's concrete choice to protect the privacy and value of students' responses, supporting the claim that he valued inner responses above all without requiring inference through student behavior.

**Question 3:** Strongest evidence overall: "He did not explain why. He did not talk about the closing, or the new building across town, or what would happen to the teachers. He just put a piece of paper on each desk and waited." Relevance: 2 (directly contrasts official explanation with silent invitation to inner response). Specificity: 2 (three specific withheld topics named, then one simple action). Directness: 2 (the absence of explanation is itself the evidence of his values). Total: 6/6. This evidence is strongest because it captures the claim's two components simultaneously — what Mr. Osei does not do (give official explanations) and what he does instead (invites silent inner response).

### Interleaved Review

**Question 4:** Evidence pair for the inference that Mr. Osei was protecting students: (1) "He did not explain why. He did not talk about the closing, or the new building across town, or what would happen to the teachers." Score: 6/6 for this claim. (2) "He gave the papers back, one to each student, sealed in a small envelope." Score: 5-6/6. Together, these pieces show two protective actions: withholding potentially distressing information and returning students' private responses without exposing them. Either piece alone would show one protective gesture; together they show a consistent pattern of protection.

**Question 5:** Sample objective summary: On the day their school's closure was announced, a teacher named Mr. Osei asked his seventh-period students to write one thing they wanted to remember, without explaining why or discussing the closing. He collected the papers without reading them, kept them visible on his desk for a week, and then returned them to each student in a sealed envelope.

### Exit Ticket
Diagnosis: relevance failure. Stronger evidence: the final sentence about writing in the notebook on the bus home. See Expected Answers above.
