# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic
Evaluating Conflicting Sources: Fact vs. Interpretation

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish clearly between factual claims (which can be verified) and interpretive claims (which involve judgment)
- Identify in a pair of conflicting texts whether the disagreement is factual, interpretive, or both
- Evaluate which source is more credible on a specific question and justify that evaluation with textual evidence

## Prerequisites
- Identifying factual and interpretive conflicts across texts (RI.8.9, Lesson 8)
- Applying the Source Reliability Checklist to paired texts (Lesson 8)
- Evaluating argument claims, reasoning, and evidence (RI.8.8, Lessons 6–7)
- Distinguishing central idea from supporting ideas (RI.8.2, Lessons 2–3)

## Materials Needed
- Lesson 9 paired passage copies
- Source Reliability Checklist (same graphic organizer from Lesson 8)
- Fact/Interpretation Classification chart (two-column: Factual Claims | Interpretive Claims, for each text)
- Highlighters (two colors — one per text)
- Student notebook

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build precise vocabulary for distinguishing fact from interpretation.

**Activity: Sort the Statements**

Read the following five statements aloud. Ask students to classify each as Fact (F), Interpretation (I), or Ambiguous (A):

1. "The average global temperature has increased by approximately 1.1°C since the pre-industrial era."
2. "Climate change is the most urgent crisis facing humanity."
3. "Plastic straws were banned in the city in 2019."
4. "The plastic straw ban has been highly effective."
5. "Carbon dioxide levels in the atmosphere are now higher than at any point in the last 800,000 years."

Allow 2 minutes for quiet sorting, then brief discussion. Focus on the ambiguous cases:
- Statement 2: "Most urgent crisis" is a comparative judgment that requires defining "crisis" and ranking all crises. This is clearly interpretive.
- Statement 4: "Highly effective" requires a definition of effectiveness (effective at what? compared to what baseline?) and a judgment about whether the measured change meets that standard. Also interpretive.

Say: *"The boundary between fact and interpretation can be subtle, but the test is: CAN THIS BE VERIFIED by measurement or documentation, independent of anyone's judgment? Facts pass the test. Interpretations require someone to decide what counts, what threshold matters, or what the facts mean."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Deepen students' ability to distinguish fact from interpretation and apply this to evaluating which of two conflicting sources is more credible.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Factual claim:** A statement about what exists, what happened, or what was measured — verifiable through evidence independent of the claimant's judgment
- **Interpretive claim:** A statement about what the facts mean, how significant they are, or what should be done in response — requires judgment, values, or framing decisions that go beyond the facts themselves
- **Credibility:** The degree to which a source's claims can be trusted — based on evidence quality, author expertise, transparency about methods, and absence of clear conflicts of interest

### Instruction Notes

Say: *"In Lesson 8 we identified that texts can conflict factually (they disagree about what happened) or interpretively (they agree about what happened but disagree about what it means). Today we sharpen that distinction and add a new skill: deciding which source is MORE CREDIBLE."*

Write on the board the three-question test for facts vs. interpretation:

1. **The Verification Test:** Can this claim be checked against external records, measurements, or documentation? If yes → more likely a factual claim.
2. **The Judgment Test:** Does accepting or rejecting this claim require deciding what counts or what matters? If yes → interpretive claim.
3. **The "Both Could Be Right" Test:** Could two intelligent, honest people who agree on all the facts still disagree on this claim? If yes → interpretive claim.

Explain credibility evaluation:

Say: *"When two sources conflict, we want to know which to trust more — especially on factual claims. For interpretive claims, reasonable people can disagree, and neither source is automatically right or wrong. But for factual claims, one source is more accurate and the other is wrong or misleading. Figuring out which is which requires examining:"*

- **Source expertise:** Is the author or organization an expert in the specific claim being made?
- **Evidence specificity:** Are the specific data points verifiable? Are they from primary research or secondary summaries?
- **Conflicts of interest:** Does the source have a financial or organizational stake in the claim being believed?
- **Transparency:** Does the source explain where its data comes from? Does it acknowledge limitations?

### Worked Examples

Read the following two passages aloud, then model the Fact/Interpretation classification and credibility comparison.

---

**Text A: "Sugar and Children's Behavior: What Research Shows"**
*(From a pediatric nutrition research summary)*

A rigorous body of research has examined the widely held belief that sugar consumption causes hyperactive behavior in children. Double-blind, placebo-controlled trials — in which neither children, parents, nor observers knew whether the children had consumed sugar or a non-sugar placebo — consistently fail to find any measurable difference in behavior. When parents are told their child consumed sugar (regardless of whether they actually did), they rate their child's behavior as more hyperactive. This finding suggests that the "sugar high" is largely a product of expectation rather than biochemistry. Nutritionists caution that this does not mean sugar is harmless; excessive sugar consumption is associated with dental caries, insulin dysregulation, and other health outcomes. But the specific claim that sugar causes hyperactivity in children is not supported by controlled research.

---

**Text B: "A Parent's Guide to Diet and Behavior"**
*(From a parenting lifestyle website)*

Most parents who have watched their children at birthday parties already know the answer: sugar makes kids hyper. It is one of the most reliable patterns in parenting. Children who eat cake and candy at celebrations are noticeably more energetic and harder to manage afterward. Some nutritionists argue that research does not support this link, but parents who observe their own children day after day are drawing on a far larger data set than any lab study. The body is complex, and what works in a controlled setting may not reflect real-world conditions. Common sense, refined through years of direct observation, is itself a form of evidence.

---

Model the Fact/Interpretation classification:

**Text A — Factual claims:** Double-blind trials show no measurable behavioral difference; parents told child consumed sugar rate behavior as more hyperactive regardless of actual consumption.
**Text A — Interpretive claims:** The "sugar high" is largely expectation, not biochemistry; sugar is not harmless in other ways.

**Text B — Factual claims:** None that are specifically verifiable. "Children are noticeably more energetic after sugar" is presented as observable but is not measured.
**Text B — Interpretive claims:** Parental observation is a valid and large data set; common sense is itself a form of evidence; lab settings may not reflect real-world conditions.

**Credibility comparison:**
- Text A explicitly describes its evidence methodology (double-blind, placebo-controlled), making its factual claims verifiable and its methods transparent. The source (pediatric nutrition research summary) has domain expertise.
- Text B's primary claim ("sugar makes kids hyper") is based on anecdotal parental observation, which Text A's research specifically addresses — and explains as expectation bias rather than biochemical reality.
- Factual conflict: Text A says controlled research shows no behavioral link. Text B says observed experience shows a link. This is both a factual and an interpretive conflict. The factual conflict is resolvable: controlled studies that eliminate expectation bias are a higher standard of evidence than uncontrolled parental observation.
- **Credibility assessment:** Text A is more credible on the factual question because its methodology specifically controls for the confounding variable (expectation) that explains Text B's observations.

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply fact/interpretation classification and credibility evaluation to a new paired text set with facilitator support.

Read both passages aloud together.

---

**Text C: "The Promise of Geoengineering"**
*(From a climate policy research institute)*

Stratospheric aerosol injection (SAI) — the deliberate injection of reflective particles into the upper atmosphere to reduce incoming solar radiation — has emerged as a potentially viable near-term response to climate change. Preliminary modeling studies suggest that SAI could reduce global average temperatures by 1–2°C within years of initial deployment, offering a breathing space during which emissions reductions can be implemented. Critics' concerns about uneven regional effects are legitimate but addressable through adaptive deployment strategies. The technology does not address ocean acidification, and it would need to be maintained indefinitely if emissions continue. However, in a scenario where emissions reductions fall short of targets, SAI may represent the difference between a manageable and catastrophic warming trajectory.

---

**Text D: "Geoengineering: Playing with Fire"**
*(From an environmental science columnist)*

Proposals to deploy stratospheric aerosol injection as a climate solution should be approached with extreme caution, if at all. The modeling studies that predict cooling effects are based on assumptions about atmospheric dynamics that are not yet validated at scale. History provides cautionary examples: the 1991 eruption of Mount Pinatubo, which injected sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere naturally, produced measurable global cooling but also disrupted monsoon patterns in South Asia and Africa, contributing to agricultural failures. Deliberately engineering a similar effect, at scale, without the ability to quickly reverse the intervention, is a profound gamble. Moreover, the availability of a perceived technological "fix" may reduce the political pressure to reduce emissions — the core problem. Geoengineering research should continue in controlled settings; actual deployment is a different matter entirely.

---

Work through the Fact/Interpretation classification together, then discuss:

- *"What factual claims does each text make? Are any of them contradictory?"*
  - Text C: SAI could reduce temperatures 1–2°C (modeling); Text D: 1991 Pinatubo disrupted monsoon patterns (historical fact). These are compatible, not contradictory.
- *"Where is the real conflict — factual or interpretive?"*
  - Primarily interpretive: Text C treats SAI as a potentially viable near-term tool; Text D treats it as a profound gamble with unacceptable risks.
- *"Which source is more credible on the specific factual claim that SAI can reduce temperatures?"*
  - Both acknowledge the modeling evidence. Text C cites it positively; Text D notes the assumptions are unvalidated. Neither source presents original primary data — both are secondary. Text D has a concrete historical example (Pinatubo) that is a form of primary evidence for unintended consequences.
- *"On the interpretive question of whether SAI should be deployed — can one source be 'more credible' than the other?"*
  - This requires judgment; both perspectives are reasonable. Students should articulate this is an interpretive conflict where neither is automatically right.

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently classify facts vs. interpretations and evaluate credibility in a new paired text set.

Read both passages, then complete the questions.

---

**Text E: "The Case for Year-Round Schooling"**
*(From an education policy research center)*

Year-round school calendars — which distribute instruction and breaks more evenly throughout the year rather than concentrating a long summer vacation — have demonstrated measurable benefits in school districts that have implemented them. Studies following students through multi-track, year-round programs find reduced "summer slide" — the loss of academic skills that occurs during extended breaks — particularly for students from lower-income households who have less access to enrichment activities during the summer. Schools operating on year-round calendars also report lower rates of teacher and student burnout compared to traditional calendars, as shorter, more frequent breaks distribute recovery time more evenly. Implementation challenges are real but administrative in nature; they do not represent fundamental barriers to adoption.

---

**Text F: "Year-Round Schools: The Evidence Is Weaker Than Claimed"**
*(From an independent education journalism outlet)*

The case for year-round schooling, as commonly presented, overstates the strength of the evidence. Studies showing academic benefits from year-round programs often compare schools that have chosen to adopt year-round calendars — which may already serve more motivated students or have more engaged administrative leadership — with schools that have not. This selection bias makes it difficult to determine whether year-round scheduling itself is producing the observed benefits, or whether the type of school that adopts year-round scheduling is simply a more effective school in general. The studies most frequently cited date from the 1980s and 1990s and have not been replicated at the same scale with more rigorous controls. Teacher satisfaction data also shows mixed results; some surveys show higher satisfaction in year-round schools, but others show concerns about the disruption to family schedules and professional development opportunities.

---

**Questions:**

1. Using the Fact/Interpretation Classification chart, list two factual claims and two interpretive claims from each text.

2. Identify one specific point where the two texts disagree. Is this a factual conflict or an interpretive conflict? Explain.

3. Based on the Source Reliability Checklist criteria, which text is more credible on the specific factual question of whether year-round schooling reduces summer slide? Justify your answer with at least two criteria from the checklist.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete questions 1 and 2. For question 2, identify the factual conflict only.

**Stretch:** Complete all three questions. For question 3, write a full paragraph evaluating both sources' credibility and identifying the specific methodological concern Text F raises about Text E's evidence.

**Challenge:** Complete all three questions. Then write a synthesis paragraph: "Given the conflicting evidence about year-round schooling, what would a policymaker need to know before recommending adoption? What would the ideal study look like?"

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1 (RI.8.8 — Irrelevant Evidence):** Text E says "Implementation challenges are real but administrative in nature; they do not represent fundamental barriers to adoption." Is this evidence for the argument that year-round schooling is beneficial, or is it irrelevant? Explain.

**Review Question 2 (RI.8.3 — Categorization):** Text F distinguishes between "schools that have chosen to adopt year-round calendars" and schools in general. What argument does this distinction serve? How does it function as a categorization move?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

Read these two statements about the same topic and answer the question.

> **Source A:** "A long-term study following 2,500 participants found that people who reported eating breakfast daily had lower rates of obesity than those who skipped breakfast regularly."
> **Source B:** "Breakfast is the most important meal of the day."

**Question:** Which statement is factual and which is interpretive? For the interpretive statement, explain specifically what judgment it requires that goes beyond the facts.

**Expected Answers:**

Source A is a factual claim — it reports what a specific study found (correlation between breakfast and lower obesity rates in a defined population). Source B is an interpretive claim. "Most important" requires a judgment about importance: compared to what? By what measure? Importance for what outcome? Even if breakfast correlates with lower obesity, calling it "most important" requires deciding that this outcome matters more than others and that breakfast's contribution is greater than all other meals — judgments that go beyond what any study can establish.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies A as factual and B as interpretive AND explains at least one specific judgment embedded in "most important" that the facts cannot settle.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Ask: "What would someone need to agree on to decide if breakfast is the MOST important meal? Could two people who read the same study still disagree about whether breakfast is more important than dinner? Why?" Walk through the verification test and judgment test from Direct Instruction.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "How might someone use Source A to argue that Source B is true? Is that argument sound? What would make it stronger?" Reinforce that factual evidence can support interpretive claims but cannot by itself establish them.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- This lesson is the most conceptually demanding in the unit. Students who have difficulty with the factual/interpretive distinction may need additional modeling before moving to the independent practice. The three-question test from Direct Instruction (Verification Test, Judgment Test, "Both Could Be Right" Test) should stay visible on the board throughout the lesson.
- The credibility evaluation in question 3 of Independent Practice is designed to reward precise reasoning, not subjective preference. Students should be challenged to articulate specific criteria (methodology, selection bias, recency of studies) rather than simply saying "I trust this one more."
- Text F's selection bias argument is the most sophisticated analytical move in the lesson. Students who identify and understand it are demonstrating strong RI.8.8/8.9 reasoning. Highlight this move for the class.
- Lessons 8 and 9 together complete the RI.8.9 sequence. Before moving to Lesson 10, briefly review: RI.8.9 requires students to identify WHERE texts disagree (Lesson 8) and to distinguish factual from interpretive conflicts and evaluate credibility (Lesson 9).
- The synthesis work in the Challenge tier is a direct preview of the research writing skills students will develop in Unit 3 and Unit 4.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Question 1:**

Text E — Factual claims: "Studies following students through multi-track, year-round programs find reduced summer slide, particularly for students from lower-income households"; "Schools operating on year-round calendars also report lower rates of teacher and student burnout."
Text E — Interpretive claims: "Implementation challenges... do not represent fundamental barriers to adoption"; "Year-round school calendars have demonstrated measurable benefits."

Text F — Factual claims: "The studies most frequently cited date from the 1980s and 1990s and have not been replicated at the same scale with more rigorous controls"; "Some surveys show higher satisfaction in year-round schools, but others show concerns about disruption to family schedules."
Text F — Interpretive claims: "The case for year-round schooling overstates the strength of the evidence"; "Selection bias makes it difficult to determine whether year-round scheduling itself is producing the observed benefits."

**Question 2:** One specific factual conflict: Text E says studies "find reduced summer slide"; Text F argues those studies may be flawed due to selection bias (schools that choose year-round may already be more effective). This has both a factual dimension (whether the studies actually show what Text E claims) and an interpretive dimension (whether the selection bias problem is severe enough to dismiss the findings). The factual question (do the studies have adequate controls?) is in principle verifiable by examining the methodology of cited studies. The interpretive question (is the evidence still useful despite its limits?) is where the two texts most fundamentally disagree.

**Question 3 (sample):** Text F is more credible on the specific factual question of whether year-round schooling reduces summer slide, for two reasons. First, Text F raises a specific methodological concern (selection bias) that Text E does not address — which means Text F's skepticism is more transparent about its reasoning. Second, Text F notes the evidence base is dated (1980s–1990s) and not replicated with rigorous controls, which is a factual observation about the evidentiary record that Text E does not dispute. Text E makes the stronger practical case for adoption, but Text F more carefully evaluates the evidence quality.

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1:** "Implementation challenges are real but administrative in nature" is partially irrelevant to the core argument (that year-round schooling produces benefits). It is a preemptive rebuttal of a counterargument — it addresses why we shouldn't dismiss year-round schooling on logistical grounds. It is not evidence that year-round schooling is beneficial; it is evidence that one objection to it is manageable. This is a common argumentative move but should be recognized as rebuttal content, not positive evidence.

**Review Question 2:** Text F distinguishes "schools that have chosen to adopt year-round calendars" from schools in general. The argument this serves: schools that voluntarily adopt year-round calendars are probably not a random sample of schools — they may be more motivated, better resourced, or more administratively innovative. By drawing this distinction, Text F argues that comparing year-round adopters to non-adopters is like comparing apples and oranges. This is a categorization move (not all schools are the same category) that serves a methodological skepticism argument.

### Exit Ticket

**Full credit:** Correctly identifies Source A as factual (specific study, specific population, specific finding) and Source B as interpretive; explains that "most important" requires a judgment about importance relative to other meals and other health outcomes — a judgment the study's correlation data cannot establish.

**Partial credit:** Correctly identifies which is factual and which is interpretive but cannot explain what specific judgment "most important" requires.
