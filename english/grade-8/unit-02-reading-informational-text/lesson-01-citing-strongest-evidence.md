# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 2, Lesson 1

## Topic
Citing the Strongest Evidence in Nonfiction

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between relevant and strongest evidence in a nonfiction text
- Apply the Evidence Strength Meter to rank multiple pieces of textual evidence
- Cite the most compelling evidence to support both explicit and inferential claims about a text

## Prerequisites
- Citing multiple pieces of textual evidence to support analysis (RI.7.1)
- Identifying explicit and implicit meaning in informational text (Grade 7)
- Writing objective summaries of informational texts (Grade 7)
- Basic close reading and annotation strategies practiced in Grade 8, Unit 1

## Materials Needed
- Lesson 1 passage copies (three passages: modeled, guided, independent)
- Evidence Strength Meter graphic organizer (three-column: Evidence Quote | Why It's Strong | Strength Rating 1–5)
- Highlighters (two colors: one for explicit claims, one for inferences)
- Student notebook or response journal

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of textual evidence and create cognitive need for the "strongest" distinction.

**Activity: The Evidence Lineup**

Place the following three statements on the board or read them aloud. Tell students they are all about the same claim: *"The Mars habitat program faces serious technical challenges."*

> **Statement A:** "The project has encountered some difficulties."
> **Statement B:** "Engineers reported that three of the five life-support prototypes failed pressure testing."
> **Statement C:** "Space exploration is complicated."

Say: *"All three of these sentences come from an article about Mars colonization. If you had to pick one to support the claim that the Mars habitat program faces serious technical challenges, which would you pick — and why?"*

Allow 60 seconds of quiet thinking, then brief discussion. Students should identify Statement B as strongest. Ask: *"What makes B stronger than A or C? What does B have that the others don't?"*

Guide students toward: specificity (three of five), concrete detail (pressure testing), direct relevance (life-support failure). Tell students: *"Today we're developing a systematic way to make this judgment every time we read nonfiction."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the Evidence Strength Meter framework and the criteria for evaluating evidence strength.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Textual evidence:** A direct quotation or close paraphrase from the text used to support a claim or analysis
- **Explicit information:** What the text directly states; can be found word-for-word
- **Inference:** A conclusion drawn from textual evidence that the author implies but does not directly state

### Instruction Notes

Say: *"In Grade 7, you learned to cite evidence — to find it, quote it, and connect it to your claim. That's a critical skill. But Grade 8 adds a new demand: not just any evidence, but the strongest evidence. Let me show you what that means."*

Introduce the Evidence Strength Meter. Write the three criteria on the board:
1. **Directness** — Does the evidence speak directly to the claim, or only loosely relate to it?
2. **Specificity** — Does the evidence include specific details, data, or concrete examples? Or is it vague and general?
3. **Sufficiency** — Does the evidence, on its own or with one other piece, fully support the claim?

Say: *"We'll rate evidence on each criterion from 1 to 5, then add the scores. The highest-scoring evidence is usually our strongest choice."*

Explain the difference between supporting explicit claims and supporting inferences:
- Explicit claim: Something the author directly states → find evidence that directly quotes or closely paraphrases that statement.
- Inferential claim: Something you conclude from reading → find evidence that most strongly implies the conclusion, even if the author never says it outright.

Say: *"Stronger evidence for an inference often does something clever: it gives you a specific detail that points powerfully in one direction without saying the conclusion. We'll practice finding that kind of evidence today."*

### Worked Examples

Read the following passage aloud, then model the Evidence Strength Meter.

---

**Passage A: "The Deep Freeze Option"**

For decades, scientists have debated whether cryopreservation — the ultra-low-temperature storage of biological material — could one day be used to preserve entire human bodies for future revival. While science fiction has dramatized the idea, real research in the field tells a more complicated story. Laboratories studying cryonics have successfully preserved small tissue samples and even some simple organisms at temperatures near absolute zero, but no team has reversed the process in a mammal of any significant size. The core problem is ice crystal formation: when water inside cells freezes, it expands and punctures cell membranes, causing damage that current technology cannot repair. Some researchers have explored using chemical antifreeze agents to prevent crystal formation, but these substances are themselves toxic at the concentrations required for whole-body preservation. Most mainstream biologists consider whole-body human cryopreservation a distant theoretical possibility at best, and a scientific impossibility at worst. The gap between preserving a sample of tissue and preserving a conscious human mind, they argue, may be unbridgeable with any foreseeable technology.

---

**Claim to support (explicit):** *The text says that cryopreservation of entire human bodies has not been achieved.*

Model filling out the Evidence Strength Meter with three candidate pieces of evidence:

| Evidence Quote | Why It's Strong | Rating (1–5 per criterion) | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| "no team has reversed the process in a mammal of any significant size" | Directly addresses the claim; specific (mammals, significant size); sufficient to support the claim | D:5, S:4, Su:5 | 14 |
| "real research in the field tells a more complicated story" | Relevant but vague — doesn't confirm what has or hasn't been achieved | D:3, S:1, Su:2 | 6 |
| "Most mainstream biologists consider whole-body human cryopreservation a distant theoretical possibility at best" | Strong but addresses expert opinion on possibility, not current achievement | D:3, S:4, Su:3 | 10 |

Say: *"The first evidence scores highest because it directly and specifically answers the claim. Let's now look at an inferential claim."*

**Claim to support (inferential):** *The article implies that the obstacles to cryopreservation are not merely technical but may be fundamentally unsolvable.*

Model: The strongest evidence for this inference is: *"The gap between preserving a sample of tissue and preserving a conscious human mind, they argue, may be unbridgeable with any foreseeable technology."* Why? It uses the word "unbridgeable" — implying a limit, not just a challenge — and "conscious human mind," elevating the problem beyond simple biology into something with no foreseeable solution.

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply the Evidence Strength Meter with facilitator support.

Read the following passage aloud together.

---

**Passage B: "The Rewilding Experiment"**

In the early 2000s, wildlife managers in a mountainous region of central Europe quietly reintroduced a small population of gray wolves to an area that had been wolf-free for more than seventy years. The decision was controversial. Local sheep farmers immediately organized protests, warning that their livelihoods would be destroyed. Government officials estimated that annual livestock losses could rise by as much as forty percent in the first five years. But something unexpected happened. Within three years of the wolves' return, the deer population — which had grown unchecked — began to decline and shift its grazing patterns away from riverbanks. This behavioral change allowed riverside vegetation to recover. Willows and aspens grew back. Their roots stabilized the riverbanks, which reduced erosion and improved water clarity downstream. Fish populations rebounded in rivers that had been murky for decades. Scientists studying the phenomenon coined a term for this cascade of effects: a trophic cascade, in which a single predator at the top of a food chain reshapes the behavior and distribution of species far below it, transforming entire landscapes. Livestock losses among farmers, it turned out, were far lower than predicted — roughly eight percent annually, a figure that government compensation programs largely offset.

---

Work through the following two claims together, completing the Evidence Strength Meter for each:

**Claim 1 (explicit):** *The text states that the reintroduction of wolves produced unexpected positive ecological effects.*

Have students identify three candidate evidence quotes. As a group, rate each on directness, specificity, and sufficiency. Likely strongest: *"Within three years of the wolves' return, the deer population... began to decline and shift its grazing patterns away from riverbanks. This behavioral change allowed riverside vegetation to recover."* — specific, directly states an effect, sufficient to support "unexpected positive ecological effects."

**Claim 2 (inferential):** *The passage implies that initial fears about the wolf reintroduction were exaggerated.*

Guide students to find: *"Livestock losses among farmers, it turned out, were far lower than predicted — roughly eight percent annually, a figure that government compensation programs largely offset."* Contrast this with the prediction of "forty percent." The specific comparison between prediction and outcome is the strongest evidence for the inference.

Ask: *"Why is the specific number (eight percent vs. forty percent) so important to evidence strength here?"* Students should articulate: precision makes the contrast undeniable; vague evidence would allow room to dispute the claim.

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently apply the Evidence Strength Meter to a new passage.

Read the following passage, then complete the questions independently.

---

**Passage C: "Signal or Noise? The Science of Sleep"**

Teenagers, many researchers now argue, are not simply lazy when they sleep until noon on weekends — they are responding to a genuine biological shift. During puberty, the human brain undergoes changes in its circadian rhythm, the internal clock that regulates the sleep-wake cycle. Studies measuring melatonin production in adolescents consistently show a delay: in teenagers, melatonin release — the hormonal signal that triggers drowsiness — begins approximately two hours later than in adults or young children. This means that asking a fourteen-year-old to fall asleep at ten o'clock at night is, from a biological standpoint, roughly equivalent to asking an adult to fall asleep at eight. Yet most middle and high schools begin classes between 7:00 and 8:00 a.m. — during what researchers describe as the peak of the adolescent sleep phase. Students forced to wake during this phase experience not just fatigue but measurable impairment in memory consolidation, attention, and emotional regulation. School districts that have experimented with later start times — moving first bell from 7:30 to 8:30 — report significant improvements in student attendance, academic performance, and self-reported wellbeing. Critics of later start times point to logistical challenges: transportation costs, after-school activities, and working parents who rely on early school schedules. These are real concerns. But researchers argue that the costs of chronic adolescent sleep deprivation — in learning loss and long-term health — may outweigh the inconvenience of schedule changes.

---

**Questions:**

1. Identify the claim: *The passage argues that adolescent sleep patterns are biologically determined, not a matter of laziness or choice.* Using the Evidence Strength Meter, find the two strongest pieces of evidence for this claim. Quote each piece of evidence and explain your rating.

2. Identify this inference: *The author implies that current school start times are designed around adult schedules rather than adolescent biology.* Find the single strongest piece of evidence to support this inference. Explain why it is stronger than other available evidence.

3. The passage also mentions critics of later start times. Is this a strong piece of evidence for or against the passage's central argument? Using the Evidence Strength Meter criteria, explain your reasoning.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete questions 1 and 2. For question 1, use the Evidence Strength Meter graphic organizer. For question 2, write 2–3 sentences explaining your choice.

**Stretch:** Complete all three questions. For question 3, write a paragraph explaining how the author uses the critics' objections strategically within the argument.

**Challenge:** Complete all three questions. Additionally, write a fourth question of your own that requires finding the strongest evidence for an inference about this passage. Then answer your own question.

### Interleaved Review

Answer these two questions reviewing prior standards:

**Review Question 1 (RI.7.2 — central idea):** In one sentence, state the central idea of Passage C. Be sure your answer is a complete claim, not just a topic.

**Review Question 2 (Grade 8, Unit 1 — Literary Text):** In literary texts, authors use figurative language to suggest meaning indirectly. In informational text, authors use evidence in a similar way — to imply as well as state. Identify one moment in Passage C where the author uses a specific detail to imply something beyond what is directly stated. What is the implied meaning?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short passage, then answer the question.

---

Urban heat islands — areas within cities that experience significantly higher temperatures than surrounding rural areas — are intensifying as global temperatures rise. In one major metropolitan region studied over five years, researchers found that surface temperatures in densely built neighborhoods averaged 6.4°C higher than in nearby parkland. Residents in these neighborhoods reported higher rates of heat-related illness during summer months. City planners have responded by expanding tree canopy cover and installing reflective rooftop materials in the highest-risk zones.

---

**Question:** A student wants to support this claim: *"The urban heat island effect creates measurable health risks for city residents."* Which piece of evidence from the passage is strongest? Quote the evidence and explain in one sentence why it is stronger than other available evidence.

**Expected Answers:**

Strongest evidence: *"Residents in these neighborhoods reported higher rates of heat-related illness during summer months."* This is the strongest choice because it directly and specifically links urban heat islands to a health outcome (heat-related illness) in the affected population. The temperature data (6.4°C difference) is also strong but speaks to the phenomenon's intensity rather than its health impact directly.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies either the illness statistic or the temperature data as strongest AND provides a clear, relevant explanation tied to at least one Evidence Strength Meter criterion (directness, specificity, or sufficiency).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Review the Evidence Strength Meter criteria with the student. Ask: "What would perfect evidence for this claim look like?" Walk the student through the rating process for each piece of evidence in the passage. Revisit Warm-Up and Direct Instruction examples before proceeding to Lesson 2.

### If Mastery Is Met
Affirm the student's reasoning, noting specifically which criterion they applied most effectively. Preview: "Tomorrow we're going to take this skill and use it to do something even more challenging — figure out the central idea of a text when the author never states it directly."

---

## Extension (Optional)

1. Find a news article or magazine piece on a topic of your choice. Using the Evidence Strength Meter, identify three pieces of evidence the author uses to support the main claim. Rank them and explain your ranking in a short paragraph.

2. Rewrite the weakest evidence from Passage C (the sleep science passage) so that it becomes stronger evidence for the same point. What specific details would you add or change?

3. Thought experiment: Can evidence ever be too specific? Write a short paragraph arguing that there are cases when extremely specific evidence can actually weaken a claim — and give an example.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The Evidence Strength Meter is the most important tool introduced in this unit. Every subsequent lesson should briefly reference it when students are asked to cite evidence, even if the lesson is focused on a different standard. Consistency of vocabulary and tool use accelerates transfer.
- Students commonly confuse "relevant evidence" with "strongest evidence." During guided practice, push back if students simply find relevant evidence without evaluating it against other options. Ask: "Is this your first choice? Why not the sentence before it?"
- The three-criterion rating system (directness, specificity, sufficiency) can produce ties. When it does, use the opportunity to discuss how two pieces of evidence can work together — and that citing more than one strong piece is often appropriate.
- Passages in this lesson are designed to be accessible while introducing substantive content. Students who are unfamiliar with cryonics or circadian rhythms may need brief vocabulary support before reading.
- The interleaved review questions in Independent Practice are intentional and important. Do not skip them. They reinforce that RI.8.1 is not a standalone skill — it is embedded in all reading and writing work.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Question 1:** Strongest evidence for the claim that adolescent sleep patterns are biologically determined:

*Evidence 1:* "Studies measuring melatonin production in adolescents consistently show a delay: in teenagers, melatonin release... begins approximately two hours later than in adults or young children." Rating: Directness 5 — directly addresses biological determination; Specificity 5 — specific hormone, specific time difference; Sufficiency 5 — sufficient on its own to establish the biological basis. Total: 15/15.

*Evidence 2:* "During puberty, the human brain undergoes changes in its circadian rhythm, the internal clock that regulates the sleep-wake cycle." Rating: Directness 5 — directly names the biological mechanism; Specificity 4 — names the circadian rhythm but is slightly less specific than the melatonin data; Sufficiency 4 — establishes the biological change but does not quantify it. Total: 13/15.

**Question 2:** Strongest evidence for the inference that school start times are designed around adult schedules: *"most middle and high schools begin classes between 7:00 and 8:00 a.m. — during what researchers describe as the peak of the adolescent sleep phase."*

This is strongest because it explicitly juxtaposes what researchers know about adolescent biology with what schools actually do. It does not directly say "schools are designed for adults," but the juxtaposition implies it powerfully. Other evidence (like the note about "working parents who rely on early school schedules") also supports the inference but speaks to the perpetuation of the schedule rather than its original design.

**Question 3:** The critics' objection (transportation costs, after-school activities, working parents) is not strong evidence for the passage's central argument. On the Evidence Strength Meter, it rates low on directness (it does not speak to the biological claim) and low on sufficiency for the main argument. However, by including and then refuting it, the author uses it strategically — it functions as a concession that makes the overall argument appear more balanced and credible. Students who can articulate this strategic use are demonstrating stretch-level thinking.

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1:** Accept answers along these lines: "Adolescent sleep deprivation caused by early school start times is a biological problem with measurable academic and health consequences that schools should address." Credit answers that contain a subject (adolescent sleep patterns, school start times) and a claim (not just a topic like "sleep science").

**Review Question 2:** Sample answer: When the author writes, "asking a fourteen-year-old to fall asleep at ten o'clock at night is, from a biological standpoint, roughly equivalent to asking an adult to fall asleep at eight," the implied meaning is that current expectations placed on teenagers are as unreasonable as we would find similar expectations placed on adults. The author uses the analogy to shift the reader's sympathy toward teenagers without directly saying "schools are treating teenagers unfairly."

### Exit Ticket

**Full credit answer:** "Residents in these neighborhoods reported higher rates of heat-related illness during summer months" — this is strongest because it directly connects the phenomenon (urban heat islands) to a health outcome in real people, which is exactly what the claim requires. The 6.4°C temperature data is relevant but speaks to intensity of the phenomenon rather than to health risk.

**Partial credit:** Student selects the temperature data as strongest but explains it in terms of health impact (e.g., "a 6.4°C difference is enough to cause health problems") — award credit for reasoning even if the sentence selection is less precise.
