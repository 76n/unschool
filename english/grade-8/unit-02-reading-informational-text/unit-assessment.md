## Section 1: Citing the Strongest Evidence (RI.8.1)

**Passage 1: "The Coral Bleaching Crisis"**

In the past two decades, coral bleaching events have become more frequent, more widespread, and more severe. Bleaching occurs when ocean temperatures rise even slightly above the coral's tolerance threshold — as little as 1°C above the typical summer maximum for as few as four weeks is sufficient to trigger mass bleaching. When water is too warm, the symbiotic algae that live within coral tissue and provide most of the coral's nutrition are expelled. The coral turns white — bleached — and without its algae, it begins to starve. If temperatures return to normal quickly enough, the coral can recover. If not, it dies.

What makes current bleaching events unprecedented is their frequency. Historically, bleaching events severe enough to threaten reef systems occurred roughly once every twenty-five to thirty years — an interval long enough for reefs to partially recover between events. Since the late 1990s, the interval between major bleaching events has compressed to an average of less than six years. Some reef systems have experienced three or four major bleaching events in a single decade. Recovery, which requires at least a decade under favorable conditions, is now being interrupted before it can complete. In several regions, reefs that recovered from one bleaching event face another before their coral populations have had time to mature.

Researchers studying long-term reef trajectories have described the current situation using a concept they call "compound disturbance": the cumulative effect of repeated stresses is not additive but multiplicative — each subsequent bleaching event damages already-weakened systems far more severely than the same event would have affected a healthy reef. The trajectory, absent significant changes in ocean temperature, points toward permanent degradation of reef systems that have existed for millions of years.

## Section 2: Central Idea and Development (RI.8.2)

**Passage 2: "The Microbiome and the Mind"**

For most of medicine's history, the brain and the gut were understood as separate systems with separate functions. The brain controlled cognition, emotion, and behavior. The gut digested food. The connection between them, if acknowledged at all, was limited to the obvious: stress can cause stomach upset; hunger can cause irritability. The relationship was one-directional and physical.

What the past two decades of research have revealed is more complex and, to many researchers, surprising. The gut houses more than 100 trillion microorganisms — bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other organisms collectively known as the gut microbiome. These organisms are not passive passengers. They produce neurotransmitters — the chemical messengers that regulate mood, attention, and behavior — and communicate with the brain through a dedicated neural pathway called the vagus nerve. Alterations in the composition of the gut microbiome are associated with changes in mood and anxiety levels in animal studies; similar associations, though less established, are emerging in human research.

The implications of this research are still being worked out. Some researchers have proposed that microbiome interventions — dietary changes, probiotic supplements, or even fecal transplants from healthy donors — could eventually treat conditions like depression, anxiety, and attention disorders. This remains speculative. The research establishes correlation, not causation; the mechanisms are still being mapped; and clinical applications are years, if not decades, away. But the foundational claim — that the gut and the brain are in continuous, bidirectional communication through biological pathways — now has enough evidence behind it to be taken seriously.

## Section 3: Analyzing Connections Among Ideas (RI.8.3)

**Passage 3: "Rewilding and the Return of the Predator"**

For most of the twentieth century, wildlife management in national parks and protected areas followed a principle of passive protection: fence the area, remove the most obvious human threats, and let nature balance itself. The model was based on an assumption that ecosystems, left alone, would tend toward equilibrium — a stable state in which species populations regulated themselves.

This model looked convincing until ecologists began studying what happened in places where apex predators — the top-of-the-food-chain hunters — had been removed. The result was not equilibrium but cascade. Without predators to regulate their populations and behavior, prey animals like deer, elk, and rabbits overgrazed vegetation, causing erosion and displacing other species. In the absence of wolf populations across large parts of North America, deer populations expanded dramatically and grazed streambeds bare. The streams eroded. Fish populations collapsed. The removal of a single species — the wolf — had destabilized ecosystems that no one imagined wolves were supporting.

The concept that explains these results is called a trophic cascade: the effect of a top predator ripples through an entire ecosystem, shaping the behavior, distribution, and even physiology of species far below it in the food chain. Think of the food web as a building: removing a load-bearing wall does not just affect that wall. It destabilizes the entire structure. Rewilding — the deliberate reintroduction of apex predators and other keystone species — applies this understanding practically: if removing apex predators destabilizes ecosystems, restoring them should trigger positive cascades.

## Section 4: Evaluating Arguments and Evidence (RI.8.8)

**Passage 4: "The Case for Mandatory Financial Literacy Education"**

Financial literacy — the ability to understand and manage personal finances, including budgeting, saving, debt, and investment — has a measurable effect on long-term economic outcomes. Surveys of adults who received formal financial literacy instruction in school report higher rates of retirement savings, lower rates of high-interest consumer debt, and better decision-making during economic crises compared to adults who received no formal instruction. Young adults who understand compound interest are less likely to carry high-interest credit card debt; those who understand the basics of market investment are more likely to begin saving for retirement before age thirty. Given these documented benefits, requiring financial literacy as part of every student's core curriculum is not a luxury — it is a public health measure. The United States has among the highest rates of consumer debt and retirement savings shortfall in the developed world. Countries that mandate financial literacy education — a list that now includes twenty-three nations — show notably better population-level financial health metrics. The argument against mandating financial literacy is largely logistical: curriculum space is finite, and adding a new requirement means displacing something else. This is a real constraint. But displacing, say, one semester of an elective to make room for financial literacy is a trade-off that serves the long-term interests of students far better than the subject being displaced.

**Passage 5A: "Screen Time and Adolescent Mental Health: The Case for Limits"**
*(From a child psychology research consortium)*

A substantial and growing body of research links excessive screen time — particularly social media use — to increased rates of depression, anxiety, and loneliness in adolescents, especially girls. Studies tracking adolescents over multiple years find consistent patterns: those who spend more than three hours per day on social media report higher rates of depressive symptoms and lower life satisfaction than those who spend less time. The mechanism appears to involve both displacement (screen time replaces in-person social interaction and sleep) and direct psychological effects (social comparison, cyberbullying, and the dopamine-reward loop created by likes and notifications). Several large-scale surveys conducted across multiple countries find that the rise in adolescent mental health difficulties closely tracks the widespread adoption of smartphones and social media beginning around 2012. Pediatric health organizations in several countries have responded by issuing formal guidelines recommending limits on recreational screen time for children and teenagers.

---

**Passage 5B: "The Screen Time Debate: What the Evidence Actually Shows"**
*(From a science journalism publication)*

The relationship between social media use and adolescent mental health is more complicated than recent headlines suggest. Several large-scale reanalyses of the most frequently cited datasets find that the effect size — the magnitude of the statistical relationship between social media use and depression — is very small, comparable in scale to the effect of wearing glasses or eating potatoes. Critics of the strongest anti-screen-time claims note that the association studies often fail to distinguish between different types of screen use (passive scrolling vs. active communication vs. gaming vs. educational use), different platforms, and different user populations. A more nuanced picture emerges when studies disaggregate: girls appear more affected than boys; passive consumption appears more harmful than active communication; nighttime use appears more harmful than daytime use. The studies that generated the most alarming headlines often did not control adequately for pre-existing mental health conditions — students who are already depressed may use social media more, rather than social media causing depression. Researchers calling for better-designed prospective studies — which track populations before and after social media exposure — are skeptical that the current evidence base is strong enough to justify the strong policy recommendations being made.

**Question 16 (Short answer — 3 pts):**

Full credit answer:

Factual conflict: Passage 5A cites studies finding consistent patterns: "those who spend more than three hours per day on social media report higher rates of depressive symptoms."
