# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 2, Lesson 7

## Topic
Assessing Evidence: Relevance and Sufficiency

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Determine whether evidence in a text is relevant to the claim it is meant to support
- Assess whether the total evidence in an argument is sufficient to support its claim
- Recognize when irrelevant evidence has been introduced, and explain what effect it has on the argument

## Prerequisites
- Distinguishing claims, reasoning, and evidence using the Argument Anatomy chart (RI.8.8, Lesson 6)
- Evaluating the soundness of reasoning (Lesson 6)
- Citing the strongest textual evidence using the Evidence Strength Meter (RI.8.1, Lesson 1)
- Tracing and evaluating arguments in informational text (RI.7.8)

## Materials Needed
- Lesson 7 passage copies
- Argument Anatomy chart (same as Lesson 6; students will focus on the Evidence row)
- Evidence Relevance/Sufficiency checklist (new tool: four questions listed below)
- Highlighters (two colors: one for relevant evidence, one for irrelevant evidence)
- Student notebook

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Create an immediate, clear experience of irrelevant evidence so students understand the concept before applying it to complex text.

**Activity: Which Evidence Doesn't Belong?**

Say: *"I'm going to give you a claim and three pieces of evidence. Two pieces support the claim. One is a red herring — it sounds related, but it doesn't actually help the argument. Your job is to find the irrelevant piece."*

**Claim:** "The new recycling program has improved waste reduction in the city."

> **Evidence A:** "The city's recycling rate increased from 22% to 38% in the first year of the program."
> **Evidence B:** "The program's director won a regional environmental leadership award."
> **Evidence C:** "Landfill deposits from residential neighborhoods decreased by 14% during the same period."

Ask: *"Which is the irrelevant evidence? Why?"*

Students should identify Evidence B. Why? The director winning an award is interesting and may signal the program is regarded well — but it does not tell us anything about whether waste was actually reduced. It's about reputation, not outcomes.

Say: *"That's the key test for relevance: does this evidence tell us something about WHETHER THE CLAIM IS TRUE? An award tells us other people think the program is good. It doesn't tell us waste was reduced. Watch for this pattern in real texts — it's very common."*

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define and distinguish relevance from sufficiency; introduce the Evidence Relevance/Sufficiency checklist.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Relevant evidence:** Evidence that directly addresses the claim — it speaks to whether the claim is true or false
- **Irrelevant evidence:** Evidence that may be true and may seem related to the subject, but does not actually address whether the specific claim is true
- **Sufficient evidence:** A quantity and quality of evidence that is strong enough to support the claim — neither too sparse nor only partially applicable
- **Insufficient evidence:** Evidence that supports the claim in some respects but does not fully establish it — often because only limited examples are used or the evidence addresses only part of the claim

### Instruction Notes

Say: *"In Lesson 6, we focused on whether the reasoning connecting evidence to claim is logical. Today we focus on the evidence itself. There are three ways evidence can fail an argument:"*

Write on the board:
1. **Irrelevant:** The evidence doesn't speak to the claim at all — it's about something adjacent, not the claim itself.
2. **Insufficient:** The evidence is relevant but not enough — too few examples, too limited in scope, or it only covers part of the claim.
3. **Both:** Some of the evidence is irrelevant AND some of what remains is insufficient.

Introduce the Evidence Relevance/Sufficiency Checklist (add to the Argument Anatomy framework):

> **For each piece of evidence, ask:**
> 1. Does this evidence speak directly to whether the claim is true? (Relevance test)
> 2. Is this evidence specific enough to be meaningful? (Specificity)
> 3. After removing irrelevant evidence, is what remains sufficient to prove the claim? (Sufficiency test)
> 4. Does including this evidence strengthen, weaken, or have no effect on the overall argument? (Effect test)

Say: *"Notice that irrelevant evidence doesn't just fail to help — it can actively hurt an argument. Here's why: if a reader or evaluator sees that you've included irrelevant evidence, they start to wonder why. Did you not have better evidence? Were you trying to distract? Irrelevant evidence signals a weak argument."*

Introduce the key RI.8.8 skill: **recognizing when irrelevant evidence is introduced.** This means:
- Identifying the specific evidence that doesn't connect to the claim
- Explaining what the evidence is about instead
- Explaining what the argument loses or what impression it creates by including it

### Worked Examples

Read the following passage aloud, then model the Evidence Relevance/Sufficiency analysis.

---

**Passage A: "The Benefits of Urban Green Space"**

City parks and green spaces provide measurable benefits to the health and wellbeing of urban residents. Studies examining neighborhoods with and without substantial green space find consistent patterns: residents with access to parks within walking distance report lower levels of stress and higher levels of overall life satisfaction. One widely cited study tracked residents before and after a new park was built in a previously park-sparse neighborhood and found significant drops in reported anxiety over the following two years. Physical activity levels also increase when green space is accessible — residents walk more and engage in outdoor recreation at higher rates. Interestingly, the neighborhoods with the best park systems also tend to have higher property values, suggesting that parks are economically beneficial to municipalities as well. Air quality in neighborhoods with tree-lined streets and green corridors is measurably better than in comparable neighborhoods without them, and better air quality is associated with lower rates of respiratory illness. The architect who designed Central Park in New York City, Frederick Law Olmsted, wrote extensively about his belief that exposure to nature was essential for human mental health. The design principles he articulated in the nineteenth century have found support in modern psychological research.

---

Model the evidence analysis:

**Claim:** City parks and green spaces provide measurable benefits to the health and wellbeing of urban residents.

| Evidence | Relevant? | Strong? | Assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residents with park access report lower stress, higher life satisfaction | Yes | Yes — specific pattern across neighborhoods | Supports claim directly |
| Before/after study: significant drops in reported anxiety | Yes | Strong — controlled comparison | Supports claim strongly |
| Physical activity increases with accessible green space | Yes | Relevant to health | Supports claim |
| Neighborhoods with best park systems have higher property values | **Partially irrelevant** | The claim is about health and wellbeing, not property values | Interesting but off-topic for this specific claim |
| Better air quality in green neighborhoods; lower respiratory illness | Yes | Relevant to health | Supports claim |
| Frederick Law Olmsted wrote about nature and mental health | **Irrelevant** | Olmsted's historical beliefs are interesting but not evidence that parks provide health benefits | Should be removed or repositioned |

**Sufficiency assessment:** Removing the Olmsted sentence and flagging the property value evidence as partially off-topic, the remaining evidence is fairly strong. But note: all the evidence is correlational (residents with parks report better health; property values are higher). The before/after study is the only quasi-experimental evidence. A strict evaluator might say the argument is under-evidenced for a causal claim.

**Effect of irrelevant evidence:** The Olmsted reference actually weakens the passage slightly by introducing a historical appeal to authority (Olmsted's beliefs) where quantitative data would be more persuasive. It signals that the author may be reaching to add length or credibility.

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students identify and evaluate evidence relevance and sufficiency in a new passage with facilitator support.

Read the following passage aloud together.

---

**Passage B: "Why We Should Require Computer Science in Schools"**

Computer science education should be a required course in every middle and high school in the country. The most frequently cited reason is economic: jobs in computing and technology are among the fastest-growing sectors of the economy, and students who graduate without computational thinking skills will be at a disadvantage in an increasingly automated labor market. But the case for computer science education extends beyond career preparation. Learning to code teaches logical problem-solving skills that transfer to other domains — mathematics, scientific reasoning, and structured argumentation all benefit from exposure to computational thinking. Computational thinking is also increasingly necessary for informed citizenship. Understanding how algorithms work — how search results are ranked, how social media content is selected, how automated systems make decisions about loans, parole, or medical diagnoses — is not optional knowledge for a person living in a technological society. Several countries whose students consistently rank among the top performers on international academic assessments have already implemented mandatory computer science curricula. The United States currently ranks seventeenth in the world for internet speed. A recent survey found that 91% of parents support computer science education in schools. The argument for requiring computer science is both practical and civic — it prepares students for the economy and for democracy.

---

Work through the evidence evaluation together:

Ask students to read through the evidence and flag any they suspect is irrelevant.

Guide discussion:
- *"What is the claim? (Computer science should be required.) What does evidence need to show to be relevant to that claim? (That CS education provides benefits that justify making it required.)"*
- *"Is the U.S. ranking seventeenth for internet speed relevant? What does internet speed have to do with whether CS should be required in schools?"* (Irrelevant — internet infrastructure speed and school curriculum are different things.)
- *"Is the 91% parent support statistic relevant?"* (Partially — it shows public support, but popular opinion is not the same as evidence that CS education benefits students. It is relevant to policy arguments about mandate feasibility but not to the core claim about educational benefits.)
- *"Is the international rankings comparison relevant?"* (Yes — it provides evidence that countries that have implemented CS requirements tend to perform better academically, though the connection is correlational.)

Discuss sufficiency: Is the evidence that remains (economic benefit, logical problem-solving transfer, civic necessity, international comparisons) sufficient? Guide students to see: the claim is broad ("we should require it nationally") and the evidence is mostly assertional rather than experimental — a critic could reasonably say the evidence is insufficient for such a sweeping mandate.

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify irrelevant evidence and assess sufficiency in a new passage.

Read the following passage, then complete the questions.

---

**Passage C: "The Problem with Standardized Testing"**

Standardized testing, as currently practiced in most school systems, fails to measure the most important outcomes of education and should be substantially reduced or redesigned. The primary concern is what standardized tests measure — and what they do not. Tests designed to measure reading comprehension and mathematical calculation capture a narrow slice of cognitive ability. Critical thinking, creativity, collaborative problem-solving, and the capacity to learn independently — skills that employers and universities consistently identify as the most valuable qualities in applicants — are largely absent from standardized test formats. Countries that have moved away from high-stakes standardized testing toward portfolio-based and project-based assessment systems report higher rates of student engagement and, in some cases, better long-term academic outcomes. Standardized tests were first developed in the early twentieth century, partly drawing on research that has since been widely discredited for its racial biases. The test-prep industry — tutoring companies, prep courses, and practice materials — generates billions of dollars in annual revenue, with families in higher-income brackets spending significantly more than families with fewer resources. This economic disparity means that standardized test scores partly reflect household income rather than inherent ability. Students from higher-income families consistently score higher on standardized tests than students from lower-income families with comparable academic records.

---

**Questions:**

1. Identify the main claim of this passage. Then go through the evidence and, using the Evidence Relevance/Sufficiency Checklist, classify each piece of evidence as relevant, partially relevant, or irrelevant to the claim.

2. The passage mentions that standardized tests were "first developed in the early twentieth century, partly drawing on research that has since been widely discredited for its racial biases." Is this relevant evidence for the claim that standardized testing should be reduced or redesigned today? Explain your reasoning carefully.

3. After removing or flagging irrelevant or weakly relevant evidence, assess whether the remaining evidence is sufficient to support the claim that standardized testing "fails to measure the most important outcomes of education and should be substantially reduced or redesigned."

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete questions 1 and 2. For question 1, use the Evidence Relevance/Sufficiency Checklist graphic organizer.

**Stretch:** Complete all three questions. For question 3, write a paragraph explaining what additional evidence would be needed to make the argument fully sufficient.

**Challenge:** Complete all three questions. Then write a paragraph responding to this prompt: "A defender of standardized testing might argue that the passage's strongest evidence (the income disparity in scores) actually argues for making tests fairer, not eliminating them. How should the author respond to this challenge? Does the passage already address it?"

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1 (RI.8.8, Lesson 6 — Reasoning Flaws):** The passage argues that because test scores reflect household income, the tests are flawed. Is this sound reasoning? Identify any potential reasoning flaw in this specific logical step.

**Review Question 2 (RI.8.2 — Central Idea Development):** The passage's central idea is stated at the beginning. Does the development of the passage (the sequence of evidence presented) effectively build toward and support that central idea? Identify the moment in the passage where the development is strongest and explain why.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

A student is writing an argument that "schools should extend the lunch period to 45 minutes." They include this evidence:

*"Research shows that students who eat a nutritious lunch perform better academically in afternoon classes."*
*"Students at our school report being hungry by the end of a 25-minute lunch period."*
*"The cafeteria was renovated last year at a cost of $200,000."*

**Question:** Which piece of evidence is irrelevant to the claim? Explain in 1–2 sentences why it doesn't support the claim, even though it mentions the cafeteria (the same physical space as lunch).

**Expected Answers:**

The irrelevant evidence is the cafeteria renovation cost ($200,000). This mentions the cafeteria but does not address whether the lunch period should be extended. The cost of renovating the space has no bearing on whether a 45-minute period is better than a 25-minute period for student hunger, health, or academic performance. It is about a different attribute of the lunch environment (physical space) rather than the time allowed for eating.

**Mastery = Student identifies the cafeteria renovation evidence as irrelevant AND explains that the cost of renovation does not speak to the length-of-period question, even though it mentions the same space.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Ask: "What question does the evidence need to answer to be relevant?" (Does this tell us whether 45 minutes is better than 25?) Walk through each piece: the nutrition research answers the question; the hunger reports answer the question; the renovation cost answers a question about budget, not about time.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "Can you think of a context where the renovation cost WOULD be relevant evidence?" (E.g., if the claim were "the school has invested in improving the lunch experience" — then cost would be relevant.) Reinforce that relevance is always relative to the specific claim.

## Extension (Optional)

1. Choose any persuasive article on a topic you care about. Use the Evidence Relevance/Sufficiency Checklist to evaluate every piece of evidence the author cites. Write a one-paragraph assessment of whether the evidence is collectively sufficient to support the main claim.

2. The passage about standardized testing includes historical information about the origins of the tests. Write a paragraph arguing that this historical information IS relevant to the claim — that knowing the origins of a practice matters for evaluating whether it should continue. Then write a counter-paragraph arguing the opposite.

3. Rewrite Passage C ("The Problem with Standardized Testing") to be stronger, removing irrelevant evidence and adding one piece of evidence that would significantly strengthen the argument. Explain your changes.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- Students often mistake "interesting" for "relevant." Reinforce consistently: relevance is always relative to the specific claim. An interesting fact about Olmsted, or about the origins of standardized testing, or about renovation costs may be true and engaging — but if it does not speak to the claim, it is irrelevant to the argument.
- The historical-origins evidence in Passage C is deliberately ambiguous. Accept student arguments that it is relevant (historical context shapes interpretation of a practice) as well as arguments that it is irrelevant (past origins don't determine present validity). What matters is that students can articulate their reasoning clearly.
- Question 2 in Independent Practice is the highest-order question in this lesson. Many students will reflexively say it is irrelevant ("that was a long time ago"). Push them to consider whether historical context is ever relevant to evaluating current practices.
- Lessons 6 and 7 together complete RI.8.8. By the end of Lesson 7, students should be able to: (1) identify the argument structure (claim, reasoning, evidence); (2) evaluate whether reasoning is sound; (3) identify irrelevant evidence; and (4) assess whether evidence is sufficient. The unit assessment will test all four skills.
- The interleaved review question in this lesson introduces a subtle reasoning flaw: the "income disparity" evidence could support reducing tests OR making them fairer. Students who notice this ambiguity are engaging in exactly the kind of sophisticated argumentative reasoning RI.8.8 demands.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Question 1:** Main claim: "Standardized testing, as currently practiced, fails to measure the most important outcomes of education and should be substantially reduced or redesigned."

| Evidence | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Tests measure narrow cognitive ability; miss critical thinking, creativity, etc. | Relevant | Directly supports "fails to measure most important outcomes" |
| Countries moving to portfolio-based assessment report better engagement and outcomes | Relevant | Supports "should be redesigned" |
| Tests first developed in early 20th century, partly drawing on since-discredited racial research | Partially relevant / Debatable | Historical origin argues against current legitimacy; but see Question 2 for full analysis |
| Test-prep industry generates billions; income families spend more | Relevant | Supports the economic inequity argument |
| Higher-income students consistently score higher than lower-income students with comparable records | Relevant — strongest | Directly supports the claim that scores reflect income, not ability |

**Question 2:** This is the most debated piece of evidence. Arguments for relevance: The original research informing the tests was racially biased, which means the test design may have embedded assumptions that disadvantage certain groups — so the historical origins are directly relevant to evaluating current validity and equity. Arguments for irrelevance: Tests have been revised many times since the early twentieth century; the origins don't necessarily determine the current instrument's validity. Credit both positions if students argue clearly. The stronger argument for relevance: if the original design reflected bias, the burden is on defenders to show the bias has been fully eliminated — making the historical origin a relevant starting point for evaluation.

**Question 3:** Remaining relevant evidence includes: (1) tests miss critical skills, (2) portfolio-based alternatives show better outcomes, (3) income disparity in scores. This is moderately sufficient for the claim that tests fail to measure important outcomes — but weaker for the claim that tests should be "substantially reduced or redesigned." The alternatives evidence (portfolio-based) suggests what the redesign should look like, but "substantially reduced" is a strong policy claim that would benefit from more direct evidence about harm caused by high-stakes testing specifically.

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1:** The reasoning has a potential flaw related to hasty generalization or insufficient distinction: "scores reflect income" shows a correlation, but the conclusion drawn (tests are flawed measures of ability) requires the additional logical step that income disparities in scores are caused by the tests rather than by actual preparation-related differences that are themselves caused by income. A precise critic would say: the disparity could mean (a) the tests are biased; (b) higher-income students are better prepared; or (c) both. The passage assumes (a) but the evidence could support (b). The strongest version of the argument would need to show that students with comparable preparation but different incomes score differently — which the phrase "comparable academic records" in the last sentence is attempting to do.

**Review Question 2:** The development is strongest in the second-to-last sentence: "This economic disparity means that standardized test scores partly reflect household income rather than inherent ability." This is where the evidence (income disparity, test-prep spending differences) converges into a clear claim about what the tests actually measure — directly supporting the central idea that tests fail to measure the right outcomes. It is the most developed moment because it connects specific evidence to the central claim with an explicit logical step.

### Exit Ticket

**Full credit:** Identifies cafeteria renovation cost as irrelevant AND explains that renovation cost addresses the physical space, not the duration of the lunch period, and therefore does not speak to whether 45 minutes is better than 25 minutes for students.

**Partial credit:** Correctly identifies it as irrelevant but explains only that "it's not about lunch time" without connecting it to the specific claim.
