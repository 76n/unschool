# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 2, Lesson 10

## Topic
Integrated Informational Text Analysis

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply all five Unit 2 standards (RI.8.1, RI.8.2, RI.8.3, RI.8.8, RI.8.9) to a complex informational text set
- Select and deploy the appropriate analytical tool (Evidence Strength Meter, Central Idea Tracker, Argument Anatomy, Source Reliability Checklist) for each analytical task
- Synthesize findings across multiple standards into a coherent written analysis

## Prerequisites
- Citing the strongest evidence (RI.8.1, Lesson 1)
- Determining and tracing central idea development (RI.8.2, Lessons 2–3)
- Analyzing connection techniques (RI.8.3, Lessons 4–5)
- Evaluating argument claims, reasoning, and evidence (RI.8.8, Lessons 6–7)
- Analyzing conflicting information and distinguishing fact from interpretation (RI.8.9, Lessons 8–9)

## Materials Needed
- Lesson 10 text set (two passages: one main text, one opposing/complementary text)
- All four graphic organizers: Evidence Strength Meter, Central Idea Tracker, Argument Anatomy, Source Reliability Checklist
- Highlighters (at least three colors)
- Student notebook
- Optional: Unit 2 strategy reference card (all four tool names and key questions)

## Warm-Up (8 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all five standards and four tools; set the task framing for the integrated lesson.

**Activity: The Analyst's Toolkit**

Say: *"This is our final lesson before the unit assessment. Today you're going to use everything we've learned together — not as separate skills but as one integrated analytical process. Before we start, let's do a quick review."*

Write the four tools on the board. For each, ask a student to supply the key question it answers:

| Tool | Key Question It Answers |
|------|------------------------|
| Evidence Strength Meter | Which evidence most strongly supports this claim — and why? |
| Central Idea Tracker | What is the text's central claim, and how does it develop? |
| Argument Anatomy | What is the claim, reasoning, and evidence — and is the reasoning sound? |
| Source Reliability Checklist | Where do these two texts conflict, and which is more credible? |

Then say: *"Real informational reading doesn't arrive labeled 'use RI.8.1 here.' You have to decide which analytical tool fits the task in front of you. Today's work will require you to make those decisions."*

Preview the texts: *"Today you'll read two texts about the same topic — one arguing for a position, one raising objections. You'll analyze each text individually, then compare them."*

## Direct Instruction (8–10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model the integration process — moving through multiple analytical lenses on the same text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Synthesis:** Combining analysis from multiple angles into a coherent, unified interpretation — not just listing observations but connecting them into a larger understanding
- **Analytical lens:** A particular standard or tool used to examine a specific aspect of a text (evidence, central idea, argument structure, connections, source reliability)
- **Text set:** Two or more texts connected by shared topic, and analyzed together for comparison or contrast

### Instruction Notes

Say: *"When you read a complex informational text, you don't apply one lens and call it done. A sophisticated reader moves between multiple analytical questions: What is this text arguing? How is it built? How strong is its evidence? How does it compare to other texts on the same topic? Today we're going to do all of that."*

Model the integration process using a brief review of the Passage A from Lesson 6 ("The Case for Later School Start Times"):

1. **RI.8.2 first:** "What is the central idea? How does it develop? Let me sketch the Central Idea Tracker." — (Quick model: Central idea is that later start times are warranted by biological evidence; development moves from biological mechanism to documented harms to policy impact to dismissal of counterargument.)
2. **RI.8.1 second:** "What's the strongest evidence? Let me run the Evidence Strength Meter on the top two candidates." — (Quick model: circadian rhythm data vs. district improvement reports; circadian data rates highest on directness and specificity.)
3. **RI.8.8 third:** "Is the argument sound? Let me sketch the Argument Anatomy." — (Quick model: Claim = delay start times; reasoning = biological harms are real and documented; weakness = "associated with" language implies correlation where causation is claimed.)
4. **RI.8.3 fourth:** "Does the author use any connection techniques? What do they accomplish?" — (Quick model: The comparison between "an adult being asked to sleep at 8 p.m." and a teenager being asked to sleep at 10 p.m. is an analogy that makes the biological argument vivid and sympathetic.)

Say: *"Notice that each lens reveals something different. The central idea analysis tells you what the text is doing; the evidence analysis tells you how well it does it; the argument anatomy tells you whether the logic holds; and the connection technique analysis tells you how the author builds persuasion. Now we apply all of this to new texts — together first, then independently."*

### Worked Example: Reading the First Text

Read the following passage aloud, then model filling in the first three tools simultaneously.

---

**Text 1: "The Case for Public Investment in Vertical Farming"**

Growing food in vertically stacked, climate-controlled indoor facilities — a practice known as vertical farming — has moved from science fiction to commercial reality in the past decade. Several dozen large-scale vertical farms now operate in North America, Europe, and Asia, producing leafy greens, herbs, and some fruiting crops at commercially viable prices. Proponents argue that vertical farming addresses multiple converging crises in the global food system. First, conventional agriculture is a significant contributor to both land use change and greenhouse gas emissions — and vertical farming, by eliminating the need for large land areas and enabling year-round production close to urban centers, reduces both. Second, conventional agriculture is highly vulnerable to climate disruption: droughts, floods, and extreme heat events have produced significant crop failures in major growing regions over the past decade. Vertical farms, operating in controlled environments, are climate-immune. Third, the use of hydroponic and aeroponic systems in vertical farming can reduce water use by as much as 95% compared to conventional field irrigation — a significant advantage as freshwater availability declines in agricultural regions worldwide. Critics note that vertical farms currently consume large amounts of electricity — a real concern in regions where the electricity grid is fossil-fuel dependent. But proponents respond that as renewable energy expands, the energy concern diminishes; the other advantages are structural.

---

Model integrated analysis (brief):

**Central Idea Tracker:** Central idea emerging — "Vertical farming addresses multiple food system crises (land use, climate vulnerability, water use) and will become more competitive as renewable energy expands." Development: establishes commercial reality (beginning), builds through three advantages (middle), dismisses energy objection (end). The central idea is fully stated only in the final sentence.

**Evidence Strength Meter on top two claims:**
- "reduce water use by as much as 95%" — Directness: 5, Specificity: 5, Sufficiency: 4. Total: 14. Strong.
- "Several dozen large-scale vertical farms now operate" — Directness: 3 (establishes viability but not advantages directly), Specificity: 2 (vague count), Sufficiency: 2. Total: 7. Weak.

**Argument Anatomy:** Claim = public investment in vertical farming is warranted. Evidence = three advantages (land, climate resilience, water). Reasoning = convergence of crises makes vertical farming's specific solutions urgent. Potential weakness = the energy objection is dismissed with "as renewable energy expands" — which is a conditional claim that assumes something not yet guaranteed.

## Guided Practice (12–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students read the second text and complete the Source Reliability Checklist with facilitator support, then begin synthesizing across both texts.

Read the following passage aloud together.

---

**Text 2: "Vertical Farming: Green Promise or Green Hype?"**
*(From an agricultural economics journal)*

The enthusiasm surrounding vertical farming in policy and popular media substantially outpaces what the current evidence supports. Vertical farms remain profitable only for high-value specialty crops — primarily leafy greens and herbs — that constitute a negligible fraction of the calories the global population needs. The crops that actually feed the world — grains, legumes, root vegetables, and oil crops — cannot be economically produced in vertical farming systems at any scale foreseeable within this decade. The water efficiency claims made by vertical farming proponents are real for the crops they grow, but those crops also constitute a tiny portion of total agricultural water use. The energy requirements of large-scale vertical farming are not a future problem contingent on grid decarbonization — they are a current problem. Life-cycle analyses comparing the full environmental cost of vertical farming against conventional or greenhouse agriculture find that, for most crops and in most locations, the energy cost of vertical farming creates a larger carbon footprint per calorie than field agriculture. This does not mean vertical farming has no role — it may be appropriate for urban environments where land is scarce and logistics costs are high. But treating it as a solution to the global food security crisis overstates what the evidence currently supports.

---

Have students complete the Source Reliability Checklist in pairs, then discuss:

- *"What factual claim in Text 2 directly contradicts or complicates a claim in Text 1?"*
  - Text 1 says energy concern "diminishes as renewable energy expands" (future conditional). Text 2 says the energy problem is current and creates a larger carbon footprint per calorie now.
  - Text 1 says vertical farms produce crops "at commercially viable prices." Text 2 says vertical farms are only profitable for high-value specialty crops — implying "commercially viable" has a very narrow meaning.

- *"Is the conflict factual, interpretive, or both?"*
  - Factual dimension: Life-cycle carbon analysis (Text 2) vs. the implied claim of environmental advantage (Text 1). Text 2's life-cycle analysis evidence is more specific.
  - Interpretive dimension: Text 1 treats the current situation as a stepping stone to broader viability; Text 2 argues the current situation does not support the claims being made.

- *"Using credibility criteria, which text do you find more credible on the specific factual question of vertical farming's current carbon footprint?"*
  - Text 2 cites "life-cycle analyses" — a specific methodology — and comes from an agricultural economics journal (domain expertise). Text 1 does not cite methodology and comes from an unspecified proponent perspective.

## Independent Practice (15–18 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently apply all five standards to the text set and write a short integrated analysis.

**Instructions:**

Using the text set (Texts 1 and 2) and all four graphic organizers, complete the following four analytical tasks:

**Task 1 (RI.8.1 + RI.8.2):** Identify the central idea of Text 1 in one sentence. Then cite the single strongest piece of evidence that supports the central idea. Apply the Evidence Strength Meter to justify your choice.

**Task 2 (RI.8.3):** Identify one connection technique used in Text 1 (comparison, contrast, analogy, or categorization/distinction). Quote the relevant passage. Explain what the technique accomplishes in the context of the argument.

**Task 3 (RI.8.8):** Using the Argument Anatomy chart, evaluate the soundness of Text 2's argument. Identify the claim, the strongest evidence, and one potential weakness in the reasoning.

**Task 4 (RI.8.9):** Using the Source Reliability Checklist, identify one factual conflict and one interpretive conflict between Texts 1 and 2. For each, specify which text you find more credible and why.

**Written Synthesis (RI.8.1–8.9):** Write a 4–6 sentence paragraph synthesizing your analysis. Your paragraph should:
- State which text makes the stronger overall argument
- Cite at least two pieces of evidence (one from each text) using the Evidence Strength Meter to support your evaluation
- Identify the key conflict (factual or interpretive) between the texts
- Conclude with a statement of what additional evidence would be needed to fully resolve the central conflict

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete Tasks 1, 3, and 4 only. Write a 3-sentence synthesis that states which text is stronger and why.

**Stretch:** Complete all four tasks and the full synthesis paragraph. In your synthesis, address this question: "Could both texts be right — for different populations or contexts?"

**Challenge:** Complete all four tasks and the synthesis paragraph. Additionally, write a second paragraph that identifies the most important question the two texts together leave unanswered — and explain what kind of study or evidence would answer it.

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1 (RI.8.2 — Central Idea Development):** In Text 2, the author writes "This does not mean vertical farming has no role — it may be appropriate for urban environments where land is scarce and logistics costs are high." How does this sentence function in the development of Text 2's central idea? Does it strengthen or weaken the argument?

**Review Question 2 (RI.8.1 — Evidence Strength across texts):** Compare the strongest evidence in Text 1 with the strongest evidence in Text 2. Which text provides stronger textual evidence overall? Apply the Evidence Strength Meter to both choices and compare the scores.

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Brief synthesis reflection — no new content, but a consolidation of the integrated analysis skills.

**Exit Ticket:**

In 3–4 sentences, answer this question:

**"A friend who hasn't read this unit's texts tells you: 'I just read an article saying vertical farming is going to replace conventional agriculture.' What would you say to them — using at least two analytical skills from this unit?"**

**Expected Answers:**

Strong responses will apply two or more of the following:
- RI.8.1: Ask what evidence the article cites for that claim — is it the strongest available evidence, or is it selected to make the most optimistic case?
- RI.8.2: Identify the central idea the article is actually making — is it "vertical farming will replace conventional agriculture" or something more limited, like "vertical farming is commercially viable for specialty crops"?
- RI.8.8: Evaluate whether the reasoning is sound — does the evidence actually prove a replacement claim, or does it show more limited viability?
- RI.8.9: Recognize that other sources (like agricultural economics researchers) present conflicting information — particularly about energy costs and crop range — that complicate an optimistic picture.

**Mastery = Student applies at least two analytical skills by name or by clear description, and demonstrates that their response is grounded in the texts rather than general opinion.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Ask: "What would the Evidence Strength Meter tell you about the article your friend read? What would the Argument Anatomy chart show you?" Use specific tool names to prompt recall. If the student is unable to apply two tools, review the tools chart from the Warm-Up before proceeding to the unit assessment.

### If Mastery Is Met
Affirm: *"You've just done what sophisticated readers do — you looked at a claim with multiple analytical lenses and evaluated it more carefully than someone who just reads for information. That's what the unit assessment will ask you to do tomorrow. You're ready."*

## Extension (Optional)

1. Find a third source on vertical farming (news article, research summary, or industry report). Add it to the Source Reliability Checklist as "Text 3." Where does it fall in the spectrum between Texts 1 and 2? Does it introduce any new factual information that changes the conflict?

2. The synthesis paragraph asks you to identify what additional evidence would resolve the central conflict. Write a 150-word description of the ideal study that would resolve the disagreement about vertical farming's environmental impact — specifying what it would measure, how it would control for variables, and who should conduct it.

3. Choose a different technology debate that has two sides (self-driving cars, AI in education, gene editing, nuclear power). Write two short paragraphs — one advocating strongly for the technology, one raising serious concerns — using the same argumentative structures you analyzed in Texts 1 and 2. Then label your own connection techniques, evidence choices, and claims.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- Allow the full 55–60 minutes for this lesson. The independent practice synthesis is substantive and should not be rushed.
- Students who struggle with the synthesis paragraph often do so because they are trying to list observations rather than integrate them. Prompt: "You don't need to report everything you found. Pick the most important insight from each lens and connect them."
- The "could both be right for different contexts?" question in the Stretch tier is genuinely important for this topic and for sophisticated RI.8.9 thinking. Texts don't always disagree because one is wrong — sometimes they address different scales, populations, or conditions. Both vertical farming texts may be right: Text 1 for urban specialty crop production; Text 2 for the claim that vertical farming cannot replace conventional agriculture at scale.
- The Written Synthesis is a preview of Unit 3 informational/explanatory writing. Students who complete the synthesis well have essentially drafted a short analytical essay. Point this out: "What you just wrote is the kind of analysis that becomes a full essay in Unit 3."
- The unit assessment follows this lesson. Ensure students know which graphic organizers they may use during the assessment (recommend: all four, as tools) and what materials they should bring.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Task 1:**
Central idea of Text 1: "Vertical farming addresses multiple converging crises in the global food system — land use, climate vulnerability, and water scarcity — and its remaining energy concern will diminish as renewable energy expands."

Strongest evidence: "The use of hydroponic and aeroponic systems in vertical farming can reduce water use by as much as 95% compared to conventional field irrigation — a significant advantage as freshwater availability declines in agricultural regions worldwide." Directness: 5 (directly speaks to a crisis the claim addresses); Specificity: 5 (95%, compared to field irrigation); Sufficiency: 4 (one of three advantages; sufficient for this specific advantage). Total: 14.

**Task 2:** Connection technique in Text 1 — Categorization: The author divides the agricultural system's problems into three categories (land use/emissions, climate vulnerability, water use) and shows vertical farming addresses each. This categorization technique serves the argument by making the text's structure match its claim: three crises = three solutions, creating an impression of systematic comprehensiveness. Without the three-part categorization, the argument would be harder to assess — the structure makes the coverage seem exhaustive.

**Task 3 (Argument Anatomy for Text 2):**
Claim: Vertical farming does not currently support the claims made by its proponents and should not be treated as a solution to global food security.
Strongest evidence: "Life-cycle analyses comparing the full environmental cost of vertical farming against conventional or greenhouse agriculture find that, for most crops and in most locations, the energy cost of vertical farming creates a larger carbon footprint per calorie than field agriculture."
Reasoning: If the environmental justification (reducing emissions) is undermined by the energy carbon footprint, and if vertical farms can only produce a "negligible fraction" of global caloric need, then they do not constitute a solution to the food security crisis as claimed.
Weakness: Text 2 acknowledges a role for vertical farming ("it may be appropriate for urban environments where land is scarce") — this concession weakens the absoluteness of the critique and suggests the conflict with Text 1 is more about scale and scope than about whether vertical farming has value.

**Task 4 (Source Reliability Checklist):**
Factual conflict: Text 1 implies vertical farming's energy concern is a future problem solvable by renewable energy. Text 2 states that life-cycle analyses currently show a larger carbon footprint per calorie for vertical farming than conventional agriculture. Text 2 is more credible on this specific factual question because it cites a specific methodology (life-cycle analysis) from a domain-expert source (agricultural economics journal), while Text 1's response is a future conditional.

Interpretive conflict: Text 1 treats vertical farming as addressing "multiple converging crises" in the global food system. Text 2 argues that vertical farming "overstates what the evidence currently supports" as a global solution. Both texts are interpreting the same landscape of facts — commercially viable specialty crop production, energy challenges, water efficiency — differently. Text 2 is more credible here because it explicitly addresses the global scale of the claim and provides crop-specific evidence for why the global claim is overstated, while Text 1's "converging crises" framing does not distinguish between the crops it can and cannot produce.

**Written Synthesis (sample):** Text 2 makes the stronger overall argument because its evidence is more methodologically specific. Text 1's strongest evidence — a 95% reduction in water use — rates highly on the Evidence Strength Meter (14/15), but it applies only to the crops vertical farming actually produces. Text 2 directly addresses this: "those crops also constitute a tiny portion of total agricultural water use." The key conflict is interpretive: Text 1 frames vertical farming as addressing global food system crises; Text 2 argues this overstates its current scope and environmental profile. Both texts could be right for different populations — vertical farming may be genuinely viable and beneficial in dense urban environments for specialty crops, while falling short of a global food solution. To resolve the conflict fully, an independent life-cycle analysis comparing the environmental costs of urban specialty crop production through vertical farming versus conventional supply chains would be needed.

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1:** "This does not mean vertical farming has no role" functions as a concession that strengthens Text 2's credibility and argument. By acknowledging a legitimate application, the author demonstrates that their critique is precise (targeted at the global-solution claim) rather than categorical. This makes the central argument — that vertical farming overstates its credentials for global food security — more defensible, because it cannot be dismissed as anti-innovation bias. The concession strengthens the argument by narrowing it to the claim the evidence actually supports.

**Review Question 2:** Text 2's strongest evidence — "Life-cycle analyses comparing the full environmental cost of vertical farming against conventional or greenhouse agriculture find that, for most crops and in most locations, the energy cost of vertical farming creates a larger carbon footprint per calorie" — scores: Directness 5 (directly addresses the environmental claim at the center of the debate), Specificity 5 (names methodology, scope, comparison), Sufficiency 5. Total: 15/15.

Text 1's strongest evidence — "reduce water use by as much as 95%" — Total: 14/15.

Text 2 provides stronger overall evidence because its life-cycle analysis evidence addresses the central point of dispute (environmental benefit claims) more directly and with higher methodological specificity than Text 1's water-use statistic.

### Exit Ticket

**Full credit:** Student applies at least two analytical skills with enough specificity to show they understand what the skill requires. Example: "I'd ask them to apply the Evidence Strength Meter — what evidence does the article cite, and is it strong on directness and specificity? I'd also check whether the article's reasoning is sound using Argument Anatomy — does the evidence actually prove a 'replacement' claim, or just that vertical farming works for some crops? A second text I read argues that vertical farming only produces specialty crops and has a high energy carbon footprint — that's a conflict the article may not acknowledge."

**Partial credit:** Student mentions using "evidence" or "comparing sources" without specifically naming the tools or applying them to the specific claim.
