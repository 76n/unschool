# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 2: Reading Informational Text

## Unit Overview

Unit 2 builds on students' foundational informational reading skills to develop sophisticated analytical competencies required for college and career readiness. Over approximately 11 sessions, students move from identifying what a text says to evaluating how and why authors construct arguments, organize ideas, and present evidence. The unit culminates in comparing conflicting sources — a critical literacy skill essential for navigating today's information environment.

**Grade Level:** 8
**Subject:** English Language Arts
**Unit Duration:** Approximately 11 sessions (2.5–3 weeks)
**Time per Session:** 50–55 minutes (Lesson 10: 55–60 minutes)
**Total Lessons:** 10
**Unit Assessment:** Administered after Lesson 10

The unit uses four named analytical strategies that students apply repeatedly across lessons:
- **Evidence Strength Meter** — a framework for ranking evidence by relevance, specificity, and directness (introduced Lesson 1)
- **Central Idea Tracker** — a graphic organizer for mapping central ideas and their supporting development (introduced Lesson 2)
- **Argument Anatomy** — a structure for dissecting claim, reasoning, and evidence in argumentative texts (introduced Lesson 6)
- **Source Reliability Checklist** — a tool for evaluating credibility and detecting conflict between sources (introduced Lesson 8)

## Prerequisite Skills

Students entering Unit 2 should have consolidated the following skills from prior instruction:

**From Grade 7 Informational Reading:**
- Citing multiple pieces of textual evidence to support analysis (RI.7.1)
- Determining two or more central ideas and analyzing their development (RI.7.2)
- Analyzing the interactions among individuals, events, and ideas in a text (RI.7.3)
- Tracing and evaluating arguments and specific claims (RI.7.8)
- Comparing and contrasting presentations of events across texts (RI.7.9)

**From Grade 8, Unit 1 (Reading Literary Text):**
- Close reading strategies for complex text
- Identifying theme and analyzing its development across a text
- Recognizing how authors use literary techniques to shape meaning
- Writing objective summaries
- Practice with the Evidence Strength Meter framework (introduced in Unit 1 as "Textual Support Ladder")

If students have not fully developed these prerequisites, prioritize review during the Warm-Up activities in Lessons 1 and 2.

## Facilitation Tips

**General Approach:**
- This unit requires students to move beyond comprehension into evaluation. Expect productive struggle. Resist giving answers; instead, prompt students to return to the text.
- Consistently use the named strategies (Evidence Strength Meter, etc.) by name so students internalize the vocabulary and transfer the tools independently.
- Every lesson contains original nonfiction passages. Read passages aloud together before analysis — this models fluency and ensures students encounter the text before working independently.

**For RI.8.1 (Lessons 1, 10):**
- The key shift from Grade 7 is the word "strongest." Students must now not just find evidence but rank it. Use the Evidence Strength Meter every time students cite evidence in this unit — not just in Lesson 1.
- A common error is selecting long quotations. Teach students that shorter, more specific quotations often rank higher on the meter.

**For RI.8.2 (Lessons 2–3):**
- Students often confuse the topic with the central idea. Reinforce: the topic is a noun phrase ("ocean plastic"); the central idea is a complete statement about the topic ("Ocean plastic poses a threat to marine ecosystems that requires coordinated international action").
- Use the Central Idea Tracker as a running document — students should add to it as they read, not fill it in after.

**For RI.8.3 (Lessons 4–5):**
- Emphasize that comparison, analogy, and categorization are author choices — deliberate rhetorical decisions. Ask: "Why did the author connect these ideas this way? What effect does it have?"
- Students often miss analogies because they don't signal them as clearly as comparisons. Point out analogy markers: "like," "just as," "similar to," "functions the way."

**For RI.8.8 (Lessons 6–7):**
- Use the Argument Anatomy chart consistently. Students who skip the chart tend to conflate claim and evidence.
- "Sound reasoning" is the hardest concept in this unit. Model with obvious examples first (circular reasoning, hasty generalization) before moving to subtler cases.
- Lesson 7's focus on irrelevant evidence is critical for standardized assessments. Give extra practice identifying "red herring" evidence that sounds related but does not actually support the claim.

**For RI.8.9 (Lessons 8–9):**
- Use authentic-sounding but fully original passages to model how real-world texts conflict. Emphasize that texts can conflict on facts (one says X, the other says Y) OR on interpretation (both cite the same data but draw different conclusions).
- Lesson 9's distinction between factual disagreement and interpretive disagreement is the most intellectually demanding content in the unit. Allow more discussion time here.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction Strategy |
|---------------|---------------------|
| "Any evidence from the text counts as strong evidence." | Use the Evidence Strength Meter. Walk through criteria: Is it directly about the claim? Is it specific? Does it come from a reliable source within the text? Rate several pieces of evidence together before students rate independently. |
| "The central idea is the same as the main topic." | Remind students: topic = a noun or phrase; central idea = a complete claim. Practice converting topics into central idea statements: "Ocean plastic" → "Ocean plastic threatens marine food chains." |
| "Supporting ideas are the same as details." | Supporting ideas are broader sub-claims that develop the central idea. Details are specific facts that support the supporting ideas. Draw the three-level hierarchy: Central Idea → Supporting Ideas → Details. |
| "An analogy is just a comparison." | An analogy maps a complex or unfamiliar concept onto a simpler or more familiar one to explain or argue. Ask: is the comparison explaining a difficult idea? If yes, it's likely an analogy. |
| "If there is evidence, the argument is good." | Distinguish three failure modes: (1) evidence is there but irrelevant; (2) evidence is relevant but insufficient; (3) reasoning connecting evidence to claim is flawed. Each is a different problem. |
| "Texts disagree because one is wrong." | Introduce the fact/interpretation distinction. Texts can present the same facts and still disagree — because interpretation involves judgment. Neither source is automatically wrong; both require evaluation. |

## Assessment Guidance

**Formative Assessment (During Lessons):**
- Exit tickets at the end of each lesson (3-minute, 1-question checks) — review before the next session to identify students who need re-teaching.
- Monitor independent practice for these specific error patterns: quoting too broadly (RI.8.1), restating topic instead of central idea (RI.8.2), describing connection without analyzing its purpose (RI.8.3), listing claims without evaluating reasoning (RI.8.8), treating all disagreements as factual (RI.8.9).

**Summative Assessment (Unit Assessment — Session 11):**
- 50–60 minutes; 60 points total; 70% mastery threshold (42/60 points).
- The assessment uses 5 original nonfiction passages covering all five standards.
- Passages are longer and more complex than lesson passages (up to 250 words).
- Students complete multiple choice, short answer, and an extended response.
- Score by standard using the scoring guide table in the unit assessment file.

**Grading Priority:**
Prioritize RI.8.1 and RI.8.8 — these are foundational to all future informational reading and writing instruction. Students who do not meet mastery on these two standards should receive targeted support before moving to Unit 3.

## Connection to Future Units

**Unit 3 (Writing Informational/Explanatory Texts):**
Unit 2's skills transfer directly to writing. Students who can evaluate evidence strength (RI.8.1) are better positioned to select strong evidence for their own writing. Students who can analyze argument anatomy (RI.8.8) can construct sound arguments. Make this connection explicit at the start of Unit 3.

**Unit 4 (Research and Synthesis):**
RI.8.9 (conflicting sources) is the direct prerequisite for research writing. Students will need to synthesize multiple conflicting sources into a coherent research paper. The Source Reliability Checklist from Lesson 8 is reused in Unit 4.

**Grade 9 Preparation:**
Grade 9 informational reading standards require students to analyze seminal U.S. documents and connect them to themes in literature. The argument evaluation skills from RI.8.8 and the multi-text comparison skills from RI.8.9 are direct preparation for these more complex tasks.
