# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 2, Lesson 6

## Topic
Evaluating Arguments: Claims and Reasoning

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the main claim and the line of reasoning in an argumentative informational text
- Distinguish between a claim, a reason, and a piece of evidence
- Evaluate whether the reasoning connecting a claim to its evidence is sound or flawed

## Prerequisites
- Tracing and evaluating arguments and specific claims (RI.7.8)
- Citing the strongest evidence to support a claim (RI.8.1, Lesson 1)
- Determining the central idea of a text (RI.8.2, Lesson 2)
- Understanding how categorization and distinction serve arguments (RI.8.3, Lesson 5)

## Materials Needed
- Lesson 6 passage copies
- Argument Anatomy chart (three rows: Claim | Reasoning | Evidence; plus a "Soundness" rating row at bottom)
- Highlighters (three colors: one each for Claim, Reasoning, Evidence)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Surface the distinction between claims, reasons, and evidence — and create a need for evaluating reasoning.

**Activity: What's the Missing Step?**

Present the following two arguments. Read both aloud.

> **Argument 1:** "Ice cream sales increase in summer. Drowning rates also increase in summer. Therefore, eating ice cream causes drowning."
> **Argument 2:** "Studies show that students who read for pleasure score higher on standardized assessments. Schools should therefore require 20 minutes of independent reading daily."

Ask: *"What is wrong with Argument 1? Is there any problem with Argument 2?"*

Brief discussion. Guide students to see:
- Argument 1: The reasoning is flawed. Both things increase in summer because of summer weather — there is no logical connection between ice cream and drowning. The evidence is real, but the reasoning leap is invalid (correlation ≠ causation).
- Argument 2: The evidence is real. The reasoning (if students read more, scores improve; therefore mandate reading time) may have flaws too — are all 20-minute blocks equally effective? Does mandated reading produce the same effect as voluntary reading? The reasoning needs scrutiny.

Say: *"Today we're introducing the Argument Anatomy framework — a tool for taking any argumentative text apart, identifying what the author is claiming, how they're reasoning, and whether that reasoning actually holds up."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the Argument Anatomy chart and teach students to evaluate the soundness of reasoning.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Claim:** The main assertion an author is arguing; the position the text asks the reader to accept
- **Reasoning:** The logical explanation of why the evidence supports the claim; the "because" connecting evidence to conclusion
- **Sound reasoning:** Reasoning in which the logic connecting evidence to claim is valid — where the conclusion follows necessarily or plausibly from the evidence
- **Flawed reasoning:** Reasoning that makes an unjustified logical leap, assumes what it needs to prove, or draws a conclusion stronger than the evidence can support

### Instruction Notes

Say: *"Arguments have three moving parts. The claim is WHAT the author wants you to believe. The evidence is WHAT THE AUTHOR GIVES YOU to believe it. The reasoning is WHY THE EVIDENCE PROVES THE CLAIM. It's the middle step — and it's the one most often left unstated, assumed, or faked."*

Introduce the Argument Anatomy chart. Write on the board:

```
CLAIM: _______________________________________________
REASONING: Because [evidence] proves [claim] because ___
EVIDENCE: _______________________________________________
SOUNDNESS: Sound / Weak / Flawed — Why?
```

Say: *"When you evaluate reasoning, ask three questions:"*

1. **Is the reasoning stated?** Many texts leave the reasoning implicit. Can you reconstruct the logical step between evidence and claim?
2. **Does the reasoning hold?** Even if stated, does the logic actually work? Or does it make an unjustified assumption?
3. **Is the claim stronger than the evidence allows?** Sometimes the evidence is good, but the author claims more than it proves.

Introduce four common reasoning flaws to watch for:

| Flaw | Description | Example |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Correlation = Causation** | Treating two things that occur together as cause and effect | "Violent crime rose after the new immigration policy" (may be unrelated) |
| **Hasty Generalization** | Drawing a broad conclusion from limited examples | "Three students failed the test; the curriculum is ineffective" |
| **Circular Reasoning** | Using the claim itself as evidence for the claim | "This policy works because policies like this are effective" |
| **False Dichotomy** | Treating a complex issue as if there are only two options | "Either we cut the program entirely or costs will spiral out of control" |

### Worked Examples

Read the following passage aloud, then model the Argument Anatomy chart.

---

**Passage A: "The Case for Later School Start Times"**

The case for delaying school start times rests on a straightforward biological argument. Research consistently demonstrates that adolescent circadian rhythms are shifted two to three hours later than adult rhythms — a biological reality, not a lifestyle choice. When schools begin at 7:30 a.m., teenagers are forced to wake during the biological equivalent of the middle of the night for an adult. The consequences are measurable: chronic sleep deprivation in adolescents is associated with reduced cognitive performance, impaired emotional regulation, higher rates of depression, and increased accident risk. School districts that have moved first bell to 8:30 or later report statistically significant improvements in attendance, graduation rates, and student self-reported wellbeing. The argument against later start times typically involves logistical concerns — transportation costs, after-school activity schedules, and working parent needs. These concerns are real. But they are logistical problems for which solutions exist. The biological reality of adolescent sleep cannot be argued away by scheduling inconvenience.

---

Model filling in the Argument Anatomy chart:

**Claim:** Schools should delay start times because the biological reality of adolescent sleep patterns makes early starts harmful.

**Evidence:**
- Adolescent circadian rhythms are shifted 2–3 hours later than adult rhythms (biological research)
- Early start times cause: reduced cognitive performance, impaired emotional regulation, depression, accident risk
- Districts with later start times show improvements in attendance, graduation, wellbeing

**Reasoning:** Because the harms of early start times are biologically caused and empirically documented — not behavioral or optional — and because solutions to logistical barriers exist, delaying start times is the appropriate response.

**Soundness evaluation:**
- The biological evidence is strong and the claim follows from it. The reasoning is largely sound.
- One potential weakness: does "associated with" (correlation) prove that early start times cause these outcomes, or just that they co-occur? The author says "associated with" — which is correlation language. A precise critic would note the claim requires causation.
- The dismissal of logistical barriers ("logistical problems for which solutions exist") is a bit summary — the author asserts solutions exist without providing them.

**Overall:** Mostly sound with minor over-claim on causation and brevity on counterargument.

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students use the Argument Anatomy chart with facilitator support on a new passage.

Read the following passage aloud together.

---

**Passage B: "Why Algorithm-Based Hiring Tools Fail"**

Technology companies have marketed algorithm-based résumé screening tools as objective, efficient solutions to the problem of human bias in hiring. The pitch is intuitive: if you remove the human — with their unconscious biases, their preference for candidates who attended their own school, their tendency to favor names that sound familiar — you get a fairer outcome. Algorithms, the argument goes, just analyze data. The problem with this argument is that algorithms do not analyze data in a vacuum; they are trained on historical hiring data, which reflects past human decisions. If, historically, a company hired fewer women for technical roles, an algorithm trained on that company's data will learn to down-score résumés with signals correlated with female applicants — because the training data tells it that those applicants were historically less likely to be hired. The algorithm does not introduce the bias; it inherits and amplifies it. Several major technology companies have quietly discontinued their algorithm-based hiring tools after internal audits revealed exactly this pattern. The conclusion that algorithms automatically produce fairer outcomes is not just unproven — it is demonstrably wrong in documented cases.

---

Work through the Argument Anatomy chart together:

- **Claim:** Algorithm-based hiring tools do not produce fairer outcomes; they inherit and amplify historical biases.
- **Evidence:** Algorithms are trained on historical data; historical data reflects past biased decisions; documented cases show companies quietly discontinued flawed tools.
- **Reasoning:** If the training data encodes historical bias, and algorithms learn from training data, then the algorithm will reproduce — not eliminate — bias.
- **Soundness questions for group discussion:**
  - Is the reasoning valid? (Yes — the logical chain from training data to biased output is well-established in machine learning theory.)
  - Is the evidence sufficient? (Mostly — the "several major technology companies" claim is vague; specific documented cases would strengthen it.)
  - Is the claim too strong? ("Demonstrably wrong in documented cases" — does this mean ALL algorithm tools are flawed, or only some? Is the author overgeneralizing?)
  - What reasoning flaw might a critic identify? (Hasty generalization: some documented failures does not prove all algorithm tools always fail.)

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently apply the Argument Anatomy chart to a new passage and evaluate reasoning soundness.

Read the following passage, then complete the questions.

---

**Passage C: "The Case Against Homework"**

Decades of research on the relationship between homework and academic achievement produce a surprisingly consistent finding: for elementary school students, there is essentially no correlation between the amount of homework assigned and academic outcomes. For middle school students, a modest positive correlation appears — but it flattens out rapidly after approximately one hour of homework per night. Beyond that threshold, additional homework is associated not with higher achievement but with higher stress levels, reduced sleep, and diminished intrinsic motivation to learn. For high school students, slightly more homework appears beneficial — but again, the benefit peaks before reaching the loads commonly assigned in competitive academic environments. These findings, replicated across dozens of studies in multiple countries, suggest that the cultural assumption driving homework practices — more homework equals more learning — is empirically unsupported for most students. Critics of these studies note that the research has methodological challenges: homework quantity is hard to standardize, student effort varies, and home environments differ dramatically. These are fair criticisms. But the burden of proof should rest with those who claim homework produces benefits — and that evidence is far weaker than its defenders acknowledge.

---

**Questions:**

1. Using the Argument Anatomy chart, identify the claim, the main evidence, and the reasoning of this passage.

2. The author acknowledges critics' methodological concerns ("These are fair criticisms") before dismissing them. Evaluate this move: does acknowledging the criticism and then setting it aside strengthen or weaken the argument? Explain.

3. The author uses "correlation" language throughout the passage ("essentially no correlation," "modest positive correlation"). Does this create a reasoning problem? Apply the correlation/causation distinction from Direct Instruction to evaluate whether the author's claim is appropriately hedged.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete questions 1 and 2. For question 1, use the Argument Anatomy chart graphic organizer.

**Stretch:** Complete all three questions. For question 3, write a paragraph explaining what additional evidence would make the argument stronger — specifically addressing the causal claim that homework beyond a threshold reduces motivation.

**Challenge:** Complete all three questions. Then write a paragraph explaining how this passage would respond to the argument: "Maybe the studies are measuring the wrong thing — maybe homework teaches discipline and time management, which don't show up in achievement test scores."

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1 (RI.8.3 — Lesson 5):** The author distinguishes between different levels of schooling (elementary, middle, high school) when presenting homework research findings. Is this a categorization that strengthens or weakens the argument? Explain.

**Review Question 2 (RI.8.1 — Evidence Strength):** Identify the single strongest piece of evidence the author cites for the claim that the "more homework = more learning" assumption is empirically unsupported. Quote it and explain your Evidence Strength Meter rating.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

Read this short argument, then answer the question.

---

A recent survey found that 78% of employees at companies with on-site fitness facilities reported feeling satisfied with their jobs. Therefore, companies that install fitness facilities will see increased employee satisfaction.

---

**Question:** Identify the reasoning flaw in this argument. Use vocabulary from today's lesson.

**Expected Answers:**

The flaw is correlation = causation. The survey shows that companies with fitness facilities have more satisfied employees — but it does not show that the fitness facilities caused the satisfaction. It is possible that companies with fitness facilities are also larger, better-funded, more employee-centered companies in general — and those characteristics, not the gym, produce satisfaction. The argument makes a causal claim ("will see increased satisfaction") from correlational evidence.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies the correlation/causation flaw AND explains why the evidence doesn't support the causal claim.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Ask: "Does the survey tell us that the fitness facility caused satisfaction? Or just that satisfied employees and fitness facilities tend to appear in the same companies?" Walk through the ice cream/drowning Warm-Up example again to reinforce why correlation doesn't equal causation.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "What would the argument need to include to legitimately claim causation?" (A study that introduced fitness facilities to previously low-satisfaction companies and measured the change — a controlled before-and-after study.) Reinforce that stronger reasoning often requires stronger evidence.

## Extension (Optional)

1. Find an editorial or opinion piece in a newspaper or magazine. Using the Argument Anatomy chart, identify the claim, evidence, and reasoning. Evaluate the soundness of the reasoning in a short paragraph.

2. Choose one of the four reasoning flaws from Direct Instruction (correlation = causation, hasty generalization, circular reasoning, false dichotomy). Find or create a real-world example of this flaw being used in a persuasive context (advertisement, political speech, social media post). Explain how the flaw works in your example.

3. The homework passage acknowledges critics' concerns but then says "the burden of proof should rest with those who claim homework produces benefits." Write a paragraph arguing the opposite — that the burden of proof should rest with those who want to reduce or eliminate homework. What evidence would you need?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The Argument Anatomy chart is a high-priority tool for the rest of the unit and the unit assessment. Students who do not internalize the Claim / Reasoning / Evidence distinction before Lesson 7 will struggle with the more nuanced evidence evaluation work.
- The most common error students make is conflating reasoning with evidence: they write evidence in the "Reasoning" row. Reinforce: evidence is WHAT you cite; reasoning is WHY the evidence proves the claim. Evidence = the facts. Reasoning = the logic.
- The four reasoning flaws introduced here are not exhaustive. Students who are interested can explore logical fallacy taxonomies as an extension, but for assessment purposes, these four are sufficient.
- Students often assume that if evidence exists, the argument is sound. Lesson 6 challenges this. Lesson 7 extends it by focusing on evidence relevance and sufficiency. Together, Lessons 6–7 build the full RI.8.8 skill.
- The homework passage is intentionally chosen because students likely have personal experience and opinions about homework. Remind students that evaluating an argument's soundness is different from agreeing or disagreeing with its conclusion.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Question 1:**

**Claim:** The cultural assumption that "more homework = more learning" is empirically unsupported for most students, and the burden of proof lies with those who claim homework benefits.

**Evidence:**
- No correlation between homework and academic outcomes for elementary students
- Modest positive correlation for middle school students that flatters out after ~1 hour
- High school benefits peak before reaching common homework loads
- Findings replicated across dozens of studies in multiple countries
- Beyond threshold: higher stress, reduced sleep, reduced intrinsic motivation

**Reasoning:** If research consistently and replicably shows no meaningful relationship between homework quantity and achievement — and in fact shows harms beyond a threshold — then the assumption that more homework produces more learning is not supported by evidence. The burden of proof should rest with those making the positive claim.

**Soundness:** Mostly sound. The replication across studies and countries is strong. The reasoning appropriately uses "suggests" and "associated with" in most places, acknowledging correlation rather than claiming causation.

**Question 2:** The concede-then-dismiss move ("These are fair criticisms. But...") is a double-edged rhetorical strategy. It strengthens the argument's credibility by showing the author has considered objections. However, simply acknowledging a criticism and asserting it doesn't overturn the burden of proof is not a refutation — it is a deflection. A stronger version of the argument would directly respond to the methodological concerns (e.g., by citing studies that controlled for home environment variation). The move works rhetorically but is not a fully sound logical response to the criticism.

**Question 3:** Using correlation language is appropriate for the evidence — the studies measure correlation, and the author says "essentially no correlation" rather than "no causal relationship." This is appropriately hedged. However, when the author says homework "is associated not with higher achievement but with higher stress levels, reduced sleep, and diminished intrinsic motivation," the "associated with" language implies correlation but the sentence reads as a causal claim. A precise critic would note: the studies show association; whether homework causes stress (as opposed to stressed students also doing more homework) is not established by the evidence cited.

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1:** The categorization by schooling level strengthens the argument because it prevents the hasty generalization that research findings about elementary students apply to high school students, and vice versa. By differentiating, the author shows the evidence is more nuanced than a blanket anti-homework claim — which makes it more credible. The distinctions also serve the argument: even at the level where homework shows some benefit (high school), the benefit is limited and the current loads exceed it.

**Review Question 2:** Strongest evidence: "These findings, replicated across dozens of studies in multiple countries, suggest that the cultural assumption driving homework practices — more homework equals more learning — is empirically unsupported for most students." Directness: 5 (directly addresses the specific claim about the assumption); Specificity: 4 (dozens of studies, multiple countries); Sufficiency: 5. Total: 14/15.

### Exit Ticket

**Full credit:** Identifies correlation = causation flaw AND explains that the survey shows companies with gyms have more satisfied employees, but not that the gym caused the satisfaction — other features of those companies could explain both.

**Partial credit:** Identifies a flaw but describes it imprecisely ("the evidence isn't good enough" without naming the correlation/causation problem).
