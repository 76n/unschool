# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 2, Lesson 8

## Topic
Analyzing Conflicting Information Across Texts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify where two texts on the same topic provide different or conflicting information
- Distinguish between factual conflicts and interpretive conflicts
- Apply the Source Reliability Checklist to compare two texts and locate the source of disagreement

## Prerequisites
- Comparing and contrasting presentations of events or ideas across texts (RI.7.9)
- Evaluating argument claims and reasoning (RI.8.8, Lesson 6)
- Assessing evidence relevance and sufficiency (RI.8.8, Lesson 7)
- Analyzing how texts make connections among ideas (RI.8.3, Lessons 4–5)

## Materials Needed
- Lesson 8 paired passage copies (two texts on the same topic)
- Source Reliability Checklist graphic organizer (two-column compare chart: rows for Author Perspective, Type of Evidence, Factual Claims, Interpretive Claims, Credibility Notes)
- Highlighters (two colors — one per text)
- Student notebook

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Create an immediate, tangible experience of conflicting information before introducing the analytical framework.

**Activity: Same Event, Different Story**

Read the following two short statements aloud:

> **Source 1:** "The new city composting program has been a resounding success. In its first year, the program diverted 12,000 tons of organic waste from landfills — exceeding projections by 40%. Participation rates in enrolled neighborhoods reached 68%."

> **Source 2:** "The city's composting program has underperformed expectations. A year in, only 34% of eligible households have enrolled in the voluntary program. The city originally projected 50% enrollment. The program currently serves fewer than a third of the city's residential waste producers."

Ask: *"Are both of these statements lying? Can they both be true at the same time? Where does the conflict come from?"*

Allow 90 seconds of discussion. Guide students to see:
- Source 1 measures success by the performance of enrolled participants (tons diverted; exceeding tonnage projections).
- Source 2 measures success by enrollment rates compared to original enrollment projections.
- Both may be reporting real numbers — but they are measuring different things and framing different aspects of the program.

Say: *"This is what conflicting information in informational text usually looks like. It's not always that one source is lying and one is telling the truth. Often, both sources are reporting something real — but they're choosing different facts, different metrics, and different frames. Our job today is to figure out exactly WHERE the conflict is and WHY it exists."*

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the Source Reliability Checklist and teach students to systematically identify and locate conflict between texts.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Conflicting information:** Information in two texts that cannot both be true simultaneously, or that presents the same situation in incompatible ways
- **Factual conflict:** A disagreement about what happened, what was measured, or what a situation involves — a claim that can in principle be verified against evidence
- **Interpretive conflict:** A disagreement about what the facts mean, how significant they are, or what conclusion they support — a claim that involves judgment, not just measurement
- **Source Reliability Checklist:** A systematic tool for comparing two texts on the same topic, analyzing author perspective, evidence type, factual claims, interpretive claims, and credibility

### Instruction Notes

Say: *"When two texts conflict, the first question is: what kind of conflict is this? There are two basic types."*

Write on the board:
- **Factual conflict:** "Text A says X happened. Text B says Y happened." One or both are wrong about the facts.
- **Interpretive conflict:** "Both texts report X happened. Text A concludes this means the situation is good. Text B concludes this means the situation is bad."

Say: *"The Warm-Up was mostly an interpretive conflict — both sources were using real numbers, but they were choosing which numbers to emphasize and what those numbers meant. Most sophisticated conflicts you'll encounter are interpretive."*

Introduce the Source Reliability Checklist. Draw the two-column format on the board:

| | Text A | Text B |
|---|---|---|
| Author/Source Perspective | | |
| Type of Evidence Used | | |
| Key Factual Claims | | |
| Key Interpretive Claims | | |
| Where Do They Conflict? | | |
| Credibility Notes | | |

Say: *"You fill this in for both texts. Then you compare. The 'Where Do They Conflict?' row is where you write: is this a factual conflict (one says X, the other says Y) or an interpretive conflict (both know X, but they disagree about what X means)?"*

Walk through each row:
- **Author/Source Perspective:** Who wrote this? What organization or viewpoint do they represent? This shapes what they emphasize.
- **Type of Evidence:** Statistical data? Expert testimony? Case studies? Anecdote? Different evidence types lead to different pictures.
- **Key Factual Claims:** What does each text assert as fact? Are these the same facts, different facts, or contradictory facts?
- **Key Interpretive Claims:** What conclusions does each text draw? These are often where the real conflict lies.
- **Where Do They Conflict?** Specifically identify: factual or interpretive.
- **Credibility Notes:** Is there any reason to trust one source more than another on this specific question?

### Worked Examples

Read the following two passages aloud, then model the Source Reliability Checklist.

---

**Text A: "Ocean Plastic: An Escalating Crisis"**
*(From an environmental advocacy organization's annual report)*

The concentration of plastic waste in the world's oceans has reached levels that scientists describe as a global ecological emergency. Current estimates place the total quantity of plastic in the oceans at over 150 million metric tons, with an additional 8 million metric tons entering every year. Marine biologists studying the effects of this pollution have documented plastic ingestion in more than 700 species of marine animals, including sea turtles, seabirds, fish, and marine mammals. Microplastics — particles less than 5mm in size produced by the breakdown of larger plastic items — have been detected in the tissues of organisms at every level of the marine food chain, including species consumed by humans. The trajectory is not improving: without significant intervention, researchers project that plastic in the ocean will outweigh fish by 2050. The policy response has been inadequate. International agreements on ocean plastic remain largely voluntary and non-binding, with compliance rates that environmental monitors describe as "deeply disappointing."

---

**Text B: "Progress on Ocean Plastic: What the Data Actually Shows"**
*(From a technology and industry publication's environmental coverage)*

Reports of a plastic "catastrophe" in the world's oceans, while well-intentioned, often obscure the genuine progress being made. Since the passage of national single-use plastic reduction laws in more than 60 countries over the past decade, annual plastic production has leveled off after decades of exponential growth — a significant achievement that earlier projections did not anticipate. Private-sector investment in ocean plastic cleanup and recycling technology has reached record levels, with startup companies developing new materials, filtration systems, and collection technologies that did not exist five years ago. While the problem remains serious and the volume of existing plastic in the ocean is not decreasing rapidly, the claim that the situation is continuously worsening does not align with the most recent production data. Critics of strong regulatory responses argue that innovation-driven solutions are more effective than restrictive legislation that can hamper economic growth.

---

Model the Source Reliability Checklist:

| | Text A | Text B |
|---|---|---|
| Perspective | Environmental advocacy org; advocates for stronger policy | Technology/industry publication; more sympathetic to innovation and industry |
| Evidence Type | Scientific data (species impact, microplastic tissue studies), projections | Policy counts (60+ countries), private-sector investment figures, production data |
| Key Factual Claims | 150M metric tons; 8M enter annually; 700+ affected species; plastic outweighs fish by 2050 | Plastic production "leveled off"; 60+ countries passed laws; record private investment |
| Key Interpretive Claims | The situation is an escalating crisis; policy response is inadequate | Progress is being made; crisis framing obscures real progress |
| Conflict | Factual: Both texts acknowledge large volumes of plastic; Text A says trajectory is not improving; Text B says annual production has leveled off. These are not directly contradictory — one is about existing stock, one is about new input. **Interpretive conflict is deeper:** Text A frames the situation as a crisis demanding stronger regulation; Text B frames it as a problem being addressed by innovation and policy, with crisis framing as exaggeration. | Same |
| Credibility Notes | Advocacy org may amplify alarming data; however, scientific documentation (species affected, microplastics) is specific and verifiable | Industry publication may downplay severity; "leveled off" claim is not quantified |

Say: *"Notice the most important finding: the core conflict is interpretive. Both texts are likely reporting true facts, but they are selecting different facts and drawing opposite conclusions. The checklist helps us see exactly where and why they conflict."*

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students apply the Source Reliability Checklist to a new pair of texts with facilitator support.

Read both passages aloud together.

---

**Text C: "The Promise of Mars Colonization"**
*(From a space exploration advocacy magazine)*

Within the next fifty years, human beings will live and work permanently on the surface of Mars. This is not speculation — it is the direction of multiple converging technological developments. Advances in rocket propulsion, life-support technology, in-situ resource utilization (the ability to make fuel, water, and oxygen from Martian materials), and additive manufacturing have collectively reduced the estimated cost of a crewed Mars mission from the trillions of dollars estimated in earlier projections to figures that are approaching practical feasibility. Private aerospace companies have demonstrated rapid iteration in launch technology that government agencies have historically been unable to match. Moreover, the argument for a multi-planetary human civilization has become more urgent as climate change, nuclear risk, and pandemic potential raise the existential stakes of remaining an exclusively Earth-bound species. Critics who argue that the cost of Mars colonization should be spent on Earth's problems set up a false choice: the same technological development that supports Mars colonization produces innovations — in energy, materials science, and medicine — that benefit life on Earth.

---

**Text D: "The Case Against Mars"**
*(From a science policy journal)*

The enthusiasm for Mars colonization, while understandable, reflects a failure to reckon honestly with the actual challenges involved. The radiation environment on Mars, without Earth's magnetic field and with a thin atmosphere, exposes inhabitants to radiation doses that would, over years of habitation, significantly elevate cancer risk by current models — a problem for which no practical shielding solution exists at scale. The distance from Earth creates communication lags of up to 24 minutes one-way, making real-time support or rescue impossible. Psychologically, the isolation, confinement, and lack of control over basic survival conditions create stressors that no long-duration mission has fully addressed. The in-situ resource utilization technologies cited by Mars proponents have been demonstrated only at laboratory scale; scaling them to support a permanent human colony involves engineering challenges of a different order of magnitude. And the resources required — conservatively in the hundreds of billions of dollars for an initial crewed mission — represent an opportunity cost: every dollar spent reaching Mars is a dollar not spent addressing solvable problems on Earth.

---

Have students complete the Source Reliability Checklist in pairs, then discuss as a group:

- *"Is the core conflict factual or interpretive? Where exactly do the texts disagree?"*
  - Both texts acknowledge the challenges; the factual conflict is about in-situ resource utilization scale (Text C implies it's advancing; Text D says it's only lab-scale). The deeper conflict is interpretive: Text C frames the challenges as solvable and argues the risks of staying Earth-bound are greater; Text D frames the challenges as potentially insurmountable and questions the priorities.
- *"Which text do you find more credible, and why? Use the Credibility Notes row."*

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently apply the Source Reliability Checklist to a new paired text set.

Read both passages, then complete the questions.

---

**Text E: "Exercise Science Supports High-Intensity Training for Teens"**

Research in exercise physiology has consistently shown that high-intensity interval training (HIIT) produces superior cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes compared to moderate, sustained-pace exercise in adolescent populations. In studies comparing groups of teenagers engaging in HIIT programs versus traditional moderate-exercise programs over 12-week periods, HIIT groups showed greater improvements in cardiovascular fitness, insulin sensitivity, and body composition. Adolescent participants in HIIT programs also reported higher levels of enjoyment and perceived exertion, suggesting better engagement and motivation. Physical education programs that have incorporated HIIT protocols report higher student participation rates and more positive attitudes toward physical activity. Given the documented rates of physical inactivity among teenagers — linked to rising rates of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular risk factors — the case for adopting HIIT in school PE programs is both scientifically grounded and practically urgent.

---

**Text F: "The Hidden Risks of High-Intensity Exercise for Adolescents"**

The recent push to introduce high-intensity interval training into school physical education programs should be approached with caution. Adolescent musculoskeletal systems are still developing — growth plates are open and vulnerable to stress fractures and overuse injuries that are not typically risks for adult athletes. Several sports medicine associations have issued position statements recommending that high-intensity training for adolescents be carefully supervised and limited in frequency to allow for adequate recovery. Studies comparing injury rates between students in HIIT-based and traditional PE programs have found higher rates of minor musculoskeletal complaints in HIIT participants, though most injuries were minor and resolved without intervention. Furthermore, the student populations studied in HIIT research tend to skew toward more physically active and motivated participants; the benefits documented in these studies may not replicate in a general school population that includes students who are sedentary, have chronic health conditions, or have not previously engaged in structured exercise.

---

**Questions:**

1. Complete the Source Reliability Checklist for Texts E and F.

2. Identify one factual conflict and one interpretive conflict between the two texts. For each, quote the relevant passages from both texts.

3. Based on the Source Reliability Checklist, which text do you find more credible on the specific question of whether HIIT should be adopted in school PE programs? Use at least two criteria from the Checklist to support your answer.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Complete questions 1 and 2. For question 2, identify only the factual conflict.

**Stretch:** Complete all three questions. For question 3, write a full paragraph evaluating credibility and acknowledging the strongest point the less-credible text makes.

**Challenge:** Complete all three questions. Then write a paragraph responding to this prompt: "Both texts might be right for different populations of students. Write a synthesis claim that incorporates the core insight from each text into a single, nuanced recommendation."

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1 (RI.8.8 — Argument Anatomy):** Using the Argument Anatomy chart, identify the main claim and one piece of evidence from Text E. Is the reasoning connecting the evidence to the claim sound? Identify any potential flaw.

**Review Question 2 (RI.8.1 — Evidence Strength):** Compare the evidence in Text E and Text F. Which text cites stronger evidence? Apply Evidence Strength Meter criteria to at least one piece of evidence from each text.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

Read these two sentences about the same topic, then answer the question.

> **Source A:** "Air pollution in the city declined by 18% last year following the introduction of the new clean vehicle incentive program."
> **Source B:** "Despite the city's clean vehicle initiative, air quality remains below federal health standards in seven of the city's twelve monitoring zones."

**Question:** Is this a factual conflict, an interpretive conflict, or both? Explain in 2–3 sentences.

**Expected Answers:**

This is primarily an interpretive conflict with a possible factual component. Both statements can be simultaneously true: air pollution could have declined by 18% AND still be below federal standards (it was much worse before, and 18% improvement still leaves it inadequate). The factual claims are not necessarily contradictory. The interpretive conflict is about what the data means: Source A frames the program as successful (18% decline); Source B frames the situation as still inadequate (still below standards in most zones). Both are using different metrics to evaluate the same program.

**Mastery = Student identifies this as primarily interpretive AND explains that both statements can be true simultaneously — the conflict is about which metric to use to evaluate success, not about whether the facts are accurate.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Ask: "Could both of these be true at the same time? If air quality was very bad before, could it drop 18% and still be below standards?" Walk students through the logical possibility. Then ask: "If they're both true, what are they arguing about?" Guide toward: they're arguing about what counts as success.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "Which source do you find more persuasive, and why? What additional information would help you decide which evaluation is correct?" Reinforce that identifying the type of conflict is the first step toward resolving it.

## Extension (Optional)

1. Find two news articles about the same recent event or policy. Using the Source Reliability Checklist, identify where they agree, where they conflict, and whether the conflict is factual or interpretive. Write a short paragraph summarizing your analysis.

2. The composting program in the Warm-Up was presented as a conflict. Write a short paragraph that synthesizes both perspectives — acknowledging what each source measured and producing a single, nuanced statement about the program's performance.

3. Sources A and B in the exit ticket conflict on how to evaluate an improvement. In public policy, debates about "how much is enough?" are common. Research (from memory or general knowledge) one other area where measuring success is controversial because different groups use different metrics. Describe the metric conflict.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The Source Reliability Checklist is a permanent tool for the rest of the unit and beyond. Students should keep their completed checklists from this and Lesson 9 as models for the unit assessment.
- The most common error in this lesson is students saying one source is "right" and the other is "wrong" without examining the type of conflict. Push students to articulate: "Are these factually contradictory? Or do they just draw different conclusions from different facts?"
- The Mars passages (Texts C and D) are useful for discussing how perspective (advocacy vs. caution) shapes what evidence is selected. Neither passage is necessarily wrong — both select real considerations.
- Source credibility analysis is a life skill. Connect this lesson to media literacy: when students encounter conflicting news stories, the Source Reliability Checklist gives them a systematic approach rather than guessing which source to trust.
- Lesson 9 will extend this work to the specific distinction between factual and interpretive disagreements, which students are beginning to encounter here. Reinforce the vocabulary today so Lesson 9 can deepen the analysis.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Question 1 (Source Reliability Checklist for Texts E and F):**

| | Text E | Text F |
|---|---|---|
| Perspective | Pro-HIIT; emphasizes documented benefits; practical urgency framing | Cautionary; emphasizes risks and limitations; sports medicine perspective |
| Evidence Type | Controlled studies (12-week, cardiovascular outcomes); self-report (enjoyment); PE program participation data | Sports medicine position statements; injury rate comparisons; research population analysis |
| Key Factual Claims | HIIT produces better cardiovascular and metabolic outcomes in teens; higher enjoyment and participation | Growth plates vulnerable; HIIT injury rates higher than traditional PE; HIIT study populations skew toward active participants |
| Key Interpretive Claims | HIIT should be adopted in school PE; scientifically grounded and practically urgent | HIIT should be approached with caution; benefits may not replicate in general school populations |
| Conflict | Factual: Text E says higher participation; Text F notes that HIIT study populations skew toward already-active students (suggesting study populations may not reflect general PE). Interpretive: Text E says adopt; Text F says use caution. | |
| Credibility Notes | Studies cited seem controlled; but "higher enjoyment" is self-report | Sports medicine position statements add professional authority; injury data is specific |

**Question 2:**

Factual conflict: Text E states that HIIT produced "greater improvements in cardiovascular fitness, insulin sensitivity, and body composition" in study participants. Text F states that "the student populations studied in HIIT research tend to skew toward more physically active and motivated participants," implying the comparison populations in Text E's studies may not represent general students — making the factual findings less generalizable, if not directly contradictory.

Interpretive conflict: Text E concludes "the case for adopting HIIT in school PE programs is both scientifically grounded and practically urgent." Text F concludes HIIT "should be approached with caution." Both acknowledge both benefits and risks — but they draw opposite conclusions about whether to proceed.

**Question 3 (sample answer):** Text F is more credible on the question of adopting HIIT for general school populations, for two reasons from the Checklist. First, Text F's evidence type includes sports medicine position statements, which represent professional medical consensus on adolescent physiology — a more authoritative source than individual studies for a medical safety question. Second, Text F raises a credibility concern about Text E's evidence: the research populations studied skew toward already-active participants, which means the benefits documented may not apply to sedentary or health-compromised students who form a significant part of any school's PE enrollment. The strongest point Text E makes is the urgency argument about teenage inactivity — but urgency does not overcome safety concerns.

### Interleaved Review

**Review Question 1:** Claim: HIIT produces superior outcomes in teens and should be adopted in school PE. Evidence: Studies comparing HIIT vs. moderate exercise groups over 12 weeks show greater improvements. Reasoning: If controlled studies show better outcomes, HIIT is the more effective approach and should be implemented. Potential flaw: the text cites "studies" without specifying how many, the quality of controls, or whether the populations are representative — making this a potential case of insufficient evidence for the broad claim (national adoption of HIIT).

**Review Question 2:** Text F cites stronger evidence for the safety concern. "Several sports medicine associations have issued position statements" — Directness: 5 (directly addresses adolescent HIIT safety); Specificity: 4 (names position statements, growth plates); Sufficiency: 4. Text E's strongest evidence: "HIIT groups showed greater improvements in cardiovascular fitness, insulin sensitivity, and body composition" — Directness: 5; Specificity: 4; Sufficiency: 3 (limited by population concern). Text F's position statement evidence is stronger for the specific claim about school adoption safety.

### Exit Ticket

**Full credit:** Identifies as primarily interpretive conflict; explains that both statements can be simultaneously true because 18% decline and below-standard are not contradictory; and identifies the metric conflict (decline vs. absolute standard) as the source of disagreement.

**Partial credit:** Correctly identifies it as interpretive but says only that "they see it differently" without explaining why both can be true simultaneously.
