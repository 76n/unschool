# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 3

## Topic
Understanding Verbals: Infinitives

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify infinitives and infinitive phrases and explain whether they function as nouns, adjectives, or adverbs
- Distinguish infinitives from prepositional phrases beginning with "to"
- Write original sentences using infinitive phrases in all three grammatical functions

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of gerunds (Lesson 1) and participles (Lesson 2) as verbals with specific functions
- Familiarity with nouns, adjectives, and adverbs as parts of speech (Grade 7)
- Understanding of prepositional phrases from Grade 7 (L.7.1a)
- Ability to identify subject, verb, object, and complement in a sentence (Grade 7)

## Materials Needed
- Student notebook or grammar journal
- Lesson 3 handout (or projected sentences)
- Highlighters (one color for infinitive phrase, one for the word it modifies or the function it serves)
- Reference anchor chart from Lessons 1–2 ("Verbals at a Glance")

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Surface students' intuitive understanding of infinitives through familiar usage; introduce the three-function framework.

**Activity: Three Sentences, Three Jobs**

Display the following:
1. *To win the championship* is every player's dream this season.
2. She had one goal: *to win the championship*.
3. The team trained for months *to win the championship*.

Say: "The phrase 'to win the championship' appears in all three sentences — but it's doing a different job each time. In sentence 1, it's the subject. In sentence 2, it's a predicate nominative — it renames the goal. In sentence 3, it tells us *why* the team trained — it's acting like an adverb. This phrase is called an infinitive phrase, and today we're going to map exactly how it works."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define infinitives and infinitive phrases; establish the three functions (noun, adjective, adverb); address the infinitive vs. prepositional phrase distinction; introduce split infinitives.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Infinitive:** The base form of a verb preceded by "to" — *to run, to think, to create.* It functions as a noun, adjective, or adverb.
- **Infinitive phrase:** An infinitive plus any objects, complements, or modifiers. The entire phrase functions as a noun, adjective, or adverb.
- **Split infinitive:** An infinitive in which an adverb is placed between "to" and the verb: *to boldly go, to quickly finish.* Generally acceptable in modern usage, though some formal contexts discourage it.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "An infinitive is simply 'to' + the base form of a verb: to run, to build, to understand. Unlike gerunds (which only function as nouns) and participles (which only function as adjectives), infinitives can do three jobs."

Write the three functions on the board with examples:

**1. Noun function** — the infinitive phrase acts like a noun (can be subject, direct object, or predicate nominative)
- Subject: *To master a new programming language* takes dedicated practice.
- Direct Object: She decided *to enter the regional debate tournament*.
- Predicate Nominative: The team's plan was *to practice every day until the qualifier*.

**2. Adjective function** — the infinitive phrase modifies a noun or pronoun (comes directly after the noun it modifies)
- The best way *to learn a new skill* is through consistent repetition.
- He was searching for a book *to read on the plane*.
- She needed a strategy *to stay calm under pressure*.

**3. Adverb function** — the infinitive phrase modifies a verb, adjective, or adverb (often tells "why" or "in order to")
- The team trained every weekend *to prepare for the state championship*. (modifies "trained" — why?)
- She stayed after school *to get extra help with the lab*. (modifies "stayed")
- He was relieved *to hear the final score*. (modifies the adjective "relieved")

**Distinguishing Infinitives from Prepositional Phrases:**

Say: "Here's the catch: the word 'to' also appears in prepositional phrases. How do you tell the difference?"

Write the test on the board:
> **Infinitive Test:** Does "to" come immediately before a verb in its base form? YES → infinitive. NO → preposition.

Examples:
- "She went *to the gym*." — "to" is followed by a noun phrase ("the gym") → **prepositional phrase**
- "She went *to train*." — "to" is followed by a base verb ("train") → **infinitive**
- "He listened *to the podcast*." — followed by noun → **prepositional phrase**
- "He stayed up late *to finish the podcast*." — followed by base verb → **infinitive**

**Split Infinitives:**

Say: "You may have heard that you should 'never split an infinitive.' A split infinitive puts an adverb between 'to' and the verb: *to quickly finish, to carefully read.* In formal writing, it's often better to move the adverb. But a split infinitive is sometimes the clearest option. The key is knowing you're making a choice."

Compare:
- Split: "She decided *to really focus* on the final week of training." (natural, conversational)
- Unsplit: "She decided *to focus really* on the final week." (awkward)
- Better revision: "She decided *to focus intensely* during the final week of training."

### Worked Examples

| Sentence | Infinitive Phrase | Function | What it modifies/does |
|----------|------------------|----------|-----------------------|
| To lead the orchestra was her childhood ambition. | to lead the orchestra | Noun (subject) | Subject of the sentence |
| Marcus wanted to redesign the school's website. | to redesign the school's website | Noun (direct object) | Object of "wanted" |
| The assignment to complete before Friday is on page 12. | to complete before Friday | Adjective | Modifies "assignment" |
| She practiced daily to improve her free-throw percentage. | to improve her free-throw percentage | Adverb | Modifies "practiced" — why? |

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Identify infinitive phrases, name their function, and distinguish infinitives from prepositional phrases.

**Part A — Identify or Distinguish:** For each sentence, underline the phrase beginning with "to" and label it: INF (infinitive phrase) or PREP (prepositional phrase).

1. After the game, the players went to the locker room.
2. The players warmed up to prepare for the overtime period.
3. She sent a message to her coach before the match.
4. His goal was to break the school record by December.
5. They drove to the regional science fair early Saturday morning.
6. To understand the algorithm, she watched the tutorial three times.

**Part B — Identify Function:** For each infinitive phrase below, label the function: Noun (N), Adjective (Adj), or Adverb (Adv).

1. To survive the first week of camp was harder than she had expected.
2. She borrowed a charger to power her laptop during the trip.
3. The best approach to solve this problem involves systematic testing.
4. He stayed late to help his teammates review for the quiz.
5. She hoped to perform in the spring recital.

**Facilitator support:** For Part B, item 3, students may debate whether "to solve this problem" is an adjective or adverb phrase. It modifies "approach" (a noun) — it's an adjective phrase. Walk through the question: "Which word is 'to solve this problem' describing? 'Approach' — a noun. So it's an adjective."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Independently identify infinitive phrases, correct errors, and produce sentences using all three infinitive functions.

**Part A — Identification and Labeling:**

Underline each infinitive phrase. If a "to" phrase is a prepositional phrase instead, circle it. Then label all infinitive phrases: N (noun), Adj (adjective), or Adv (adverb).

1. To build a sustainable energy grid requires cooperation across many industries.
2. She rode her bike to the library every afternoon after school.
3. He was the first student to complete the advanced placement exam early.
4. The coach's plan to adjust the defensive lineup surprised everyone.
5. They stayed up past midnight to finish the animation project.
6. She needs a quiet space to concentrate on her composition.

**Part B — Error Correction:**

A student wrote these sentences. Find and fix two errors: one split infinitive that creates awkwardness, and one confusion of an infinitive with a prepositional phrase.

> "My goal is to eventually, hopefully, finish the mural before spring. I walked to quickly reach the art room before anyone else. The best time to paint is early in the morning."

**Part C — Sentence Writing:**

Write three original sentences:
1. A sentence where an infinitive phrase is the **subject** of the sentence.
2. A sentence where an infinitive phrase functions as an **adjective** modifying a noun. (Hint: place the phrase right after the noun.)
3. A sentence where an infinitive phrase functions as an **adverb** explaining why someone did something.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Parts A and B; write any 2 of the 3 sentences in Part C.
**Stretch:** Complete all parts; in Part C, extend each infinitive phrase to at least 6 words.
**Challenge:** Complete all parts; then write a paragraph of 4–5 sentences using at least one infinitive phrase in each function (noun, adjective, adverb). Label each infinitive phrase with its function in the margin.

### Interleaved Review
1. *Lesson 2 review — Participles:* What is wrong with this sentence and how would you fix it? "Scrolling through her feed, the notification appeared at the top of the screen."
2. *Grade 7/Unit 1 review — Sentence structure:* Label this sentence as simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex: "Although she had studied for two weeks, she still felt nervous, and she asked her tutor for one more session."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Underline the infinitive phrase and name its function: "The simplest way to improve your writing is to read widely and often."
2. Is "to the library" in this sentence an infinitive or a prepositional phrase? "She went to the library to return three overdue books." Explain.
3. Write one original sentence in which an infinitive phrase functions as an adverb.

**Expected Answers:**
1. "to improve your writing" — adjective (modifies "way"); "to read widely and often" — noun (predicate nominative, renames "way")
2. "to the library" — prepositional phrase ("to" + noun phrase "the library"). "to return three overdue books" — infinitive phrase (adverb, tells why she went).
3. Any sentence where an infinitive phrase tells why/for what purpose, modifying a verb or adjective.

**Mastery = 2 out of 3 correct, including item 3 (production)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Use the Infinitive Test at the start of the next session: give the student 5 "to ___" phrases and ask them to sort into "infinitive" vs. "prepositional phrase" before moving on. Focus reteaching on the noun/adj/adv function distinction.

### If Mastery Is Met
Challenge the student: find one example each of an infinitive used as a noun, adjective, and adverb in a current reading text. Bring examples to Lesson 4.

## Extension (Optional)
1. Examine a paragraph from a science textbook or nonfiction article. Identify every phrase beginning with "to." Label each as an infinitive phrase (and its function) or a prepositional phrase. How many infinitives did the author use? What jobs do they do?
2. Rewrite a paragraph from a recent writing assignment, deliberately adding one infinitive phrase as a noun, one as an adjective, and one as an adverb. Underline and label each.
3. Research the debate over split infinitives. Write a brief argument (3–4 sentences) taking a position: should writers avoid split infinitives, or is it acceptable? Use at least one example to support your view.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most important distinction in this lesson is between infinitives and prepositional phrases with "to." The Infinitive Test (does "to" precede a base verb?) is reliable and worth posting on the anchor chart alongside the Verbal ID Test from Lesson 1.
- Students often default to labeling all infinitives as nouns. Spend extra time on the adjective and adverb functions — particularly the adverb function, since students tend to see "to + verb" as always naming a thing rather than explaining a reason.
- For the adjective function, emphasize placement: adjective infinitive phrases come directly after the noun they modify. This distinguishes them from adverb phrases, which can move more freely.
- The split infinitive discussion can briefly become a class debate. Keep it focused: the goal is awareness that this is a stylistic choice, not a forbidden construction.
- Note that the verb "to" can also appear as part of a two-word prepositional phrase ("according to," "due to") — these do not contain infinitives. If the student raises this, acknowledge it as an exception to the "to + noun = prepositional phrase" pattern.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A:**
1. *To build a sustainable energy grid* — INF, N (subject)
2. *to the library* — PREP (circle it)
3. *to complete the advanced placement exam early* — INF, Adj (modifies "student")
4. *to adjust the defensive lineup* — INF, Adj (modifies "plan")
5. *to finish the animation project* — INF, Adv (modifies "stayed up past midnight" — why?)
6. *to concentrate on her composition* — INF, Adj (modifies "space")

**Part B:**
- Error 1 (split infinitive): "to eventually, hopefully, finish" is clunky with two adverbs wedged in. Fix: "My goal is to finish the mural before spring." (or "My goal is to finally finish the mural before spring.")
- Error 2 (infinitive/prepositional confusion): "I walked to quickly reach the art room" — "to quickly reach" is an infinitive phrase, but "walked to quickly reach" is awkward. The intended meaning is probably "I walked quickly to reach the art room." (The student seems to have confused the infinitive structure with the prepositional phrase pattern.)

**Part C — Sample Answers:**
1. (Noun/Subject) To compose an original song from scratch was something she had dreamed about since middle school.
2. (Adjective) The only tool to solve this kind of equation is a graphing calculator.
3. (Adverb) He set three alarms to make sure he would be on time for the early bus.

### Interleaved Review
1. Dangling participle — "Scrolling through her feed" should modify a person, not "the notification." Fix: "Scrolling through her feed, she noticed the notification at the top of the screen."
2. Compound-complex sentence — it contains two independent clauses ("she still felt nervous" and "she asked her tutor for one more session" joined by "and") and one dependent clause ("Although she had studied for two weeks").

### Exit Ticket
1. "to improve your writing" — adjective phrase (modifies "way"); "to read widely and often" — noun/predicate nominative (renames "way")
2. "to the library" is a prepositional phrase (to + noun phrase). "to return three overdue books" is an infinitive phrase functioning as an adverb (modifies the verb "went" — explains why).
3. Any correctly formed sentence with an infinitive phrase that tells why/for what purpose, modifying a verb or adjective, e.g., "She practiced her speech every night to build her confidence before the tournament."
