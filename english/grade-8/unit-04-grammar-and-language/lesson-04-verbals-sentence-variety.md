# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 4

## Topic
Using Verbals for Sentence Variety

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Combine simple or choppy sentences using gerund, participial, and infinitive phrases to create varied, fluent sentences
- Select the most effective verbal type for a given rhetorical purpose (adding description, showing cause, expressing purpose)
- Revise a short passage to incorporate at least two types of verbal phrases, improving style without changing meaning

## Prerequisites
- Gerunds and their four functions (Lesson 1, L.8.1a)
- Participial phrases, placement, and punctuation (Lesson 2, L.8.1a)
- Infinitives and their three functions (Lesson 3, L.8.1a)
- Sentence variety techniques from Grade 8 Unit 1 (varying sentence openings, combining sentences)

## Materials Needed
- Student notebook or grammar journal
- Lesson 4 handout (or projected exercises)
- Verbal reference anchor chart from Lessons 1–3
- Optional: a recent student writing draft for the revision activity

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Highlight the stylistic difference between flat, repetitive sentences and sentences enriched by verbal phrases.

**Activity: Flat vs. Fluent**

Display the following passage:
> "Marcus loves basketball. He practices every day. He wants to improve his ball-handling. He also works on his defense. The work is hard. It pays off during games."

Say: "This passage communicates information — but how does it feel to read it? Let's count the sentence structures." (Six consecutive simple sentences. Same pattern every time. Flat.)

Now display this revised version:
> "Loving basketball more than almost anything, Marcus practices every day. To improve his ball-handling and sharpen his defense, he pushes through tough workouts. The hard work, though demanding, always pays off during games."

Ask: "What changed? The information is almost identical. But the second version uses verbal phrases to combine ideas and open sentences differently. That's what we're exploring today."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach three sentence-combining moves using verbals; introduce the "Verbal Choice" framework for selecting the right verbal type for a given effect.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sentence combining:** Merging two or more short or related sentences into one more complex, fluent sentence.
- **Verbal phrase:** A phrase built around a gerund, participle, or infinitive that can be embedded into a sentence to add information efficiently.
- **Verbal Choice:** A strategy for deciding which verbal type best achieves the intended effect in a sentence revision.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Each verbal type has a slightly different strength. Knowing which one to use — and when — is a writing skill, not just a grammar skill."

Write the **Verbal Choice Framework** on the board:

| Verbal Type | Best for... | Typical Position |
|-------------|-------------|-----------------|
| Gerund phrase | Naming an action as a topic, subject, or object | Subject, after prepositions, as complements |
| Participial phrase | Adding descriptive detail about a noun; showing simultaneous or prior action | Opening or near the modified noun |
| Infinitive phrase | Showing purpose ("in order to"); expressing goals or plans | End of sentence, as noun subject/object |

**Move 1: Combine two sentences using a participial phrase (adds description or shows simultaneous action)**

Before: "She crossed the finish line. She raised both arms in triumph."
After: *Crossing the finish line, she raised both arms in triumph.*
OR: *She crossed the finish line, raising both arms in triumph.*

Say: "The participial phrase shows both actions happening together and adds energy. Notice the comma placement changes depending on whether the phrase opens or closes the sentence."

**Move 2: Combine using an infinitive phrase (shows purpose or goal)**

Before: "He studied the weather patterns. He wanted to predict the storm's path."
After: *He studied the weather patterns to predict the storm's path.*

Say: "The infinitive phrase at the end of the sentence now explains *why* — the purpose of the action. This is more efficient than two separate sentences."

**Move 3: Combine using a gerund phrase (names an action as the topic)**

Before: "She rehearses her speech every morning. This has made her more confident."
After: *Rehearsing her speech every morning has made her more confident.*

Say: "The gerund phrase now acts as the subject — it names the activity that caused the result. This structure is powerful in explanatory writing."

**Choosing the right verbal — the test:**
- Do I want to describe a noun? → Participial phrase
- Do I want to name an activity as the topic? → Gerund phrase
- Do I want to show purpose or a goal? → Infinitive phrase

Show a revision sequence:

Original: "The app crashed. The students lost their data. They had spent two hours on the project."

Option A (participial): *Crashing without warning, the app caused the students to lose the data from their two-hour project.*
Option B (infinitive): *The students needed to recover their data after the app crashed mid-project.*
Option C (gerund): *Recovering lost data became the main task after the app crashed without warning.*

Discuss: Which version emphasizes the loss most? Which emphasizes the recovery? The verbal choice shapes the focus.

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice combining sentences using each verbal type; begin selecting the most effective verbal for a given purpose.

**Part A — Sentence Combining (combine each pair into one sentence using the verbal type indicated):**

1. [Participial] Tomás recorded the experiment. He narrated each step for the class.
2. [Infinitive] She memorized the periodic table. She wanted to be ready for the chemistry exam.
3. [Gerund] They collaborated on the mural. This brought the whole class together.
4. [Participial] The storm knocked out the power. The storm left the neighborhood dark for hours.
5. [Infinitive] The band rehearsed three times a week. They hoped to perform at the city talent showcase.

**Part B — Verbal Choice:** Two combinations are offered for each original sentence. Discuss: which version is more effective, and why?

Original: "Jaylen practices free throws. He does this to build consistency."
- Version A: *Practicing free throws builds consistency for Jaylen.*
- Version B: *Jaylen practices free throws to build consistency.*

Original: "She opened the message. She felt her heart race."
- Version A: *Opening the message, she felt her heart race.*
- Version B: *To open the message made her heart race.*

**Facilitator support:** In Part B, there isn't always one "right" answer — guide the student to articulate *why* one version feels more natural or effective. In the first pair, Version B is more direct (he is the actor). In the second pair, Version A is more natural (gerund as subject is less common and slightly awkward in this context).

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Independently revise a flat passage and write original sentences using deliberate verbal choices.

**Part A — Passage Revision:**

The following passage is flat and repetitive in structure. Rewrite it using at least three verbal phrases (one gerund, one participial, and one infinitive). Do not change the meaning.

> "Priya signed up for robotics club. She wanted to learn how to code. She worked hard every week. She built a robot with her team. They had to finish their robot by Friday. They were preparing for the regional competition. They were nervous. They also felt excited."

**Part B — Sentence Combining:**

Combine each group of sentences into one fluent sentence. Use the verbal type you think is most effective. Label the verbal phrase you used.

1. The researcher analyzed the data. She looked for patterns in ocean temperatures. She found three significant trends.
2. Omar plays guitar every day. He is becoming more skilled. His friends love to listen to him.
3. The power went out. The students could not finish the presentation. They had worked on it for a week.

**Part C — Sentence Writing:**

Write two original sentences about any of the following topics: music, technology, friendship, sports, or science. Each sentence must include at least one verbal phrase. Label each verbal phrase with its type (G, PP, or INF) and its grammatical function.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Parts A (use any 3 verbals) and B; write 1 of the 2 sentences in Part C.
**Stretch:** Complete all parts; in Part A, label every verbal phrase you added with its type and function.
**Challenge:** Complete all parts; then write a second version of your Part A revision that uses different verbal choices. Compare the two versions: which do you prefer and why? Write 2–3 sentences of analysis.

### Interleaved Review
1. *Lesson 1 review — Gerund function:* Is "running" a gerund, participle, or part of a verb phrase in this sentence? "Running the school's new environmental club is more work than she expected." Explain.
2. *Grade 7/Grade 8 Unit 1 — Sentence types:* Identify the type (simple, compound, complex, or compound-complex): "After the team won the semifinal, they celebrated at the diner, and the coach bought everyone milkshakes."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Combine these two sentences into one using a participial phrase: "The photographer adjusted the lens. She captured the perfect shot of the eclipse."
2. Which verbal type would best combine these sentences, and why? "He needed extra time. He wanted to finish the essay carefully."
3. Write one original sentence that opens with any verbal phrase.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Sample: "Adjusting the lens, the photographer captured the perfect shot of the eclipse." (Other placements acceptable if correctly punctuated and the modified noun is clear.)
2. Infinitive phrase — "He needed extra time to finish the essay carefully." Rationale: the infinitive shows purpose (why he needed the time).
3. Any correctly formed sentence with an opening verbal phrase, correctly punctuated.

**Mastery = 2 out of 3 correct, including item 3 (production)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to the Verbal Choice Framework chart. Use 3–4 additional sentence pairs and ask the student to simply label "which verbal type would work here?" before attempting the combining. Build up slowly.

### If Mastery Is Met
Invite the student to apply this lesson's skills to a paragraph from a current writing draft. Ask them to find one place to combine two sentences using a verbal phrase, revise it, and bring the before/after to the next lesson.

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. Take a paragraph from a previous writing assignment and revise it to include at least one of each verbal type (gerund, participial, infinitive phrase). Label each addition and note what it replaced.
2. Find a short passage from a published author (novel, essay, or article) that uses verbal phrases effectively. Copy 3–4 sentences and label every verbal phrase. Write 2–3 sentences analyzing what effect the verbal phrases create.
3. Write a short sports game recap (6–8 sentences) using at least four verbal phrases. Try to vary the placement: open some sentences with participial phrases, use gerunds as subjects, and use infinitives to show purpose.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is the synthesis of the verbal trilogy (Lessons 1–3). Students should be feeling confident with identification; the new challenge here is production under stylistic judgment. Celebrate revision — it is a sophisticated skill.
- Part A of independent practice (passage revision) is the anchor task. Allow sufficient time. Some students will revise only mechanically (inserting a gerund at random). Ask them to read aloud both versions to see if the revised one sounds better — reading aloud is the best fluency test.
- In Part B of guided practice, the "Verbal Choice" discussions are deliberately open-ended. There is often more than one defensible answer. The goal is articulation: can the student explain *why* a choice feels more effective? That metacognitive step is the real learning.
- Watch for students who place participial phrases at the end of a sentence without a comma when the phrase is nonessential — this is the most common punctuation error in sentence combining.
- If a student has a recent writing draft, the extension activity is highly valuable. Real revision cements the connection between grammar and writing quality more effectively than any exercise.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A — Sample Revision:**
> Signing up for robotics club, Priya was eager to learn how to code. She worked hard every week, building a robot alongside her team. To finish the robot before Friday, they pushed through long afternoon sessions, preparing for the regional competition. Nervous but excited, they waited for the day to arrive.

(Accept any revision that uses one gerund phrase, one participial phrase, and one infinitive phrase, correctly punctuated, without changing meaning.)

**Part B — Sample Answers:**
1. "Analyzing the data for patterns in ocean temperatures, the researcher found three significant trends." (Participial phrase) — OR — "The researcher analyzed the data to find patterns in ocean temperatures, ultimately identifying three significant trends." (Infinitive phrase)
2. "Playing guitar every day, Omar has become increasingly skilled, and his friends love to listen to him." (Participial phrase) — OR — "Omar's daily guitar playing has made him noticeably more skilled, much to his friends' delight." (Gerund phrase as subject)
3. "Having worked on the presentation for a week, the students were unable to finish it when the power went out." (Participial phrase)

**Part C — Sample Answers:**
1. *Playing her new EP for the first time on a real stage* [G — subject], she felt the nervous energy transform into excitement.
2. She stayed up past midnight *to polish the code* [INF — adverb: why?], determined to make the app work before morning.

### Interleaved Review
1. "Running" is a **gerund** — it functions as the subject of the sentence. The Verbal ID Test: "It is more work than she expected" — yes. Running = noun/gerund.
2. Compound-complex sentence — one dependent clause ("After the team won the semifinal") + two independent clauses ("they celebrated at the diner" and "the coach bought everyone milkshakes").

### Exit Ticket
1. "Adjusting the lens, the photographer captured the perfect shot of the eclipse." — participial phrase at opening, correctly punctuated.
2. Infinitive phrase: "He needed extra time to finish the essay carefully." The infinitive phrase expresses purpose (why he needed the time).
3. Any correctly formed sentence with an opening verbal phrase, correctly punctuated.
