# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 1

## Topic
Understanding Verbals: Gerunds

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify gerunds and gerund phrases in sentences and explain their grammatical function (subject, direct object, object of a preposition, or predicate nominative)
- Distinguish gerunds from present participles using the "Verbal ID Test"
- Write original sentences that use gerunds in at least three different grammatical roles

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of nouns and their functions (subject, object, complement) from Grade 7
- Familiarity with verb phrases and present participles as introduced in Grade 7 (L.7.1)
- Ability to identify prepositional phrases from Grade 7 grammar work
- Basic understanding of phrases vs. clauses from Grade 7 (L.7.1a)

## Materials Needed
- Student notebook or grammar journal
- Lesson 1 handout (or projected sentences)
- Two colors of highlighter or pencil
- Anchor chart space or whiteboard for the "Verbal ID Test" strategy

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of verbs and nouns; surface the idea that a single word can serve different grammatical roles.

**Activity: Noun or Verb?**

Write the following sentences on the board. Ask the student to read each pair and identify whether the underlined word functions as a noun or a verb.

1. The coach decided to *run* a new play in the second half. (verb)
2. *Running* every morning has improved her stamina significantly. (?)
3. She was *running* toward the finish line when the crowd went wild. (verb — part of verb phrase)
4. His favorite hobby is *running*. (?)

Say: "Two of these sentences have the word 'running' — but it's doing a different job each time. Today we're going to figure out exactly what's happening in sentences like number 2 and number 4, and why it matters for your writing."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce gerunds, gerund phrases, their four grammatical functions, and the Verbal ID Test for distinguishing gerunds from present participles.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Gerund:** A verb form ending in -ing that functions as a noun in a sentence.
- **Gerund phrase:** A gerund plus any modifiers, objects, or complements — the whole phrase acts as a noun.
- **Verbal:** A verb form (gerund, participle, or infinitive) that functions as a different part of speech.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "We've already worked with verbs as action words — things subjects do. Today we're meeting a verb that has gone undercover as a noun. It's called a gerund."

Write on the board: **GERUND = verb + -ing, working as a NOUN**

Say: "The word 'swimming' is a verb — it describes an action. But watch what happens when I put it in this sentence: *Swimming is my favorite sport.* What is the subject of that sentence? 'Swimming.' A noun does the job of a subject. That's a gerund."

**The Four Functions of Gerunds — write these on the board:**
1. **Subject:** Swimming is great exercise.
2. **Direct Object:** She loves practicing her guitar.
3. **Object of a Preposition:** He got better at listening.
4. **Predicate Nominative:** Her biggest challenge is staying focused.

Say: "The Verbal ID Test helps you figure out whether an -ing word is a gerund or a present participle."

Write the **Verbal ID Test** on an anchor chart:
> **Verbal ID Test:** Can you replace the -ing word (or phrase) with a pronoun like *it* or *this* and have the sentence still make sense?
> - YES → It's probably a **gerund** (acting as a noun).
> - NO → It's probably a **participle** (acting as an adjective) or part of a **verb phrase**.

Model the test:
- *Swimming is great exercise.* → Can I say "It is great exercise"? Yes. Gerund.
- *The swimming fish escaped the net.* → Can I say "The it fish escaped"? No. Participle.
- *She is swimming across the bay.* → Part of a verb phrase — not a verbal.

### Worked Examples

| Sentence | -ing Word | Replace Test | Function | Gerund? |
|----------|-----------|--------------|----------|---------|
| Coding requires patience and focus. | Coding | "It requires patience" — Yes | Subject | Yes |
| The team celebrated by winning the championship. | winning | "by it" — awkward but yes | Object of preposition | Yes |
| The winning team celebrated loudly. | winning | "The it team" — No | Adjective (participle) | No |
| Maya's goal is finishing the novel by June. | finishing | "Her goal is it" — Yes | Predicate nominative | Yes |

Say: "Notice that gerund phrases include the gerund plus everything that goes with it. In 'finishing the novel by June,' the whole phrase — finishing the novel by June — acts as the predicate nominative."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply the Verbal ID Test collaboratively; identify gerunds and gerund phrases and name their function.

Work through the following sentences together. For each, ask the student to:
(a) Underline the gerund or gerund phrase.
(b) Apply the Verbal ID Test.
(c) Name the function (subject, direct object, object of preposition, or predicate nominative).

1. Streaming music all day drained his phone battery by noon.
2. The athlete thanked her coach for believing in her.
3. His least favorite task was memorizing all the vocabulary terms.
4. After finishing the lab report, she finally allowed herself to relax.
5. The growling engine startled the students in the parking lot.
6. They improved their project by adding more specific evidence.

**Facilitator support:** For sentence 5, students may identify "growling" as a gerund. Walk through the Verbal ID Test: "The it engine startled..." — no, that doesn't work. "Growling" is a participle modifying "engine."

**Discussion prompt:** Ask: "In sentence 4, how do you know 'finishing the lab report' is a gerund phrase rather than just the word 'finishing'?" (Because the gerund has a direct object — "the lab report" — and a modifier; the whole phrase acts as one noun.)

---

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Independently identify gerunds, distinguish them from participles, and produce original sentences using gerunds in varied roles.

**Part A — Identification (label each underlined element as G = Gerund, P = Participle, or VP = Verb Phrase):**

1. *Practicing free throws* every day turned Marcus into the team's most reliable shooter.
2. The *humming* refrigerator kept him awake for hours.
3. She is *finishing* her science fair poster right now.
4. One downside of *staying up late* is feeling exhausted the next morning.
5. His specialty is *analyzing data from environmental sensors*.

**Part B — Error Correction:**

A student wrote the following sentences. Some use gerunds correctly; one contains a common confusion. Find and fix the error.

> "Running everyday keeps me in shape. I also enjoy to swim in the mornings. My coach says that lifting weights would help my speed."

**Part C — Sentence Writing:**

Write four original sentences:
1. A sentence where a gerund phrase is the **subject**.
2. A sentence where a gerund phrase is the **direct object**.
3. A sentence where a gerund phrase follows a **preposition**.
4. A sentence where a gerund phrase is the **predicate nominative**.

Use these contexts if you need inspiration: music, technology, sports, science, school life.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Parts A and B fully; write at least 2 of the 4 sentences in Part C.
**Stretch:** Complete all parts; in Part C, write gerund phrases of at least 5 words each.
**Challenge:** Complete all parts; then write a paragraph of 4–5 sentences on a topic of your choice in which you deliberately use at least three gerund phrases in different grammatical roles. Label each one.

### Interleaved Review
1. *Grade 7 review — Phrases and Clauses:* Identify whether the following is a phrase or a clause, and name its type: "because the final score was tied after overtime."
2. *Grade 8 Unit 1 review — Sentence Fluency:* Combine these two sentences into one, using any strategy you've learned: "She studied for three hours. She felt prepared for the exam."

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Underline the gerund or gerund phrase in this sentence and name its function: "Her new hobby is building custom mechanical keyboards."
2. Is "barking" a gerund or a participle in this sentence? "The barking dog woke the entire neighborhood." Explain using the Verbal ID Test.
3. Write one sentence using a gerund phrase as the direct object.

**Expected Answers:**
1. "building custom mechanical keyboards" — predicate nominative (subject is "hobby"; "building custom mechanical keyboards" renames it)
2. Participle. "The *it* dog woke the neighborhood" doesn't make sense; "barking" modifies "dog" as an adjective.
3. Acceptable: any sentence where an -ing phrase follows a transitive verb and answers "what?" (e.g., "He enjoys playing video games with his friends.")

**Mastery = 2 out of 3 correct, including item 3 (production)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Begin the next session with a 5-minute reteach focused on the Verbal ID Test using 2–3 new examples. Have the student sort six -ing words into gerund/participle/verb-phrase categories before proceeding.

### If Mastery Is Met
Invite the student to find one gerund and one participle in a current independent reading book and note the page. Share findings at the start of Lesson 2.

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. Find three gerund phrases in a news article or nonfiction book. Copy each sentence, underline the gerund phrase, and label its function. Bring your examples to the next lesson.
2. Rewrite a paragraph from a recent writing assignment, deliberately adding at least two gerund phrases in different grammatical roles.
3. Create a reference card (half-page or index card) titled "Gerunds at a Glance" that includes the definition, the four functions with one example each, and the Verbal ID Test. Keep it in your grammar journal.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students frequently confuse gerunds with present participles because both end in -ing. Reinforce the Verbal ID Test repeatedly — it works reliably and gives students an independent check strategy.
- The most commonly missed function is "object of a preposition." Make sure students see several preposition + gerund phrase examples (by running, after finishing, without practicing) so the pattern becomes familiar.
- In the guided practice, sentence 5 ("The growling engine") is a deliberate distractor. Pause on it and be explicit: "Not every -ing word is a gerund. The test tells us the answer."
- During Part C of independent practice, circulate and check that students' gerund phrases are genuinely acting as the stated function, not just containing -ing words. A common error: "I enjoy running fast" is a gerund (direct object), but "I enjoy to run fast" is not — address this if it appears.
- Save two or three strong student-generated sentences from Part C for the anchor chart or a future lesson opener. Seeing peers' examples reinforces that gerunds appear in natural, interesting sentences.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A:**
1. *Practicing free throws* — G (subject)
2. *humming* — P (adjective modifying "refrigerator")
3. *finishing* — VP (part of present progressive verb phrase "is finishing")
4. *staying up late* — G (object of preposition "of")
5. *analyzing data from environmental sensors* — G (predicate nominative)

**Part B:**
Error: "I also enjoy to swim in the mornings." — Should be "I also enjoy *swimming* in the mornings." (A gerund, not an infinitive, follows the verb "enjoy." Note: infinitives are grammatically possible after some verbs but not "enjoy.")

**Part C — Sample Answers:**
1. (Subject) *Debugging code for hours* is the part of programming that tests your patience the most.
2. (Direct Object) She finally finished *recording the last track for her EP*.
3. (Object of Preposition) He got much better at *reading the defense before the snap*.
4. (Predicate Nominative) The hardest part of the semester was *keeping up with all the reading assignments*.

### Interleaved Review
1. "because the final score was tied after overtime" — This is a **dependent (subordinate) clause**. It has a subject ("the final score") and a verb ("was tied") but cannot stand alone; it begins with the subordinating conjunction "because."
2. Sample combined sentence: "After studying for three hours, she felt prepared for the exam." (Or: "She studied for three hours and felt prepared for the exam.")

### Exit Ticket
1. "building custom mechanical keyboards" — predicate nominative
2. "barking" is a participle; it modifies "dog" (adjective). The Verbal ID Test fails: "The it dog" makes no sense.
3. Any sentence with a gerund phrase correctly functioning as a direct object receives full credit.
