# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 7

## Topic
Verb Moods: Indicative and Imperative

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define verb mood and explain the difference between indicative and imperative moods
- Identify the mood of verbs in sentences and explain the function each mood serves
- Write original sentences in both indicative and imperative mood for varied communicative purposes

## Prerequisites
- Solid understanding of verbs (action, linking, helping) from Grade 7 (L.7.1)
- Active and passive voice (Lessons 5–6) — mood is a separate dimension from voice
- Understanding of sentence types (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory) from Grade 7
- Ability to identify subject and predicate in simple and complex sentences (Grade 7)

## Materials Needed
- Student notebook or grammar journal
- Lesson 7 handout (or projected sentences)
- "Mood Detector" reference chart (created during instruction)
- Whiteboard or chart paper for the Mood Detector strategy

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate students' intuitive sense of the difference between stating facts and giving commands; introduce the concept of mood as the speaker's attitude toward reality.

**Activity: What Is the Speaker Doing?**

Display these four sentences. Ask the student to describe in one word what the speaker is doing with each sentence (not the topic — the communicative act):

1. *The robotics team won first place at the regional competition.*
2. *Charge the battery before the competition begins.*
3. *The school's new policy takes effect on Monday.*
4. *Submit your permission slip by Friday.*

Say: "Sentences 1 and 3 state facts about the world — they're making assertions. Sentences 2 and 4 issue instructions — they're directing someone to do something. Grammar has a name for this distinction: **verb mood.** Mood signals the speaker's or writer's relationship to reality — are they reporting what is, asking what could be, or commanding what should be done? Today we'll look at two of those moods closely."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define verb mood; present indicative and imperative moods with formation and examples; introduce the Mood Detector strategy.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Verb mood:** A grammatical category that expresses the speaker's or writer's attitude toward the reality or likelihood of what is being said.
- **Indicative mood:** Used to make statements of fact, opinion, or belief, and to ask questions. This is the most common mood in English.
- **Imperative mood:** Used to give commands, directions, instructions, or requests. The subject is typically "you" (often unstated).

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Mood is a feature of verbs. Think of it as the 'mode' a sentence is running in: reporting mode, commanding mode, questioning mode, hypothetical mode. Today we're focusing on the two most common moods: indicative and imperative."

**Indicative Mood:**

Say: "The indicative mood is the default — it's the mood you use most of the time. It reports facts, states opinions, and asks questions."

Write on the board:
- Statements of fact: *The average ocean temperature has risen 0.9 degrees Celsius since 1900.*
- Opinion/belief: *Listening to music while studying helps some people concentrate.*
- Questions: *How many students participated in the science fair this year?*

Say: "Indicative mood can be in any tense (past, present, future, perfect) and either active or passive voice. It's simply the mode of 'this is what is or was or will be real.'"

**Imperative Mood:**

Say: "The imperative mood gives commands, directions, or requests. The subject is almost always 'you' — but 'you' is typically not written or spoken. It's the 'understood you.'"

Write on the board:
- Command: *Close your laptop before the presentation begins.*
- Instruction: *Double-click the icon and enter your password.*
- Request (polite): *Please bring your permission slip by Monday morning.*
- Instruction set: *Add the salt, stir for two minutes, then reduce the heat.*

Say: "Notice: the subject 'you' is absent from all these sentences — it's understood. The verb appears in its base form (first-person present): close, double-click, bring, add, stir, reduce."

**An important note on tone:** Say: "Imperative mood does not equal harsh or rude. 'Please take your time' is as much an imperative as 'Stop talking.' The mood is determined by the grammatical structure — a missing 'you' subject and a base form verb — not by the emotional tone."

**The Mood Detector Strategy:**

Write on the board:
> **Mood Detector — ask these questions:**
> 1. Is the sentence stating a fact, opinion, or belief? → Indicative
> 2. Is the sentence asking a question about facts or reality? → Indicative (interrogative form)
> 3. Is the sentence giving a command, instruction, or request — with no stated subject? → Imperative
> 4. Does the verb appear in its base form with an understood "you" subject? → Imperative
> *(Other moods — conditional, subjunctive — appear in Lesson 8.)*

### Worked Examples

| Sentence | Mood | Evidence |
|----------|------|---------|
| The school district updated its technology policy last month. | Indicative | Statement of fact; subject + past-tense verb |
| Turn off your notifications during class. | Imperative | Command; base form verb; understood "you" subject |
| Does the new policy apply to school-issued devices? | Indicative | Question about reality; subject-verb inversion |
| Always back up your files before updating the software. | Imperative | Instruction; base form verbs "back up"; understood "you" |
| She believes the project will be finished on time. | Indicative | Statement of belief/opinion; subject + verb |
| Please notify a teacher if you feel unwell during the day. | Imperative | Polite request; base form "notify"; understood "you" |

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply the Mood Detector to identify indicative and imperative moods; analyze how imperative sentences can have different tones; begin writing sentences in each mood.

**Part A — Mood Detector:** Apply the Mood Detector and label each sentence IND (Indicative) or IMP (Imperative).

1. She submitted her application three days before the deadline.
2. Read the passage carefully before answering the questions.
3. The science fair project must include an original hypothesis.
4. Label each diagram with the correct scientific terminology.
5. Why did the team's performance drop in the second half?
6. The school band will perform at the district showcase next spring.
7. Never share your password with anyone, even your friends.
8. Many students find the school's new lunch schedule confusing.

**Part B — Tone Shift:** Rewrite each imperative sentence with a different level of formality or politeness. Keep the imperative mood but shift the tone.

1. Original: "Sit down." → Make it polite and appropriate for a formal setting.
2. Original: "Please review the attached document at your earliest convenience." → Make it more casual and direct.
3. Original: "Stop running in the hallway." → Make it a firm but constructive classroom instruction.

**Part C — Writing Practice:** Write one indicative sentence and one imperative sentence for each of the following situations:

1. A coach giving instructions before a game
2. A student explaining why the experiment failed

**Facilitator support:** In Part A, items 4 and 7 may generate discussion. Item 4 ("Label each diagram") — confirm that this is imperative: base form "label," understood "you." Item 7 ("Never share your password") — imperative despite the adverb "never"; "share" is the base form verb with understood "you."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Independently identify mood, correct mood errors, and produce sentences in both moods for distinct purposes.

**Part A — Identification:** Label each sentence IND (Indicative) or IMP (Imperative).

1. Every student should have completed the pre-reading by today.
2. Compare your answers with a partner before submitting.
3. The documentary explores the impact of social media on teen mental health.
4. Rinse the equipment thoroughly and allow it to dry overnight.
5. Did the team qualify for the state competition this year?
6. Write your name and date in the upper right corner of the page.
7. The final chapter of the novel ties together all the major themes.
8. Reach out to your teacher if you have questions about the assignment.

**Part B — Error Correction:**

A student wrote these sentences with mood errors (confusing indicative and imperative structures). Identify the problem and rewrite each sentence correctly.

1. "The coach told us to leaves the equipment in the locker room." (verb form error)
2. "You should to turn in your draft before class on Thursday." (extra word creating confusion)
3. "She remembered to brought her permission slip." (verb form error in embedded instruction)

**Part C — Sentence Writing:**

Write a set of five sentences for ONE of the following scenarios. Your set must include at least two indicative sentences and at least two imperative sentences. One sentence should be a question in indicative mood.

- **Scenario A:** A student is writing instructions for a younger student on how to navigate the school's online learning portal.
- **Scenario B:** A team captain is addressing teammates before a big game, mixing motivational statements with specific directions.
- **Scenario C:** A science teacher is opening a lab by explaining what students will observe and what they need to do.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Parts A and B; write the Part C set with 2 indicative and 2 imperative sentences (the question is optional).
**Stretch:** Complete all parts; in Part C, include all three required sentence types and vary the tone of the imperative sentences (at least one polite, one direct).
**Challenge:** Complete all parts; then write a second scenario set from Part C (choose a different scenario) and analyze: how does the ratio of indicative to imperative sentences change the overall feel of the writing? Write 2–3 sentences of reflection.

### Interleaved Review
1. *Lesson 5/6 review — Voice:* Rewrite this sentence in passive voice and explain whether active or passive is more effective here: "Hundreds of volunteers built the community garden in under three weeks."
2. *Grade 7 — Adjective/adverb clauses:* Identify the subordinate clause and label it adjective (ADJ) or adverb (ADV): "The student who won the poetry competition has been writing since she was nine years old."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Identify the mood of this sentence and explain how you know: "Keep your essay focused on one central argument throughout."
2. Identify the mood of this sentence and explain how you know: "The essay draft was submitted two days before the deadline."
3. Write one imperative sentence and one indicative sentence about the same topic (your choice).

**Expected Answers:**
1. Imperative — base form verb "keep"; no stated subject (understood "you"); giving a direction/instruction.
2. Indicative — statement of fact; subject ("The essay draft") performs or receives a real action in the past.
3. Any pair of correctly formed sentences in the two moods, on the same topic.

**Mastery = 2 out of 3 correct, including item 3 (production)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Use the Mood Detector with 4–5 new sentences at the start of the next session. Focus especially on helping the student identify the "understood you" in imperative sentences — this is the key structural marker that students miss.

### If Mastery Is Met
Preview Lesson 8: ask the student to write one sentence expressing a wish (something they wish were true right now) and one sentence expressing a condition ("If _____, then _____"). Bring these to Lesson 8 as a starting point.

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. Find an instruction manual, recipe, or tutorial (online or in print). List five imperative sentences and five indicative sentences from the text. What is the ratio of imperative to indicative? Why do you think the writer uses each type?
2. Rewrite a set of school rules or lab safety instructions using only indicative sentences instead of imperative. Compare the two versions: which feels more authoritative? Which is clearer?
3. Write a persuasive speech opening (5–6 sentences) that uses indicative mood to state facts and build credibility, then transitions to imperative mood to issue a call to action. Label each mood.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students are often surprised that "mood" refers to grammar, not emotion. Clarify early: mood is the grammatical mode of the verb — how it relates to reality — not the emotional feeling of the sentence.
- The "understood you" in imperative sentences is the most important structural concept in this lesson. Students who struggle to identify imperative sentences often miss this feature. Try: "If you added 'you' before the verb and the sentence still worked as a command, it's likely imperative."
- Avoid conflating declarative sentence type (a category of sentence purpose/form) with indicative mood (a grammatical feature of the verb). Many declarative sentences use indicative mood, but they are not the same thing. If the student asks, acknowledge the distinction without over-complicating it at this stage.
- In Part B of guided practice (tone shift), the goal is creative engagement with the imperative form — students should see that the same grammatical structure can be formal, casual, polite, or curt depending on word choice.
- Lessons 7 and 8 work as a pair. If time is tight, the transition into conditional and subjunctive mood (Lesson 8) is the heavier cognitive load — protect time for that lesson.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A:**
1. IND — statement; subject + verb ("should have completed")
2. IMP — instruction; base form "compare"; understood "you"
3. IND — statement of fact; subject + verb ("explores")
4. IMP — instruction; base forms "rinse," "allow"; understood "you"
5. IND — question about reality; subject-verb inversion ("Did the team qualify")
6. IMP — instruction; base form "write"; understood "you"
7. IND — statement of fact/analysis; subject + verb ("ties")
8. IMP — polite request; base form "reach out"; understood "you"

**Part B:**
1. Error: "leaves" should be base form. Fix: "The coach told us to leave the equipment in the locker room." (Note: this is actually an infinitive in a reported speech construction — "to leave" — not technically a direct imperative. Accept either reading.)
2. Error: "should to" — "should" is a modal and does not take "to" before a base verb. Fix: "You should turn in your draft before class on Thursday." (Indicative, not imperative — this is fine.)
3. Error: "to brought" — should be base form after "to." Fix: "She remembered to bring her permission slip." (Infinitive construction, not imperative — correcting the verb form is the goal.)

**Part C — Sample (Scenario B — team captain):**
1. (IMP) "Keep your energy focused from the first whistle." [imperative, direct]
2. (IND) "We've practiced this formation for three weeks, and I know we're ready." [indicative, statement of fact/opinion]
3. (IND) "The other team's defense is strong on the left side, so our approach needs to adjust." [indicative]
4. (IMP) "Look for the open player before committing to the shot." [imperative]
5. (IND/question) "Does everyone know the signal for the defensive switch?" [indicative, question]

### Interleaved Review
1. Passive: "The community garden was built by hundreds of volunteers in under three weeks." Active voice is more effective here — the volunteers' achievement is the story; making them the subject honors their effort. Passive moves them to the background unnecessarily.
2. "who won the poetry competition" — ADJ (adjective/relative) clause modifying "student."

### Exit Ticket
1. Imperative — the verb "keep" is in base form; there is no stated subject (understood "you"); the sentence is giving a directive/instruction.
2. Indicative — states a fact about what happened ("was submitted" — note: passive voice but still indicative mood); subject performs/receives a real past action.
3. Any pair of correctly formed sentences in the two moods on the same topic. Sample: IMP: "Start every practice with ten minutes of cardio." IND: "Regular cardio training improves endurance and reaction time over time."
