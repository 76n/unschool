# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 2

## Topic
Understanding Verbals: Participles

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify present and past participial phrases in sentences and explain their adjectival function
- Apply correct placement and punctuation rules for participial phrases (commas with nonrestrictive phrases)
- Recognize and revise dangling and misplaced participial phrases

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of gerunds and the Verbal ID Test from Lesson 1 (L.8.1a)
- Familiarity with adjectives and adjective phrases from Grade 7 (L.7.1)
- Introduction to dangling and misplaced modifiers from Grade 7 (L.7.1c)
- Understanding of restrictive vs. nonrestrictive clauses (Grade 7, L.7.2)

## Materials Needed
- Student notebook or grammar journal
- Lesson 2 handout (or projected sentences)
- Two colors of highlighter (one for participial phrase, one for modified noun)
- Whiteboard or chart paper for modeling phrase placement

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect yesterday's learning (gerunds) to today's (participles); use the Verbal ID Test to surface the distinction.

**Activity: Same Word, Different Job**

Display these two sentences:
1. *Exhausted by the long practice, the team sat silently on the bench.*
2. *Exhausting the bench's depth was the coach's biggest concern.*

Say: "Both sentences use a form of the word 'exhaust.' In sentence 1, 'exhausted' describes the team — it's working like an adjective. In sentence 2, 'exhausting' is the subject of the sentence — it's working like a noun. Yesterday we called that a gerund. Today we're studying the adjective type: the participle."

Quick review: Ask the student to apply the Verbal ID Test to sentence 2 ("It was the coach's biggest concern" — yes, gerund confirmed). Then show that the test fails for sentence 1 ("The it team sat silently" — no, so not a gerund).

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce present and past participles as adjectives, participial phrases, punctuation rules, and the dangers of dangling/misplaced participles.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Participle:** A verb form used as an adjective to modify a noun or pronoun. Present participles end in -ing; past participles typically end in -ed, -en, -d, -t, or -n.
- **Participial phrase:** A participle plus its modifiers, objects, or complements — the whole phrase modifies a noun or pronoun.
- **Dangling participle:** A participial phrase that does not logically modify the noun closest to it (often because the intended noun is absent from the sentence).
- **Misplaced participial phrase:** A participial phrase positioned too far from the noun it modifies, creating ambiguity or unintended meaning.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Participles are verb forms that moonlight as adjectives. A present participle ends in -ing; a past participle usually ends in -ed or an irregular form: broken, stolen, frozen, worn."

Write on the board:
- Present participle: *Struggling* to stay awake, Darnell splashed cold water on his face.
- Past participle: *Exhausted* after the tournament, the players collapsed onto the field.
- Past participle (irregular): *Broken* by the fall, the screen was impossible to read.

Say: "In each sentence, the participial phrase modifies a noun — it tells us more about 'Darnell,' 'the players,' and 'the screen.' Notice that when the participial phrase comes at the beginning of a sentence, we use a comma after it."

**Punctuation Rule — write on board:**
> - Participial phrase at the **beginning**: always use a comma.
> - Participial phrase at the **end**: use a comma if the phrase adds nonessential information; no comma if it identifies which one.
> - Participial phrase in the **middle**: set off with two commas if nonessential.

**Examples:**
- Beginning: *Exhausted from rehearsal*, Lena closed her laptop and went to bed. [comma]
- End (nonessential): The athlete jogged back to the bench, *grinning at the crowd*. [comma — adds detail]
- End (essential/restrictive): The student *scoring the highest* will advance to regionals. [no comma — identifies which student]
- Middle: Marcus, *recognizing the tune immediately*, started playing along on his guitar. [two commas]

**Dangling and Misplaced Phrases:**

Say: "Here's where participles get tricky. The participial phrase must be near the noun it modifies — and that noun must actually be in the sentence."

Write: *Running toward the bus stop, the backpack fell off my shoulder.*
Ask: "What is 'running toward the bus stop'? It should modify the person running. But the subject of the sentence is 'the backpack.' Backpacks don't run. This is a dangling participle."
Fix: *Running toward the bus stop, I felt my backpack fall off my shoulder.*

Write: *I saw a shooting star walking home from the game.*
Ask: "Was the star walking home from the game? No — that's a misplaced participial phrase. The phrase 'walking home from the game' is too far from 'I.'"
Fix: *Walking home from the game, I saw a shooting star.*

### Worked Examples

| Original | Problem | Revised |
|----------|---------|---------|
| Turning the corner, the old theater came into view. | Dangling — the theater isn't turning | Turning the corner, we saw the old theater come into view. |
| She sent a photo of her dog playing guitar. | Misplaced — ambiguous modifier | She, playing guitar, sent a photo of her dog. OR She sent a photo of her dog while playing guitar. |
| Covered in mud, the coach pulled the player off the field. | Ambiguous — who is covered in mud? | Covered in mud, the player was pulled off the field by the coach. |

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply punctuation rules, identify correct vs. incorrect participial phrase placement, and revise dangling/misplaced phrases.

**Part A — Punctuation Practice:** Add or remove commas as needed.

1. Disappointed by the test score, Kezia decided to meet with her teacher.
2. The student scoring the highest on the science exam earned extra credit.
3. Jonas stared at the screen reading the message three times.
4. The documentary exploring ocean biodiversity premiered last Friday.
5. Encouraged by her progress Elena doubled her practice sessions.

**Part B — Dangling or Misplaced? Identify the error and revise the sentence.**

1. Looking out the window, the rain began to fall harder.
2. I found a crumpled note searching through my backpack.
3. Worn from three hours of debate practice, the chair looked very comfortable.
4. She uploaded a video of her singing edited on her phone.

**Facilitator support:** For item 1, ask: "What is 'looking out the window'? Who is looking? Is that subject in the sentence?" For item 4, discuss how rearranging the phrase resolves the ambiguity.

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Independently identify, punctuate, and produce participial phrases.

**Part A — Identification:** Underline each participial phrase and draw an arrow to the noun it modifies. Label PP (present participle) or PastP (past participle).

1. The message, written in three different languages, confused everyone at the table.
2. Disappointed by the film's ending, the audience filed out in silence.
3. The code running on the old laptop finally crashed after two hours.
4. Delighted by the unexpected news, she called her best friend immediately.
5. The team, exhausted but satisfied, posed for a photo with the trophy.

**Part B — Error Correction:** The following sentences contain dangling or misplaced participial phrases. Rewrite each sentence correctly.

1. Waiting for the bus, my earbuds started playing the perfect song.
2. The scientist discovered a rare bird using a telescope.
3. Broken into three pieces, she carefully picked up the tablet.
4. He described the match playing in the championship.

**Part C — Sentence Writing:** Write three original sentences using participial phrases.
1. A sentence that opens with a **present participial phrase** (use a comma).
2. A sentence with a **past participial phrase** in the middle of the sentence (set off with two commas).
3. A sentence ending with a **participial phrase** that requires a comma (nonessential detail).

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Parts A and B; write any 2 of the 3 sentences in Part C.
**Stretch:** Complete all parts; in Part C, write participial phrases of at least 6 words each, ensuring the modified noun is clear.
**Challenge:** Complete all parts; then find two participial phrases in a current reading text, copy the sentences, and analyze the placement and punctuation choices the author made.

### Interleaved Review
1. *Lesson 1 review — Gerunds:* Identify the gerund phrase and name its function: "She improved her grades by reviewing notes before every class."
2. *Grade 7 review — Adjective clauses:* Underline the adjective clause and name the noun it modifies: "The track coach who trained her for two years attended the national championship."

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Add correct punctuation to this sentence and explain your choice: "Distracted by the notification Marcus missed the teacher's explanation."
2. What is wrong with this sentence? Revise it: "Hiking through the forest, the trees seemed to close in around us."
3. Write one sentence that opens with a participial phrase. Make sure the modified noun follows the comma.

**Expected Answers:**
1. "Distracted by the notification, Marcus missed the teacher's explanation." — Comma after the introductory participial phrase.
2. Dangling participle — the trees aren't hiking. Fix: "Hiking through the forest, we felt the trees closing in around us." (or similar)
3. Any correctly formed sentence with an opening participial phrase and clear modified noun.

**Mastery = 2 out of 3 correct, including item 3 (production)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Reteach the placement rule using a physical activity: write three sentences on strips and let the student physically rearrange the participial phrase. Focus especially on identifying the noun being modified.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask the student to try the challenge extension: find one participial phrase and one dangling participle in published student-level writing (real examples from drafts, if available, work well).

## Extension (Optional)
1. Rewrite a paragraph from a recent writing assignment, adding at least two participial phrases to vary your sentence structure. Check that each phrase is correctly placed and punctuated.
2. Search a news article or sports recap for three examples of participial phrases. Write each sentence, underline the phrase, and note whether a comma is used and why.
3. Write a brief "before and after" showing how adding a participial phrase to a flat sentence adds descriptive detail. Do this for three sentences.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The single most common error with participial phrases is the dangling participle. Students often write "Walking into class, the bell rang" without noticing that the bell isn't walking. Build in time to examine the subject of the main clause whenever a participial phrase opens a sentence.
- Students sometimes over-comma: they add commas around every participial phrase at the end of a sentence regardless of whether it is essential or not. The key distinction — does the phrase identify which one? — is worth reinforcing with multiple examples.
- For Part B of guided practice, students may disagree about which reading of sentence 4 ("She uploaded a video of her singing edited on her phone") is the problem. Accept any well-reasoned revision. The goal is noticing that the phrase could modify either "singing" or "uploaded," making the sentence ambiguous.
- When students write participial phrases in Part C, look especially for subject-phrase agreement: the noun immediately after the comma must be the one being modified.
- Connect explicitly to writing: opening a sentence with a participial phrase is a powerful technique for adding detail and varying rhythm. Show one or two published examples to illustrate the stylistic effect.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A:**
1. "written in three different languages" — PastP, modifies "message" (nonrestrictive, set off with commas — correctly punctuated)
2. "Disappointed by the film's ending" — PastP, modifies "audience"
3. "running on the old laptop" — PP, modifies "code" (restrictive — no comma, correctly punctuated)
4. "Delighted by the unexpected news" — PastP, modifies "she"
5. "exhausted but satisfied" — PastP, modifies "team" (nonrestrictive, set off with commas — correctly punctuated)

**Part B:**
1. Dangling — earbuds aren't waiting. Fix: "Waiting for the bus, I noticed my earbuds started playing the perfect song."
2. Misplaced — the bird wasn't using the telescope. Fix: "Using a telescope, the scientist discovered a rare bird."
3. Dangling — she didn't break into three pieces. Fix: "Broken into three pieces, the tablet was carefully picked up."
4. Misplaced — he was playing in the championship, not the match. Fix: "Playing in the championship, he described the match." (or: "He described the match he played in the championship.")

**Part C — Sample Answers:**
1. Typing faster than anyone else in the class, Jordan finished the essay draft in under an hour.
2. The guitar, worn from years of touring, still produced a rich and resonant sound.
3. She handed in her lab report, satisfied with the data she had collected.

### Interleaved Review
1. "reviewing notes before every class" — gerund phrase, object of the preposition "by"
2. "who trained her for two years" — adjective (relative) clause modifying "coach"

### Exit Ticket
1. "Distracted by the notification, Marcus missed the teacher's explanation." — Introductory participial phrase requires a comma.
2. Dangling participle; trees can't hike. Sample fix: "Hiking through the forest, we felt the trees close in around us."
3. Any sentence with an opening participial phrase followed by a comma and a subject that logically performs or receives the action in the phrase.
