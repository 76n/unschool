# English Language Arts — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 6

## Topic
Choosing Voice for Purpose and Effect

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain when active voice is the more effective choice (clarity, directness, strong narration)
- Explain when passive voice is the more effective choice (emphasizing the receiver, unknown agent, formal/scientific writing, avoiding blame attribution)
- Revise sentences and a short passage by making deliberate, purposeful voice choices with written justification

## Prerequisites
- Structural understanding of active and passive voice and the Voice Flip strategy (Lesson 5, L.8.1b)
- Awareness of audience and purpose in writing (Grade 8 Units 1–3)
- Experience with revision for clarity and style (Grade 7 and Grade 8 Units 1–3)

## Materials Needed
- Student notebook or grammar journal
- Lesson 6 handout (or projected passages)
- Two colors of highlighter (one for active constructions, one for passive)
- Optional: samples from students' own recent writing drafts

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Illustrate through side-by-side comparison that voice choices can shift emphasis and implication in meaningful ways.

**Activity: Same Fact, Different Focus**

Display the following pairs. Ask the student: "What is the same in each pair? What changes?"

Pair 1:
- a. *The city council approved the new park renovation plan.*
- b. *The new park renovation plan was approved.*

Pair 2:
- a. *A cyberattack compromised the personal data of 50,000 students.*
- b. *The personal data of 50,000 students was compromised in a cyberattack.*

Pair 3:
- a. *My teammate knocked over the equipment cart during warm-ups.*
- b. *The equipment cart was knocked over during warm-ups.*

Say: "In each pair, the facts are the same — but the emphasis shifts. In Pair 3, version (b) quietly removes the agent. Is that a problem? It depends on the situation. Today we're going to build a decision framework for choosing voice intentionally."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Present the "Voice Choice" decision framework with clear contexts for when each voice is more effective; model analysis of published examples.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Emphasis:** The element of a sentence that receives the most focus, often determined by what appears in the subject position.
- **Agent omission:** Removing the "by + agent" phrase from a passive sentence — effective when the agent is unknown, irrelevant, or deliberately withheld.
- **Purposeful voice choice:** Selecting active or passive voice based on what the writing needs to communicate, rather than habit or assumption.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "There is a common rule: 'Always use active voice.' That rule is an oversimplification. Both voices have jobs. The real question is: *what do I want my reader to focus on?* And *who needs to be in the subject slot to make this sentence work best?*"

Write the **Voice Choice Framework** on the board:

**Choose ACTIVE voice when:**
1. You want clarity and directness — the reader should see clearly who is acting.
   - "The lead researcher presented the findings." (clear, direct)
2. You are writing narrative or personal prose — active voice keeps the energy moving.
   - "She sprinted the last 100 meters and crossed the finish line first."
3. The agent is important and should receive emphasis.
   - "The mayor personally signed the new environmental bill." (the mayor's action matters)
4. You want to avoid wordiness — active voice is typically shorter.

**Choose PASSIVE voice when:**
1. The receiver of the action is more important than the agent.
   - "Twelve new scholarships were awarded to students in the district." (the scholarships and their recipients are the story, not who awarded them)
2. The agent is unknown, irrelevant, or obvious.
   - "The lab results were contaminated." (we may not know who contaminated them)
   - "The suspect was arrested early this morning." (the police are obvious; the arrest is the story)
3. You want to avoid assigning blame or responsibility directly.
   - "Several errors were made in the calculation." (diplomatic; used in formal and professional contexts)
4. You are writing in scientific or academic registers where the method or result matters more than who performed it.
   - "The samples were heated to 200 degrees Celsius for 30 minutes."

Say: "Notice that in scientific writing, passive voice is not a weakness — it's a convention. Scientists often deliberately omit themselves as agents to focus on the process and result, not the researcher."

**Analysis of real examples:**

Display and discuss:
1. From a science lab report: *"The solution was diluted to a concentration of 0.1 mol/L and allowed to settle for five minutes."*
   - Why passive? The method is the focus; who did it is irrelevant or assumed.

2. From a sports narrative: *"With 3 seconds left, Jordan intercepted the pass and drove the length of the court for the game-winning layup."*
   - Why active? Narrative energy; the actor's agency is the entire point.

3. From a diplomatic statement: *"Mistakes were made during the planning process, and steps are being taken to address them."*
   - Why passive? Avoids assigning blame; maintains formal, careful tone.

Say: "The third example also illustrates that passive voice can sometimes be evasive or manipulative — when it hides accountability that readers deserve to see. Good writers recognize both the legitimate and the problematic uses."

## Guided Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Analyze voice choices in sample sentences and passages; justify choices; begin purposeful revision.

**Part A — Analyze and Justify:** For each sentence, identify the voice (Active/Passive) and decide whether the choice is effective for the stated context. Explain your reasoning in one sentence.

1. [Science lab report] *The bacteria were grown on agar plates and observed for 48 hours.* [Passive — effective or not?]
2. [News article about a student achievement] *Awards were given to three students by the principal at the ceremony.* [Passive — effective or not?]
3. [Personal narrative] *My first attempt at the recipe was not exactly enjoyed by my family.* [Passive — effective or not?]
4. [Argumentative essay] *It is argued by many scientists that renewable energy can meet global demand.* [Passive — effective or not?]

**Part B — Revision:** Revise the sentences in items 2–4 above that you judged ineffective. Write your revision and briefly state why your version is better.

**Part C — Passage Analysis:** Read the following passage and highlight every passive construction. Then mark each one: (K) keep — appropriate passive, or (R) revise — should be active. Discuss your choices.

> "The spring concert was organized by the student activities committee. All instruments were tuned before the performance. Three original songs were composed by members of the jazz ensemble. The audience was clearly moved by the performance. During the final number, a mic stand was accidentally knocked over, though no injuries were reported. The performance was described as 'one of the best in years' by the principal."

## Independent Practice (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply Voice Choice Framework to revise a passage and write original sentences with deliberate, justified voice choices.

**Part A — Passage Revision:**

Revise the following passage. Some sentences should stay passive; others should be changed to active. For each sentence you change, mark it with a "C" (changed) and write one phrase explaining why (e.g., "active = clearer agent," "passive = agent unknown").

> "A pollution warning was issued for the river near the school last Thursday. The warning was prompted by elevated levels of nitrates found in water samples. The samples were collected by the environmental science class as part of their field study unit. The results were reported to the district health office by the teacher. A follow-up investigation will be conducted to identify the source. Students are advised to avoid contact with the river water until the situation has been resolved."

**Part B — Sentence Writing with Justification:**

Write three original sentences. For each, include a one-sentence justification of your voice choice.

1. Write about a **scientific process** — use passive voice appropriately.
2. Write about an **exciting moment in a game or performance** — use active voice for narrative energy.
3. Write a **diplomatic or formal statement** about a mistake or error — use passive voice to omit the agent appropriately.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete Part A (revise 3–4 sentences and explain changes) and Part B items 1 and 2.
**Stretch:** Complete all parts; in Part A, revise all sentences that need changing with explanations; include all three sentences in Part B.
**Challenge:** Complete all parts; then take a paragraph from a recent argumentative or informational writing piece you have produced. Identify every passive construction and decide: keep or revise? Apply your changes and write a 2–3 sentence reflection on how the revision affected clarity.

### Interleaved Review
1. *Lesson 4 review — Sentence combining with verbals:* Combine these two sentences into one using any verbal phrase. Label the verbal type you used: "She read the research article twice. She wanted to understand the methodology."
2. *Grade 7 review — Word choice and connotation:* What is the connotation difference between "The politician said" and "The politician claimed" in a news article? What effect does the word choice create?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Give one context where passive voice is clearly the more effective choice. Write an example sentence for that context.
2. Revise this sentence to active voice and explain why the revision is better for a news article: "The record for the 400-meter hurdles was broken by Sofia Rivera at last night's regional track meet."
3. Read this sentence: "The decision to cut funding for the arts program was made." Is omitting the agent here appropriate or potentially problematic? Explain.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Any well-explained context (science writing, unknown agent, emphasizing receiver, formal/diplomatic use) with a correctly formed passive example sentence.
2. "Sofia Rivera broke the record for the 400-meter hurdles at last night's regional track meet." The active version emphasizes Sofia (the agent who achieved the feat), which is more appropriate for a story celebrating her accomplishment.
3. Open-ended — the student might say: omitting the agent is potentially problematic because it avoids accountability for a significant decision. Readers deserve to know who made the decision. Acceptable if the student articulates the tension between diplomatic tone and transparency.

**Mastery = 2 out of 3 correct, including item 1 (production with justification)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to the Voice Choice Framework and work through one additional pair of contexts with contrasting examples. Have the student write one justified example for each context before proceeding.

### If Mastery Is Met
Connect to the student's own writing: ask them to identify one place in a recent piece where they used passive voice and evaluate whether it was intentional and effective.

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. Find an example of passive voice used to avoid assigning blame in a real news article, press release, or public statement. Copy the sentence, identify what it's hiding, and rewrite it in active voice with the agent named. Reflect: what changes?
2. Examine the passive voice conventions in a science lab report (from school or a textbook). Write 4–5 sentences in science lab report style about an imaginary experiment, using passive voice correctly throughout.
3. Write a short narrative (6–8 sentences) about a competitive event (game, performance, debate). Use active voice throughout. Then rewrite it from the perspective of a formal summary, converting it to more passive/formal constructions. Compare the tone of the two versions.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson shifts from structural knowledge to rhetorical judgment — a more demanding cognitive move. Validate that there isn't always a single right answer; what matters is the quality of the reasoning.
- The passage in Part C of guided practice is designed to include a mix: some passive is appropriate (the formal documentary tone of a concert recap), some is unnecessarily distant ("was clearly moved by"). Encourage the student to debate — the goal is the thinking process, not a single correct labeling.
- The exit ticket item 3 (the funding cut) is intentionally provocative. Students may give different answers, and all can be correct if well-reasoned. This is a good opportunity to discuss how grammar choices carry ethical and rhetorical weight.
- If the student has a writing draft available, the challenge extension is highly valuable: real revision in authentic writing contexts cements this lesson's learning in lasting ways.
- Reinforce repeatedly: "neither voice is always right." The goal is awareness and intentionality — two hallmarks of mature writing.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

**Part A — Sample Revisions:**
1. "A pollution warning was issued for the river near the school last Thursday." — This could remain passive (appropriate if source is unknown or official), or could be changed to active if the issuing body is known: "The county health department issued a pollution warning for the river near the school last Thursday." [C — active = clearer agent]
2. "The warning was prompted by elevated levels of nitrates found in water samples." — Keep passive; the warning responding to the data is the focus, not who prompted it. [K]
3. "The samples were collected by the environmental science class as part of their field study unit." — Change to active: "The environmental science class collected the samples as part of their field study unit." [C — active = clearer; class as agent is important context]
4. "The results were reported to the district health office by the teacher." — Change to active: "The teacher reported the results to the district health office." [C — active = more direct, agent is relevant]
5. "A follow-up investigation will be conducted to identify the source." — Keep passive; the investigation is the focus, and who conducts it may be unknown. [K]
6. "Students are advised to avoid contact with the river water until the situation has been resolved." — Keep passive; "advised" is appropriate formal passive for a public health warning. [K]

**Part B — Sample Answers:**
1. (Science) "The DNA samples were extracted using a standardized centrifuge protocol and stored at -20 degrees Celsius." — Passive is appropriate; the method is the focus, not who performed it.
2. (Narrative) "With ten seconds left in the final quarter, she stole the ball, crossed half-court, and launched a buzzer-beater that won the championship." — Active voice propels the narrative forward and puts the hero in the subject slot.
3. (Diplomatic) "Errors in the data entry process were identified during the review, and corrective measures have been implemented." — Passive omits who made the errors — appropriate for a formal report where accountability attribution is diplomatically avoided.

### Interleaved Review
1. Sample: "She read the research article twice to understand the methodology." — Infinitive phrase (adverb: explains why). OR: "Reading the research article twice, she finally understood the methodology." — Participial phrase.
2. "Said" is neutral. "Claimed" carries a connotation of doubt or skepticism — the word suggests the journalist is not fully endorsing the politician's statement as fact. This subtle word choice signals the writer's stance and influences how the reader interprets the politician's credibility.

### Exit Ticket
1. Sample: Scientific writing — "The compound was heated to 150°C for ten minutes and then cooled to room temperature." (Agent omitted; the process is the focus, not who performed it.)
2. "Sofia Rivera broke the record for the 400-meter hurdles at last night's regional track meet." Active version is better for a news article because Sofia's achievement is the story — she deserves the emphasis that comes from being the subject.
3. Problematic — omitting who made the decision hides accountability for a decision affecting many people (students, teachers, arts programs). Readers deserve to know who is responsible. The passive voice here may be deliberately evasive.
