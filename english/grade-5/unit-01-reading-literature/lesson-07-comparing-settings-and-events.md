# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 7

## Topic
Comparing Settings and Events

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare and contrast two or more settings in a story, identifying how each setting influences characters and events
- Compare and contrast two or more events in a story, analyzing similarities and differences in how they unfold
- Support all comparisons with specific details and accurate quotes from the text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to compare and contrast characters using specific text details (Lesson 6, RL.5.3)
- Ability to describe settings and events in depth (RL.4.3)
- Ability to quote accurately from text (Lessons 1-2, RL.5.1)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils and colored pencils
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)
- T-chart or Venn diagram templates

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students understand that settings and events, not just characters, can be compared and contrasted.

**Activity: Two Places, Two Feelings**

Say: "In Lesson 6, we compared and contrasted characters. Today, we are expanding our comparison skills to settings and events. Think about two places you know well — maybe your bedroom and a classroom, or a park and a grocery store. How are those two places similar? How are they different? And here is the important question: How does each place make you feel or act differently?"

Allow 60 seconds for partner sharing.

Say: "The place where a story happens — the setting — is not just a background. It shapes how characters behave, how they feel, and what happens. When a story has two different settings, comparing them can reveal a lot. The same goes for events. When two similar events happen in a story, the differences between them often carry meaning."

Write on the board:
- **Comparing settings:** How are the places alike or different? How does each setting affect the characters?
- **Comparing events:** How are the events alike or different? What changes between them?

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to compare settings and events systematically, using specific details.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Setting:** The time and place where a story takes place, including physical details, atmosphere, and mood.
- **Atmosphere:** The overall feeling or mood of a setting (e.g., peaceful, tense, gloomy, cheerful).
- **Event:** A significant thing that happens in the story — an action, decision, or turning point.
- **Parallel events:** Two events in a story that are similar in structure but different in outcome or meaning.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When comparing settings, ask yourself these questions."

Write on the board:
**Setting Comparison Questions:**
1. What does each place look like, sound like, feel like?
2. How does each setting affect the characters' moods or actions?
3. What is similar about the two settings? What is different?
4. Why did the author use two different settings?

Say: "When comparing events, ask yourself these questions."

Write on the board:
**Event Comparison Questions:**
1. What happens in each event?
2. How does the character respond each time?
3. What is similar? What is different?
4. What does the change between the two events reveal?

Say: "Let me model comparing settings first."

### Worked Examples

**Passage: "The Two Kitchens"**

> Whenever Nia visited her grandmother in the country, the first place she went was the kitchen. Grandma Rose's kitchen was warm and cluttered. Jars of dried herbs lined the windowsill. A cast-iron pot sat permanently on the stove, always simmering something. The wooden table was scarred with knife marks from decades of chopping. Flour dust coated the counter like snow. The room smelled of cinnamon and onions and bread — all at the same time.
>
> "Nothing in this kitchen matches," Grandma Rose always said, "but everything in this kitchen works."
>
> Back home in the city, Nia's family kitchen was the opposite. The counters were granite, smooth and gray. The appliances were stainless steel and hummed quietly. Everything had a place — the spices in a rotating rack, the knives in a magnetic strip, the cookbooks arranged by color on a shelf. The room smelled of lemon cleaning spray.
>
> Nia's mother cooked dinner every night, and the food was always good. But Nia noticed something. In her mother's kitchen, cooking was a task to be completed. In Grandma Rose's kitchen, cooking was a conversation. Grandma Rose talked while she stirred, tasted while she told stories, and never once looked at a recipe.
>
> When Nia asked Grandma Rose how she knew what to add, Grandma Rose tapped her chest. "The recipe is in here," she said. "It got passed down from my mother, and her mother before that."

Say: "Let me compare these two settings."

Write on the board:

**Grandma Rose's Kitchen:**
- "Warm and cluttered" — physically cozy and lived-in
- Sensory details: herbs, simmering pot, knife-scarred table, flour dust, smells of "cinnamon and onions and bread"
- Atmosphere: welcoming, full of history and tradition
- Cooking is "a conversation" — social, storytelling, intuitive

**Nia's Family Kitchen:**
- Granite, stainless steel — modern, organized
- "Everything had a place" — neat and efficient
- Smells of "lemon cleaning spray" — clean but not warm
- Cooking is "a task to be completed" — functional, not emotional

**Both:**
- Both are kitchens where family meals are prepared
- Both represent care for the family (Grandma Rose cooks with love; Nia's mother cooks "every night" and the food is "always good")

Say: "The comparison reveals something deeper. The two kitchens represent two different approaches to life — one values tradition and connection, the other values order and efficiency. Both work, but they feel different to Nia."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice comparing events in a story with teacher support.

Say: "Now let us compare two events in the same story. As I read, notice how a similar situation happens twice — but with a key difference."

**Passage: "Two Speeches"**

> The first time Rashid stood in front of the class to give a presentation, he read every word from his note cards. His voice was flat, and he stared at the cards the whole time. When he looked up at the end, most of the class was staring at their desks. His teacher, Ms. Gentry, gave him a C and wrote, "Good information. Try making eye contact and using your own words next time."
>
> Rashid was disappointed, but he did not argue. He took the note card home and studied it again — not the words, but the ideas behind them. He practiced in front of his bathroom mirror, saying the information in his own words. He timed himself. He practiced looking at his own reflection as if it were an audience.
>
> Three weeks later, Rashid gave his second presentation. This time, he brought only a single index card with three bullet points — just reminders, not a script. When he stood in front of the class, he took a breath and started talking. His voice wavered at first, but by the second minute, he was speaking clearly and looking at his classmates. He even cracked a small joke that made a few people laugh.
>
> Ms. Gentry gave him a B+. She wrote, "Huge improvement. You found your voice."

Say: "We have two events here — two presentations. Let us compare them. What happened in the first presentation?"

Expected: Rashid read from note cards, spoke in a flat voice, did not make eye contact, and got a C.

Say: "What happened in the second presentation?"

Expected: Rashid used only a few bullet points, spoke in his own words, made eye contact, told a joke, and got a B+.

Say: "What is similar about the two events?"

Expected: Same speaker, same class, same teacher, same type of assignment.

Say: "What is different?"

Expected: Rashid's preparation method, his delivery, his confidence, and his grade all changed.

Say: "What does the change between the two events reveal?"

Guide toward: The change reveals that Rashid learned from his first experience and was willing to put in the effort to improve. The two events show growth — from hiding behind note cards to finding his own voice.

Say: "Now write a 3-4 sentence comparison of the two events using at least one quote from each event."

**Model response:** In his first presentation, Rashid "read every word from his note cards" and spoke in a "flat" voice, earning a C. In his second presentation, he brought "only a single index card with three bullet points" and spoke "clearly," even making a joke. The two events are similar because they are both class presentations for the same teacher, but the key difference is Rashid's preparation and delivery. Ms. Gentry's comment — "You found your voice" — captures what changed: Rashid moved from reciting someone else's words to expressing ideas in his own way.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently compare settings and events in a story.

**Passage: "Before and After the Rain"**

> The morning of the neighborhood block party dawned bright and hot. By ten o'clock, tables lined the street, covered with checkered cloths and plates of food. Music played from a speaker balanced on Mr. Warren's porch railing. Children chased each other between the tables, and the air smelled of grilled corn and sunscreen. Everyone was laughing, talking, waving — the street felt more like a living room than a road.
>
> Then the sky changed. Clouds rolled in from the west, thick and dark as bruises. The wind picked up, flipping napkins off tables and sending paper plates cartwheeling down the sidewalk. The first drops fell heavy and warm, and within minutes, the rain came down in sheets.
>
> People grabbed food and ran for cover. Some ducked into Mr. Warren's garage. Others crowded under Mrs. Chen's carport. The music cut off when someone yanked the speaker inside. The street that had been full of laughter was now empty except for the rain hammering the asphalt and the checkered cloths soaking through.
>
> But then something unexpected happened. From inside the garage, someone started clapping a rhythm. Others joined in. Marisol's mother started singing a song in Spanish, and Mr. Warren found a harmonica in his toolbox and played along. The children, packed together under the carport, started a clapping game. Mrs. Chen brought out a pot of hot chocolate.
>
> The block party did not end. It just moved.
>
> Later, when the sun came out again and people drifted back into the street, Marisol's mother said, "You know, I think the rain made it better." And looking around at the wet, laughing faces, it was hard to disagree.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences. Use accurate quotes from the passage.

1. Compare the setting BEFORE the rain and DURING the rain. Describe at least two specific differences. Use quotes from the text.
2. Compare the two versions of the block party — the outdoor version and the garage/carport version. What is similar? What is different?
3. What is the significance of the line "The block party did not end. It just moved"? What does this tell us about the community?
4. Compare the beginning of the passage to the end. How has the mood or atmosphere changed, and what caused that change?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 as written.
**Stretch:** After answering questions 1-4, explain how comparing the before-rain and after-rain settings helps reveal the theme of the passage.
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph comparing the TWO events in "Two Speeches" (from Guided Practice) with the TWO settings in this passage. How does the comparison in each story serve a similar purpose?

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.3) Describe the overall mood of the passage at the very end. Use at least two details to support your answer.
6. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.2) What is the theme of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following mini-passage:

> At the beginning of the hike, the trail was wide and flat. Sunlight filtered through the trees, and birds called from every direction. By the summit, the trail was narrow and rocky. The wind stung Ava's ears, and the trees had thinned to stubby shrubs. But the view stretched for miles, and Ava whispered, "This was worth it."

1. Name one difference between the setting at the beginning and the setting at the summit.
2. What does Ava's response to the summit suggest? Quote the text.

**Expected Answers:**
1. At the beginning, the trail was "wide and flat" with "sunlight" and "birds," creating a comfortable, easy atmosphere. At the summit, the trail was "narrow and rocky" with "wind" that "stung" and only "stubby shrubs," creating a harsh, exposed atmosphere.
2. Despite the difficult conditions at the summit, Ava's response — "This was worth it" — suggests that the effort and discomfort of the hard climb were rewarded by the view. She values the experience at the top more than the comfort of the bottom.

**Mastery = Student identifies at least one specific difference between the two settings (with detail, not just "it was different") and explains what Ava's response suggests, quoting the text.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the setting comparison questions from Direct Instruction.
- Have the student draw a simple picture of each setting, then list what is different in words.
- Provide a sentence frame: "At the beginning, the setting was ___, as shown by '___.' At the end, the setting was ___, as shown by '___.' This change suggests ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8, which introduces visual elements in literature.
- Encourage the student to notice setting shifts in their independent reading and think about why the author changes settings.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Setting Sketch and Compare:** The student draws two settings from a book they are reading and writes a paragraph comparing them. They include details about atmosphere, mood, and how each setting affects the characters.
2. **Event Timeline:** The student creates a timeline of key events in a story and identifies two events that are parallel (similar structure, different outcome). They write a comparison paragraph.
3. **Setting Swap:** The student rewrites a scene from a story, placing the characters in a completely different setting. They reflect on how the new setting changes the mood, the characters' behavior, and the events.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Most common mistake:** Students will describe each setting or event separately without making explicit comparisons. Redirect: "You have told me about each one. Now put them together — what is the same, and what is different?"
- **Connecting to Lesson 6:** Remind students that comparing settings and events uses the same thinking skills as comparing characters — finding similarities and differences, and supporting every claim with evidence.
- **The block party passage:** This passage works on multiple levels. Students may want to discuss community, resilience, or adaptability. Allow brief discussion if it deepens understanding, but keep focus on the comparison skill.
- **Pacing note:** If the Guided Practice comparison takes longer than expected, abbreviate the discussion and give students extra time on Independent Practice.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Before vs. during the rain:** Before the rain, the setting was bright, social, and festive — "tables lined the street, covered with checkered cloths," "music played from a speaker," and "everyone was laughing, talking, waving." During the rain, the setting became chaotic and empty — "the wind picked up, flipping napkins off tables," "the rain came down in sheets," and "the street that had been full of laughter was now empty except for the rain hammering the asphalt."
2. **Outdoor vs. indoor block party:** Both versions are community gatherings with music, food, and togetherness. In the outdoor version, the celebration is organized and planned (tables, music, grilled corn). In the garage/carport version, the celebration is spontaneous and improvised — "someone started clapping a rhythm," "Mr. Warren found a harmonica in his toolbox," and "Mrs. Chen brought out a pot of hot chocolate." The indoor version is smaller, more crowded, and less orderly, but arguably more intimate and joyful.
3. **Significance of "The block party did not end. It just moved":** This line reveals that the community's connection to each other is more important than the setting or the plan. The party was not really about the tables and the food — it was about the people being together. When the setting changed, the spirit did not. This suggests the community is adaptable, close-knit, and capable of finding joy in any circumstances.
4. **Beginning vs. end mood:** At the beginning, the mood is cheerful and relaxed — sunshine, music, laughter. At the end, the mood is warm, connected, and even more joyful than before. Marisol's mother says, "I think the rain made it better," and the narrator observes "wet, laughing faces." The storm disrupted the party but ultimately brought people closer together, creating a deeper sense of community than the original, more casual gathering.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.4.3) The mood at the end of the passage is warm, grateful, and joyful. Marisol's mother says, "I think the rain made it better," showing appreciation. The "wet, laughing faces" suggest the community is happy and united. The rain has passed, the sun is out, and the people have shared an experience that brought them even closer.
6. (RL.4.2) Theme: Challenges and unexpected disruptions can actually bring people closer together and create deeper connections than easy, planned experiences. (Accept variations about community resilience, adaptability, or finding joy in unexpected circumstances.)
