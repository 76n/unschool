# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 1: Reading Literature

## Unit Assessment

### Overview
This assessment evaluates student mastery of the four standards addressed in Unit 1:
- **RL.5.1** — Quote accurately when explaining what the text says explicitly and when drawing inferences from the text
- **RL.5.2** — Determine a theme of a story, drama, or poem from details in the text, including how characters respond to challenges or how the speaker reflects upon a topic; summarize the text
- **RL.5.3** — Compare and contrast two or more characters, settings, or events in a story or drama, drawing on specific details in the text
- **RL.5.7** — Analyze how visual and multimedia elements contribute to the meaning, tone, or beauty of a text

### Administration Notes
- **Estimated time:** 40-50 minutes
- **Format:** 5 passages with 4-5 questions each (24 questions total)
- **Scoring:** Each question is worth 1 point. Total = 24 points.
- **Mastery threshold:** 70% overall (17/24) AND at least 50% in each standard
- Students may reread passages as needed
- Read directions aloud if needed for students with accommodations

## Passage 1: "The Compass" (Prose)
*Standards assessed: RL.5.1 (Quoting/Inference), RL.5.2 (Theme)*

> Davi found the old compass in a shoebox at the back of his father's closet. It was brass, scratched and dented, with a glass face that had a thin crack running through it. The needle still moved, though — spinning slowly until it pointed north.
>
> "Where did this come from?" Davi asked at dinner.
>
> His father set down his fork. "That was your grandfather's. He used it when he worked on the fishing boats. He said it saved his life once — a fog came in so thick he could not see the bow of his own boat. The compass was the only thing that got him home."
>
> "Can I keep it?" Davi asked.
>
> His father hesitated. "It does not work perfectly anymore. The crack in the glass throws the needle off by a few degrees. You would never want to depend on it in a real emergency."
>
> Davi turned the compass in his hands. The brass was worn smooth where his grandfather's thumb must have rested. "I do not need it to work perfectly," Davi said. "I just need it to remind me which direction to go."
>
> His father smiled — a slow, surprised kind of smile. "Your grandfather would have said exactly the same thing."

**Questions:**

**1.** What does Davi's father say about the compass's condition? Quote the text accurately in your answer. (RL.5.1)

**2.** The text says the brass was "worn smooth where his grandfather's thumb must have rested." What can you infer about how Davi's grandfather used the compass? Support your inference with this quote and your own reasoning. (RL.5.1)

**3.** How does Davi respond to learning that the compass is not perfectly accurate? Quote his response and explain what it reveals about him. (RL.5.2)

**4.** What is the theme of this passage? Write your answer as a complete sentence and explain how Davi's response to the situation reveals it. (RL.5.2)

**5.** Write a 2-3 sentence summary of this passage that includes the main events and theme. (RL.5.2)

## Passage 2: "The Relay" (First-Person Narrative)
*Standards assessed: RL.5.1 (Quoting/Inference), RL.5.3 (Comparing Characters)*

> I was the third runner on our relay team, sandwiched between Kayla and Andre. We had been practicing for three weeks, and the handoff was finally smooth — most of the time.
>
> Kayla was the fastest runner on the team, no question. She exploded out of the blocks like she had springs in her shoes and always built a lead before passing the baton to me. But Kayla hated practicing. She showed up late to half our practices, and when she did show up, she spent most of the time talking to friends on the sideline. "I do not need to practice," she said once. "Speed is speed."
>
> Andre was different. He was not the fastest — he knew that and admitted it openly. But Andre was the most dependable person I had ever met. He arrived twenty minutes early to every practice. He studied videos of relay handoffs and tested different grip positions on the baton. When our team lost a practice race because of a fumbled handoff, Andre stayed after everyone left and practiced the handoff with me until we got it right ten times in a row.
>
> On the day of the district meet, Kayla ran a blazing first leg and handed the baton to me with a two-meter lead. I held the lead and passed it cleanly to Andre. On the final stretch, another team's anchor runner was faster — clearly faster. Andre's legs were pumping, but the other runner was pulling ahead. Then, right before the finish line, the other runner's hand opened. His baton tumbled to the track. He stumbled, grabbing for it.
>
> Andre crossed the finish line first. But it was not because he was the fastest. It was because he was holding on.

**Questions:**

**6.** How does Kayla feel about practicing? Quote the text accurately in your answer. (RL.5.1)

**7.** Compare and contrast Kayla and Andre. Identify at least one similarity and two differences, using specific details or quotes from the text. (RL.5.3)

**8.** What can you infer about the narrator's attitude toward Andre versus Kayla? The text does not state this directly. Use evidence from the passage to support your inference. (RL.5.1)

**9.** The passage ends with: "It was not because he was the fastest. It was because he was holding on." What does this final sentence mean, beyond the literal action of holding a baton? (RL.5.1)

**10.** How does Andre respond to the challenge of not being the fastest runner? How does his response contrast with Kayla's approach? Use specific details from the text. (RL.5.3)

## Passage 3: Two Settings — Paired Passage
*Standards assessed: RL.5.3 (Comparing Settings), RL.5.2 (Theme)*

**Setting A: "The Old Library"**

> The town's original library was a narrow stone building on Elm Street. The wooden shelves were packed so tightly that you had to turn sideways to walk between them. The ceiling was low, and the lights were dim — two yellow bulbs behind frosted glass. The air smelled of old paper and dust. Mrs. Okoro, the librarian, kept a handwritten catalog in a leather notebook because the library had never been given a computer.
>
> But everyone in town had a story about the old library. Mr. Benitez learned to read English there. The Pham sisters spent every Saturday afternoon in the corner with the picture books. Darian wrote his college application essay at the table by the window because it was the only quiet place he could find.
>
> "That library was small," Mrs. Okoro said. "But it was full."

**Setting B: "The New Library"**

> The new library opened on a Saturday in October. It was three times the size of the old one, with tall windows, white walls, and rows of gleaming computers. The shelves were spaced wide apart, and the books were organized by colored stickers — green for children, blue for young adult, red for nonfiction. The air smelled of new carpet and fresh paint.
>
> A self-checkout machine stood by the door. The catalog was entirely digital. The children's section had bean bag chairs and a screen that played educational videos on a loop.
>
> On opening day, people walked through slowly, looking around. Mr. Benitez ran his hand along the smooth new shelves. The Pham sisters sat in the bean bag chairs and opened a picture book. Mrs. Okoro stood behind the new circulation desk, her leather notebook tucked under one arm.
>
> "It is beautiful," she said quietly. Then she opened her notebook and began copying the entries into the computer, one by one.

**Questions:**

**11.** Compare and contrast the two library settings. Identify at least two specific differences and one similarity, using quotes from the text. (RL.5.3)

**12.** What can you infer about how Mrs. Okoro feels about the change from the old library to the new one? Use evidence from both passages to support your inference. (RL.5.1)

**13.** The text says the old library "was small, but it was full." What does Mrs. Okoro mean by "full"? This is not about the number of books. Explain using evidence from the passage. (RL.5.1)

**14.** What theme do these two passages share when read together? Write your answer as a complete sentence. (RL.5.2)

## Passage 4: A Poem
*Standards assessed: RL.5.2 (Theme in Poetry), RL.5.7 (Visual/Multimedia Elements)*

> **My Mother's Garden**
>
> She plants in rows
> the way her mother did,
> and her mother before that —
> tomatoes on the south side,
> beans climbing the fence,
> marigolds circling the border
> like bright orange guards.
>
> She talks to the plants
> the way some people talk to friends —
> coaxing the stubborn tomatoes,
> praising the beans for reaching the top,
> scolding the weeds like uninvited guests.
>
> I used to think it was silly,
> kneeling in the dirt,
> hands dark with soil,
> sweat dripping off her chin.
>
> But this morning I found myself
> outside before she woke,
> pulling weeds by the marigolds,
> whispering to a tomato plant
> that looked like it needed encouragement.
>
> I did not plan it.
> My hands just knew what to do.

**Questions:**

**16.** What is the theme of this poem? Write it as a complete sentence and explain how the speaker's reflection reveals it. (RL.5.2)

**17.** Imagine this poem is presented as a multimedia piece with the following elements: soft guitar music playing in the background, birdsong and garden sounds (wind, rustling leaves), and the narrator's voice shifting from a slightly embarrassed tone in stanza 3 to a warm, surprised tone in stanza 4. How would the music and sound design contribute to the meaning or tone of the poem? (RL.5.7)

**18.** If an artist illustrated this poem, what would be the most important visual element to include in the final stanza? Explain what it would add to the poem's meaning. (RL.5.7)

## Passage 5: A Drama Scene with Visual Description
*Standards assessed: RL.5.3 (Comparing Events), RL.5.7 (Visual Elements), RL.5.1 (Quoting)*

> SCENE: A school art room. Paintings hang on a drying rack. NOAH stands at an easel, staring at a blank canvas. His friend PRIYA walks in carrying a sketchbook.
>
> PRIYA: You have not started yet?
>
> NOAH: (rubbing his forehead) I have been standing here for twenty minutes. Ms. Larsen said to paint "something that matters to you." I do not even know where to begin.
>
> PRIYA: (sitting on a stool, opening her sketchbook) I had the same problem. I sat in my room for an hour staring at a blank page. Then I just started drawing lines. No plan. Just lines.
>
> NOAH: And that worked?
>
> PRIYA: (turning the sketchbook toward him) See for yourself.
>
> (NOAH leans in. The page shows a detailed drawing of a pair of hands holding a cracked clay bowl. The hands are clearly an older person's — wrinkled, with short nails and a thin ring on one finger.)
>
> NOAH: (quietly) Whose hands are those?
>
> PRIYA: My grandmother's. She makes pottery. That bowl cracked in the kiln last week, and she was going to throw it away. But I told her to keep it because the crack made it look like a river running through the clay. (Pause.) I did not know I was going to draw that. I just started, and it came out.
>
> NOAH: (looking back at his blank canvas) Maybe that is my problem. I am trying to think of what matters BEFORE I start. Maybe I should just start and find out.
>
> (NOAH picks up a brush, dips it in blue paint, and makes one long stroke across the canvas. He steps back and tilts his head.)
>
> NOAH: I do not know what that is yet.
>
> PRIYA: (smiling) That is exactly what it should be right now.
>
> **Description of Priya's drawing (as a visual element):**
> The drawing shows two aged hands cupping a small clay bowl. The bowl has a jagged crack running from the rim to the base, but instead of looking broken, the crack has been drawn with incredible detail — the edges of the crack are slightly darker, as if the artist spent more time on the imperfection than on the smooth parts. The hands are drawn with care: every wrinkle, every knuckle, the thin ring catching a glint of light. The background is left blank — no table, no room, just the hands and the bowl floating in white space, making them the sole focus.

**Questions:**

**19.** Compare how Priya and Noah approach the art assignment. What is similar about their initial experience? What is different about where they are when the scene takes place? Use specific details or quotes. (RL.5.3)

**20.** What does Priya's drawing reveal about what she values? Quote from her dialogue to support your answer. (RL.5.1)

**21.** Analyze the description of Priya's drawing. Name two visual choices the artist (Priya) made and explain what each choice contributes to the meaning of the artwork. (RL.5.7)

**22.** Compare two events in this scene: Priya describing how she started her drawing and Noah making his first brushstroke. How are these two moments similar? What does the similarity suggest? (RL.5.3)

**23.** Noah says, "Maybe I should just start and find out." How does this response to his challenge connect to the theme of the scene? Quote the text to support your answer. (RL.5.2)

**24.** Priya responds to Noah's first brushstroke by saying, "That is exactly what it should be right now." What can you infer she means by this? (RL.5.1)

## Answer Key

### Passage 1: "The Compass"

**1.** (RL.5.1) Davi's father says the compass "does not work perfectly anymore. The crack in the glass throws the needle off by a few degrees. You would never want to depend on it in a real emergency."

**2.** (RL.5.1) The brass being "worn smooth where his grandfather's thumb must have rested" suggests that Davi's grandfather held the compass frequently and in the same position. This implies he relied on it heavily during his work on the fishing boats, likely checking it many times throughout his days at sea. The wear pattern shows years of use — this was not an occasional tool but a constant companion.

**3.** (RL.5.2) When Davi learns the compass is not accurate, he says, "I do not need it to work perfectly. I just need it to remind me which direction to go." This reveals that Davi values the compass for its emotional and symbolic meaning — its connection to his grandfather and the guidance it represents — more than for its literal function. He understands that some things matter because of what they represent, not because of how perfectly they perform.

**4.** (RL.5.2) Theme: The most valuable things in life are not always the ones that work perfectly — sometimes what matters most is the connection, memory, and direction they provide. Davi's response reveals this because he chooses to keep the flawed compass not for navigation but for the sense of purpose and family connection it gives him. His father's surprised smile and comment that "your grandfather would have said exactly the same thing" confirms that this wisdom is a family value passed down through generations.

**5.** (RL.5.2) Davi finds his grandfather's old, cracked compass and learns it was a lifesaving tool during the grandfather's fishing days, though it no longer works perfectly. When his father cautions him about its inaccuracy, Davi says he values it as a reminder of which direction to go, showing he treasures its symbolic meaning over its functionality. The theme is that objects connected to family history carry meaning beyond their practical use.

### Passage 2: "The Relay"

**6.** (RL.5.1) Kayla does not value practicing. The text says she "showed up late to half our practices" and "spent most of the time talking to friends on the sideline." When challenged, she said, "I do not need to practice. Speed is speed."

**7.** (RL.5.3) **Similarity:** Both Kayla and Andre are members of the relay team and both care about the race in their own way — Kayla through natural talent and Andre through dedicated preparation. **Differences:** (1) Kayla is naturally fast — she "exploded out of the blocks like she had springs in her shoes" — while Andre "was not the fastest" and "knew that and admitted it openly." (2) Kayla "hated practicing" and showed up late, while Andre "arrived twenty minutes early to every practice" and "studied videos of relay handoffs." Their attitudes toward preparation are opposite — Kayla relies on raw talent, while Andre relies on effort and dependability.

**8.** (RL.5.1) The narrator clearly admires Andre more than Kayla. The narrator describes Kayla's speed factually but then emphasizes her laziness ("showed up late," "talking to friends"). In contrast, the narrator calls Andre "the most dependable person I had ever met" and describes his dedication in detail (arriving early, studying videos, staying after practice). The narrator's detailed, admiring description of Andre versus the critical tone toward Kayla suggests the narrator values effort and reliability over natural ability.

**9.** (RL.5.1) The final sentence — "It was not because he was the fastest. It was because he was holding on" — means more than literally gripping the baton. It suggests that Andre won because of his character traits: his dependability, his preparation, his refusal to let go of his commitment even when facing a faster opponent. "Holding on" represents perseverance and dedication. The other runner, like Kayla, may have had more speed but lacked the reliability that comes from disciplined practice. Andre's victory came from his work ethic, not his legs.

**10.** (RL.5.3) Andre responds to not being the fastest by working harder than anyone else. He arrives early, studies technique, and practices handoffs until they are perfect — "he practiced the handoff with me until we got it right ten times in a row." He compensates for his lack of speed with preparation and reliability. This contrasts sharply with Kayla, who has natural speed but does not bother to develop other skills. Kayla's approach is "I do not need to practice. Speed is speed," while Andre's approach is to outwork his limitations. The race proves that Andre's method is more reliable.

### Passage 3: Two Libraries

**11.** (RL.5.3) **Differences:** (1) The old library was physically small and cramped — "a narrow stone building" with shelves "packed so tightly that you had to turn sideways" — while the new library is "three times the size" with "tall windows, white walls, and rows of gleaming computers." (2) The old library was low-tech — "Mrs. Okoro kept a handwritten catalog in a leather notebook because the library had never been given a computer" — while the new library has "a self-checkout machine" and an "entirely digital" catalog. **Similarity:** Both libraries serve the same community and the same purpose — the same people who used the old library (Mr. Benitez, the Pham sisters, Mrs. Okoro) are present at the opening of the new one, continuing their connection to the space.

**12.** (RL.5.1) Mrs. Okoro has mixed feelings — she appreciates the new library but holds onto the old one. She says the new library is "beautiful," showing genuine admiration. But she also brings her leather notebook and begins "copying the entries into the computer, one by one," suggesting she is not ready to let go of the old system entirely. The fact that she tucks the notebook "under one arm" throughout the opening shows it is important to her. She is transitioning respectfully, carrying the past into the future rather than discarding it.

**13.** (RL.5.1) When Mrs. Okoro says the old library "was small, but it was full," she means it was full of people's stories, memories, and experiences — not just books. The passage provides evidence: "Mr. Benitez learned to read English there," "the Pham sisters spent every Saturday afternoon in the corner," and "Darian wrote his college application essay at the table by the window." The library was physically small but emotionally and communally rich. "Full" refers to the lives that filled the space.

**14.** (RL.5.2) Theme: Progress and improvement are valuable, but the memories, relationships, and experiences built in the places we leave behind are what truly make those places meaningful, and they deserve to be carried forward. (Accept reasonable variations about honoring the past while embracing the future, or about how a place's value comes from the people and experiences it holds, not its physical features.)

### Passage 4: "My Mother's Garden" (Poem)

**15.** (RL.5.2) At the beginning, the speaker sees the garden work as foolish or pointless. The speaker says, "I used to think it was silly, / kneeling in the dirt, / hands dark with soil, / sweat dripping off her chin." By the end, the speaker has unknowingly adopted the same practice: "I found myself / outside before she woke, / pulling weeds by the marigolds, / whispering to a tomato plant." The shift is from dismissing the garden to participating in it instinctively — "I did not plan it. / My hands just knew what to do."

**16.** (RL.5.2) Theme: The traditions, habits, and values of the people who raise us become part of who we are, often in ways we do not realize until we find ourselves carrying them on. The speaker's reflection reveals this because the speaker did not consciously decide to garden — the knowledge and care were absorbed over years of watching the mother (and grandmother before her). The final lines — "I did not plan it. / My hands just knew what to do" — show that the tradition has become instinctive, passed down not through instruction but through presence and observation.

**17.** (RL.5.7) The soft guitar music and garden sounds (birdsong, wind, rustling leaves) would create a warm, peaceful atmosphere that matches the poem's setting and emotional tone. The ambient garden sounds would make the listener feel present in the garden alongside the mother and speaker. The shift in the narrator's voice from "slightly embarrassed" in stanza 3 (when the speaker admits thinking it was silly) to "warm, surprised" in stanza 4 (when the speaker realizes they are doing the same thing) would make the emotional turning point of the poem audible — the listener would hear the moment of realization rather than just reading it, adding emotional depth and intimacy.

**18.** (RL.5.7) The most important visual element would be the speaker's hands in the soil, drawn to look similar to the mother's hands from earlier stanzas — showing the family resemblance and the continuation of the tradition. This would visually reinforce the theme of traditions being passed down through generations. If the artist drew the speaker's hands and the mother's hands in a similar style or position (both kneeling, both dirt-darkened), the viewer would immediately see the connection — what the speaker inherited without realizing it. The visual parallel would make the theme visible rather than just stated.

### Passage 5: Drama — Noah and Priya

**19.** (RL.5.3) **Similarity:** Both Priya and Noah initially struggled with the same problem — not knowing how to start the assignment. Priya says, "I had the same problem. I sat in my room for an hour staring at a blank page." Noah has been "standing here for twenty minutes" staring at a blank canvas. Both experienced creative paralysis. **Difference:** By the time of the scene, Priya has already solved her problem — she "just started drawing lines" and created a detailed, meaningful drawing. Noah is still stuck and has not yet made a mark. Priya's experience and advice help Noah take his first step.

**20.** (RL.5.1) Priya's drawing reveals that she values imperfection, family, and finding beauty in unexpected places. She chose to draw her grandmother's cracked bowl, and she told her grandmother to keep it "because the crack made it look like a river running through the clay." This shows Priya sees beauty in flaws and finds meaning in objects that others would discard. She also values her grandmother and the connection to her pottery — choosing to draw "my grandmother's" hands and bowl shows family is what matters to her.

**21.** (RL.5.7) Two visual choices: (1) The crack in the bowl is drawn with "incredible detail — the edges of the crack are slightly darker, as if the artist spent more time on the imperfection than on the smooth parts." This choice makes the crack the focal point rather than the flaw, reinforcing the idea that imperfection can be the most interesting and meaningful part of something. (2) "The background is left blank — no table, no room, just the hands and the bowl floating in white space." This choice removes all distractions and forces the viewer to focus entirely on the hands and the bowl. It suggests that this simple moment — an elder's hands holding a cracked creation — contains everything that matters. The white space makes the image feel timeless and universal.

**22.** (RL.5.3) Both moments involve a person taking action without knowing what the result will be. Priya describes how she "just started drawing lines. No plan. Just lines," and the drawing became something meaningful. Noah makes "one long stroke across the canvas" and admits, "I do not know what that is yet." Both moments are acts of creative courage — beginning before knowing the end. The similarity suggests that creating something meaningful starts not with having a perfect plan but with being willing to take the first step and discover the meaning along the way.

**23.** (RL.5.2) Noah's response — "Maybe I should just start and find out" — connects to the theme that the act of beginning is more important than having a perfect plan. He was paralyzed by the assignment because he was "trying to think of what matters BEFORE I start." Priya's example taught him that meaning can emerge from the process of creating, not just from careful planning. The theme is that sometimes you discover what matters to you not by thinking about it but by doing something and letting the meaning reveal itself.

**24.** (RL.5.1) When Priya says, "That is exactly what it should be right now," she means that not knowing what the painting will become is a natural and healthy part of the creative process. She is reassuring Noah that a single blue brushstroke with no clear purpose is the right starting point — because her own meaningful drawing also began without a plan ("I just started drawing lines"). Her statement validates uncertainty and encourages Noah to trust the process rather than needing immediate answers.

### Scoring Notes
- Questions requiring multiple items (e.g., "two differences and one similarity," "two visual choices") should receive full credit if all items are complete and accurate. Award half credit if at least one item is provided correctly.
- Accept reasonable variations in theme statements as long as the student has expressed a complete thought (not just a topic word) and it is supported by the text.
- For quoting questions, the student must use quotation marks correctly around the exact words from the text. Minor punctuation errors in quotation formatting should not cost full credit if the quote itself is accurate.
- For inference questions, the student must provide a logical conclusion supported by text evidence — unsupported guesses do not earn credit.
- For visual/multimedia questions, the student must connect the element to meaning, tone, or beauty — simply describing what they see without analysis does not earn full credit.
- For comparison questions, the student must address both similarities AND differences (where requested) with specific text details.
