# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 2

## Topic
Drawing Inferences from Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define inference as a conclusion drawn from text evidence combined with background knowledge
- Distinguish between explicit information (stated directly) and inferred information (not stated but supported by evidence)
- Quote accurately from the text when explaining an inference

## Prerequisites
- Ability to locate and quote text evidence accurately (Lesson 1)
- Ability to refer to details and examples when explaining what the text says explicitly (RL.4.1)
- Understanding of character traits and feelings (RL.4.3)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Sticky notes (3-4 per student)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build intuition about inference through an everyday scenario before applying it to text.

**Activity: What Can You Figure Out?**

Say: "Imagine you walk into your kitchen and see these clues: a mixing bowl with chocolate batter on the sides, an open bag of flour, and the oven light is on. Nobody is in the room. What can you figure out?"

Allow students to share ideas (someone is baking a cake, brownies, etc.).

Say: "Did anyone tell you someone was baking? No. The clues did not say it directly. But you used what you saw plus what you already know about baking to draw a conclusion. That is called making an inference. Today, we are going to make inferences from text — figuring out things the author does not say directly by using the clues the author DOES give us."

Write on the board: **Inference = Text Evidence + What I Already Know**

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the difference between explicit information and inference, and model how to support inferences with accurate quotes.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Inference:** A conclusion reached by using clues from the text combined with background knowledge. The answer is not stated directly but is supported by evidence.
- **Explicit:** Information that is clearly and directly stated in the text.
- **Implicit:** Information that is suggested or hinted at but not directly stated.
- **Background knowledge:** What the reader already knows from personal experience or prior learning.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In Lesson 1, we practiced finding information that is right there in the text — explicit information. Today, we are going beyond what the text says directly. Sometimes authors do not tell you everything. They leave clues, and it is the reader's job to put those clues together."

Write on the board:
- **Explicit:** The text SAYS it directly. You can point to the exact sentence.
- **Inference:** The text does NOT say it directly, but the clues lead you to a logical conclusion.

Say: "Here is the key — an inference is NOT a guess. It must be supported by evidence from the text. You should be able to point to specific words or sentences that led you to your conclusion. And in fifth grade, when you explain your inference, you need to quote those words accurately."

Write on the board:
**Inference Formula:**
1. Find the clues in the text (quote them).
2. Add what you already know.
3. State your inference.

Say: "Let me show you how this works."

### Worked Examples

Say: "Follow along as I read this passage."

**Passage: "The Letter"**

> Deshawn checked the mailbox every day after school for two weeks. Each day, he flipped through the envelopes — bills for his mother, a magazine, an advertisement — and walked back inside without saying anything. On the fifteenth day, he found a thin white envelope with his name on it and a return address from the Riverside Youth Orchestra. His hands trembled as he tore it open.
>
> He read the first line three times. Then he ran inside, almost tripping on the front step, and shouted, "Mom! MOM!" He was waving the letter over his head when she came around the corner.
>
> She looked at his face and started smiling before he even said a word. "You got in?" she asked.
>
> Deshawn could not stop grinning. "I got in."

Say: "Here is an explicit question first: Who sent the letter? I can find the answer right in the text — the return address is from 'the Riverside Youth Orchestra.' That is explicit."

Say: "Now here is an inference question: Was Deshawn nervous about whether he would be accepted? The text never says 'Deshawn was nervous.' But watch what I do."

Write on the board:
- **Clue 1:** "Deshawn checked the mailbox every day after school for two weeks" — he was anxiously waiting.
- **Clue 2:** "His hands trembled as he tore it open" — his body showed nervousness.
- **What I already know:** When people wait a long time for important news and their hands tremble, it usually means they are anxious about the result.
- **My inference:** Deshawn was very nervous about whether he would be accepted into the orchestra.

Say: "Now I write my answer with quotes: Deshawn was nervous about the result. The text says he 'checked the mailbox every day after school for two weeks,' showing he was anxiously waiting, and 'his hands trembled as he tore it open,' which reveals his anxiety about the answer inside."

Say: "See how I used the exact words from the text in quotation marks to support my inference? That is the standard for fifth grade — quote accurately when drawing inferences."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice distinguishing explicit information from inferences and supporting inferences with quotes.

Say: "Let us try a new passage together. As I read, use your sticky notes to mark any clues that help you figure out something the author does not say directly."

**Passage: "The Garden Bench"**

> Every Saturday morning, Mrs. Zhao sat on the wooden bench in her front garden. She brought a thermos of tea and a book, but she rarely opened the book. Instead, she watched the street — the mail carrier making rounds, the joggers passing by, the children riding their bicycles.
>
> When anyone walked past her fence, she waved and called out, "Good morning! Beautiful day, is it not?" Most people waved back. A few stopped to chat about the weather or their gardens.
>
> One Saturday, the bench was empty. The thermos sat on the porch railing, untouched. The garden gate was closed and latched. A small paper sign was taped to the fence post, but the wind had folded it over so no one could read what it said.
>
> The mail carrier paused at the mailbox. He looked at the empty bench for a long moment, then tucked the mail gently into the box instead of pushing it through roughly like he usually did.

Say: "First, an explicit question: What did Mrs. Zhao bring to the bench every Saturday? Find the quote."

Expected: "She brought a thermos of tea and a book."

Say: "Good. Now an inference question: Why do you think Mrs. Zhao rarely opened her book? The text does not tell us directly, but what clues are there?"

Guide students to notice: "Instead, she watched the street — the mail carrier making rounds, the joggers passing by, the children riding their bicycles." Also, she greeted everyone who passed and chatted with those who stopped.

Guide students toward: Mrs. Zhao brought the book as something to do, but what she really enjoyed was connecting with her neighbors. The text says she "rarely opened the book" and "instead, she watched the street" and called out to people, suggesting that being part of the community mattered more to her than reading.

Say: "Now, a harder inference: What might have happened to Mrs. Zhao on the Saturday the bench was empty? The text does not tell us. What clues does it give?"

Guide students to notice: the untouched thermos, the closed gate, the unreadable sign, and the mail carrier's gentle, respectful behavior (he "looked at the empty bench for a long moment" and "tucked the mail gently").

Expected inference: Something serious happened to Mrs. Zhao — she may be ill, in the hospital, or may have passed away. The clues are the untouched thermos (she could not come outside), the closed gate (no one was tending the garden), the sign (possibly a notice), and the mail carrier's unusually gentle behavior, which suggests sadness or respect.

Say: "Notice that the text never says what happened. But the clues all point in the same direction, and our background knowledge helps us interpret them. That is a strong inference."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently draw inferences and support them with accurate quotes.

**Passage: "The Science Fair"**

> Priya had spent six weeks on her science fair project — a model of the water cycle made from recycled materials. She had tested it twelve times to make sure the condensation chamber worked properly. On the morning of the fair, she carried it carefully into the gymnasium and set it up on Table 14.
>
> The gymnasium buzzed with students arranging their projects. Priya glanced at the table next to hers. A boy she did not recognize had built a working solar-powered car. Across the aisle, twin sisters had constructed a detailed model of the human heart that actually pumped red liquid through clear tubes. Priya looked back at her water cycle model — the plastic bottles, the aluminum foil, the small fan she had taken from a broken computer.
>
> When the judges reached Table 14, Priya explained her project clearly. She described each stage — evaporation, condensation, precipitation — and showed how her model demonstrated the cycle in real time. One of the judges leaned forward and asked, "And you built this entirely from recycled materials?"
>
> "Yes, ma'am," Priya said. "Everything except the batteries."
>
> The judge wrote something on her clipboard and smiled. "Very resourceful," she said.
>
> That afternoon, when the winners were announced, Priya did not hear her name called for first place. She did not hear it for second, either. But when the announcer said, "And the award for Most Innovative Design goes to Priya Mehta, Table 14," Priya stood up so fast her chair nearly fell over.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences. Support each answer with at least one accurate quote from the passage.

1. What is one thing the text says explicitly about Priya's project? Quote the text in your answer.
2. How did Priya feel when she compared her project to the others? The text does not say her feelings directly — make an inference and support it with a quote.
3. What can you infer about why the judge called Priya's project "Very resourceful"? Use evidence from the text.
4. How did Priya feel when she won the award? Make an inference based on her actions. Quote the text.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 as written, with at least one quote per answer.
**Stretch:** After answering questions 1-4, explain the difference between what Priya probably expected to win and what she actually won. Use text evidence to support your explanation.
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph explaining what this story suggests about the different ways success can be measured. Support your ideas with at least two quotes from the passage.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.2) What is the theme of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.3) Describe Priya using two character traits. For each trait, provide specific evidence from the text.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following mini-passage:

> Tomas carefully wrapped the painting in brown paper and tied it with string. He wrote "For Abuela" on a small card and tucked it under the string. Then he stood back, tilted his head, and changed the card to read "For the best Abuela in the world." He placed the package by the front door next to a bunch of bright yellow flowers.

1. Is it explicitly stated that today is a special occasion? (yes or no)
2. What can you infer about why Tomas is wrapping the painting? Use at least one quote from the passage to support your inference.

**Expected Answers:**
1. No, it is not explicitly stated.
2. Tomas is probably preparing a gift for his grandmother's birthday or another celebration. The text says he wrote "For the best Abuela in the world" on the card and placed the package "next to a bunch of bright yellow flowers," which suggests he is celebrating his grandmother on a special day. He also changed the card from a simple message to a more loving one, showing he wants her to feel appreciated.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies that the occasion is inferred (not explicit) and provides at least one accurate quote to support a logical inference.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Pull the student for a brief re-teach using the inference formula: Text Evidence + Background Knowledge = Inference.
- Practice with three simple scenarios (e.g., "A girl walks in with a dripping umbrella" — what can you infer?).
- Provide a sentence frame: "The text says '___,' which makes me think ___ because ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3, which applies quoting and inference skills to determining theme through character responses.
- Encourage the student to practice identifying inferences versus explicit information in their independent reading.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Inference Detective Journal:** The student picks a passage from independent reading and creates a two-column chart: "What the Text Says" (with quotes) and "What I Can Infer." They write at least three pairs.
2. **Explicit or Inference Sort:** Give the student a set of ten statements about a familiar story. The student sorts them into "Explicit" (directly stated) and "Inference" (figured out from clues).
3. **Write the Clues:** The student writes a 4-5 sentence paragraph about a character WITHOUT directly stating the character's emotion. A partner reads it and tries to infer the emotion using quotes from the paragraph.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Most common mistake:** Students will state inferences as if they are facts without providing text support. Redirect: "That could be true, but what words in the text led you to that conclusion? Quote them."
- **Anchor chart:** Add to the class anchor chart from Lesson 1. Create a new section: "Explicit vs. Inference" with examples. Keep the inference formula visible: Text Evidence + Background Knowledge = Inference.
- **Emotional content:** "The Garden Bench" passage touches on loss. Preview this passage and be prepared to acknowledge that the topic can feel sad. Frame the discussion around how authors use clues to communicate difficult ideas gently.
- **Pacing note:** If students grasp the explicit/inference distinction quickly, spend more time on the Guided Practice passage, which has multiple layers of inference.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Explicit information:** The text states explicitly that Priya "had spent six weeks on her science fair project" and that it was "a model of the water cycle made from recycled materials." She also "had tested it twelve times to make sure the condensation chamber worked properly."
2. **Inference about Priya's feelings:** Priya likely felt insecure or intimidated when she compared her project to others. The text says she "glanced at the table next to hers" and saw a "working solar-powered car" and a heart model that "actually pumped red liquid." Then she "looked back at her water cycle model — the plastic bottles, the aluminum foil, the small fan she had taken from a broken computer." The way the text lists her simple materials right after describing the impressive projects suggests she was feeling that her project did not measure up.
3. **Inference about "Very resourceful":** The judge likely called the project "Very resourceful" because Priya built a functional scientific model entirely from items most people would throw away. The judge specifically asked, "And you built this entirely from recycled materials?" showing she was impressed that Priya achieved so much with so little. Being resourceful means making the most of what you have, which is exactly what Priya did.
4. **Inference about Priya's reaction:** Priya was surprised and thrilled when she won. The text says, "Priya stood up so fast her chair nearly fell over," which suggests she was not expecting to win anything after missing first and second place. Her sudden, dramatic movement shows excitement and shock.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.4.2) Theme: Success is not always about having the most impressive resources — creativity, hard work, and resourcefulness can earn their own kind of recognition. (Accept reasonable variations about the value of making the most of what you have.)
6. (RL.4.3) Priya is hardworking — she spent six weeks on her project and tested it twelve times. She is also resourceful — she built her entire model from recycled materials and a fan from a broken computer, showing she could create something effective without expensive supplies.
