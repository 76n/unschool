# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 10

## Topic
Reading Literature Celebration

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply all four unit standards (RL.5.1, RL.5.2, RL.5.3, RL.5.7) to a single literary text
- Demonstrate the ability to quote accurately, determine theme, compare story elements, and analyze visual/multimedia elements
- Reflect on their growth as readers throughout the unit

## Prerequisites
- All skills from Lessons 1-9: quoting accurately, drawing inferences, determining theme from character responses and poetry, summarizing, comparing characters/settings/events, and analyzing visual and multimedia elements
- Familiarity with how to support answers with text evidence

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils and colored pencils
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)
- Optional: access to classroom library for self-selected reading
- Optional: art supplies for the extension project

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Celebrate student growth and preview how all the unit's skills work together.

**Activity: Our Reading Toolkit**

Say: "We have spent nine lessons building a toolkit of reading skills. Let us review what is in that toolkit. I am going to name a skill, and I want you to give me a thumbs up if you feel confident about it, thumbs sideways if you are still working on it, and thumbs down if you need more practice."

List on the board and check each one:
1. Quoting accurately from text (RL.5.1)
2. Drawing inferences from text (RL.5.1)
3. Determining theme from character responses (RL.5.2)
4. Determining theme in poetry (RL.5.2)
5. Summarizing stories and drama (RL.5.2)
6. Comparing and contrasting characters (RL.5.3)
7. Comparing settings and events (RL.5.3)
8. Analyzing visual elements (RL.5.7)
9. Analyzing multimedia elements (RL.5.7)

Say: "Today, you are going to use ALL of these skills together on one passage. This is your chance to show what you have learned. Think of it as a celebration of everything you can do as a reader."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model how to apply multiple standards to a single passage, showing students how the skills connect.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Synthesis:** Combining multiple skills, ideas, or pieces of information to create a deeper understanding.
- **Analysis:** Examining something carefully by looking at its parts and explaining how they work together.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "So far, we have practiced each skill separately. Today, we are putting them all together. When you read a text closely, you do not just use one skill — you use them all at once. Let me show you what that looks like."

### Worked Examples

**Passage and Visual Description: "The Mural"**

> Tomás walked past the old brick wall every morning on his way to school. For as long as he could remember, it had been covered in faded advertisements and peeling paint. Nobody noticed the wall. It was just there — part of the background.
>
> Then one Monday, a woman appeared with buckets of paint and a ladder. She wore a bandana over her hair and had paint already spattered on her jeans. She did not talk to anyone. She just started painting.
>
> By Wednesday, the wall had changed. Where there had been faded letters and crumbling brick, there was now a tree — its trunk rising from the sidewalk and its branches spreading across the entire wall. The leaves were every color of autumn: red, gold, orange, amber.
>
> By Friday, the tree had birds in its branches and children playing beneath it. The children in the painting were of different ages, different sizes, different skin tones. Some were reading. Some were running. One was sitting alone under the tree, looking up at the sky.
>
> **Description of the mural (as a visual element):**
> The mural stretches twenty feet across the wall. The colors are warm and vivid against the old brick. The tree's roots are painted to look like they grow from the actual cracks in the wall, as if the art is emerging from the building itself. The child sitting alone is placed at the center of the mural, slightly larger than the others, looking upward. The sky above the tree shifts from blue at the edges to gold at the center, as if the tree is creating its own light.

> Tomás stood in front of the finished mural on Saturday morning. An old man walking his dog stopped next to him.
>
> "Used to be just a wall," the man said.
>
> Tomás nodded. "Now it is something you actually want to look at."
>
> The man tilted his head. "More than that, son. Now it is something that looks back at you."

Say: "Watch how I apply multiple skills to this passage."

Write on the board:

**Quoting Accurately (RL.5.1):**
When asked what the wall used to be, I can quote: The wall "had been covered in faded advertisements and peeling paint" and "nobody noticed the wall."

**Inference (RL.5.1):**
The text does not say the mural changed the neighborhood's spirit, but we can infer it. The old man stops to look — he says the mural "looks back at you," suggesting the art made the space feel alive and connected to the people.

**Theme (RL.5.2):**
The artist responded to an ugly, ignored wall by transforming it into something beautiful and meaningful. The theme is that art has the power to transform forgotten places and make people feel seen.

**Comparing (RL.5.3):**
We can compare the wall before and after: Before, it was "faded" and "crumbling" — part of the background nobody noticed. After, it was vivid, alive, and something that "looks back at you." The change shows how one person's creative action can transform a whole space.

**Visual Elements (RL.5.7):**
The roots growing from the actual cracks in the wall blur the line between the art and the building — suggesting the beauty was always there, hidden in the cracks. The child at the center, slightly larger, looking upward, draws the viewer's eye and creates a sense of hope. The sky shifting from blue to gold suggests the tree creates its own light — art brings light to dark places.

Say: "See how all the skills work together? Each one gives me a different lens to look through, and together they create a rich, complete understanding."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice applying multiple standards to a single text with teacher support.

Say: "Now let us apply our toolkit together to a new passage."

**Passage: "The Bridge Builder"**

> Two towns sat on opposite sides of a deep river canyon. For generations, the people in each town kept to themselves. They had their own markets, their own festivals, their own stories. The canyon was too wide and too deep to cross easily, so neither town bothered trying.
>
> Then a girl named Esi, from the town on the east side, started building a bridge. She used ropes and wooden planks and whatever she could find. People in her town laughed. "You cannot build a bridge by yourself," they said. "And even if you could, why would you? Everything we need is right here."
>
> Esi did not argue. She just kept building.
>
> One morning, a boy named Samuel from the west side of the canyon woke up and saw a rope stretching across the gap. He walked to the edge and saw Esi on the other side, tying a plank.
>
> "What are you building?" he shouted.
>
> "A bridge!" she shouted back. "But I can only build from this side. I need someone on your side too."
>
> Samuel thought about it for half a second. Then he picked up a rope and started building from his end.
>
> It took them three months. The bridge swayed in the wind and creaked when you walked on it. It was not pretty. But it held.
>
> The first person to cross was Esi's grandmother, carrying a basket of oranges. She handed them to Samuel's mother on the other side. "From our trees to your table," she said.
>
> Within a year, the two towns had a shared market, a shared festival, and more stories than either town had ever had before.

Say: "Let us go through our skills one by one. First — quote a sentence that shows what the towns were like before the bridge."

Expected: "For generations, the people in each town kept to themselves" or "The canyon was too wide and too deep to cross easily, so neither town bothered trying."

Say: "Now, what can you infer about why the people laughed at Esi?"

Expected: They thought it was impossible and unnecessary because they were comfortable with the way things were. The text says they asked, "Why would you? Everything we need is right here."

Say: "What is the theme? Think about Esi's response to the challenge."

Expected: When one person takes the initiative to connect with others, it can transform entire communities. Esi kept building despite being laughed at, and her effort eventually inspired Samuel and led to the two towns sharing markets, festivals, and stories.

Say: "Compare the two towns before and after the bridge."

Expected: Before — separated, self-contained, "kept to themselves," had their own everything. After — "a shared market, a shared festival, and more stories than either town had ever had before." The bridge transformed isolation into community.

Say: "If this story were illustrated, what visual element would be most important for communicating the theme?"

Allow open discussion. Guide toward: The bridge itself — showing it as imperfect ("swayed in the wind," "not pretty") but connecting two vibrant communities. An illustrator might show the two towns in warm, different colors, connected by the bridge in the middle. The grandmother crossing with oranges would be a powerful visual moment.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently apply all four standards to a new passage.

**Passage and Visual Description: "The Lantern Walk"**

> On the shortest night of winter, the people of Millbrook gathered in the town square for the Lantern Walk. Each family brought a handmade lantern — some made from glass jars, some from paper, some from carved gourds. No two lanterns were the same.
>
> Eleven-year-old Samir had made his lantern from a tin can with holes punched in the shape of a crescent moon. His younger sister, Farah, had made hers from a paper bag with crayon drawings of flowers on every side. Their mother carried a lantern she had inherited from her own mother — an old brass lamp with colored glass panels that cast red and green and blue light.
>
> "Why do we do this?" Farah asked as they walked.
>
> "Because in the darkest time of year, we make our own light," their mother said. "And we walk together so no one has to carry their light alone."
>
> The procession wound through the streets. Samir watched the lanterns ahead of them — dozens of small lights bobbing in the darkness, each one different, all moving in the same direction. When they reached the hilltop, everyone stopped and turned to look back at the town below. It was dark except for the trail of light they had left behind — a glowing thread connecting the square to the hill.
>
> "Look at that," Samir whispered. From up here, the individual lanterns blended into one unbroken line of light.
>
> **Description of the illustration:**
> A full-page illustration shows the hilltop scene. The sky is deep indigo, scattered with stars. The town below is a silhouette of dark buildings. The path of lantern light curves from the town square up to the hill like a golden river. On the hilltop, the faces of the townspeople are lit from below by their lanterns, creating warm upward shadows. Samir and Farah stand at the edge of the hill, looking down. Samir's tin-can lantern casts crescent-shaped light spots on the ground around his feet. Their mother stands behind them, her brass lantern painting them in red, green, and blue.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences. Use accurate quotes from the passage where required.

1. **(RL.5.1 — Quoting)** Why does Samir's family participate in the Lantern Walk? Answer using a direct quote from the text.
2. **(RL.5.1 — Inference)** What can you infer about the community of Millbrook based on their tradition? Support your inference with evidence from the text.
3. **(RL.5.2 — Theme)** What is the theme of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence and explain how it is revealed through the events and the mother's words.
4. **(RL.5.3 — Comparing)** Compare the three lanterns described in the passage (Samir's, Farah's, and their mother's). What is different about each one? What do they have in common? What does this suggest?
5. **(RL.5.7 — Visual Elements)** Analyze the illustration. Name two visual choices the artist made and explain what each adds to the meaning, tone, or beauty of the passage.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-5 as written.
**Stretch:** After answering questions 1-5, write a 3-4 sentence summary of this passage that includes the theme.
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a reflection paragraph (4-6 sentences) about which skill from this unit was the most challenging for you and which was the most rewarding. Use a specific example from the lessons.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.5) What literary form is the main passage (not the illustration description)? Name two structural elements that helped you identify it.
7. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.6) This passage is written in third person. Rewrite the first line of the last paragraph ("Look at that," Samir whispered.) as if Samir were the first-person narrator. What changes?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following mini-passage:

> When the music teacher retired, two students organized a farewell concert. Mika chose to perform a complicated piano piece, practicing for weeks until every note was perfect. Aaliyah chose to sing a simple song that the teacher had taught the class in first grade. Mika's performance impressed the audience. Aaliyah's performance made the teacher cry.

1. Compare Mika's and Aaliyah's approaches. Use a quote from the text.
2. What is the theme of this passage?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Mika focused on technical excellence — she "chose to perform a complicated piano piece" and practiced "until every note was perfect." Aaliyah focused on emotional connection — she "chose to sing a simple song that the teacher had taught the class in first grade." Both approaches showed care for the teacher, but in different ways: Mika's "impressed the audience" while Aaliyah's "made the teacher cry."
2. Theme: The most meaningful gestures are often the ones that come from the heart and shared history, not the ones that demonstrate the most skill. (Accept variations about the power of personal connection, simplicity, or honoring shared experiences.)

**Mastery = Student provides a valid comparison with at least one quote AND states a theme as a complete sentence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Identify which specific skill the student struggled with (quoting, comparing, or theme) and review the relevant lesson's anchor chart.
- Provide targeted practice using a simple passage and the sentence frames from earlier lessons.
- Consider small-group re-teaching before the unit assessment.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for the Unit Assessment.
- Celebrate their growth and encourage them to continue using their reading toolkit in all future reading.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Celebration tone:** This lesson should feel like a culmination, not a test. Frame the Independent Practice as a chance to show off skills rather than a high-pressure assessment. The actual unit assessment comes separately.
- **Flexible structure:** If time allows, consider having students share their responses to one question with a partner or the class. Hearing different interpretations deepens understanding.
- **Review of anchor charts:** Before starting Independent Practice, briefly review the anchor charts created throughout the unit. This activates all the strategies students have learned and reminds them of the tools available.
- **Pacing note:** This lesson runs slightly longer than others (45-50 minutes). If pressed for time, reduce the Guided Practice by having students discuss in pairs rather than whole-group, and extend Independent Practice time.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **(RL.5.1 — Quoting)** Samir's family participates in the Lantern Walk because of a community tradition centered on hope and togetherness. Their mother explains, "Because in the darkest time of year, we make our own light. And we walk together so no one has to carry their light alone."
2. **(RL.5.1 — Inference)** The community of Millbrook is close-knit, inclusive, and values togetherness. The text says the walk happens "on the shortest night of winter" and that "each family brought a handmade lantern," showing universal participation. The mother's statement that "we walk together so no one has to carry their light alone" implies the community believes in supporting each other through difficult times. The fact that "no two lanterns were the same" but they all move "in the same direction" suggests that individuality is celebrated within a unified community.
3. **(RL.5.2 — Theme)** Theme: Even in the darkest times, people create hope by coming together, each contributing their own unique light to form something larger and more beautiful than any one person could create alone. This is revealed through the mother's words — "we make our own light" and "we walk together" — and through the final image, where the individual lanterns "blended into one unbroken line of light." The events show that each family brings a different lantern, but together they create a single glowing path, demonstrating that collective effort creates beauty that individual effort cannot.
4. **(RL.5.3 — Comparing)** Samir's lantern is made from a tin can with holes punched in a crescent moon shape — practical and creative, using simple materials in a clever way. Farah's is a paper bag with crayon drawings of flowers — childlike, colorful, and personal. Their mother's is an inherited brass lamp with colored glass panels that casts multicolored light — traditional, beautiful, and connected to family history. All three are different in material, design, and complexity, but all serve the same purpose: making light. This suggests that there are many ways to contribute to a shared tradition, and each person's contribution — no matter how simple or elaborate — adds something valuable.
5. **(RL.5.7 — Visual Elements)** Two visual choices:
   - The lantern path is painted as "a golden river" curving from the town to the hilltop. This adds to the meaning by visually transforming individual lights into a single flowing line, reinforcing the theme that separate contributions merge into something unified and beautiful. The warm gold color against the dark indigo sky creates a tone of hope and warmth.
   - Samir's tin-can lantern casts "crescent-shaped light spots on the ground around his feet." This detail adds beauty and specificity — it shows how his unique lantern creates its own unique pattern of light, visually demonstrating the idea that each person's individual contribution has its own distinctive character even within the larger community event.

### Interleaved Review
6. (RL.4.5) The main passage is prose (a short story). Structural elements: it is written in paragraphs (not stanzas or script format), it has a narrator who describes events and characters, it includes dialogue in quotation marks, and it tells a story with a beginning, middle, and end.
7. (RL.4.6) First-person version: "Look at that," I whispered. The change is that "Samir" becomes "I" — the reader now experiences the moment directly through Samir's eyes rather than watching him from outside. In first person, the reader would also have access to Samir's internal thoughts and feelings, making the moment feel more personal and intimate.
