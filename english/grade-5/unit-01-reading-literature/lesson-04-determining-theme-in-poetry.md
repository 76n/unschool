# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 4

## Topic
Determining Theme in Poetry

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the theme of a poem by analyzing how the speaker reflects upon a topic
- Explain how poetic details (imagery, word choice, repetition) contribute to theme
- Quote specific lines from a poem to support a theme statement

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine theme from character responses in prose (Lesson 3, RL.5.2)
- Understanding that poems use stanzas, line breaks, and compressed language (RL.4.5)
- Ability to quote accurately from text (Lessons 1-2, RL.5.1)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students recognize that poets reflect on topics to communicate deeper messages, just as story characters do through their responses to challenges.

**Activity: The Poet's Lens**

Say: "In the last lesson, we found themes in stories by looking at how characters respond to challenges. Poems work a little differently. In a poem, there is usually a speaker — like the narrator — who thinks deeply about something: a moment, a feeling, a memory, an idea. The way the speaker reflects on that topic is how we find the theme."

Write on the board:
- **Story theme:** Found through how characters respond to challenges.
- **Poem theme:** Found through how the speaker reflects upon a topic.

Say: "Think about it this way. A poet looks at something — a rainy day, a grandmother's hands, a sunset — and shares what they think and feel about it. The theme is what the poet wants the reader to understand about that topic. Let me show you."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to trace a poem's theme by examining the speaker's reflection, word choice, and imagery.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Speaker:** The voice in the poem — the person whose thoughts, feelings, and observations the poem expresses. The speaker is not always the poet.
- **Reflect:** To think deeply and carefully about something.
- **Imagery:** Descriptive language that creates pictures in the reader's mind by appealing to the senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell).
- **Repetition:** When a word, phrase, or line appears more than once in a poem, often to emphasize an important idea.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Finding theme in a poem is similar to finding theme in a story, but the clues look different. In a story, we follow a character through events. In a poem, we follow a speaker through thoughts and reflections. Here is our strategy."

Write on the board:
**Finding Theme in Poetry:**
1. Identify the topic — what is the poem about on the surface?
2. Examine the speaker's reflection — how does the speaker think and feel about the topic?
3. Look for poetic clues — imagery, word choice, repetition.
4. State the theme — what message does the speaker's reflection communicate?

Say: "Let me walk through this with a poem."

### Worked Examples

**Poem: "My Grandfather's Hands"**

> My grandfather's hands are maps
> of every field he ever plowed,
> every fence he ever mended,
> every loaf of bread he kneaded
> on cold mornings before dawn.
>
> His knuckles are mountains,
> his palms wide valleys
> crisscrossed with rivers of lines.
>
> When he holds my hand,
> I feel the weight of all those years —
> not heavy, but steady,
> like an anchor in deep water.
>
> One day my hands will carry
> their own maps, their own stories.
> But for now, I hold his hand
> and read the roads.

Say: "Let me use our strategy."

Write on the board:
1. **Topic:** A grandfather's hands.
2. **Speaker's reflection:** The speaker does not just describe the hands — they see them as "maps" that tell the story of a lifetime of hard work. When the speaker holds the grandfather's hand, they feel "the weight of all those years" but describe it as "steady, like an anchor." The speaker also looks forward to the future: "One day my hands will carry their own maps."
3. **Poetic clues:**
   - **Imagery:** "His knuckles are mountains, his palms wide valleys crisscrossed with rivers of lines" — the hands are described as a landscape, making them seem large and meaningful.
   - **Repetition:** "every field... every fence... every loaf" — the repetition of "every" emphasizes how much work the grandfather has done.
   - **Word choice:** "anchor in deep water" — the grandfather provides stability.
4. **Theme:** The experiences and hard work of the older generation are carried in their very being, and connecting with that history gives the younger generation a sense of stability and purpose.

Say: "Notice that I quoted specific lines from the poem to support the theme. The theme is not about hands — it is about what the hands represent: a lifetime of work, connection between generations, and finding strength in family history."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice finding theme in a poem with teacher guidance.

Say: "Let us work through a new poem together. As I read it aloud, listen for what the speaker is reflecting on and how they feel about it."

**Poem: "The Last Day of Summer"**

> The sprinkler still spins on the lawn,
> throwing diamonds into the afternoon light,
> but the shadows stretch longer now,
> reaching for the far side of the yard.
>
> My towel is still damp from the morning swim.
> The popsicle stain on my shirt
> has not yet faded.
> But I can feel it —
> the shift, the tilt,
> like a book with only one chapter left.
>
> Tomorrow, new shoes.
> Tomorrow, a backpack full of sharp pencils.
> Tomorrow, the alarm clock wins.
>
> But today — today the sprinkler still spins.
> And I stand in it one more time,
> letting the cold water
> catch me off guard,
> the way summer always does
> just before it slips away.

Say: "Step 1 — what is the topic of this poem?"

Expected: The last day of summer / the end of summer vacation.

Say: "Step 2 — how does the speaker reflect on this topic? What are they feeling?"

Guide students to notice: The speaker is savoring the final moments, aware that summer is ending. They describe the shift "like a book with only one chapter left." They contrast today with tomorrow ("new shoes," "alarm clock"). But they choose to stand in the sprinkler "one more time."

Say: "Step 3 — what poetic clues do you notice?"

Guide students to identify:
- **Imagery:** "throwing diamonds into the afternoon light," "shadows stretch longer," "cold water catch me off guard"
- **Repetition:** "Tomorrow" repeated three times to emphasize what is coming; "still" appears twice to emphasize what remains
- **Word choice:** "slips away" suggests summer leaves quietly and quickly

Say: "Step 4 — what is the theme?"

Guide toward: **Appreciating the present moment is important, especially when you know change is coming. Rather than worrying about what is ahead, there is value in fully experiencing what is right in front of you.** (Accept variations about savoring the present, transitions, or the bittersweet nature of change.)

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently determine the theme of a poem and support it with quoted lines.

**Poem: "The Dandelion"**

> Everyone pulls you from the garden,
> calls you a weed,
> tosses you into the pile with the cuttings
> and the clippings and the things
> nobody wanted.
>
> But I have watched you
> push through cracks in the sidewalk
> where nothing else dares to grow.
> I have seen you bloom bright yellow
> in fields that have been mowed
> three times this month.
>
> You do not ask for special soil
> or careful watering.
> You do not wait for someone
> to plant you in a row.
>
> You just grow.
>
> And when you are finished blooming,
> you turn yourself into a hundred tiny wishes
> and let the wind carry you
> to places no one else would think to go.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences. Quote specific lines from the poem to support your answers.

1. What is the topic of this poem on the surface?
2. How does the speaker feel about the dandelion? Is the speaker's view the same as "everyone" else's view? Quote lines to support your answer.
3. What poetic details (imagery, word choice, repetition) help communicate the speaker's message? Identify at least two and explain how they contribute.
4. What is the theme of this poem? Write it as a complete sentence and explain how the speaker's reflection reveals it.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 as written.
**Stretch:** After answering questions 1-4, explain how this poem's theme is similar to or different from the theme of the passage "The Science Fair" from Lesson 2 (about Priya's recycled-material project).
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write your own short poem (6-10 lines) in which a speaker reflects on something ordinary and reveals a deeper message. Identify the theme of your poem in one sentence.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.5) What literary form is this text? Name two structural elements that helped you identify it.
6. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.2) In one sentence, explain the difference between the topic of this poem and the theme of this poem.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following short poem:

> **Footprints**
>
> After the snow falls,
> I trace my path from the front door
> to the bus stop and back.
> Two lines of footprints —
> one going, one coming home.
>
> By afternoon, they have already started to fill,
> softened by new snow.
> By tomorrow, they will be gone.
>
> But I was here. I walked this way.
> And the ground remembers, even if the snow does not.

1. What is the speaker reflecting on?
2. What is the theme of this poem? Write it as a complete sentence.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The speaker is reflecting on the impermanence of their footprints in the snow — how they disappear quickly — and what that means about being present and leaving a mark.
2. Theme: Even though the visible signs of our presence may fade, the fact that we were here and the experiences we had still matter. (Accept reasonable variations about impermanence, memory, or the significance of small moments.)

**Mastery = Student identifies the speaker's topic of reflection AND states a theme as a complete sentence that goes beyond the surface topic.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the difference between topic and theme using the poem from Direct Instruction.
- Have the student practice: "The topic is ___, but the theme is what the speaker is SAYING about that topic."
- Re-read the exit ticket poem together and point out specific lines that reveal the speaker's feelings.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 5, which focuses on summarizing stories and drama.
- Encourage the student to find and read a poem independently and identify its theme using the four-step strategy.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Poetry Theme Collection:** The student reads two additional short poems and records each poem's topic, the speaker's reflection, and the theme in a three-column chart.
2. **Imagery Inventory:** The student selects one poem from the lesson and highlights every image (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell), then explains in a short paragraph how the imagery supports the theme.
3. **Poet's Chair:** The student shares the dandelion poem or another poem from the lesson with a family member and explains the theme using quoted lines.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Most common mistake:** Students will say the theme of a poem is the topic ("The theme is dandelions"). Redirect: "Dandelions is the topic. What is the poet saying ABOUT dandelions? What lesson or message is the speaker sharing?"
- **Reading poems aloud:** Always read poems aloud at least twice — once for enjoyment and once for analysis. Poetry is meant to be heard, and students will catch nuances on the second reading.
- **Connecting to Lesson 3:** Remind students that in stories, theme comes from character responses to challenges. In poems, theme comes from the speaker's reflection. Both require looking beneath the surface.
- **Pacing note:** The dandelion poem may generate rich discussion about what it means to be underestimated or overlooked. Allow this conversation if time permits — it deepens theme understanding.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Topic:** A dandelion (or dandelions / a common flower that people consider a weed).
2. **Speaker's view:** The speaker admires the dandelion and sees it very differently from "everyone" else. While "everyone pulls you from the garden, / calls you a weed, / tosses you into the pile," the speaker celebrates the dandelion's strength: "I have watched you / push through cracks in the sidewalk / where nothing else dares to grow." The speaker sees beauty and resilience where others see a nuisance.
3. **Poetic details (any two):**
   - **Imagery:** "push through cracks in the sidewalk" and "bloom bright yellow / in fields that have been mowed / three times this month" create a vivid picture of the dandelion's persistence and strength. These images make the reader admire the dandelion's ability to thrive despite harsh conditions.
   - **Repetition / Parallel structure:** "You do not ask for special soil / or careful watering. / You do not wait for someone / to plant you in a row." The repeated "You do not" emphasizes the dandelion's independence and self-reliance.
   - **Word choice:** "a hundred tiny wishes" transforms the dandelion's seeds from something ordinary into something magical and hopeful. "Let the wind carry you / to places no one else would think to go" suggests that being ordinary allows the dandelion to go where others cannot.
4. **Theme:** Strength and beauty are not always found where people expect — those who are overlooked or dismissed often possess the most resilience and the ability to thrive where others cannot. The speaker reveals this by contrasting how "everyone" treats the dandelion (pulling it, calling it a weed) with the dandelion's remarkable qualities (growing through cracks, blooming after being mowed, spreading to new places). The speaker's admiration for the dandelion shows that what the world dismisses can be what the world most needs to notice.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.4.5) This text is a poem. Structural elements: it is organized into stanzas (five stanzas separated by blank lines), it uses line breaks (each idea starts on a new line rather than flowing in paragraphs), and it uses imagery and compressed language rather than narrative prose.
6. (RL.4.2) The topic of the poem is a dandelion (one word), but the theme is a complete message about that topic: being underestimated or overlooked does not mean you lack strength or value. The topic tells what the poem is about; the theme tells what the poem is saying about that subject.
