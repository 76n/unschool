# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 8

## Topic
Visual Elements in Literature

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify visual elements in illustrated texts, graphic novels, and picture-text combinations
- Analyze how visual elements (color, size, layout, facial expressions, panel arrangement) contribute to the meaning and tone of a text
- Explain in writing how a visual element adds something that the words alone do not convey

## Prerequisites
- Ability to compare and contrast elements of a text using specific details (Lessons 6-7, RL.5.3)
- Understanding of story elements: character, setting, mood, theme (RL.4.2, RL.4.3)
- Ability to quote from and reference specific parts of a text (Lessons 1-2, RL.5.1)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils and colored pencils
- Printed or projected reading passages with visual descriptions (provided below)
- Sample graphic novel pages or illustrated text excerpts (teacher-prepared)
- Optional: actual graphic novel pages to project or distribute

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students recognize that visual elements carry meaning, not just decoration.

**Activity: A Picture Is Worth...**

Say: "Think about this: If you see a book cover with dark colors, a shadowy forest, and a character looking over their shoulder, what kind of story do you expect? Now imagine a different cover — bright colors, a character laughing, sunshine everywhere. Same question: What kind of story do you expect?"

Allow students to share predictions.

Say: "You just did something important. You read the visual elements — the colors, the expressions, the setting — and they told you something about the story's tone and mood BEFORE you read a single word. In literature that includes illustrations, graphic novels, and multimedia, the visual elements are not just decoration. They are part of the storytelling. Today, we are going to learn how to analyze them."

Write on the board: **Visual elements = colors, expressions, sizes, layout, panel arrangement, perspective**

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students the vocabulary and framework for analyzing visual elements in illustrated literary texts.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Visual element:** Any part of a text you can see rather than read — illustrations, colors, panel layout, character expressions, size and scale, borders, and backgrounds.
- **Tone:** The feeling or attitude conveyed by a text (or image) — e.g., serious, playful, mysterious, hopeful.
- **Panel:** A single frame or box in a graphic novel or comic that contains an image and sometimes text.
- **Layout:** How images and text are arranged on a page — what is big, what is small, what is centered, what is in the margin.
- **Perspective:** The angle or viewpoint from which an image is shown (e.g., close-up, wide shot, bird's-eye view).

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When we read a story that is only words, the author uses language to create images in our minds. But when a story includes illustrations or is told as a graphic novel, the artist makes choices that add layers of meaning. We need to learn to read those choices."

Write on the board:
**Questions to Ask About Visual Elements:**
1. What do I SEE? (Describe the image.)
2. What CHOICES did the artist make? (Color, size, expression, layout)
3. How does this image make me FEEL? (Tone/mood)
4. What does the image ADD that the words alone do not tell me?

Say: "That fourth question is the most important. A good illustration does not just repeat what the words say — it adds something new. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

Say: "I am going to describe a page from an illustrated story. In a real classroom, you would see this image projected or printed. For now, I will describe the visual elements so we can analyze them together."

**Passage and Visual Description: "The Ocean Room" (Illustrated Story)**

> **Text on the page:**
> Kaya had not spoken a single word since moving to the new apartment. Her mother tried everything — favorite meals, trips to the park, new crayons. But Kaya just sat by the window, watching the pigeons on the fire escape and thinking about the ocean they had left behind.
>
> **Description of the illustration:**
> The illustration shows Kaya sitting on a windowsill, knees drawn up, looking out. The room behind her is drawn in muted grays and browns — the walls are bare, boxes are stacked in corners, and the light is dim. But through the window, the artist has painted a vivid blue sky with hints of turquoise, and if you look closely, the clouds are shaped like waves. Kaya's reflection in the window glass shows a faint smile, even though her actual face, turned away from the viewer, shows no expression. The illustration takes up three-quarters of the page, with the text in a small block at the bottom.

Say: "Let me analyze this using our four questions."

Write on the board:
1. **What do I see?** Kaya sitting at a window. The room is gray and dull. The view outside is vivid blue with wave-shaped clouds. Her reflection shows a smile her face does not.
2. **What choices did the artist make?**
   - Color: gray/brown room vs. vivid blue outside — contrast between her current life and what she misses
   - Detail: wave-shaped clouds — connecting the sky to the ocean she left behind
   - Reflection: showing a smile in the glass that is not on her actual face — suggesting her inner feelings differ from what she shows
   - Layout: the image dominates the page, with minimal text — making the visual the primary storytelling tool
3. **How does this make me feel?** Lonely and sad, but also hopeful — the blue outside and the hidden smile suggest Kaya still has joy inside, even if she is not expressing it.
4. **What does the image ADD that the words do not?** The text says Kaya has not spoken and is "thinking about the ocean." But the illustration shows us the wave-shaped clouds and the hidden smile — we can see that Kaya's imagination is still alive and that she finds comfort in seeing the sky. The text tells us she is silent; the image shows us she is not empty.

Say: "This is what we mean by visual elements contributing to meaning. The image and the text work together, but the image tells us things the words cannot."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice analyzing visual elements with teacher support.

Say: "Now let us analyze another illustrated passage together."

**Passage and Visual Description: "The Final Out" (Graphic Novel Page)**

> **Panel 1 (large, top half of page):**
> Text: "Bottom of the ninth. Two outs. Full count."
> Image: A wide shot of a baseball field at dusk. The stadium lights are on, casting long shadows. The batter (Zoe) stands at the plate, bat raised. The image is drawn from behind her, so the reader sees the vast field and the pitcher in the distance. The perspective makes the pitcher look far away and the field enormous. Zoe appears small compared to the space around her.
>
> **Panel 2 (small, bottom left):**
> Text: No words.
> Image: Close-up of Zoe's hands gripping the bat. Her knuckles are white. A bead of sweat runs down her wrist.
>
> **Panel 3 (small, bottom center):**
> Text: No words.
> Image: Close-up of the pitcher's face. He is squinting, jaw tight, eyes locked on the catcher's mitt.
>
> **Panel 4 (small, bottom right):**
> Text: "She swung."
> Image: Motion lines show the bat cutting through the air. The ball is a blur of white. The image is tilted slightly, creating a sense of energy and movement.

Say: "Let us use our four questions. What do you see in Panel 1?"

Expected: Wide shot of the field at dusk, Zoe at the plate, drawn from behind her. She looks small; the field looks huge.

Say: "What choice did the artist make by drawing Zoe from behind and making her look small?"

Guide students: This perspective makes the moment feel overwhelming. The reader sees what Zoe sees — a giant field, a distant pitcher, the weight of the situation. It communicates pressure and isolation.

Say: "What do Panels 2 and 3 add? They have no words."

Guide students: Panel 2 (Zoe's white knuckles, sweat) and Panel 3 (the pitcher's squinting, tight jaw) show physical tension that the words do not describe. The close-ups make the reader feel the intensity of the moment. Using no words creates silence — the reader experiences the suspense.

Say: "How does Panel 4 use visual elements?"

Guide students: The motion lines and tilted frame create energy and speed. The ball is a blur, showing how fast everything happened. The text — just two words, "She swung" — combined with the dynamic image makes the moment feel explosive.

Say: "What does the whole page add that words alone could not? Write one sentence in your notebook."

Expected: The visual elements create suspense and intensity that words alone cannot — the silence of wordless panels, the contrast between the wide field and tight close-ups, and the motion lines all make the reader feel like they are in the stadium watching the moment unfold.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently analyze visual elements and their contribution to meaning.

**Passage and Visual Description: "The Memory Box" (Illustrated Story)**

> **Text:**
> Grandpa Isaac opened the wooden box slowly. Inside were things that did not look special — a brass button, a folded letter, a pressed flower, and a small black-and-white photograph of a woman standing in front of a house.
>
> "Who is she?" Amira asked.
>
> "That is your great-grandmother, Esther," Grandpa Isaac said. "She carried this button from the old country. It was the only thing she brought."
>
> **Description of the illustration:**
> The illustration fills the entire page. Grandpa Isaac and Amira sit at a kitchen table, the box between them. The room around them is drawn in soft, warm tones — golden light from a window, a yellow tablecloth, a green plant on the counter. But INSIDE the box, the artist uses a completely different style. The objects in the box are drawn in fine, detailed black-and-white ink, as if they belong to a different time. The brass button catches a tiny glint of gold — the only color inside the box. The photograph of Esther is drawn with incredible detail, as if she could step out of the picture. Grandpa Isaac's hands, holding the box open, are wrinkled and spotted with age, drawn with more detail than anything else on the page. Amira's face is turned toward Grandpa Isaac, her eyes wide with attention.

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences.

1. Describe the artist's color choices. What colors are used for the room? What style is used for the objects inside the box? Why might the artist have made this choice?
2. The brass button has "a tiny glint of gold — the only color inside the box." What might this detail suggest about the button's importance?
3. Why do you think the artist drew Grandpa Isaac's hands with "more detail than anything else on the page"?
4. How do the visual elements on this page contribute to the meaning of the story? Explain what the illustration adds that the words alone do not convey.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 as written.
**Stretch:** After answering questions 1-4, explain how the visual elements help establish the tone of this page. What is the tone, and which visual choices create it?
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, imagine you are the illustrator for the next page — where Grandpa Isaac tells the story of Esther's journey. Describe what you would draw and explain your visual choices (color, perspective, layout, expressions).

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.2) What is the theme of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.3) Describe Grandpa Isaac using one character trait. Provide specific evidence from either the text or the illustration description.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following brief description:

> A graphic novel page shows a character standing at the edge of a cliff. The character is drawn very small at the bottom of the panel. The sky above takes up most of the image and is filled with swirling, dark purple and gray clouds. The character's face is not visible — they are shown from behind.

1. What mood or tone do the visual elements create?
2. Name one visual choice the artist made and explain what it adds to the meaning.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The visual elements create a mood of loneliness, uncertainty, or feeling overwhelmed. The small character against the vast, dark sky suggests vulnerability or facing something much bigger than themselves.
2. Any one of the following: (a) The character is drawn very small while the sky is enormous — this makes the character seem insignificant or overwhelmed by what they are facing. (b) The dark purple and gray swirling clouds suggest turmoil, danger, or emotional intensity. (c) The character is shown from behind, so we cannot see their face — this creates mystery about how they feel and also allows the reader to project their own emotions onto the character.

**Mastery = Student identifies a mood/tone AND explains one visual choice with a clear connection to meaning.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the four analysis questions (See, Choices, Feel, Add).
- Practice with a simple example: show (or describe) a bright, colorful illustration and a dark, shadowy one. Ask: "How does each make you feel? What choices create that feeling?"
- Provide a sentence frame: "The artist chose to ___, which makes the reader feel ___ because ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9, which extends visual analysis to multimedia elements.
- Encourage the student to examine illustrations in books they are reading and think about what the images add to the story.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Illustration Redesign:** The student picks one passage from an earlier lesson in this unit and designs an illustration for it. They write a paragraph explaining their visual choices (color, perspective, expressions) and what their illustration adds to the text.
2. **Graphic Novel Page:** The student creates a single graphic novel page (3-4 panels) retelling a scene from a story they know. They include visual elements that communicate tone and meaning.
3. **Visual Element Review:** The student finds an illustrated book or graphic novel and writes a short analysis of one page, answering the four questions from this lesson.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Adapting for visual access:** If you cannot project actual graphic novel pages, the written visual descriptions in this lesson are designed to be analyzed through careful reading. However, if possible, supplement with actual images — even a few pages from a graphic novel make a significant difference.
- **Most common mistake:** Students will describe what they see without analyzing WHY the artist made those choices. Redirect: "You told me what the picture shows. Now tell me why the artist drew it that way. What does it add?"
- **Connecting to prior lessons:** Remind students that all their skills — quoting, inferring, identifying theme, comparing — apply when analyzing visual elements too. They are reading images the same way they read words.
- **Pacing note:** This lesson introduces new vocabulary (panel, layout, perspective). If students need extra time with vocabulary, spend less time on the extension and more time on the Guided Practice analysis.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Color choices:** The room is drawn in warm tones — "golden light from a window, a yellow tablecloth, a green plant" — creating a cozy, safe, present-day atmosphere. The objects inside the box are drawn in "fine, detailed black-and-white ink, as if they belong to a different time." The artist likely made this choice to show the contrast between the warm, living present and the preserved, historical past. The different styles visually separate "now" from "then" while keeping them on the same page, showing how memory and the present coexist.
2. **The gold glint on the button:** The fact that the button has "the only color inside the box" draws the reader's eye to it and suggests it holds special significance. Since the text says it was "the only thing she brought" from the old country, the gold glint may represent hope, value, or survival — a small but precious object that connects the family to their history. The single point of color in the black-and-white box makes the button feel like a treasure.
3. **Grandpa Isaac's hands:** The artist drew his hands with the most detail because his hands are the connection between past and present — they hold the box open, literally bridging the old memories and the new generation. The "wrinkled and spotted" detail shows his age and suggests that his hands, like the objects in the box, carry the marks of time and experience. They are the most important visual element because they represent the act of passing down stories and memories.
4. **Visual contribution to meaning:** The illustration adds several things the words alone do not convey: the contrast between warm present and black-and-white past visually shows how memory exists alongside daily life; the gold glint on the button emphasizes its importance without needing words; Grandpa Isaac's detailed hands suggest the physicality of holding history; and Amira's wide-eyed expression shows her engagement and wonder, making the reader feel the importance of this moment of intergenerational connection.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.4.2) Theme: The objects and stories passed down through families may seem small or ordinary, but they carry deep meaning and connect us to the people and experiences that shaped who we are. (Accept reasonable variations about the value of family history, memory, and storytelling.)
6. (RL.4.3) Grandpa Isaac is sentimental and values family history. He keeps the wooden box with its simple objects — a button, a letter, a flower, a photograph — and carefully opens it to share the stories behind them with his granddaughter. The fact that he knows the history of each item and shares it with Amira shows he treasures these connections to the past.
