# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 1

## Topic
Quoting Accurately from Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Locate specific evidence in a text that answers a question about what the text says explicitly
- Use quotation marks correctly when citing words directly from a passage
- Explain how a direct quote supports a claim or answer about the text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to refer to details and examples in a text when explaining what the text says (RL.4.1)
- Ability to determine a theme of a story from details in the text (RL.4.2)
- Understanding of how to identify key events and characters in a story

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or chart paper and markers
- Student notebooks or lined paper
- Pencils
- Sticky notes (3-4 per student)
- Printed or projected reading passages (provided below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about using text evidence and introduce the concept of quoting.

**Activity: The Exact Words Matter**

Say: "Imagine your friend tells you something surprising, and you want to tell someone else exactly what your friend said. You would not change the words around — you would repeat the exact words. That is what quoting means. When we quote from a text, we use the author's exact words and put them inside quotation marks."

Write on the board: **Quoting = Using the EXACT words from the text inside quotation marks.**

Say: "Last year, you practiced finding evidence in a text to support your answers. This year, we are going to take that skill further. Instead of just pointing to a part of the text, you are going to learn to pull out the exact words and put them in your writing. Let me show you what that looks like."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to identify relevant quotes, copy them accurately, and integrate them into a written response.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Quote:** The exact words taken from a text, placed inside quotation marks.
- **Quotation marks:** The punctuation marks (" ") placed around a speaker's or author's exact words.
- **Explicit:** Clearly stated in the text — the information is right there on the page.
- **Text evidence:** Specific details, words, or sentences from a passage that support an answer or claim.

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When someone asks you a question about a story, you need to go back to the text and find the exact words that answer the question. Then you put those words in quotation marks in your response. Watch how I do this."

Write on the board:
- **Weak answer:** Maya was sad about moving.
- **Strong answer:** Maya was sad about moving. The text states, "She sat on the empty floor and stared at the bare walls, wishing she could stay."

Say: "See the difference? The strong answer uses the exact words from the text. The quotation marks show which words came from the story, not from me. This is what we mean by quoting accurately."

Write on the board — **Three Steps to Quoting:**
1. Read the question carefully.
2. Go back to the text and find the exact words that answer the question.
3. Copy the words exactly and put them in quotation marks.

Say: "Let me show you these steps in action with a real passage."

### Worked Examples

Say: "Listen carefully while I read this short story aloud. Follow along and notice the details."

**Passage: "The Violin"**

> Amara had never held a violin before the day Ms. Tran placed one in her hands. It was lighter than she expected, and the wood was smooth and cool against her chin. When Ms. Tran showed her how to draw the bow across the strings, the sound that came out was thin and scratchy, like a door creaking open.
>
> "That is completely normal," Ms. Tran said, smiling. "Every musician starts with that sound."
>
> Amara practiced every afternoon for three weeks. Her fingers ached from pressing the strings, and the scratchy sound did not go away for days. But slowly, note by note, the music began to change. By the end of the month, she could play a simple melody — just eight notes in a row — without a single screech.
>
> She played it for her grandmother that evening. When she finished, her grandmother wiped her eyes and said, "That was the most beautiful thing I have heard all week."
>
> Amara smiled. The violin did not feel strange in her hands anymore. It felt like it belonged there.

Say: "Now, here is a question: How did Amara feel about the violin at the end of the passage? Let me model my thinking."

Say: "I need to go back to the text and find the exact words that tell me how Amara feels at the end. Let me reread the last paragraph. I see this sentence: 'The violin did not feel strange in her hands anymore. It felt like it belonged there.' Those are the exact words I need."

Write on the board:
**Question:** How did Amara feel about the violin at the end of the passage?
**Answer:** By the end of the passage, Amara felt comfortable and connected to the violin. The text says, "The violin did not feel strange in her hands anymore. It felt like it belonged there."

Say: "Notice three things about my answer. First, I answered the question in my own words. Second, I found the exact quote from the text. Third, I put quotation marks around the author's exact words. That is quoting accurately."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice locating and quoting text evidence with teacher support.

Say: "Now it is your turn. I will read a new passage, and we will practice finding quotes together. Use your sticky notes to mark sentences that seem important."

**Passage: "The Bridge"**

> Kenji stood at the edge of the rope bridge and looked down. The river below was narrow but fast, churning white around the rocks. The bridge swayed gently even though nobody was walking on it.
>
> "You do not have to cross if you do not want to," said his cousin Hana, who was already standing on the other side. She had run across in about ten seconds, laughing the whole way.
>
> Kenji gripped the rope railings. His palms were sweating. He told himself that hundreds of people crossed this bridge every day, that the ropes were thick and strong, that the wooden planks were bolted down tight. None of that made his feet want to move.
>
> "I am coming," Kenji said, even though his voice did not sound very convincing. He took one step, then another. The bridge bounced slightly with each step. He kept his eyes on Hana and did not look down again.
>
> When he reached the other side, Hana grinned. "See? Not so bad."
>
> Kenji let out a breath he did not know he had been holding. "Not so bad," he repeated, and he meant it.

Say: "Here is our question: Was Kenji afraid of crossing the bridge? Find the exact words from the text that prove your answer. Write your quote on your sticky note."

Allow 60 seconds for students to find evidence.

Say: "What did you find? Let us share."

Expected responses:
- "His palms were sweating."
- "None of that made his feet want to move."
- "His voice did not sound very convincing."

Say: "Excellent. Now let us put it into a full answer together."

Guide students to write: **Yes, Kenji was afraid of crossing the bridge. The text tells us, "His palms were sweating," and "None of that made his feet want to move," which shows he was nervous and reluctant.**

Say: "Now try a second question on your own. How did Kenji feel after he crossed the bridge? Find the quote and write a complete answer with quotation marks."

Expected answer: After crossing the bridge, Kenji felt relieved and even a little proud. The text says, "Kenji let out a breath he did not know he had been holding" and he repeated "Not so bad" and "meant it," showing he realized the experience was not as frightening as he had expected.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently locate quotes and integrate them into written responses.

**Passage: "The Seed Jar"**

> Every spring, Nadia and her father walked through the neighborhood collecting seeds. They gathered them in a glass jar — tiny black specks from marigolds, flat pale discs from sunflowers, and rough brown pods from the bean plants that grew along Mr. Ortega's fence.
>
> "Why do we save seeds when we can just buy new ones at the store?" Nadia asked one morning, pinching a sunflower seed between her fingers.
>
> Her father knelt beside a patch of wildflowers. "Because these seeds know this soil," he said. "They grew here. They survived the heat and the cold and the rain. When we plant them again, they already know how to thrive in this place."
>
> Nadia looked at the small seed in her hand. It seemed impossible that something so tiny could remember anything. But when she thought about it, the sunflowers along their fence did seem to grow taller every year, their golden faces wider and brighter.
>
> She dropped the seed into the jar with a soft clink. The jar was almost full now, packed with hundreds of tiny beginnings.
>
> "Ready for next spring," Nadia said.
>
> Her father smiled. "Ready for next spring."

**Directions:** Answer the following questions in complete sentences. Each answer MUST include at least one direct quote from the passage, properly placed in quotation marks.

1. Why does Nadia's father prefer saving seeds instead of buying new ones? Use a quote from the text in your answer.
2. What does Nadia think about her father's explanation? Quote the text to support your answer.
3. What does the sentence "The jar was almost full now, packed with hundreds of tiny beginnings" suggest about how Nadia feels about the seed collection?
4. How does the passage end? Use a direct quote to describe the final moment between Nadia and her father.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4 as written, using at least one quote per answer.
**Stretch:** After answering questions 1-4, explain in 2-3 sentences why quoting from the text makes your answers stronger than just summarizing what happened.
**Challenge:** After answering all questions, write a paragraph that compares Nadia's attitude toward the seeds at the beginning of the passage to her attitude at the end. Use at least two quotes to support your comparison.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.2) What is the theme of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence. Remember, the theme is the lesson or message, not just the topic.
6. (Grade 4 review — RL.4.3) Describe Nadia's father using one character trait. Provide evidence from the text to support the trait you chose.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following mini-passage:

> Leo wanted to quit the swim team after he finished last in every race. But Coach Daniels pulled him aside and said, "Improvement is not about where you finish — it is about whether you are faster today than you were yesterday." Leo looked at his time sheet and realized he had shaved two seconds off his best time.

1. What did Coach Daniels say to encourage Leo? Use the exact quote from the text.
2. What evidence from the passage shows that Coach Daniels was right?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Coach Daniels said, "Improvement is not about where you finish — it is about whether you are faster today than you were yesterday."
2. The text states that "he had shaved two seconds off his best time," which proves he was improving even though he finished last.

**Mastery = Student correctly copies the quote with quotation marks and uses at least one piece of text evidence in a complete sentence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Pull the student for a brief re-teach during the next session's warm-up.
- Practice the three-step process (read the question, find the exact words, copy with quotation marks) using a simple paragraph.
- Provide a sentence frame: "The text says, '___,' which shows that ___."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 2, which builds on quoting by teaching students to draw inferences from text.
- Encourage the student to practice quoting in their independent reading responses.

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Quote Collector:** The student starts a section in their notebook titled "Powerful Quotes." Each time they read, they copy one sentence that stood out and write one sentence explaining why it matters to the story.
2. **Quote vs. Paraphrase:** Give the student a set of five statements about a passage. The student identifies which statements are direct quotes and which are paraphrases, then converts the paraphrases into properly formatted quotes.
3. **Write and Quote:** The student writes a short book recommendation (3-4 sentences) for a book they have read, including at least one direct quote from the book to support why they liked it.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- **Most common mistake:** Students will paraphrase instead of quoting. Remind them every time: "Did you use the exact words from the text? Show me the quotation marks."
- **Anchor chart:** After this lesson, create a class anchor chart titled "How to Quote from a Text" with the three-step process and an example. Reference this chart throughout the unit.
- **Quotation mark placement:** Some students may struggle with where to place quotation marks. Emphasize that the marks go around the author's exact words only, not around the student's own words.
- **Pacing note:** If students grasp the quoting mechanics quickly during Guided Practice, spend extra time on Independent Practice having them explain WHY the quote supports their answer.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. **Nadia's father prefers saving seeds because they are adapted to the local environment.** He explains, "Because these seeds know this soil. They grew here. They survived the heat and the cold and the rain. When we plant them again, they already know how to thrive in this place."
2. **Nadia is skeptical at first but begins to see the truth in her father's words.** The text says, "It seemed impossible that something so tiny could remember anything," showing her doubt. But then she notices "the sunflowers along their fence did seem to grow taller every year, their golden faces wider and brighter," suggesting she is beginning to believe.
3. **The phrase "packed with hundreds of tiny beginnings" suggests Nadia sees the seeds as full of promise and potential.** By calling them "beginnings," the text shows that Nadia appreciates that each seed represents a future plant, making the collection feel hopeful and meaningful rather than just a jar of small objects.
4. **The passage ends with Nadia and her father sharing a quiet, content moment.** Nadia says, "Ready for next spring," and her father repeats the same words: "Ready for next spring." The repetition and his smile suggest they are connected in their shared tradition and looking forward to continuing it.

### Interleaved Review
5. (RL.4.2) Theme: Things that have survived challenges carry wisdom and strength, and traditions passed down through generations have lasting value. (Accept reasonable variations about the value of experience, tradition, or working with nature rather than replacing it.)
6. (RL.4.3) Nadia's father is patient and thoughtful. He takes the time to kneel beside the wildflowers and explain to Nadia why they save seeds rather than simply telling her to do it. His explanation — "these seeds know this soil" — shows he thinks carefully about the natural world and wants to share that understanding with his daughter.
