# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 4: Grammar and Language

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview

Unit 4 deepens students' command of English grammar and language conventions, building on the foundations established in Grade 4. Students learn to use correlative conjunctions to create balanced, sophisticated sentences; to manage verb tense with precision, recognizing and correcting inappropriate shifts; to punctuate items in a series using commas and semicolons; and to expand, combine, and reduce sentences for meaning, interest, and style. Throughout the unit, grammar instruction is explicit and systematic, with frequent opportunities for both identification (finding errors) and production (writing correctly). The unit culminates in a review and celebration lesson where students demonstrate mastery across all four standards.

- **L.4.1a:** Use relative pronouns (who, whose, whom, which, that) and relative adverbs (where, when, why)
- **L.4.1b:** Form and use the progressive verb tenses (e.g., I was walking, I am walking, I will be walking)

### Facilitation Tips

- **Model thinking aloud.** When demonstrating grammar concepts, narrate your decision-making process so students can internalize the reasoning, not just the rules. For example, say "I see two things being connected, so I need a correlative conjunction pair."
- **Use color-coding consistently.** Assign a specific color to each grammar concept throughout the unit (e.g., blue for conjunctions, red for verb tense, green for punctuation, purple for sentence structure). This visual anchor helps students categorize and recall concepts.
- **Provide sentence frames.** For students who struggle with production tasks, offer sentence starters that incorporate the target grammar structure. Gradually remove scaffolding as confidence builds.
- **Connect grammar to writing purpose.** Regularly ask students why a particular grammar choice matters for meaning or reader experience, rather than treating grammar as arbitrary rules.
- **Use student-generated examples.** After introducing a concept, invite students to create their own example sentences. Peer-generated examples often resonate more than textbook sentences.
- **Revisit previous lessons briefly.** Spend 1-2 minutes at the start of each lesson reviewing a concept from the prior lesson. Cumulative review prevents forgetting and builds connections across the unit.
- **Celebrate growth, not perfection.** Grammar can feel intimidating. Acknowledge effort and improvement, and normalize the process of drafting, revising, and correcting.

### Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| Students treat correlative conjunctions as interchangeable (e.g., using "either/and" or "both/or"). | Emphasize that correlative conjunctions come in fixed pairs. Create a reference chart: either/or, neither/nor, both/and, not only/but also, whether/or. Practice matching pairs before using them in sentences. |
| Students believe verb tense must stay the same throughout an entire piece of writing. | Clarify that tense shifts are appropriate when the time actually changes (e.g., "Yesterday I walked to school, but today I am riding the bus"). The error is shifting tense when the time has not changed. |
| Students place commas after every word in a series, including before the first item. | Show that commas separate items in a series but do not precede the first item. Model counting items and placing commas only between them, with the final comma before "and" or "or." |
| Students think longer sentences are always better than shorter ones. | Teach that sentence length should serve the writer's purpose. Sometimes a short, punchy sentence creates more impact than a long one. Combining and expanding should improve clarity and interest, not just add length. |
| Students confuse semicolons with colons or use them in simple series without internal commas. | Explain that semicolons in a series are specifically needed when items already contain commas. In a simple series, regular commas are sufficient. |

### Assessment Guidance

The Unit Assessment contains 24 items distributed across all four standards. Students should be assessed after completing all 10 lessons. Administer the assessment in a single sitting of approximately 35-40 minutes.

**Scoring:** Each item is worth 1 point for a total of 24 points. The scoring guide at the end of the assessment provides exact expected answers and notes on acceptable variations.

**Mastery Thresholds:**
- **Exceeds expectations:** 22-24 points (92-100%)
- **Meets expectations:** 18-21 points (75-88%)
- **Approaching expectations:** 14-17 points (58-71%)
- **Below expectations:** 0-13 points (0-54%)

**Re-teaching Guidance:** If a student scores below mastery on a specific standard, revisit the corresponding lessons using the "If Mastery Is Not Met" strategies provided in each lesson plan. Focus on the identification tasks first, then progress to production tasks.

### Differentiation at a Glance

**Below Level:**
- Provide a grammar reference card with correlative conjunction pairs and verb tense charts
- Reduce the number of Independent Practice items (complete odd-numbered items only)
- Offer sentence frames for production tasks
- Allow oral responses before written ones
- Pair with an on-level peer for guided practice activities

**On Level:**
- Complete all Independent Practice items as written
- Use the standard lesson pacing
- Encourage self-checking with the answer key after independent work

**Above Level:**
- Assign the Challenge tier in Independent Practice
- Ask students to write short paragraphs incorporating multiple grammar concepts from the unit
- Have students identify and analyze grammar choices in published texts (mentor sentences)
- Encourage students to "teach" a concept to a peer, reinforcing their own understanding
- Provide more complex sentence combining tasks with three or four source sentences

### Connection to Future Units

The grammar and language skills developed in Unit 4 form a critical bridge to Grade 6 expectations. In Grade 6, students will encounter more sophisticated sentence structures, including complex-compound sentences and varied sentence openings for rhetorical effect. The correlative conjunction work in Lessons 1-2 prepares students for parallel structure, a major focus in middle school writing. Verb tense consistency (Lessons 3-5) underpins the narrative and argumentative writing students will do in Grade 6, where maintaining or purposefully shifting tense is essential for coherence. The punctuation skills from Lessons 6-7 extend directly into Grade 6 standards on using commas, dashes, and parentheses for clarity. Finally, the sentence expansion and combination skills from Lessons 8-9 are foundational for the revision and editing work that becomes increasingly important in middle school English Language Arts.
