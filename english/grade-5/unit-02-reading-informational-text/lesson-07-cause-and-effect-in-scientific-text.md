# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 7

## Topic
Cause-and-Effect in Scientific Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify cause-and-effect relationships in scientific informational text
- Explain how one event, process, or condition leads to another using specific evidence from the text
- Recognize signal words that indicate cause-and-effect relationships (because, therefore, as a result, due to, leads to, consequently)

## Prerequisites
- Ability to explain relationships between individuals and events in informational text (Lesson 6, RI.5.3)
- Understanding of basic cause-and-effect from Grade 4 (RI.4.3)
- Ability to quote accurately from text (Lesson 1, RI.5.1)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Cause-and-Effect Chain graphic organizer (linked boxes showing Cause --> Effect/Cause --> Effect)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review cause and effect from everyday life and transition to scientific text.

**Activity: Science Chain Reaction**

Say: "In our last lesson, we looked at how people and events are connected in historical text. Today, we are shifting to science. In scientific text, the connections we see most often are cause-and-effect relationships. A cause is why something happens. An effect is what happens as a result."

Write on the board:
Cause: The sun heats the ocean water.
Effect: Water evaporates into the atmosphere.

Say: "But here is what makes science interesting -- an effect can become the cause of the next event. What happens after water evaporates?"

Allow students to respond. (Expected: It forms clouds, then it rains.)

Write on the board:
Cause: Water evaporates --> Effect/Cause: Clouds form --> Effect/Cause: Rain falls --> Effect: Water flows into rivers and back to the ocean.

Say: "That is a cause-and-effect chain. In scientific text, events are often linked together like this, where one effect becomes the cause of the next event. Today, you are going to learn how to identify and explain these chains in the passages you read."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify cause-and-effect relationships in scientific text, including chains of causes and effects.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Cause:** The reason something happens; the event or condition that produces a result
- **Effect:** The result or outcome of a cause; what happens because of something else
- **Cause-and-effect chain:** A sequence in which one effect becomes the cause of the next event
- **Signal words:** Words that indicate cause-and-effect relationships (because, since, therefore, as a result, due to, leads to, consequently, if...then)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When reading scientific text, here is a strategy for finding cause-and-effect relationships. I call it the Why-What-Next method."

Write on the board:
1. **Why** did this happen? (Find the cause)
2. **What** happened as a result? (Find the effect)
3. **Next** -- did that effect cause something else? (Look for a chain)

Say: "Signal words are your best friends here. Let me give you a list to watch for."

Write on the board:
- Cause signal words: because, since, due to, as a result of, caused by
- Effect signal words: therefore, as a result, consequently, led to, which caused, so

Say: "Let me show you how to use the Why-What-Next method."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "How Earthquakes Happen"**

> Earth's outer layer, called the crust, is not a single solid shell. It is broken into enormous pieces called tectonic plates that fit together like a giant jigsaw puzzle. These plates float on a layer of hot, semi-liquid rock called the mantle. Heat from Earth's core causes the mantle to move in slow, circular currents. As a result, the tectonic plates above shift and grind against each other, usually at a rate of only a few inches per year. Over time, enormous pressure builds up along the boundaries where plates meet. When the pressure becomes too great, the rocks along the boundary suddenly break or slip, releasing the stored energy as seismic waves that travel through the ground. This sudden release of energy is what we feel as an earthquake. The point underground where the rocks first break is called the focus, and the point on the surface directly above it is called the epicenter. The most powerful earthquakes occur along subduction zones, where one plate is being forced beneath another. These earthquakes can trigger tsunamis -- massive ocean waves -- when the sudden movement of the ocean floor displaces huge volumes of water.

Say: "Let me use the Why-What-Next method."

Write on the board:
- **Why:** Heat from Earth's core causes the mantle to move in circular currents.
- **What:** As a result, tectonic plates shift and grind against each other, building up pressure.
- **Next:** When pressure becomes too great, rocks break or slip, releasing energy as seismic waves -- an earthquake.
- **Next:** Powerful earthquakes along subduction zones can trigger tsunamis by displacing ocean water.

Say: "Look at this chain. Heat causes currents. Currents cause plate movement. Plate movement causes pressure buildup. Pressure causes rocks to break. Breaking rocks cause earthquakes. Earthquakes can cause tsunamis. Each effect becomes the cause of the next event."

Draw the Cause-and-Effect Chain:
Heat from core --> Mantle currents --> Plates shift and grind --> Pressure builds --> Rocks break/slip --> Earthquake --> (possible) Tsunami

Say: "Notice the signal words the author used: 'causes,' 'as a result,' 'when...too great.' These words are clues that tell you a cause-and-effect relationship is happening."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying cause-and-effect relationships in scientific text with facilitator support.

Say: "Let us work through a new passage together. Listen for the causes, effects, and signal words."

**Passage B: "Why Leaves Change Color in Autumn"**

> Every autumn in temperate regions, the leaves of many trees transform from green to brilliant shades of yellow, orange, and red. This spectacular change is driven by shifts in sunlight and temperature. During spring and summer, leaves are green because they contain large amounts of a pigment called chlorophyll, which absorbs sunlight and uses it to convert carbon dioxide and water into food for the tree through a process called photosynthesis. As autumn approaches, the days grow shorter and temperatures drop. Because the tree receives less sunlight, it can no longer produce food efficiently. As a result, the tree stops producing chlorophyll. When chlorophyll breaks down and disappears, other pigments that were hidden beneath the green become visible. Yellow and orange colors come from pigments called carotenoids, which were present in the leaves all along but were masked by chlorophyll. Red and purple colors are produced by pigments called anthocyanins, which some trees manufacture in autumn when sugars become trapped in the leaves. Eventually, a special layer of cells forms at the base of each leaf stem, cutting off the flow of water and nutrients. The leaf dries out, dies, and falls to the ground, where it decomposes and returns nutrients to the soil.

Say: "Let us use the Why-What-Next method. What is the first cause in this chain? Find the signal word."

Allow 30 seconds. Call on a student.

Expected: "As autumn approaches, the days grow shorter and temperatures drop." (Signal word: "as")

Say: "What is the effect of shorter days and cooler temperatures? Find the signal word."

Expected: "Because the tree receives less sunlight, it can no longer produce food efficiently." (Signal word: "because")

Say: "What happens next? What is the next link in the chain?"

Expected: "As a result, the tree stops producing chlorophyll." (Signal word: "as a result")

Say: "And then?"

Expected: "When chlorophyll breaks down and disappears, other pigments that were hidden beneath the green become visible." (Signal word: "when")

Say: "Now I want you to complete the cause-and-effect chain on your graphic organizer. Add the remaining links -- what happens to the leaves at the very end?"

Allow 2 minutes. Circulate and check.

Expected chain: Shorter days/cooler temps --> Less sunlight for photosynthesis --> Tree stops producing chlorophyll --> Chlorophyll disappears, hidden pigments show --> Special cells cut off water/nutrients --> Leaf dries out, dies, and falls --> Leaf decomposes, returning nutrients to soil

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify and explain cause-and-effect relationships in scientific text.

**Passage C: "The Greenhouse Effect and Climate Change"**

> Earth is surrounded by a layer of gases called the atmosphere. Some of these gases, including carbon dioxide, methane, and water vapor, are known as greenhouse gases because they act like the glass walls of a greenhouse. When sunlight reaches Earth's surface, the ground absorbs the energy and releases it back as heat. Greenhouse gases trap some of this heat in the atmosphere, preventing it from escaping into space. This natural process, called the greenhouse effect, keeps Earth warm enough to support life. Without it, the planet's average temperature would be about zero degrees Fahrenheit -- far too cold for most living things. However, human activities have dramatically increased the amount of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere. Burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas releases large quantities of carbon dioxide. Deforestation removes trees that would otherwise absorb carbon dioxide from the air. Livestock farming produces significant amounts of methane. Because greenhouse gas levels have risen sharply, more heat is being trapped in the atmosphere than before. Consequently, global temperatures have increased by approximately 2 degrees Fahrenheit over the past century. This warming has led to melting glaciers, rising sea levels, more frequent and intense storms, and shifts in wildlife habitats around the world.

**Directions:**

1. What is the natural greenhouse effect, and what causes it? Quote at least one detail from the text.
2. Identify three human activities that contribute to increased greenhouse gases. Quote the text for each.
3. Create a cause-and-effect chain with at least four links that shows how human activities lead to changes in the environment. Use signal words to connect each link.
4. The passage says that without the greenhouse effect, Earth would be "about zero degrees Fahrenheit." Why does the author include this detail? What does it help the reader understand?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions with accurate quotes and signal words.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a 3-4 sentence paragraph explaining the difference between the natural greenhouse effect (helpful) and the enhanced greenhouse effect caused by humans (harmful). Use evidence from the passage.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.2) What are the two main ideas of this passage? Write each as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.1) Quote one sentence from the passage that shows the greenhouse effect is normally beneficial to life on Earth.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Coral bleaching occurs when ocean water temperatures rise even slightly above normal levels. The warmer water causes coral to expel the tiny algae that live inside them and give them their color. Without these algae, the coral turns white and loses its main source of food. If the water stays warm for too long, the coral starves and dies.

1. What causes coral bleaching? Quote the text.
2. Describe the cause-and-effect chain in this passage using at least two signal words.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Coral bleaching occurs "when ocean water temperatures rise even slightly above normal levels."
2. When water temperatures rise, the warmer water causes coral to expel the algae living inside them. As a result, the coral turns white and loses its food source. Consequently, if warm conditions continue, the coral starves and dies. (Accept any answer with at least two correct cause-effect links and two signal words.)

**Mastery = Both questions answered correctly with accurate quotes and at least two signal words**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to Passage A and walk through the Why-What-Next method one step at a time
- Provide a simple cause-and-effect passage (3-4 sentences) and highlight the signal words together
- Practice filling in a Cause-and-Effect Chain with a familiar topic (e.g., the water cycle)

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8 on integrating information from two texts
- Encourage the student to identify cause-and-effect chains in science reading at home

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Science Chain Challenge:** Students choose a scientific process they know about (water cycle, food chain, rock cycle) and create a cause-and-effect chain with at least five links, using signal words at each step.
2. **Signal Word Hunt:** Students read a science article and highlight every signal word they find. They then classify each as indicating a cause or an effect.
3. **Reverse Engineering:** Give students a list of effects and ask them to find the causes in the passage. Then give them a list of causes and ask them to find the effects.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students sometimes confuse sequence (first, then, next) with cause and effect (because, therefore, as a result). Emphasize that just because one event follows another does not mean the first caused the second. A cause-and-effect relationship means one event directly makes the next event happen.
- The cause-and-effect chain is one of the most important concepts in this lesson. Science passages often present long chains where each effect becomes the next cause. Encourage students to map the full chain before writing their explanations.
- Signal words are not always present. Some authors imply cause-and-effect relationships without using obvious signal words. If students are ready, introduce this idea with examples.
- This lesson pairs with Lesson 6 to form the RI.5.3 sequence. While Lesson 6 focused on historical relationships between people and events, this lesson focuses on scientific processes. The skill is the same -- explaining connections -- but the content type is different.
- Some science concepts in these passages may be unfamiliar to students. Pre-teach key concepts (tectonic plates, chlorophyll, greenhouse gases) if needed.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The natural greenhouse effect is the process by which greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere. The passage states, "Greenhouse gases trap some of this heat in the atmosphere, preventing it from escaping into space." This process is caused by gases like "carbon dioxide, methane, and water vapor."
2. Three human activities: (a) "Burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and natural gas releases large quantities of carbon dioxide." (b) "Deforestation removes trees that would otherwise absorb carbon dioxide from the air." (c) "Livestock farming produces significant amounts of methane."
3. Cause-and-effect chain: Human activities such as burning fossil fuels, deforestation, and livestock farming release greenhouse gases into the atmosphere. Because greenhouse gas levels have risen sharply, more heat is trapped than before. Consequently, global temperatures have increased by approximately 2 degrees Fahrenheit. As a result, this warming has led to melting glaciers, rising sea levels, more intense storms, and shifts in wildlife habitats.
4. The author includes the detail about zero degrees Fahrenheit to help the reader understand that the greenhouse effect is naturally beneficial and necessary. Without it, Earth would be too cold for life. This contrast helps the reader see that the problem is not the greenhouse effect itself, but the fact that humans have made it too strong by adding extra gases.
