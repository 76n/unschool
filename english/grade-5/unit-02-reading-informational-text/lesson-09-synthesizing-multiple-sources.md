# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic
Synthesizing Multiple Sources

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read three or more sources on the same topic and identify what each contributes
- Synthesize information by drawing conclusions that go beyond any single source
- Write or speak knowledgeably about a topic by combining evidence from multiple texts

## Prerequisites
- Ability to integrate information from two texts (Lesson 8, RI.5.9)
- Ability to identify main ideas and key details (Lessons 3-4, RI.5.2)
- Ability to quote accurately from text (Lesson 1, RI.5.1)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages (three passages per section)
- Source Synthesis Chart graphic organizer (rows for each source, columns for: Source, Main Idea, Unique Information, Shared Information)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in three colors (one per source)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review two-text integration from Lesson 8 and introduce the concept of synthesizing three or more sources.

**Activity: The Puzzle Pieces**

Say: "In our last lesson, you learned how to combine information from two texts on the same topic. That was like putting together two pieces of a puzzle. Today, we are adding more pieces. When you read three or more texts on the same topic and combine all the information, you create a much more complete picture. This is called synthesis."

Write on the board:
- **Integration** (Lesson 8) = Combining information from two sources
- **Synthesis** (Today) = Combining information from three or more sources to build a deeper understanding

Say: "Think of it this way. If one friend tells you about an animal, you learn some things. If two friends each tell you about the same animal, you learn more. But if three people -- a zookeeper, a scientist, and a nature photographer -- each tell you about the same animal, you learn even more because each person brings a different kind of knowledge. That is the power of synthesis."

Say: "By the end of today, you will be able to read three texts about a topic and write about it as if you are an expert. Let us get started."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to synthesize information from three sources into a cohesive understanding.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Synthesize:** To combine information from multiple sources to create a new, deeper understanding
- **Source:** Any text, article, or document that provides information on a topic
- **Corroborate:** When two or more sources provide the same information, confirming its accuracy
- **Perspective:** The particular angle or focus from which a source approaches a topic

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Synthesis goes beyond just listing facts from each source. It means combining, connecting, and drawing conclusions. Here is the process."

Write on the board:
**Synthesis Steps:**
1. Read all sources and note the main idea of each.
2. Identify what is shared across sources (corroborated information).
3. Identify what is unique to each source.
4. Draw a conclusion or build an understanding that no single source gives you alone.
5. Write about the topic using evidence from all sources.

Say: "Step 4 is the key. Synthesis means you create a new, bigger understanding by putting all the pieces together. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

**Source 1: "The Monarch Butterfly's Migration"**

> Every fall, millions of monarch butterflies leave their summer homes in the United States and Canada and fly up to 3,000 miles to reach their winter habitat in the mountain forests of central Mexico. This migration is one of the longest of any insect. Monarchs navigate using a combination of the sun's position and Earth's magnetic field to find their way. Remarkably, the butterflies that make the return trip north in spring are not the same individuals that flew south -- they are the grandchildren or great-grandchildren of the original migrants.

**Source 2: "Monarch Populations in Decline"**

> Monarch butterfly populations have dropped by approximately 80 percent over the past two decades. Scientists point to three main causes: the loss of milkweed plants due to herbicide use in agricultural areas, deforestation in the monarchs' Mexican wintering grounds, and the effects of climate change on weather patterns during migration. Milkweed is especially critical because it is the only plant on which monarch caterpillars can feed. Without milkweed, monarchs cannot reproduce.

**Source 3: "Efforts to Save the Monarchs"**

> Conservation groups, schools, and individuals across North America are taking action to help monarch butterflies. Thousands of people have planted milkweed gardens in their yards and communities, creating "monarch waystation" habitats along the migration route. The Mexican government has expanded protected areas around the monarchs' wintering forests. In the United States, several states have adopted the monarch as their official state butterfly to raise awareness. Scientists are also studying whether assisted migration -- helping monarchs relocate to areas with better conditions -- could help the species survive.

Say: "Let me fill in the Source Synthesis Chart."

| Source | Main Idea | Unique Information | Shared Information |
|--------|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
| Source 1 | Monarchs make a remarkable 3,000-mile migration | Navigate using sun and magnetic field; multi-generational return trip | Monarchs migrate between US/Canada and Mexico |
| Source 2 | Monarch populations have declined 80% | Three main causes: milkweed loss, deforestation, climate change | Milkweed is critical; wintering in Mexico |
| Source 3 | People are working to save monarchs | Milkweed gardens, protected areas in Mexico, state butterfly designations, assisted migration research | Conservation efforts; milkweed importance |

Say: "Now, Step 4. What do I understand about monarchs from all three sources together that I would not understand from just one?"

Write on the board:
"Monarch butterflies make one of the most extraordinary migrations in the natural world, traveling up to 3,000 miles across multiple generations. However, this remarkable species is in serious danger, with populations declining by 80 percent due to milkweed loss, deforestation, and climate change. In response, conservation efforts across North America -- from backyard milkweed gardens to government-protected forests -- are working to ensure that the monarch migration continues for future generations."

Say: "This paragraph uses facts from all three sources. No single source gave me the full picture -- the migration itself, the threats, and the solutions. Together, they give me a complete understanding."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice synthesizing three sources with facilitator support.

Say: "Let us try this together with three new texts."

**Source 1: "What Is the Great Barrier Reef?"**

> The Great Barrier Reef, located off the coast of Queensland, Australia, is the largest coral reef system in the world. Stretching over 1,400 miles, it is so large that it can be seen from space. The reef is made up of nearly 3,000 individual reef systems and hundreds of islands. It supports an astonishing diversity of life, including more than 1,500 species of fish, 400 types of coral, and 30 species of whales and dolphins.

**Source 2: "Threats to the Great Barrier Reef"**

> Rising ocean temperatures caused by climate change have triggered mass coral bleaching events on the Great Barrier Reef. When water gets too warm, coral expels the algae living inside it, turning white and losing its primary food source. The reef experienced severe bleaching events in 2016, 2017, and 2020. Scientists estimate that more than half of the reef's coral has been lost since 1995. Pollution from agricultural runoff and coastal development also harms the reef.

**Source 3: "Protecting the Great Barrier Reef"**

> The Australian government has invested billions of dollars in reef protection, including water quality improvement programs that reduce pollution from farming and industry. Marine scientists are experimenting with coral restoration techniques, growing heat-resistant coral in laboratories and transplanting it onto damaged sections of the reef. International climate agreements aim to limit global warming, which would reduce the severity of future bleaching events.

Say: "Start by filling in your Source Synthesis Chart. What is the main idea of each source? What is unique? What is shared? You have 3 minutes."

Allow 3 minutes. Then discuss.

Say: "Now, Step 4 -- what do you understand about the Great Barrier Reef from all three sources together? Work with a partner to write a 3-sentence synthesis."

Allow 3 minutes. Call on a student.

Sample: "The Great Barrier Reef is the largest coral reef system in the world, stretching over 1,400 miles and supporting thousands of species. However, climate change has caused mass bleaching events that have destroyed more than half of its coral since 1995. Scientists and the Australian government are fighting to save the reef through coral restoration, pollution reduction, and international climate action."

Say: "That synthesis includes information from all three sources. You are writing about the reef as someone who is knowledgeable about the whole picture."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently synthesize three sources on the same topic.

**Source 1: "The History of the Internet"**

> The internet began as a military research project in the late 1960s. The United States Department of Defense created a computer network called ARPANET to allow researchers at different universities to share information. ARPANET connected its first four computers in 1969, linking the University of California, Los Angeles; the Stanford Research Institute; the University of California, Santa Barbara; and the University of Utah. By the 1980s, the network had grown to include thousands of computers at universities and government agencies across the country.

**Source 2: "How the Internet Changed Communication"**

> The invention of the World Wide Web by Tim Berners-Lee in 1989 transformed the internet from a tool used mainly by scientists into a global communication platform. Email replaced letters for many people, and websites made information available to anyone with a computer and an internet connection. Social media platforms, which emerged in the early 2000s, changed how people interact, allowing instant communication with friends, family, and strangers around the world. Today, more than 5 billion people use the internet, and the average person spends nearly seven hours online each day.

**Source 3: "Concerns About the Internet"**

> Despite its many benefits, the internet has also created new challenges. Cyberbullying has become a serious problem, particularly among young people, with studies showing that nearly 37 percent of students between the ages of 12 and 17 have experienced online harassment. The spread of misinformation -- false or misleading information presented as fact -- has made it harder for people to know what to trust. Privacy is another concern, as companies collect vast amounts of personal data from internet users, often without their full understanding or consent. Digital addiction, especially among children and teenagers, has prompted some countries to limit screen time for young people.

**Directions:**

1. Fill in a Source Synthesis Chart for all three sources.
2. What information is corroborated (confirmed) across two or more sources? List at least one example.
3. Write a 4-5 sentence synthesis paragraph about the internet that uses at least one specific fact from each source. Your paragraph should show a complete understanding of the topic -- its history, its impact, and its challenges.
4. Could someone who read only Source 1 write knowledgeably about the internet? Why or why not?

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions with accurate quotes from at least two of the three sources.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write a concluding sentence for your synthesis that states your own evidence-based conclusion about the internet's overall impact on society.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.2) What are the two main ideas of Source 3? Write each as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.1) Quote one sentence from Source 2 that shows how much the internet has grown since its beginning as a military project.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

**Source A:**
> Wolves were nearly eliminated from Yellowstone National Park by the 1920s due to government-sponsored hunting programs.

**Source B:**
> In 1995, wolves were reintroduced to Yellowstone. Their return caused elk to move away from riverbanks, allowing trees and shrubs to regrow along the water.

**Source C:**
> The regrowth of vegetation along Yellowstone's rivers has stabilized riverbanks, reduced erosion, and created new habitat for birds, beavers, and fish.

Write 2-3 sentences that synthesize information from all three sources.

**Expected Answers:**
Sample: "Wolves were nearly eliminated from Yellowstone by the 1920s, but their reintroduction in 1995 had a dramatic positive effect on the ecosystem. When wolves returned, elk moved away from riverbanks, allowing trees and shrubs to regrow. This vegetation regrowth stabilized riverbanks, reduced erosion, and created new habitat for birds, beavers, and fish." (Accept any synthesis that accurately combines information from all three sources.)

**Mastery = Response includes at least one fact from each source, combined into a coherent synthesis**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the monarch butterfly example and walk through the synthesis process step by step
- Give the student the Source Synthesis Chart already partially filled in and ask them to complete it
- Start with just two sources and build to three

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10, the review and celebration lesson
- Encourage the student to practice synthesis by reading multiple articles about the same current event

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Expert Panel:** Students each read three sources on a different topic (space exploration, ocean conservation, ancient civilizations). They then present a 1-minute "expert talk" to the class, synthesizing information from all three sources.
2. **Source Scavenger Hunt:** Provide a synthesis paragraph about a topic and ask students to determine which facts came from which source. Give them the three source texts to match.
3. **Create a Source Set:** Students choose a topic and find or write three short passages that each contribute unique information. They then swap source sets with a partner, who synthesizes the information.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Synthesis is a higher-order thinking skill that takes practice. Students may initially produce lists of facts from each source rather than a woven, coherent paragraph. Model the difference between listing and synthesizing repeatedly.
- Encourage students to think of themselves as "experts" after reading multiple sources. This mindset shift helps them understand the purpose of synthesis -- to build knowledge, not just collect facts.
- The Source Synthesis Chart is essential scaffolding. Do not remove it until students can synthesize mentally without it.
- Remind students that corroboration (when multiple sources agree) increases confidence in the accuracy of information. This is an early introduction to evaluating source reliability.
- This lesson builds directly on Lesson 8. If students struggled with two-text integration, provide additional practice with two texts before introducing three.
- The distinction between synthesis and summary is important. A summary condenses one text. A synthesis combines multiple texts and may produce a conclusion or understanding that goes beyond any individual source.

1. Source Synthesis Chart:

   |--------|-----------|--------------------|--------------------|
   | Source 1 | The internet began as a military research project | ARPANET created by DOD; first 4 computers connected in 1969; grew to thousands by 1980s | The internet's origins and growth |

2. Corroborated information: Sources 2 and 3 both address the widespread impact of the internet on society (Source 2 focuses on positive communication changes; Source 3 focuses on negative challenges). Sources 1 and 2 both reference the internet's growth from a small network to a massive global system.

3. Sample synthesis: "The internet began as a small military research project called ARPANET, which connected just four computers in 1969. After Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1989, the internet grew into a global communication platform used by more than 5 billion people today. However, this rapid growth has also created serious challenges, including cyberbullying, the spread of misinformation, and privacy concerns. Understanding the internet's history, impact, and challenges is essential for using it wisely."

4. Someone who read only Source 1 could not write knowledgeably about the internet because they would only know about its military origins and early growth. They would not know about the World Wide Web, email, social media, or the challenges the internet has created. A knowledgeable understanding requires information from multiple sources covering different aspects of the topic.
