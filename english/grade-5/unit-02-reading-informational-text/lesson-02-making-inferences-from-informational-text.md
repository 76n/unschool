# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 2

## Topic
Making Inferences from Informational Text

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between explicit information (stated directly) and inferences (conclusions drawn from clues in the text)
- Make logical inferences based on evidence in an informational passage
- Support inferences with accurate quotes from the text

## Prerequisites
- Ability to quote accurately from informational text (Lesson 1, RI.5.1)
- Understanding of the difference between facts and opinions (Grade 4)
- Ability to make simple inferences from narrative text (Grade 4)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Inference Chart graphic organizer (three columns: Text Evidence, What I Already Know, My Inference)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about inference and distinguish it from explicit information.

**Activity: What Can You Figure Out?**

Say: "In our last lesson, we learned how to quote accurately from a text -- using the author's exact words. Today, we are going to learn about a different kind of reading skill. Sometimes the author does not state everything directly. Sometimes you have to figure things out based on clues."

Write on the board:
> Maya grabbed her umbrella, pulled on her rain boots, and zipped up her waterproof jacket before stepping outside.

Say: "Does this sentence tell you directly that it is raining outside?"

Allow students to respond. (Expected: No, it does not say it directly.)

Say: "But can you figure it out from the clues? What clues tell you it is probably raining?"

Allow students to respond. (Expected: umbrella, rain boots, waterproof jacket.)

Say: "That is an inference. An inference is a conclusion you draw based on evidence in the text combined with what you already know. The text gave you clues, and your brain put the clues together to figure out something the author did not say directly. Today, we are going to make inferences from informational text and support them with accurate quotes."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to identify when information is explicit versus when it requires an inference, and how to support inferences with text evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Inference:** A conclusion you reach based on evidence in the text and your own background knowledge
- **Explicit:** Stated directly and clearly in the text
- **Implicit:** Not stated directly; must be figured out from clues
- **Text evidence:** Specific words, phrases, or sentences from the passage that support a conclusion

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When we read informational text, some questions can be answered by pointing to an exact sentence. We call that explicit information -- it is right there on the page. Other questions require us to think beyond what is written. We use clues from the text plus what we already know to draw a conclusion. That is an inference."

Write on the board:
- Explicit = Stated directly. You can point to the exact sentence.
- Inference = Not stated directly. You combine text clues + background knowledge.

Say: "Here is the important part for fifth grade: even when you make an inference, you still need to support it with an accurate quote from the text. You are not just guessing. You are building your conclusion on evidence."

Write on the board:
**Inference Formula:** Text Evidence (quote) + Background Knowledge = Inference

Say: "Let me show you how this works."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "Life on the International Space Station"**

> Astronauts aboard the International Space Station live and work in an environment unlike anywhere on Earth. Because there is almost no gravity, everything floats -- including the astronauts themselves. They must strap themselves into sleeping bags attached to the wall so they do not drift around while they sleep. Meals are carefully packaged in sealed containers to prevent crumbs and liquids from floating into equipment and causing damage. Astronauts exercise for at least two hours every day using specially designed machines that keep their bodies strapped in place. Without this daily exercise, their muscles would weaken and their bones would lose density. Water is a precious resource on the station, so the crew recycles almost all of it, including moisture from their own breath and sweat.

Say: "Let me ask two questions about this passage. The first is explicit, and the second requires an inference."

Write on the board:
- Question 1 (Explicit): How long do astronauts exercise each day?
- Question 2 (Inference): Why might living in space be uncomfortable for someone used to life on Earth?

Say: "For Question 1, I can point right to the sentence: 'Astronauts exercise for at least two hours every day.' That is explicit. The answer is stated directly."

Say: "For Question 2, the passage never says 'living in space is uncomfortable.' But look at the clues. The passage says astronauts 'must strap themselves into sleeping bags attached to the wall.' It says their meals are 'carefully packaged in sealed containers.' It says they must 'exercise for at least two hours every day' just to keep their muscles from weakening. All of those clues suggest that daily life in space requires a lot of extra effort and adjustments."

Write on the board:
- Text Evidence: "must strap themselves into sleeping bags attached to the wall" and "exercise for at least two hours every day"
- Background Knowledge: On Earth, we sleep in beds and move freely without straps or special equipment.
- Inference: Living in space would be uncomfortable for someone used to Earth because everyday activities like sleeping, eating, and exercising require special equipment and extra effort.

Say: "Notice that my inference is supported by specific quotes from the text. I am not guessing. I am combining evidence from the passage with what I already know about life on Earth."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice distinguishing explicit information from inferences and supporting inferences with text evidence.

Say: "Let us try this together with a new passage. As I read, listen for both explicit facts and clues that might help you make inferences."

**Passage B: "The Dust Bowl"**

> During the 1930s, a terrible environmental disaster struck the Great Plains of the United States. Years of drought combined with decades of farming practices that had stripped the land of its natural grasses turned millions of acres of fertile topsoil into dust. Massive dust storms, sometimes called "black blizzards," swept across the plains, burying homes, killing livestock, and making it nearly impossible to breathe outdoors. Some storms were so large that they carried dust all the way to cities on the East Coast, more than 1,500 miles away. Thousands of families, unable to grow crops or earn a living, packed their belongings and left their farms. Many headed west to California, hoping to find work as farm laborers. The federal government responded by creating programs to teach farmers better soil conservation methods, including planting rows of trees as windbreaks and rotating crops to keep the soil healthy.

Say: "Question 1 is explicit: What caused the dust storms on the Great Plains? Search the text and find the exact words."

Allow 30 seconds. Call on a student.

Expected response: "Years of drought combined with decades of farming practices that had stripped the land of its natural grasses turned millions of acres of fertile topsoil into dust."

Say: "Perfect quoting. Now Question 2 is an inference: Do you think the families who left their farms during the Dust Bowl had an easy journey to California? The text does not say directly. What clues can you find?"

Allow 1 minute for students to think and discuss with a partner.

Call on students and guide the discussion:

Expected reasoning: The passage says families "packed their belongings and left their farms" and were "hoping to find work as farm laborers." The word "hoping" suggests they were not sure they would find work. Also, they had already lost their ability to "grow crops or earn a living," so they probably did not have much money for the journey.

Say: "Excellent inference. Now let us write it out using our formula."

Write on the board:
- Text Evidence: Families were "unable to grow crops or earn a living" and were "hoping to find work as farm laborers."
- Background Knowledge: Moving across the country is expensive and difficult, especially without money or a guaranteed job.
- Inference: The journey to California was probably very difficult for Dust Bowl families because they had lost their income and had no guarantee of finding work when they arrived.

Say: "See how the inference goes beyond what the text says, but it is still grounded in specific evidence from the passage."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify explicit information and make inferences supported by text evidence.

**Passage C: "The Hidden World of Tide Pools"**

> Along rocky coastlines around the world, shallow pools of seawater form in the crevices and hollows of the rocks when the tide goes out. These tide pools may look like simple puddles, but they are actually thriving ecosystems filled with a remarkable variety of life. Sea stars cling to the rocks with hundreds of tiny tube feet, slowly prying open mussels and clams for their meals. Hermit crabs scurry across the pool floor, searching for larger shells to move into as they grow. Sea anemones wave their colorful tentacles in the water, waiting to catch tiny fish and shrimp that swim too close. The creatures that live in tide pools must be tough. Twice a day, the tide changes, and the animals endure dramatic shifts in water temperature, salinity, and exposure to air and sun. During low tide, some pools can heat up to more than 100 degrees Fahrenheit, while others are pounded by waves crashing over the rocks. Yet these hardy creatures survive and even thrive in conditions that would be fatal to most ocean animals.

**Directions:**

1. (Explicit) According to the passage, how do sea stars eat? Quote the text.
2. (Explicit) What challenges do tide pool creatures face? Quote at least one specific detail from the text.
3. (Inference) The passage says hermit crabs search for "larger shells to move into as they grow." What can you infer about hermit crabs and their shells? Use evidence from the text plus your own reasoning.
4. (Inference) Based on the passage, why do you think the author calls these creatures "hardy"? Support your inference with a quote from the passage.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3 with accurate quotes and clear inferences.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions. For each inference, write out the full formula: Text Evidence + Background Knowledge = Inference.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then write your own inference question about this passage and provide a model answer with a supporting quote.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.2) What is the main idea of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.
6. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.7) If the author included a diagram of a tide pool, what information could it show that the passage describes in words? Name two things a diagram could illustrate.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> The Sahara Desert receives less than three inches of rain per year, yet more than 500 species of plants grow there. Many of these plants have developed extremely long root systems that reach deep underground to find water. Some Sahara plants can survive for years without any rainfall at all, storing water in their thick, waxy leaves.

1. (Explicit) How much rain does the Sahara Desert receive per year? Quote the text.
2. (Inference) What can you infer about the soil deep underground in the Sahara? Support your inference with a quote from the passage.

**Expected Answers:**
1. The Sahara Desert receives "less than three inches of rain per year."
2. Inference: There must be water deep underground in the Sahara because plants have developed "extremely long root systems that reach deep underground to find water." If there were no water below the surface, the long roots would serve no purpose. (Accept any logical inference supported by an accurate quote.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct, with accurate quotes and a logical inference supported by text evidence**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Inference Formula (Text Evidence + Background Knowledge = Inference) using the Warm-Up example
- Provide a simpler passage and walk through the process of identifying one explicit fact and one inference step by step
- Use the Inference Chart graphic organizer to scaffold the student's thinking

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3 on identifying multiple main ideas
- Encourage the student to practice making inferences while reading at home and writing down one inference per reading session

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Inference Journal:** Students read a short informational article and record three inferences in their Inference Chart, each supported by a specific quote from the text.
2. **Explicit or Inference?** Give students a passage and a list of 8-10 statements. Students sort each statement into "Explicit" (directly stated) or "Inference" (figured out from clues) and explain their reasoning.
3. **Partner Inference Challenge:** One student reads a passage and writes two inference questions. The other student answers using the Inference Formula with accurate quotes.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often struggle to distinguish between a guess and an inference. Emphasize that a guess has no evidence, while an inference is always supported by specific text clues. If a student cannot point to a quote, it is a guess, not an inference.
- Some students may make inferences that are logical but not supported by the text. Redirect them by asking, "Where in the passage can you find evidence for that conclusion?"
- The Inference Formula (Text Evidence + Background Knowledge = Inference) is a powerful scaffold. Post it in the classroom and refer to it throughout the unit.
- This lesson connects directly to Lesson 1. Reinforce that even when making inferences, students must quote the text accurately to show their evidence.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. According to the passage, sea stars eat by "slowly prying open mussels and clams for their meals." They hold on to rocks "with hundreds of tiny tube feet."
2. The passage states that tide pool creatures "endure dramatic shifts in water temperature, salinity, and exposure to air and sun." It also says "some pools can heat up to more than 100 degrees Fahrenheit, while others are pounded by waves crashing over the rocks."
3. Inference: Hermit crabs do not grow their own shells. They must borrow shells from other creatures and find new, bigger ones as they get larger. The text says they search for "larger shells to move into as they grow," which implies their current shell becomes too small over time. (Accept any logical inference about hermit crabs using borrowed shells.)
4. The author calls these creatures "hardy" because they survive conditions that "would be fatal to most ocean animals," including extreme heat, crashing waves, and dramatic changes in their environment. This suggests the tide pool animals are exceptionally tough and well-adapted to harsh conditions.

### Interleaved Review
5. Main idea: Tide pools are thriving ecosystems along rocky coastlines where remarkably tough creatures survive despite harsh and constantly changing conditions. (Accept reasonable variations.)
6. A diagram of a tide pool could illustrate: (any two) the location of different creatures (sea stars on rocks, hermit crabs on the floor, anemones waving tentacles); the rocky hollows and crevices where water collects; the position of the tide pool relative to the ocean and the tide line; the different zones within the pool. (Accept any two reasonable suggestions.)
