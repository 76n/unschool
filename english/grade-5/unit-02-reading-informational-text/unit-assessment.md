# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 2: Reading Informational Text

## Unit Assessment

### Assessment Overview
This assessment covers all four standards addressed in Unit 2:
- **RI.5.1:** Quote accurately from a text when explaining what the text says explicitly and when drawing inferences
- **RI.5.2:** Determine two or more main ideas of a text and explain how they are supported by key details; summarize the text
- **RI.5.3:** Explain the relationships or interactions between two or more individuals, events, ideas, or concepts in a historical, scientific, or technical text
- **RI.5.9:** Integrate information from several texts on the same topic in order to write or speak about the subject knowledgeably

## Part 1: Passage A — "The Journey to the South Pole"

Read the passage. Then answer questions 1-5.

> In December 1911, two teams of explorers raced to become the first humans to reach the South Pole. The Norwegian team, led by Roald Amundsen, and the British team, led by Robert Falcon Scott, had been preparing for years. Their approaches, however, were vastly different.
>
> Amundsen had spent time living with the Inuit people of the Arctic, learning their techniques for surviving in extreme cold. He chose to use sled dogs as his primary means of transportation because they were fast, reliable, and well-suited to polar conditions. His team traveled light, established supply depots along their planned route, and focused on a single goal: reaching the pole as quickly as possible. Amundsen was also a careful planner who studied weather patterns and terrain before selecting his route.
>
> Scott took a different approach. He relied on a combination of motor sledges, ponies, and human-powered man-hauling to transport supplies. The motor sledges broke down early in the journey, and the ponies struggled in the harsh conditions and had to be put down. Scott's team was left to pull their heavy sledges themselves across hundreds of miles of ice. Scott also carried scientific instruments and geological samples, adding weight to their loads but reflecting his commitment to scientific research alongside exploration.
>
> Amundsen's team reached the South Pole on December 14, 1911, planting the Norwegian flag in the ice. Scott's team arrived 34 days later, on January 17, 1912, only to find Amundsen's flag already flying. Exhausted and running low on supplies, Scott's team began the return journey but was trapped by a brutal blizzard. All five members of Scott's polar party died on the return trip. Their bodies, journals, and scientific samples were found eight months later. Scott's journals, which documented the team's courage and suffering, made him a national hero in Britain despite his failure to reach the pole first.

### Questions

**1. (RI.5.1)** According to the passage, how did Amundsen prepare for his South Pole expedition? Quote at least one detail from the text.

**2. (RI.5.1)** The passage does not directly say that Scott made planning mistakes. However, what can you infer about Scott's planning compared to Amundsen's? Support your inference with a quote from the passage.

**3. (RI.5.2)** This passage has two main ideas. What are they? Write each as a complete sentence.

**4. (RI.5.3)** Explain the relationship between Amundsen's preparation methods and the outcome of his expedition. Use at least one signal word (because, as a result, led to, etc.) and quote from the text.

**5. (RI.5.3)** Explain the cause-and-effect chain that led to the death of Scott's team. Include at least three links in the chain, using details from the passage.

## Part 2: Passage B — "How Volcanoes Shape the Earth"

Read the passage. Then answer questions 6-10.

> Deep beneath the Earth's surface, temperatures are hot enough to melt rock. This molten rock, called magma, rises slowly through cracks and weak spots in the Earth's crust. When magma reaches the surface and erupts, it is called lava. Over thousands or millions of years, repeated eruptions can build enormous mountains known as volcanoes.
>
> Volcanoes are found along the edges of tectonic plates, the massive slabs of rock that make up Earth's outer shell. About 75 percent of the world's active volcanoes are located in a horseshoe-shaped zone around the Pacific Ocean known as the Ring of Fire. This zone stretches from New Zealand, up through Indonesia and Japan, across to Alaska, and down the western coasts of North and South America. The Ring of Fire exists because the Pacific plate is colliding with and sliding beneath several neighboring plates, creating conditions that allow magma to rise to the surface.
>
> Volcanic eruptions can be both destructive and creative. Explosive eruptions can launch rocks, ash, and gas high into the atmosphere, bury landscapes under layers of debris, and trigger deadly mudflows called lahars. The eruption of Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines in 1991 ejected so much ash into the atmosphere that global temperatures dropped by about one degree Fahrenheit for the following year. On the other hand, volcanic activity creates new land. The Hawaiian Islands were formed entirely by eruptions from an underwater volcanic hotspot over millions of years. Volcanic soil is extraordinarily fertile because it is rich in minerals like potassium, phosphorus, and calcium. Some of the world's most productive farmland, including regions in Italy, Indonesia, and Central America, sits on volcanic soil.

### Questions

**6. (RI.5.1)** What is the Ring of Fire? Quote the text to define it.

**7. (RI.5.2)** Identify two main ideas of this passage. Write each as a complete sentence, and list one key detail that supports each main idea.

**8. (RI.5.2)** Write a 3-4 sentence summary of this passage that includes both main ideas and at least two key details. Do not include personal opinions.

**9. (RI.5.3)** Explain the cause-and-effect chain that leads from magma underground to the formation of volcanoes. Use at least three links in the chain.

**10. (RI.5.3)** The passage describes the eruption of Mount Pinatubo. What was the cause, and what was the effect on global temperatures? Quote the text in your answer.

## Part 3: Passage C — "The Life of Harriet Tubman"

Read the passage. Then answer questions 11-14.

> Harriet Tubman was born into slavery around 1822 on a plantation in Maryland. As a child, she was forced to do hard labor, and at the age of twelve, she suffered a severe head injury when an overseer threw a heavy metal weight that struck her. The injury caused her to experience sudden blackouts and vivid dreams for the rest of her life. Despite these challenges, Tubman's determination to be free only grew stronger.
>
> In 1849, Tubman escaped from slavery by traveling nearly 90 miles on foot to Pennsylvania, a free state. Rather than remaining safely in the North, she made the extraordinary decision to return to the South repeatedly to help others escape. Over the course of approximately thirteen missions, Tubman guided more than 70 enslaved people to freedom along the Underground Railroad, a network of secret routes and safe houses. She became known as "Moses" because, like the biblical figure, she led her people out of bondage. Tubman never lost a single passenger on the Underground Railroad, a fact she stated with pride.
>
> During the Civil War, Tubman served the Union Army as a scout, spy, and nurse. In 1863, she became the first woman to lead an armed military raid in American history when she guided the Combahee River Raid in South Carolina, which freed more than 700 enslaved people. After the war, Tubman settled in Auburn, New York, where she continued to fight for the rights of African Americans and women. She established a home for elderly African Americans and spoke publicly in support of women's suffrage until her death in 1913 at approximately 91 years of age.

### Questions

**11. (RI.5.1)** According to the passage, why was Tubman called "Moses"? Quote the text in your answer.

**12. (RI.5.1)** What can you infer about Tubman's character based on her decision to return to the South after escaping? Support your inference with a quote from the passage.

**13. (RI.5.3)** Explain the relationship between Tubman's early life experiences and her later actions. How did the events of her childhood and young adulthood influence what she did later? Use details from the text.

**14. (RI.5.2)** This passage covers several stages of Tubman's life. Identify two main ideas and explain how each is supported by key details from the text.

## Part 4: Paired Passages D and E — "The Water Crisis"

Read both passages. Then answer questions 15-20.

### Passage D: "Earth's Water Supply"

> Although water covers about 71 percent of the Earth's surface, only a tiny fraction is available for human use. Approximately 97 percent of all water on Earth is saltwater found in the oceans, which is not suitable for drinking or farming without expensive treatment. Of the remaining 3 percent that is freshwater, about two-thirds is locked in glaciers and polar ice caps. This means that less than 1 percent of all the water on Earth is readily available freshwater in rivers, lakes, and underground aquifers. The world's population currently uses approximately 4 trillion cubic meters of freshwater each year for drinking, agriculture, and industry. Agriculture alone accounts for about 70 percent of all freshwater use, as crops require enormous amounts of water to grow.

### Passage E: "Water Scarcity Around the World"

> More than 2 billion people around the world do not have reliable access to clean, safe drinking water. In many parts of Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia, families must walk miles to collect water from wells or rivers, and the water they find is often contaminated with bacteria and parasites that cause disease. The World Health Organization estimates that contaminated water causes more than 500,000 deaths per year, many of them children under the age of five. Climate change is making the problem worse by altering rainfall patterns and causing more frequent and severe droughts. Meanwhile, growing populations and expanding agriculture are increasing the demand for freshwater at the same time that supplies are shrinking. Scientists and engineers are developing solutions, including desalination plants that convert seawater to freshwater, drip irrigation systems that use less water for farming, and water recycling programs that treat and reuse wastewater.

### Questions

**17. (RI.5.9)** What information does Passage E provide that Passage D does not? Name at least two specific facts.

**18. (RI.5.9)** Using information from BOTH passages, write a 4-5 sentence synthesis paragraph about the world's water challenges. Include at least one quote from each passage.

**19. (RI.5.1)** Based on both passages, what can you infer about the future of the world's water supply if no action is taken? Support your inference with quotes from both passages.

**20. (RI.5.2)** Identify the main idea of Passage D and the main idea of Passage E. How do these two main ideas work together to give you a more complete understanding of the water crisis?

## Part 5: Passage F — "The Science of Sleep"

Read the passage. Then answer questions 21-24.

> Sleep is far more than simple rest -- it is a complex biological process that is essential to human health and development. During sleep, the brain cycles through several stages, including light sleep, deep sleep, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. Each stage serves a different purpose. During deep sleep, the body repairs muscles, strengthens the immune system, and releases growth hormones that are especially important for children and teenagers. During REM sleep, the brain processes information from the day, consolidates memories, and supports emotional regulation. Scientists have discovered that during REM sleep, the brain is nearly as active as it is when a person is awake.
>
> Lack of sleep has serious consequences. Studies show that students who sleep fewer than eight hours per night perform significantly worse on tests of memory, attention, and problem-solving than students who get a full night's rest. Sleep deprivation also weakens the immune system, making people more likely to get sick. Research has linked chronic sleep loss to long-term health problems including obesity, heart disease, and depression. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends that children ages 6 to 12 get 9 to 12 hours of sleep per night, while teenagers ages 13 to 18 need 8 to 10 hours. Despite these recommendations, surveys indicate that more than 60 percent of middle school students do not get enough sleep on school nights, often due to homework, screen time, and early school start times.

### Questions

**21. (RI.5.1)** Quote the sentence from the passage that explains what happens during deep sleep.

**22. (RI.5.2)** What are the two main ideas of this passage? Write each as a complete sentence and name one supporting detail for each.

**23. (RI.5.3)** Explain the cause-and-effect relationship between sleep deprivation and student performance. Use a signal word and quote from the text.

**24. (RI.5.1)** The passage says "more than 60 percent of middle school students do not get enough sleep on school nights." The passage also lists reasons. What can you infer about how schools and families could help students get more sleep? Support your inference with a quote from the passage.

## Remediation Guidance

If a student does not meet mastery on a specific standard, use the following targeted reteaching plan:

**RI.5.1 (Quoting Accurately and Making Inferences):**
- Reteach using Lessons 1-2, focusing on the Quote It Right steps and the Inference Formula
- Provide two additional short passages and have the student practice finding exact quotes to answer explicit questions
- For inference, use the Inference Chart (Text Evidence + Background Knowledge = Inference) with a simple passage
- Check that the student understands the difference between quoting (exact words in quotation marks) and paraphrasing

**RI.5.2 (Multiple Main Ideas and Summarizing):**
- Reteach using Lessons 3-5, focusing on the Section Scan and SMID method
- Provide a two-paragraph passage with clearly distinct main ideas and walk the student through identifying each
- Use the Multiple Main Ideas Map to scaffold the student's thinking
- For summarizing, provide a completed SMID chart and ask the student to write the summary from the organized notes

**RI.5.3 (Relationships and Cause-and-Effect):**
- Reteach using Lessons 6-7, focusing on the Why-What-Next method and signal words
- Provide a simple historical passage and guide the student through identifying individuals, events, and connections
- For cause-and-effect, start with a familiar process (e.g., the water cycle) and practice building a chain before moving to a new passage
- Post signal words (because, therefore, as a result, led to, due to) and have the student practice using them in sentences

**RI.5.9 (Integrating and Synthesizing Multiple Sources):**
- Reteach using Lessons 8-9, focusing on the Read, Sort, Combine process
- Provide two very short texts (3-4 sentences each) on a familiar topic and guide the student through the Text Integration Matrix
- Start with identifying what is shared and what is unique before asking for a synthesis paragraph
- Gradually increase to three sources once the student is comfortable with two
