# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 4

## Topic
Explaining How Details Support Main Ideas

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify key details in an informational text and explain their role in supporting a main idea
- Distinguish between key details (essential to the main idea) and interesting but nonessential details
- Write clear explanations of how specific details develop, illustrate, or prove a main idea

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify two or more main ideas in a text (Lesson 3, RI.5.2)
- Ability to distinguish between a topic, main idea, and detail (Grade 4, Unit 2)
- Ability to quote accurately from text (Lesson 1, RI.5.1)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Key Detail Analyzer graphic organizer (main idea at top, three columns below: Detail, How It Supports the Main Idea, Key or Interesting?)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the relationship between main ideas and details and introduce the concept that not all details are equally important.

**Activity: Key Detail or Fun Fact?**

Say: "In our last lesson, we learned how to find two or more main ideas in a passage. Today, we are going to zoom in on the details. Not all details are created equal. Some details are key details -- they directly support or prove the main idea. Other details are interesting but not essential."

Write on the board:
Main Idea: Dogs need regular exercise to stay healthy.
- Detail A: Dogs that do not exercise regularly can develop obesity, joint problems, and behavior issues.
- Detail B: Some people like to dress their dogs in matching outfits when they go for walks.
- Detail C: Veterinarians recommend at least 30 minutes of activity per day for most breeds.

Say: "Which details are key details that support the main idea, and which is interesting but not essential?"

Allow students to respond.

Say: "Details A and C are key details because they directly explain why dogs need exercise or how much exercise they need. Detail B is interesting, but it does not help prove the main idea. Today you will learn how to identify key details and explain exactly how they support the main idea."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to analyze the role of details in supporting a main idea and articulate that connection.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Key detail:** A specific fact, example, or piece of information that directly supports, explains, or proves a main idea
- **Nonessential detail:** An interesting fact that relates to the topic but does not directly support the main idea
- **Support:** To provide evidence or explanation that strengthens the main idea
- **Explain:** To make something clear by providing reasons, facts, or descriptions

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Identifying a key detail is only half the job. In fifth grade, you also need to explain HOW that detail supports the main idea. Here is a sentence frame you can use."

Write on the board:
**Detail-to-Main-Idea Connection Frame:**
"The detail _______ supports the main idea by showing/proving/explaining that _______."

Say: "Let me model this with a passage."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Power of Coral Reefs"**

> Coral reefs are critical to the health of the world's oceans. They serve as nurseries for young fish, providing safe spaces where small fish can grow before venturing into open water. Reefs also act as natural barriers that protect coastlines from the damaging effects of storms and waves. Scientists estimate that coral reefs reduce wave energy by an average of 97 percent before waves reach the shore. Additionally, coral reefs support the livelihoods of millions of people around the world. Fishing communities in tropical regions depend on healthy reefs for their daily catch, and reef tourism generates billions of dollars each year. Despite covering less than one percent of the ocean floor, coral reefs are home to more than 25 percent of all marine species.

Say: "First, let me identify the main idea. The first sentence tells me: 'Coral reefs are critical to the health of the world's oceans.' Now let me find the key details and explain how each one supports this main idea."

Write on the board:

Detail 1: "They serve as nurseries for young fish, providing safe spaces where small fish can grow."
- How it supports the main idea: This detail supports the main idea by showing that coral reefs play an essential role in helping fish populations grow, which is important for ocean health.

Detail 2: "Scientists estimate that coral reefs reduce wave energy by an average of 97 percent."
- How it supports the main idea: This detail supports the main idea by proving that reefs protect coastlines, which shows they are critical to the physical health of coastal environments.

Detail 3: "Coral reefs support the livelihoods of millions of people around the world."
- How it supports the main idea: This detail supports the main idea by explaining that reefs are critical not just to ocean life but also to human communities that depend on them.

Say: "Notice that I did not just list the details. I explained the connection between each detail and the main idea. That is the skill we are building today."

Say: "Now, here is a fact from the passage: coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor. Is this a key detail or an interesting fact? It does not directly prove that reefs are critical -- it tells us about their size. It is interesting context, but it is not the main evidence. However, paired with the fact that reefs are home to 25 percent of marine species, the two together become a powerful key detail because the contrast shows how important reefs are despite their small size."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying key details and explaining how they support the main idea with facilitator guidance.

Say: "Let us work through a new passage together."

**Passage B: "Protecting the Bald Eagle"**

> The bald eagle, America's national symbol, nearly disappeared from the wild in the twentieth century. By the early 1960s, fewer than 500 nesting pairs of bald eagles remained in the lower 48 states. The primary cause of their decline was the widespread use of a pesticide called DDT. When DDT washed into rivers and lakes, it entered the food chain through fish. Eagles that ate contaminated fish produced eggs with shells so thin that they cracked before the chicks could develop. In 1972, the United States government banned the use of DDT. Conservation groups also established breeding programs and protected eagle habitats. The recovery was remarkable. By 2007, the bald eagle population had grown to nearly 10,000 nesting pairs, and the species was officially removed from the endangered species list. Today, more than 300,000 bald eagles live in the United States.

Say: "What is the main idea of this passage? Think about it and share with a partner."

Allow 30 seconds. Call on a student.

Expected response: The bald eagle nearly went extinct but was saved through government action and conservation efforts.

Say: "Good. Now I want you to find three key details that support this main idea. For each one, explain HOW it supports the main idea using our connection frame. Work with a partner."

Allow 3-4 minutes. Then call on students to share.

Expected responses:
- Detail: "By the early 1960s, fewer than 500 nesting pairs of bald eagles remained." This supports the main idea by showing how close to extinction the eagles were, which makes the recovery more remarkable.
- Detail: "In 1972, the United States government banned the use of DDT." This supports the main idea by identifying a specific government action that helped save the eagles.
- Detail: "By 2007, the bald eagle population had grown to nearly 10,000 nesting pairs." This supports the main idea by proving that the conservation efforts worked and the eagle population recovered.

Say: "Excellent. Now, is the fact that the bald eagle is America's national symbol a key detail or an interesting fact? It is interesting context, but it does not directly prove that the eagle nearly went extinct or that it was saved through conservation. It is nonessential to the main idea."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify key details and explain how they support a main idea.

**Passage C: "The Engineering Feat of the Panama Canal"**

> The Panama Canal is one of the greatest engineering achievements in history. Completed in 1914 after ten years of construction, the canal stretches 50 miles across the Isthmus of Panama, connecting the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. Before the canal existed, ships traveling between the east and west coasts of the Americas had to sail around the southern tip of South America, a journey of approximately 12,000 additional miles. Building the canal was extraordinarily difficult. Workers had to cut through dense jungle, blast through solid rock, and move nearly 240 million cubic yards of earth. Tropical diseases, especially malaria and yellow fever, killed thousands of workers during the construction. American doctor William Gorgas led a campaign to control the mosquitoes that spread these diseases, which dramatically reduced the death toll and allowed construction to continue. The canal uses a system of locks -- enormous water-filled chambers -- that raise and lower ships 85 feet above sea level as they pass through. Today, approximately 14,000 ships pass through the Panama Canal each year, making it one of the busiest and most important waterways in the world.

**Directions:**

1. What is the main idea of this passage? Write it as a complete sentence.
2. Identify three key details that support the main idea. Quote each detail from the text.
3. For each key detail, explain HOW it supports the main idea using the connection frame: "This detail supports the main idea by showing/proving/explaining that ______."
4. Identify one detail in the passage that is interesting but NOT essential to the main idea. Explain why it is nonessential.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-3.
**Stretch:** Answer all four questions with accurate quotes and thorough explanations.
**Challenge:** Answer all four questions, then rank your three key details from most important to least important and explain your ranking.

### Interleaved Review
5. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.3) According to the passage, what problem did tropical diseases cause during the construction of the canal, and how was it solved? Explain using details from the text.
6. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.2) Write a two-sentence summary of this passage. Include the main idea and at least one key detail.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Monarch butterflies depend on milkweed plants for their survival. Female monarchs lay their eggs exclusively on milkweed leaves because milkweed is the only plant that monarch caterpillars can eat. The milkweed plant also contains a bitter chemical that makes the caterpillars taste bad to predators, providing a natural defense. However, milkweed habitat has declined by more than 20 percent in the past two decades due to farming and development.

1. What is the main idea of this passage?
2. Name one key detail and explain how it supports the main idea.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Main idea: Monarch butterflies depend on milkweed plants for their survival.
2. Any one of: (a) "Milkweed is the only plant that monarch caterpillars can eat" -- this supports the main idea by proving that monarchs cannot survive without milkweed. (b) "The milkweed plant also contains a bitter chemical that makes the caterpillars taste bad to predators" -- this supports the main idea by showing that milkweed protects monarchs from being eaten. (c) "Milkweed habitat has declined by more than 20 percent" -- this supports the main idea by suggesting that milkweed loss threatens monarch survival.

**Mastery = Main idea correctly identified and one detail with a clear explanation of how it supports the main idea**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to Passage A and model the connection between detail and main idea one more time
- Provide the student with a main idea and three details (one nonessential) and ask them to sort and explain
- Use the sentence frame to scaffold the explanation

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 5 on summarizing informational text
- Encourage the student to practice identifying key details in nonfiction reading at home

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Detail Detective Board:** Create a display where students post main ideas and their supporting details from independent reading. Other students can check whether each detail is truly a key detail or just an interesting fact.
2. **Rebuild the Passage:** Give students a main idea and six details (three key, three nonessential). Students select only the key details and write a paragraph using them.
3. **Key Detail Debate:** Students work in pairs. Each pair reads the same passage and identifies what they think are the three most important key details. Pairs then compare their choices and debate any differences.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The distinction between key details and interesting details can be subtle. Emphasize that a key detail directly supports the main idea -- if you removed it, the main idea would be weaker. An interesting detail adds flavor but does not change the strength of the argument.
- Students may initially struggle with the "explain how" component. The sentence frame ("This detail supports the main idea by showing/proving/explaining that ___") provides crucial scaffolding. Use it consistently until students internalize the pattern.
- Watch for students who copy details without explaining the connection. Encourage them to think: "Why did the author include this fact? What does it prove about the main idea?"
- This lesson pairs naturally with Lesson 3. If students struggled with identifying multiple main ideas, review that skill before diving into the detail analysis.
