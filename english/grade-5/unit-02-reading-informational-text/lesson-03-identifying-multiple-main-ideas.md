# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 3

## Topic
Identifying Multiple Main Ideas

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Recognize that a single informational text can have more than one main idea
- Identify two or more main ideas within an informational passage
- Explain how different sections or paragraphs of a text develop different main ideas

## Prerequisites
- Ability to determine the main idea of a text and explain how it is supported by key details (RI.4.2)
- Understanding of the difference between a topic and a main idea (Grade 4, Unit 2)
- Ability to quote accurately from informational text (Lesson 1, RI.5.1)

## Materials Needed
- Printed or projected lesson passages
- Multiple Main Ideas Map graphic organizer (two side-by-side webs, each with a center circle for a main idea and surrounding circles for details)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Pencils and paper for student responses
- Highlighters in two colors

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review single main idea identification and introduce the concept that a text can have more than one main idea.

**Activity: One Topic, Two Points**

Say: "In fourth grade, you learned how to find the main idea of a passage. Usually you looked for one big point the author was making. But think about this: when you write a report about an animal, do you only make one point? Or do you make several important points -- maybe one about where it lives and another about what it eats?"

Allow students to respond.

Say: "Authors of informational texts often make more than one important point. A passage about dolphins might have one main idea about how dolphins communicate and another main idea about how dolphins work together to hunt. Both are big, important ideas -- not just details. Today, we are going to learn how to find two or more main ideas in a single passage."

Write on the board:
- Grade 4: Find THE main idea (one)
- Grade 5: Find TWO OR MORE main ideas

Say: "This is a big step up. Let us get started."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students how to identify multiple main ideas by analyzing different sections of a text.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Main idea:** The most important point the author makes about a topic, expressed as a complete sentence
- **Multiple main ideas:** Two or more central points developed within a single text
- **Section:** A distinct part of a text, often marked by a paragraph or group of paragraphs, that focuses on one aspect of the topic
- **Key details:** Specific facts, examples, or pieces of information that support or explain a main idea

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Here is a strategy for finding multiple main ideas. I call it the Section Scan."

Write on the board:
1. Read the whole passage to understand the topic.
2. Look at each section or paragraph -- what is the most important point in this section?
3. Ask: Are these points different enough to be separate main ideas, or are they all supporting the same one?
4. Write each main idea as a complete sentence.

Say: "The key question is Step 3. A detail supports a main idea, but a second main idea is a new, big point that could almost stand on its own. Let me show you."

### Worked Examples

**Passage A: "The Amazing Honeybee"**

> Honeybees are remarkable communicators. When a honeybee discovers a good source of nectar, it returns to the hive and performs a special movement called a "waggle dance." The direction the bee moves during the dance tells the other bees which direction to fly to find the flowers. The speed of the dance tells them how far away the flowers are. Scientists have found that bees can communicate the location of food sources more than six miles away using this dance.
>
> Honeybees are also essential to the world's food supply. As bees travel from flower to flower collecting nectar, they carry pollen on their fuzzy bodies. This process, called pollination, is necessary for many plants to produce fruit and seeds. In fact, honeybees pollinate approximately 80 percent of all flowering plants in the United States. Without bees, many of the fruits, vegetables, and nuts that people eat every day -- including apples, almonds, and blueberries -- would become extremely scarce.

Say: "Let me use the Section Scan. First, I read the whole passage. The topic is honeybees."

Say: "Now I look at each paragraph. The first paragraph is about how bees communicate using the waggle dance. Every detail in that paragraph supports the idea that bees are remarkable communicators."

Say: "The second paragraph shifts. It is about how bees help the food supply through pollination. Every detail supports the idea that bees are essential to our food."

Say: "Now Step 3 -- are these different enough to be separate main ideas? Communication and pollination are two very different concepts. Yes, these are two separate main ideas."

Write on the board:
- Main Idea 1: Honeybees are remarkable communicators that use a waggle dance to share information.
- Main Idea 2: Honeybees are essential to the world's food supply because they pollinate most flowering plants.

Say: "Notice that both are big ideas -- not small details. Each one has its own set of supporting details."

Draw the Multiple Main Ideas Map:
- Web 1 center: "Honeybees are remarkable communicators."
  - Detail: Waggle dance tells direction and distance
  - Detail: Can communicate locations over 6 miles away
- Web 2 center: "Honeybees are essential to the food supply."
  - Detail: Carry pollen from flower to flower
  - Detail: Pollinate 80% of flowering plants in the US
  - Detail: Without bees, many fruits and nuts would be scarce

Say: "Each main idea has its own web of details. That is how you know you have found two real main ideas and not just one main idea with details."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice identifying multiple main ideas with facilitator support.

Say: "Let us try the Section Scan together with a new passage."

**Passage B: "Volcanoes: Destroyers and Creators"**

> When a volcano erupts, the results can be catastrophic. Rivers of molten lava can destroy everything in their path, burning forests and burying entire towns. Volcanic ash can rise miles into the atmosphere, blocking sunlight and disrupting air travel across continents. Pyroclastic flows -- fast-moving currents of hot gas and volcanic matter -- can race down a mountainside at speeds over 400 miles per hour, giving people almost no time to escape. The eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD buried the Roman city of Pompeii under nearly 20 feet of ash and pumice, preserving it for almost 2,000 years.
>
> Yet volcanoes also create. Over millions of years, volcanic eruptions have built some of the most beautiful landscapes on Earth. The Hawaiian Islands were formed entirely by underwater volcanic eruptions that gradually built up layers of rock above the ocean surface. Volcanic soil is among the richest on the planet because it contains minerals like potassium, phosphorus, and calcium that plants need to grow. Farmers around the world prize volcanic soil for its extraordinary fertility. In Iceland, people harness the geothermal energy produced by volcanic activity to heat their homes and generate electricity.

Say: "Step 1 -- what is the overall topic?"

Allow students to respond. (Expected: Volcanoes.)

Say: "Step 2 -- look at each paragraph. What is the most important point of the first paragraph? Turn and talk to a partner."

Allow 30 seconds. Call on a student.

Expected response: Volcanic eruptions can be extremely destructive and dangerous.

Say: "Good. Now what is the most important point of the second paragraph?"

Allow 30 seconds. Call on a student.

Expected response: Volcanoes also create landscapes, rich soil, and energy sources.

Say: "Step 3 -- are these different enough to be separate main ideas?"

Allow students to respond. (Expected: Yes -- destruction and creation are opposite ideas.)

Say: "Exactly. Now write both main ideas as complete sentences in your Multiple Main Ideas Map, and add at least two supporting details for each."

Allow 2-3 minutes for students to complete the organizer. Circulate and check.

Expected map:
- Main Idea 1: Volcanic eruptions can cause catastrophic destruction.
  - Details: Lava burns forests and buries towns; ash blocks sunlight; pyroclastic flows move at 400+ mph; Vesuvius buried Pompeii
- Main Idea 2: Volcanoes also create beautiful landscapes, rich soil, and useful energy.
  - Details: Hawaiian Islands formed by eruptions; volcanic soil is rich in minerals; Iceland uses geothermal energy

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently identify multiple main ideas in a new passage.

**Passage C: "The History and Science of Chocolate"**

> Chocolate has a long and fascinating history that stretches back thousands of years. The ancient Maya and Aztec civilizations of Central America were the first to cultivate cacao trees and use their beans to make a bitter drink. The Aztec emperor Montezuma reportedly drank fifty cups of chocolate a day, believing it gave him strength and wisdom. When Spanish explorers arrived in the Americas in the 1500s, they brought cacao beans back to Europe. Europeans added sugar to the bitter drink, and chocolate quickly became a luxury enjoyed by the wealthy. By the 1800s, new manufacturing processes made chocolate affordable for ordinary people, and the first chocolate bars appeared in stores.
>
> Making chocolate is a surprisingly complex scientific process. After cacao beans are harvested from the pods of the cacao tree, they must be fermented for several days to develop their flavor. Next, the beans are dried, roasted, and cracked open to reveal the inner nibs. These nibs are ground into a thick paste called chocolate liquor, which contains both cocoa solids and cocoa butter. Manufacturers carefully control the temperature during a process called tempering, which gives finished chocolate its smooth texture and satisfying snap when broken. The entire process from bean to bar can take several weeks and requires precise control at every stage.

**Directions:**

1. What is the overall topic of this passage?
2. What is the main idea of the first paragraph? Write it as a complete sentence.
3. What is the main idea of the second paragraph? Write it as a complete sentence.
4. List two key details that support each main idea (four details total).
5. Explain in your own words why this passage has two main ideas instead of one.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Answer questions 1-4.
**Stretch:** Answer all five questions, and include at least one accurate quote from the text for each main idea.
**Challenge:** Answer all five questions with quotes, then write a third paragraph that could add a third main idea to this passage (for example, about the health effects of chocolate or the economics of cacao farming). Write 3-4 sentences.

### Interleaved Review
6. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.2) Reread only the second paragraph. Write a one-sentence summary of that paragraph that includes the main idea and one key detail.
7. (Grade 4 review -- RI.4.1) Quote one sentence from the passage that tells you how long chocolate has been used by humans.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

> Elephants are highly social animals that live in close family groups led by the oldest female, called the matriarch. The matriarch uses her decades of experience to guide the herd to water and food sources, especially during droughts. Other members of the herd help care for young calves, protecting them from predators and teaching them survival skills.
>
> Elephants are also among the most intelligent animals on the planet. They have been observed using tools, such as sticks to scratch themselves and branches to swat flies. Elephants can recognize themselves in mirrors, a sign of self-awareness that is rare in the animal kingdom. They also appear to mourn their dead, returning to the bones of deceased family members and touching them gently with their trunks.

1. What are the two main ideas of this passage?
2. Name one supporting detail for each main idea.

**Expected Answers:**
1. Main Idea 1: Elephants are highly social animals that live in close family groups led by the matriarch. Main Idea 2: Elephants are among the most intelligent animals on the planet.
2. Detail for MI1: (any one) The matriarch guides the herd to water and food; herd members help care for young calves. Detail for MI2: (any one) They use tools; they recognize themselves in mirrors; they appear to mourn their dead.

**Mastery = Both main ideas correctly identified and at least one valid detail for each**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to Passage A and walk through the Section Scan step by step with the student
- Provide a two-paragraph passage on a familiar topic and help the student identify the shift between paragraphs
- Ask guiding questions: "What is this paragraph mostly about? Is the next paragraph about the same thing or something different?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 4 on explaining how details support main ideas
- Challenge the student to find two main ideas in an informational article during independent reading

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Main Idea Headlines:** Students read a two-paragraph informational passage and write a newspaper headline for each paragraph that captures its main idea.
2. **Build a Passage:** Give students two main idea sentences and ask them to write a short informational paragraph (3-4 sentences) for each, adding key details they create.
3. **Main Idea Sort:** Provide a passage, a list of main ideas, and a list of details all mixed together. Students sort the statements into main ideas and details, then match each detail to the correct main idea.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The biggest conceptual leap in this lesson is understanding that a text can have more than one main idea. Students who have practiced finding a single main idea may want to combine everything into one statement. Encourage them to ask, "Does this passage make two distinct points?"
- Paragraph structure is a helpful guide. In well-organized informational text, each paragraph often develops its own main idea. Teach students to check whether each paragraph introduces a new big point.
- Some students may identify details as main ideas. Use the "umbrella" test: a main idea is broad enough to cover several details underneath it. If the statement is too specific, it is probably a detail.
- Color-coded highlighting is very effective here. Have students use one color for details supporting Main Idea 1 and a different color for details supporting Main Idea 2.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice
1. The overall topic is chocolate (or the history and science of chocolate).
2. Main Idea 1: Chocolate has a long and fascinating history that stretches back thousands of years. (Accept variations such as: Chocolate has been used by humans for thousands of years and has evolved from a bitter drink to a worldwide treat.)
3. Main Idea 2: Making chocolate is a surprisingly complex scientific process. (Accept variations such as: Turning cacao beans into chocolate requires a complex, multi-step scientific process.)
4. Details for Main Idea 1: (any two) The Maya and Aztec civilizations were the first to use cacao beans; Montezuma reportedly drank fifty cups a day; Spanish explorers brought cacao to Europe; Europeans added sugar; the first chocolate bars appeared in the 1800s.
   Details for Main Idea 2: (any two) Beans must be fermented, dried, roasted, and cracked; nibs are ground into chocolate liquor; tempering controls the temperature for smooth texture; the process can take several weeks.
5. This passage has two main ideas because the first paragraph focuses on the history of chocolate (how it was discovered and spread), while the second paragraph focuses on the science of making chocolate (the steps in the production process). These are two separate topics that each require their own set of details.

### Interleaved Review
6. Summary: Making chocolate is a complex process that involves fermenting, roasting, and grinding cacao beans, with precise temperature control during tempering to create the final product. (Accept reasonable variations.)
7. The passage states, "Chocolate has a long and fascinating history that stretches back thousands of years." (Also accept: "The ancient Maya and Aztec civilizations of Central America were the first to cultivate cacao trees and use their beans to make a bitter drink.")
