# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Opinion Writing: Stating and Supporting a Position

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between a personal preference and a supportable opinion
- Write a clear opinion statement that takes a position on a topic
- Identify at least two reasons that support an opinion and connect them to supporting evidence

## Prerequisites
- W.4.1: Experience writing opinion pieces with reasons supported by facts and details
- Understanding of basic paragraph structure (topic sentence, supporting details, concluding sentence)
- Familiarity with the difference between fact and opinion from prior reading units

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Copies of the model opinion text (provided below)
- Graphic organizer: Opinion Planning Web (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about opinions and engage students in thinking about arguable claims.

**Activity: Agree or Disagree**

Post the following statements on the board one at a time. Students stand on one side of the room for "Agree" and the other for "Disagree." After each statement, ask one student from each side to explain their reasoning.

1. "Chocolate ice cream is the best flavor." (preference — hard to argue with evidence)
2. "All fifth graders should be required to learn a musical instrument." (arguable opinion — can be supported with evidence)
3. "The sun rises in the east." (fact — not an opinion)

Say: "Notice that statement 2 is the kind of opinion we write about in academic writing. It is something people can reasonably disagree about, AND it can be supported with reasons and evidence. That is what we are going to focus on today."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students the structure of an opinion statement and introduce the concept of supporting an opinion with reasons and evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Opinion statement:** A sentence that clearly states the writer's position on a debatable topic
- **Reason:** A logical point that explains WHY the writer holds the opinion
- **Evidence:** Facts, details, examples, or expert statements that back up a reason
- **Organizational structure:** The planned arrangement of ideas in a piece of writing

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In fourth grade, you learned to write opinion pieces with reasons and details. In fifth grade, we are going to level up. Your opinion writing will have a clear organizational structure, stronger evidence, and more sophisticated language to connect your ideas."

Write on the board:

**Formula for a Strong Opinion Statement:**
Topic + Position + Hint at Reasons = Opinion Statement

Say: "Watch me write an opinion statement. My topic is 'recess.' My position is that it should be longer. My hint at reasons is about health and focus. So my opinion statement is: 'Elementary schools should extend recess to at least 30 minutes each day because longer breaks improve both physical health and classroom focus.'"

Point out: "Notice that my statement tells the reader exactly what I believe AND gives a preview of why. This is the anchor of the whole piece."

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Read the following model opinion text aloud. Display it on chart paper or a projector so all students can follow along.

> **Should Schools Replace Paper Textbooks with Tablets?**
>
> Many schools across the country are debating whether to replace traditional paper textbooks with digital tablets. After considering the evidence, I believe that schools should make the switch to tablets because they reduce costs over time, they allow for interactive learning, and they are better for the environment.
>
> First, tablets save money in the long run. According to a report by the Federal Communications Commission, schools spend an average of $250 per student on textbooks each year, while a tablet loaded with digital books costs about $150 per year after the initial purchase. Over five years, that adds up to significant savings that schools could use for other programs.
>
> In addition, tablets make learning more interactive. Students can watch videos, complete practice quizzes, and explore simulations right inside their digital textbook. Research from the University of Southern California found that students who used interactive digital materials scored 20% higher on science assessments than those who used paper textbooks alone.
>
> Finally, switching to tablets helps protect the environment. The paper industry is one of the largest contributors to deforestation. By reducing the demand for paper textbooks, schools can help conserve forests and reduce waste in landfills.
>
> For all of these reasons, schools should embrace tablets as a smarter, greener, and more engaging alternative to paper textbooks. The evidence is clear: tablets benefit students, schools, and the planet.

Say: "Let's annotate this together." Use two highlighter colors:
- **Color 1:** Highlight the opinion statement (first paragraph, last sentence) and the concluding statement (last paragraph).
- **Color 2:** Highlight the three reasons (first sentence of each body paragraph).

Ask: "What evidence does the writer use to support each reason?" Chart student responses.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively plan an opinion piece using a graphic organizer.

**Activity: Shared Opinion Planning Web**

Draw the following graphic organizer on chart paper:

```
                    [OPINION STATEMENT]
                    /       |        \
              [Reason 1] [Reason 2] [Reason 3]
                 |          |          |
           [Evidence]  [Evidence]  [Evidence]
```

Say: "Let's plan an opinion piece together. Our topic is: 'Should students be allowed to have phones in school?' First, we need to decide our position."

Take a class vote, then write the majority opinion as the opinion statement together. Guide students to:

1. State the opinion clearly using the formula: Topic + Position + Hint at Reasons
2. Brainstorm three reasons (write all suggestions, then select the three strongest)
3. For each reason, identify one piece of evidence (a fact, example, or logical explanation)

Sample completed web:

- **Opinion:** Students should not be allowed to use phones during school hours because phones distract from learning, create unfair social situations, and increase cyberbullying.
- **Reason 1:** Phones distract students. **Evidence:** A study found that students who had phones on their desks scored 20% lower on tests.
- **Reason 2:** Phones create inequality. **Evidence:** Not all students can afford the latest phones, which leads to social pressure.
- **Reason 3:** Phones increase cyberbullying. **Evidence:** 70% of students report seeing cyberbullying happen during the school day.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently plan and draft an opinion statement with supporting reasons.

Choose ONE of the following topics (or propose your own, approved by the teacher):

1. Should homework be eliminated for fifth graders?
2. Should schools have a longer summer break or year-round school?
3. Should students be required to wear uniforms?

**Task:** Using the Opinion Planning Web graphic organizer, plan your opinion piece. Then write your introductory paragraph, which must include:
- A sentence that introduces the topic and gets the reader's attention
- A clear opinion statement using the formula (Topic + Position + Hint at Reasons)

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete the planning web and write the introductory paragraph as described above.
**Stretch:** Complete the planning web, write the introductory paragraph, AND write one body paragraph with a reason and supporting evidence.
**Challenge:** Complete the full planning web, write the introductory paragraph, write one body paragraph, AND draft a concluding statement that goes beyond simply restating the opinion.

### Interleaved Review
1. **Grammar review (L.4.1):** Correct the following sentence: "Me and my friend thinks that recess should be longer." (Expected: "My friend and I think that recess should be longer.")
2. **Writing review (W.4.2):** In one sentence, explain the difference between informative writing and opinion writing. (Expected: Informative writing explains a topic with facts, while opinion writing tries to convince the reader of a particular point of view.)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Write an opinion statement about the following topic: "Whether fifth graders should be allowed to choose their own seats in the classroom." Your statement must include your position and a hint at your reasons.

**Expected Response:**
A complete sentence that names the topic, states a clear position (for or against), and previews at least one reason. Example: "Fifth graders should be allowed to choose their own seats because it builds responsibility and helps students feel more comfortable in class."

**Mastery = The opinion statement clearly states a position AND previews at least one reason.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Conference individually with the student to distinguish between a preference ("I like picking my seat") and an arguable opinion ("Students should be allowed to choose seats because...")
- Provide the sentence frame: "[Topic] should/should not [position] because [reason 1] and [reason 2]."
- Revisit the model text and highlight the opinion statement together

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 2, where they will organize reasons in a logical order and develop evidence more fully
- Encourage the student to begin drafting body paragraphs for the piece they planned today

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Real-world opinions:** Find an editorial or opinion article from a kid-friendly news source (e.g., Newsela, Scholastic News). Identify the opinion statement, reasons, and evidence.
- **Debate prep:** With a partner, take opposite sides of the topic you chose. Practice stating your opinion and giving one reason aloud in 30 seconds.
- **Opinion journal:** Start a running list of topics you have opinions about. For each topic, jot down your position and at least one reason. This will serve as a bank of ideas for future writing.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students sometimes confuse strong preferences with arguable opinions. Keep returning to the test: "Can this be supported with facts and evidence, or is it purely personal taste?"
- The Opinion Planning Web is intentionally simple for Lesson 1. In Lesson 2, students will learn to order their reasons strategically (strongest first or strongest last) and to develop more detailed evidence.
- If students struggle to find evidence, remind them that evidence can include logical reasoning ("It makes sense that..."), real-world examples, or facts they have learned in other subjects. They do not need to cite formal sources at this stage, but they should practice attributing information (e.g., "According to..." or "Studies show that...").
- For students who finish early, ask them to swap their opinion statement with a partner and give feedback: "Is the position clear? Can you tell what the reasons will be?"

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Opinion Statement | No clear opinion is stated, or the statement is a personal preference without reasons | An opinion is stated but is vague or does not preview reasons | A clear opinion is stated with a position and at least one reason previewed | A compelling opinion is stated with a clear position and multiple reasons previewed in a sophisticated sentence |
| Reasons | No reasons are provided | One reason is provided but is weak or unrelated to the opinion | Two or more logical reasons are provided that clearly support the opinion | Three or more well-chosen reasons are provided and logically ordered |
| Evidence | No evidence is provided | Evidence is attempted but is vague or unconnected to reasons | At least one piece of relevant evidence supports each reason | Multiple pieces of specific, convincing evidence support each reason, with attribution |
| Introduction | No introduction is written, or it does not introduce the topic | The topic is mentioned but the introduction lacks a hook or context | The introduction names the topic, provides context, and states the opinion clearly | The introduction engages the reader with a compelling hook, provides relevant context, and presents the opinion in a polished sentence |
