# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 5

## Topic
Narrative Writing: Orienting the Reader

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write an opening that establishes the setting, introduces the narrator or characters, and establishes the situation
- Organize events in a natural, logical sequence
- Use concrete words and sensory details to help the reader picture the story

## Prerequisites
- W.4.3: Experience writing narratives with a clear event sequence, descriptive details, and a conclusion
- W.4.3a: Orienting the reader by establishing a situation and introducing a narrator and/or characters
- Familiarity with the five senses as a tool for descriptive writing

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model narrative text (provided below)
- Graphic organizer: Story Map (described below)
- Colored pencils for illustration (optional)
- Sensory Details Chart (five senses reference poster)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Immerse students in the power of a strong narrative opening and build excitement for storytelling.

**Activity: First Lines Gallery**

Display the following opening lines from well-known children's books (or similar examples). Read each one aloud dramatically.

1. "It was a dark and stormy night — well, actually, it was a bright Tuesday afternoon, which made what happened next all the more unexpected."
2. "The last thing Maya expected to find in her grandmother's attic was a map with her own name written on it."
3. "If you have ever stood at the edge of a cliff and felt the wind push against your chest, then you know exactly how Jonas felt on the morning everything changed."

Ask: "What do all three openings have in common?" Guide students to notice:
- Each one makes you want to keep reading
- Each one tells you something about the character, setting, or situation
- Each one creates a mood or feeling

Say: "Today we are going to learn how to write narrative openings that pull the reader into the story from the very first line."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the three elements of orienting the reader and the use of sensory details in narrative writing.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Narrator:** The person or voice telling the story (first person = "I"; third person = "he/she/they")
- **Setting:** The time and place where the story happens
- **Situation:** The circumstance or problem that sets the story in motion
- **Event sequence:** The order of events in a narrative — what happens first, next, and last
- **Sensory details:** Descriptions that appeal to the five senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "When you start a narrative, you are orienting the reader — which means you are giving them everything they need to step into your story. A reader who is well-oriented knows three things right away."

Write on the board:

**Orienting the Reader — Three Questions to Answer:**
1. **WHO** is in this story? (character/narrator)
2. **WHERE and WHEN** does it take place? (setting)
3. **WHAT** is going on? (situation)

Say: "Watch me write a narrative opening. I will think aloud so you can hear my decisions."

Think aloud and write on chart paper:

"I need a character. I will write about a girl named Kayla. She is 11 years old and nervous. Now I need a setting. The story takes place at a community swimming pool on the first day of summer. Now I need a situation: Kayla has just moved to a new town and does not know anyone at the pool."

Draft on chart paper:

"Kayla gripped the chain-link fence and peered through the diamond-shaped gaps at the crowded swimming pool. The sharp scent of chlorine hit her nose. Laughter and splashing echoed off the concrete deck, but none of it was for her — she had moved to Riverside three days ago, and she did not know a single person on the other side of that fence."

Say: "Let's check — WHO? Kayla. WHERE and WHEN? A swimming pool on what seems like a summer day. WHAT is going on? She is new in town and nervous about going in. Now notice the sensory details." Underline:
- "gripped the chain-link fence" (touch)
- "sharp scent of chlorine" (smell)
- "laughter and splashing echoed" (sound)
- "diamond-shaped gaps" (sight)

Say: "Sensory details make the reader FEEL like they are there. In fifth grade, we push beyond just sight — we use all five senses."

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Display the full model narrative opening (first two paragraphs):

> **The Longest Minute**
>
> The gymnasium smelled like old rubber and floor wax. Marcus stood at the free-throw line, bouncing the basketball three times the way his father had taught him — bounce, bounce, bounce, then hold. The scoreboard behind the hoop glowed red: HOME 47, VISITORS 48. Eight seconds remained on the clock. The entire fifth-grade section had risen to their feet, their sneakers squeaking against the metal bleachers, and every single one of them was staring at Marcus.
>
> He could feel the seams of the ball pressing into his fingertips. His jersey stuck to his back with sweat. Coach Davis stood at the sideline with her arms crossed, nodding once — a small, steady nod that said, "You can do this." But Marcus was not so sure. He had missed his last three free throws in practice, and that memory sat in his stomach like a stone. He took a breath, bent his knees, and lifted the ball to eye level.

Ask students to identify:
- **WHO:** Marcus (the narrator is third person, following Marcus)
- **WHERE/WHEN:** A gymnasium, during a basketball game, with eight seconds on the clock
- **WHAT:** Marcus must make a free throw to tie or win the game, and he is nervous

Then ask students to find at least four sensory details:
- Smell: "old rubber and floor wax"
- Touch: "seams of the ball pressing into his fingertips," "jersey stuck to his back with sweat"
- Sound: "sneakers squeaking against the metal bleachers"
- Sight: "scoreboard glowed red," "Coach Davis nodding"
- Internal feeling: "memory sat in his stomach like a stone"

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively plan a narrative using a Story Map and draft an opening together.

**Activity: Shared Story Map**

Draw the following Story Map on chart paper:

```
STORY MAP
---------
Character(s): _______________
Setting (where & when): _______________
Situation (problem): _______________
Event 1: _______________
Event 2: _______________
Event 3: _______________
Conclusion (how it ends): _______________
```

Say: "Let's plan a story together. Our scenario is: A student discovers something unexpected in a school locker that has been locked since the beginning of the year."

Guide students to fill in the Story Map collaboratively:
- **Character:** Aiden, a curious fifth grader who just transferred to the school
- **Setting:** Wilson Elementary School, a rainy Wednesday morning in October
- **Situation:** Aiden is assigned a locker that has been jammed shut since last year. When the custodian finally opens it, something surprising is inside.
- **Event 1:** Aiden tries the locker on his first day; it will not open
- **Event 2:** The custodian uses a special tool to pry it open; they find a sealed envelope with a hand-drawn map inside
- **Event 3:** Aiden follows the map to a hidden time capsule buried under the school playground
- **Conclusion:** The time capsule contains letters and drawings from students who attended the school 25 years ago

Now collaboratively draft the opening paragraph:

Say: "Let's write the first paragraph together, making sure we answer WHO, WHERE/WHEN, and WHAT. Let's include at least two sensory details."

Write on chart paper as students contribute ideas. Sample:

"Rain drummed against the tall windows of Wilson Elementary as Aiden dragged his backpack down the B-wing hallway. His new sneakers squeaked on the freshly waxed floor — everything about this school was new. New hallways. New teachers. New locker. He stopped in front of locker 247 and checked the combination on the sticky note his homeroom teacher had given him: 14-32-8. He spun the dial right, left, right, and pulled. Nothing. He pulled harder. The locker did not budge."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently plan and draft the opening of a narrative using sensory details.

Choose ONE of the following story starters (or create your own):

1. A student wakes up to discover that school has been canceled — but not for the reason anyone expects.
2. During a family road trip, the car breaks down in a town that is not on any map.
3. A kid finds an old journal buried in their backyard. The last entry is dated exactly 100 years ago today.

**Task:**
1. Complete a Story Map (characters, setting, situation, three events, conclusion).
2. Draft your opening paragraph (at least 5 sentences). Your opening must:
   - Introduce the main character
   - Establish the setting (where and when)
   - Hint at the situation or problem
   - Include at least three sensory details (from at least two different senses)

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete the Story Map and draft the opening paragraph as described.
**Stretch:** Complete the Story Map and draft the opening paragraph AND a second paragraph that develops the first event. Include at least one simile or metaphor.
**Challenge:** Complete the Story Map and draft the first three paragraphs (opening + two events). Experiment with starting the story in the middle of the action (in medias res) and then filling in context.

### Interleaved Review
1. **Punctuation review (L.4.2b):** Add the correct punctuation to the following sentence: "Wait shouted Marcus dont open that locker yet" (Expected: "'Wait!' shouted Marcus. 'Don't open that locker yet!'")
2. **Informative writing review (W.5.2):** What is the purpose of a focus statement in an informative introduction? (Expected: A focus statement tells the reader what specific subtopics or categories the piece will cover.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Write 2-3 sentences that orient the reader for the following scenario: A girl named Sofia finds a stray kitten on her walk home from school. Include at least two sensory details.

**Expected Response:**
Sentences that establish WHO (Sofia), WHERE/WHEN (walking home from school), and WHAT (finding a stray kitten), with at least two sensory details. Example: "Sofia was halfway down Maple Street when she heard a tiny, high-pitched mewing coming from behind the recycling bins. She knelt on the damp sidewalk and peered into the shadows. A pair of bright green eyes blinked back at her."

**Mastery = The response introduces the character, establishes the setting, hints at the situation, and includes at least two sensory details.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three orienting questions (WHO, WHERE/WHEN, WHAT) and ask the student to answer each one verbally before writing
- Provide the Sensory Details Chart and ask the student to choose two senses to include
- Offer a sentence starter: "As [character] [action], [sensory detail]..."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 6 (Dialogue and Pacing)
- Encourage the student to continue drafting their narrative during free writing time

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Setting sketch:** Draw the setting of your story using colored pencils, then label the drawing with sensory details from each of the five senses. Use these details in your next draft.
- **Point-of-view experiment:** Rewrite your opening in a different point of view. If you wrote in third person ("Marcus stood..."), try first person ("I stood..."). How does it change the feeling?
- **Mentor text hunt:** Find the opening paragraph of a favorite book. Identify how the author orients the reader (WHO, WHERE/WHEN, WHAT). Share with the class.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Narrative writing is often students' favorite mode because they get to tell stories. Channel this enthusiasm by allowing creative freedom in topic choice while maintaining structural expectations.
- The Story Map is essential for students who tend to write narratives that ramble without direction. Insist on completing the map before drafting. Students who skip planning often write openings that fail to establish the situation because they have not yet decided what the story is about.
- Sensory details beyond sight are challenging for most students. The Sensory Details Chart (a poster listing the five senses with example words for each) should be posted visibly. During conferences, ask: "I can see this story. Can I hear it? Can I smell it?"
- Some students will write openings that begin with "One day..." or "Once upon a time..." Encourage them to find more engaging openings: start with dialogue, start with action, start with a sensory detail. The First Lines Gallery warm-up provides models for this.

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Character Introduction | No character is introduced, or the character is named without any description | A character is named and briefly described, but the reader does not get a sense of personality or emotion | The character is introduced with enough detail that the reader understands who they are and how they feel about the situation | The character comes alive through vivid description, internal thoughts, and distinctive actions that reveal personality |
| Setting | No setting is established | A setting is mentioned but vaguely ("at school," "outside") | The setting is clearly established with specific time and place details | The setting is richly described with sensory details that create a mood and immerse the reader in the world of the story |
| Situation | No situation or problem is established in the opening | A situation is hinted at but is unclear or confusing | The situation is clearly established, giving the reader a reason to keep reading | The situation is compelling and creates immediate tension or curiosity; the reader urgently wants to know what happens next |
| Sensory Details | No sensory details are present | One or two sensory details are present, all from the same sense (usually sight) | At least three sensory details from at least two different senses are naturally woven into the narrative | Multiple sensory details from three or more senses are skillfully integrated, creating a vivid, immersive experience for the reader |
| Event Sequence | Events are not organized or no sequence is apparent | Events are listed but the order is confusing or illogical | Events are organized in a natural sequence that the reader can follow clearly | Events are organized with deliberate pacing — some moments are expanded for effect while others move quickly |
