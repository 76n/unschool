# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 2

## Topic
Opinion Writing: Organizing Reasons with Evidence

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Arrange reasons in a logical order (from least to most important or most to least important)
- Support each reason with at least one specific fact, detail, or example
- Write a concluding statement that reinforces the opinion without simply restating the introduction

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 1: Writing a clear opinion statement with reasons
- W.4.1b: Experience providing reasons supported by facts and details
- Understanding of paragraph structure with topic sentences

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Students' Opinion Planning Webs from Lesson 1
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model opinion text (provided below)
- Highlighters (three colors)
- Graphic organizer: Reason-Evidence Ladder (described below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review opinion statements from Lesson 1 and introduce the concept of strategic ordering.

**Activity: Strongest Reason Sort**

Display three reasons for the opinion "Schools should serve breakfast every morning":

1. Some students enjoy eating breakfast at school. (weak — preference, not evidence-based)
2. Research shows that students who eat breakfast score 17% higher on math tests. (strong — specific data)
3. Many families are too rushed in the morning to prepare breakfast at home. (medium — logical but no data)

Say: "Rank these from strongest to weakest. What makes a reason strong?" Guide students to see that specific evidence and clear logic make reasons powerful, while personal preferences are weak.

Say: "Today we will learn how to put our reasons in the best order AND make each one stronger with solid evidence."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach strategic ordering of reasons and the skill of developing each reason with specific evidence.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Logical order:** Arranging ideas so they make sense and build on each other
- **Strongest-last order:** Saving the most compelling reason for the end to leave a lasting impression
- **Supporting evidence:** Specific facts, statistics, examples, or expert opinions that prove a reason is valid
- **Concluding statement:** A final sentence or sentences that tie the piece together and leave the reader thinking

### Instruction Notes

Say: "There are two common ways to order your reasons. Strategy 1: Start with your strongest reason to grab the reader's attention right away. Strategy 2: Save your strongest reason for last so it is the final thing the reader remembers. Both work — the key is that you CHOOSE deliberately, not randomly."

Write on the board:

**Reason-Evidence Ladder:**
```
Reason (topic sentence for the paragraph)
   |
   v
Evidence (fact, statistic, example)
   |
   v
Explanation (how the evidence supports the reason)
```

Say: "Each body paragraph follows this ladder. You state your reason, present evidence, and then EXPLAIN how that evidence connects back to your opinion. The explanation is the part many writers forget — but it is what makes your argument convincing."

Model this with a think-aloud:

Say: "My reason is: 'Longer recess improves students' ability to focus in class.' My evidence is: 'A study at Stanford University found that students who had 30 minutes of recess performed 40% better on afternoon reading comprehension tests.' Now I need to EXPLAIN: 'This shows that when students have enough time to move and play, their brains are recharged and ready to learn. Without that break, students lose concentration and miss important instruction.'"

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Display the following model opinion text with all body paragraphs visible:

> **Why Every School Needs a Garden**
>
> Imagine walking outside during science class and picking a ripe tomato that you planted yourself. This is not a dream — it is a reality at schools with gardens. Every elementary school should have a school garden because gardens teach responsibility, connect classroom learning to real life, and improve students' eating habits.
>
> To begin with, school gardens teach students genuine responsibility. When students are in charge of watering, weeding, and caring for living plants, they learn that their actions have real consequences. If they forget to water the seedlings, the plants wilt. According to the National Gardening Association, 83% of teachers who use school gardens report that their students show improved responsibility in other areas of school life as well. This demonstrates that the habits students build in the garden carry over into the classroom.
>
> Furthermore, gardens bring classroom subjects to life in ways that textbooks cannot. In a garden, students measure plant growth for math, observe life cycles for science, and write descriptive paragraphs for language arts. A study published in the Journal of Environmental Education found that students who participated in garden-based learning scored significantly higher on science achievement tests than students who learned only from textbooks. Clearly, hands-on experience deepens understanding far beyond what reading alone can accomplish.
>
> Most importantly, school gardens improve the way students eat. Research from the University of California shows that children who grow their own vegetables are 2.5 times more likely to eat five servings of fruits and vegetables a day. Many students who claim to dislike vegetables change their minds once they taste a cherry tomato or a sugar snap pea that they grew themselves. In a time when childhood obesity is a serious concern, school gardens offer a practical, proven solution.
>
> For all of these reasons, school gardens deserve a place in every elementary school. They do more than grow plants — they grow responsible, curious, healthy students who are better prepared for the future.

Annotate together using three highlighter colors:
- **Color 1:** Topic sentences for each body paragraph (the reason)
- **Color 2:** Evidence (facts, statistics, citations)
- **Color 3:** Explanation sentences (how the evidence supports the reason)

Ask: "Which reason did the writer save for last? Why?" Guide students to notice "Most importantly" signals the strongest reason, and the concluding paragraph echoes all three reasons without copying the introduction word for word.

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students collaboratively develop and order reasons for a shared opinion.

**Activity: Build the Body Together**

Using the class opinion piece planned in Lesson 1 (about phones in school), guide students through the following steps:

1. **Rank the reasons.** Display the three reasons from the Lesson 1 planning web. Ask: "Which reason is strongest? Which should we save for last?" Vote and reorder.

2. **Develop one body paragraph together.** Choose one reason and complete the Reason-Evidence Ladder as a class:
   - Write the reason as a topic sentence on chart paper
   - Add evidence (brainstorm as a class; teacher selects the most specific option)
   - Write the explanation sentence together

3. **Draft the concluding statement together.** Ask: "How can we end without just copying our introduction?" Brainstorm options:
   - End with a call to action ("Schools should adopt this policy immediately.")
   - End with a forward-looking statement ("When students can focus without distractions, they will be more prepared for middle school and beyond.")
   - End with a thought-provoking question ("If phones hurt learning, why would we allow them in classrooms?")

Write one concluding statement together as a class.

---

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently organize and draft body paragraphs and a conclusion for their opinion piece.

Using the Opinion Planning Web from Lesson 1, complete the following:

1. **Reorder** your reasons from least to most important (or most to least — choose deliberately).
2. **Draft two body paragraphs** using the Reason-Evidence Ladder structure (Reason → Evidence → Explanation).
3. **Draft a concluding statement** that reinforces your opinion using one of the three strategies discussed (call to action, forward-looking, thought-provoking question).

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Draft two body paragraphs and a concluding statement as described.
**Stretch:** Draft all three body paragraphs and a concluding statement. Ensure each paragraph uses a different transitional word or phrase to begin (e.g., "First," "In addition," "Most importantly").
**Challenge:** Draft the complete opinion piece (introduction from Lesson 1 + three body paragraphs + conclusion). Add at least one counterargument acknowledgment (e.g., "Some people might argue that... However,...").

### Interleaved Review
1. **Capitalization review (L.4.2a):** Rewrite the following sentence with correct capitalization: "last tuesday, our principal, mrs. garcia, announced a new reading program." (Expected: "Last Tuesday, our principal, Mrs. Garcia, announced a new reading program.")
2. **Opinion review (W.4.1):** What is the difference between a reason and evidence? Write one sentence explaining. (Expected: A reason tells WHY you hold your opinion, while evidence is the specific fact, detail, or example that proves the reason is true.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following reason and evidence. Write the missing explanation sentence.

**Reason:** Recess should be at least 30 minutes long because physical activity reduces stress.
**Evidence:** The American Heart Association reports that children who exercise for 30 minutes a day have lower levels of the stress hormone cortisol.
**Explanation:** _______________

**Expected Response:**
A sentence that connects the evidence back to the opinion. Example: "This means that when students have enough recess time to be truly active, they return to class feeling calmer and more ready to learn, which benefits everyone."

**Mastery = The explanation clearly connects the evidence to the reason and relates back to the overall opinion.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Provide a sentence frame: "This shows that _______, which means _______."
- Revisit the model text and identify the explanation sentences together
- Practice with a simpler reason-evidence pair before returning to their own writing

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 3 (Informative Writing)
- Encourage the student to finish drafting their complete opinion piece for their portfolio

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Counterargument challenge:** Write one paragraph that acknowledges and responds to an opposing viewpoint. Use the frame: "Some people believe _______ . However, _______ ."
- **Evidence hunt:** Find two real facts or statistics (from books, approved websites, or classroom resources) that could strengthen one of your body paragraphs. Add them to your draft.
- **Peer feedback:** Exchange your draft with a partner. Each partner highlights the reasons in one color and the evidence in another. Discuss: Are any reasons missing evidence? Is any evidence vague?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The Reason-Evidence Ladder is the most important takeaway from this lesson. Students should internalize the three-step structure (Reason → Evidence → Explanation) and use it automatically when writing persuasive paragraphs.
- Many students will write reasons without evidence or evidence without explanation. During conferences, ask: "Where is your proof?" (for missing evidence) or "So what? How does this connect to your opinion?" (for missing explanation).
- The concluding statement is often the hardest part for students. Avoid letting them simply copy or rephrase their introduction. The three strategies (call to action, forward-looking, thought-provoking question) give concrete options.
- Save students' drafts in writing folders — they will revisit them in Lesson 7 (Transitions) and Lesson 9 (Revising and Editing Workshop).

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Organization of Reasons | Reasons are listed randomly with no apparent order | Reasons have some order but no strategic arrangement; no signal words used | Reasons are logically ordered with clear signal words (e.g., "First," "Furthermore," "Most importantly") | Reasons are strategically ordered for maximum impact with sophisticated transitions and deliberate pacing |
| Evidence Quality | No evidence is provided for reasons | Evidence is vague or unrelated to the reason (e.g., "Everyone knows that...") | Each reason includes at least one specific, relevant piece of evidence | Each reason includes multiple pieces of specific, well-attributed evidence (e.g., statistics, expert opinions, real-world examples) |
| Explanation / Analysis | No explanation connects evidence to reasons | Some explanation is attempted but does not clearly connect evidence to the opinion | Each body paragraph includes an explanation that clearly links evidence to the reason and opinion | Explanations are insightful, going beyond simple restatement to show deeper understanding of why the evidence matters |
| Concluding Statement | No conclusion or the piece ends abruptly | The conclusion restates the opinion but adds nothing new | The conclusion reinforces the opinion using a strategy (call to action, forward-looking, question) without simply copying the introduction | The conclusion leaves a lasting impression, synthesizes the argument, and inspires the reader to think or act |
