# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 8

## Topic
Writing for Task, Purpose, and Audience

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the task, purpose, and audience for a given writing assignment
- Adjust tone, vocabulary, and level of detail based on who will read the piece and why
- Write the same information in two different ways to suit two different audiences

## Prerequisites
- W.4.4: Producing writing appropriate to task and purpose with guidance and support
- Lessons 1-7: Experience writing opinion, informative, and narrative pieces
- Understanding of the three writing modes and their different purposes (persuade, inform, narrate)

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Task-Purpose-Audience (TPA) planning cards (described below)
- Model paired texts (provided below — same topic, different audiences)
- Highlighters (two colors)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate that we already adjust our communication for different audiences in everyday life.

**Activity: Same Story, Different Listener**

Say: "Imagine you just scored the winning goal in a soccer game. How would you describe the moment to..."

Ask three students to role-play telling the story to:
1. **Your best friend** ("Dude, you won't believe it! I totally nailed a bicycle kick in the last ten seconds!")
2. **Your principal** ("In yesterday's game, I scored the final goal with about ten seconds remaining. It was a really exciting moment for the team.")
3. **A newspaper reporter** ("With less than ten seconds on the clock, I received a cross from the left wing and scored the winning goal with an overhead kick, securing a 3-2 victory for our school.")

Say: "Same event, three totally different versions. Why? Because you automatically adjust how you speak based on WHO you are talking to and WHY. Writers do the same thing. Today we learn how to make those adjustments deliberately in our writing."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the three components of writing context (task, purpose, audience) and how each one shapes writing decisions.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Task:** WHAT you are being asked to write (a letter, a report, an essay, a story, etc.)
- **Purpose:** WHY you are writing (to persuade, to inform/explain, to narrate/entertain, to request, to describe)
- **Audience:** WHO will read your writing (classmates, teacher, parents, a younger student, a community member, a general public)
- **Tone:** The attitude or feeling conveyed through word choice and sentence structure (formal, informal, serious, playful, respectful, urgent)
- **Register:** The level of formality in language (casual/informal vs. academic/formal)

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Every time you write, you should ask yourself three questions before you begin." Write on the board:

**The TPA Check:**
1. **T — Task:** What am I being asked to write? (genre, format, length)
2. **P — Purpose:** Why am I writing this? (What do I want the reader to think, feel, or do?)
3. **A — Audience:** Who will read this? (What do they already know? What tone do they expect?)

Say: "The answers to these three questions shape EVERYTHING — your word choice, your tone, your level of detail, and even your sentence structure."

Create a chart showing how audience changes the writing:

| Decision | Writing for Kindergartners | Writing for Classmates | Writing for the Principal |
|----------|---------------------------|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Vocabulary | Simple, everyday words | Grade-level words, some slang | Formal vocabulary, no slang |
| Sentence length | Short and simple | Mixed lengths | Longer, more complex |
| Tone | Friendly, playful | Conversational but clear | Respectful, professional |
| Detail level | Basic, with pictures | Moderate, with examples | Thorough, with evidence |
| Examples | "like a really big cat" | "about the size of a golden retriever" | "approximately 3 feet in length and weighing up to 50 pounds" |

Say: "Let me show you what this looks like with a real example."

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Display two versions of the same informative content — one written for a younger student and one written for a school board:

> **Version A — Written for a second grader:**
>
> **Why Do We Need Bees?**
>
> Have you ever eaten a strawberry or a slice of watermelon? You can thank a bee for that! Bees fly from flower to flower, and while they are visiting, they carry tiny yellow dust called pollen. The pollen helps flowers grow into the fruits and vegetables we eat. Without bees, we would not have many of our favorite foods. So the next time you see a bee buzzing around a garden, remember — that little bee is helping to make your lunch!

> **Version B — Written for a school board presentation:**
>
> **The Critical Role of Pollinators in Local Ecosystems and School Gardens**
>
> Pollinators, particularly honeybees, are essential to both agricultural production and ecological balance. According to the United States Department of Agriculture, pollinators are responsible for approximately one out of every three bites of food Americans consume. In our school district, the integration of pollinator gardens into campus landscaping would serve a dual purpose: providing habitat for declining bee populations and creating hands-on learning opportunities aligned with Next Generation Science Standards. Research published in the Journal of Environmental Education demonstrates that students who participate in garden-based learning programs show measurable gains in science achievement and environmental awareness. We respectfully request that the board allocate funding for pollinator garden installations at three pilot campuses in the upcoming fiscal year.

Say: "Both pieces are about bees and pollination. But look how different they are." Use two highlighter colors:
- **Color 1:** Highlight vocabulary differences (pollen vs. pollinators; tiny yellow dust vs. agricultural production)
- **Color 2:** Highlight tone differences (friendly and playful vs. formal and data-driven)

Ask: "What is the TASK for each?" (A: short explanation. B: formal proposal.) "What is the PURPOSE?" (A: to teach. B: to persuade/request funding.) "What is the AUDIENCE?" (A: a second grader. B: school board members.)

Say: "The writer knew the TPA before starting, and that knowledge guided every choice."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students collaboratively analyze and adjust writing for different audiences.

**Activity: Audience Switch**

Present the following writing scenario:

"You want to convince someone that your school should start a weekly 'Read for Fun' time where students can read any book they choose for 30 minutes every Friday."

Divide the class into three groups. Each group will plan a short piece (introduction + one reason) for a different audience:

- **Group 1:** A letter to the principal (formal, persuasive, evidence-based)
- **Group 2:** A speech to classmates (conversational, enthusiastic, relatable examples)
- **Group 3:** An article for the school newspaper (informative tone, balanced, includes a quote)

Give groups 5 minutes to plan. Then each group shares their opening paragraph. Discuss as a class:

- How did the vocabulary differ?
- How did the tone change?
- What kind of evidence or examples did each group use?
- Which version would be most effective for its audience? Why?

Sample comparison:

**Group 1 (to principal):** "Dear Principal Okafor, I am writing to propose the implementation of a weekly independent reading period for all fifth-grade students. Research from the National Council of Teachers of English indicates that students who engage in self-selected reading for at least 20 minutes per day demonstrate significant gains in reading comprehension and vocabulary."

**Group 2 (to classmates):** "Imagine this — every Friday afternoon, instead of another worksheet, you get to curl up with whatever book you want and just read. No quiz. No book report. Just reading for fun. Sounds amazing, right?"

**Group 3 (for newspaper):** "A group of fifth-grade students is proposing a new 'Read for Fun' period on Fridays. 'We think students would be more excited about reading if they could choose their own books,' said student leader Amira Chen. Studies show that independent reading time can boost test scores and build a lifelong love of books."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write the same content for two different audiences.

**Task:** Choose ONE of the following topics:

1. Why your school should (or should not) have a dress code
2. The importance of getting enough sleep
3. How to stay safe online

Complete the TPA card for TWO different audiences. Then write ONE paragraph for each audience (two paragraphs total).

**TPA Planning Card:**

| | Audience 1 | Audience 2 |
|--|-----------|-----------|
| Task | | |
| Purpose | | |
| Audience | | |
| Tone | | |
| Vocabulary level | | |

Audience options: a younger student (Grade 1-2), a classmate, a parent, the principal, a newspaper reader, a city council member.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete two TPA cards and write one paragraph per audience as described.
**Stretch:** Write two full paragraphs per audience (introduction + one body paragraph for each). Underline three words in each version that you chose specifically because of the audience.
**Challenge:** Write for three different audiences instead of two. After writing, compose a short reflection (3-4 sentences) explaining how and why you adjusted your writing for each audience.

### Interleaved Review
1. **Transition review (W.5.1c):** Write a transitional clause that could begin a sentence in an opinion piece about school uniforms. (Expected: Something like "Because studies have shown that uniforms reduce morning stress," or "Although some students prefer to express themselves through clothing,")
2. **Informative review (W.5.2d):** What does "domain-specific vocabulary" mean? Give one example related to weather. (Expected: Domain-specific vocabulary means technical words used by experts in a particular field. Example: "precipitation," "barometric pressure," or "meteorologist.")

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

A teacher asks you to "write a letter to the mayor explaining why your neighborhood needs a new park." Identify the Task, the Purpose, and the Audience. Then describe the tone you would use and explain why.

**Expected Response:**
- Task: A letter (formal format)
- Purpose: To persuade/explain (convince the mayor to build a park)
- Audience: The mayor (a public official)
- Tone: Formal, respectful, evidence-based — because the mayor is a public official, the writer should use professional language and support claims with facts and community data rather than casual opinions.

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies all three TPA elements and provides a reasonable description of tone with justification.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the TPA Check anchor chart and walk through one more example together
- Provide a TPA card with the Task and Purpose pre-filled; have the student complete the Audience and Tone sections
- Use the "soccer goal" warm-up as a reference — connect the idea of adjusting speech to adjusting writing

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 9 (Revising and Editing Workshop)
- Encourage the student to review one of their earlier drafts and evaluate whether the tone matches the intended audience

## Extension (Optional)
- **Genre flip:** Take your opinion piece from Lessons 1-2 and rewrite the introduction as a narrative opening instead. Keep the same core message, but use characters, setting, and dialogue to present it as a story. Reflect: How did changing the genre change your approach?
- **Audience analysis:** Find two articles about the same topic written for different audiences (e.g., a news article for adults and a Newsela article for kids). Compare vocabulary, sentence length, tone, and level of detail in a T-chart.
- **Letter writing:** Write a real letter to a real audience — a local business, a community leader, or the school board — about an issue that matters to you. Use everything you have learned about task, purpose, and audience.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is about metacognition — thinking about thinking. Many students write without considering who will read their work. The TPA Check should become a habit: before every writing assignment, students should pause and answer the three questions.
- The paired texts (bees for a second grader vs. bees for a school board) are the anchor for this lesson. Make copies or display them side by side so students can refer to them during independent practice.
- Students sometimes interpret "write for a younger audience" as "dumb it down." Clarify that writing for a younger audience means choosing simpler vocabulary and shorter sentences, but it does NOT mean making the content less accurate or important.
- Tone is the concept students struggle with most. Use concrete comparisons: "A text to your friend has a casual tone. A letter to the president has a formal tone. Your writing is like choosing what to wear — you would not wear pajamas to a job interview, and you would not wear a suit to a pool party."
- This lesson connects powerfully to real-world writing. Encourage students to think about audience whenever they write — emails, notes, social media posts (age-appropriate discussion), letters, etc.

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| TPA Identification | Cannot identify the task, purpose, or audience | Identifies one or two of the three elements but struggles with the third | Correctly identifies all three elements (task, purpose, audience) for a given writing situation | Identifies all three elements and explains how each one will influence specific writing decisions |
| Audience Awareness | Writing does not adjust for the audience; both versions sound the same | Some adjustment is attempted, but the differences are minor (e.g., slightly different vocabulary) | Clear differences between versions demonstrate audience awareness: vocabulary, tone, and detail level are adjusted appropriately | The writing demonstrates sophisticated audience awareness with nuanced adjustments in vocabulary, tone, sentence structure, and evidence type |
| Tone | Tone is inconsistent or inappropriate for the audience (e.g., casual language in a formal letter) | Tone is mostly appropriate but inconsistent within the piece | Tone is consistently appropriate for the audience throughout the piece | Tone is precisely calibrated — formal when needed, conversational when appropriate — and enhances the effectiveness of the writing |
| Vocabulary Adjustment | Vocabulary does not change between audiences | Some vocabulary is adjusted, but the writer relies on the same words and phrases in both versions | Vocabulary is clearly adjusted for each audience, with simpler words for younger readers and more formal or technical terms for professional audiences | Vocabulary is expertly tailored, demonstrating the writer's ability to communicate the same idea with precision at different levels of formality and complexity |
