# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3: Writing

## Facilitator Guide

### Unit Overview

Unit 3 immerses fifth-grade students in the three core modes of writing: opinion, informative/explanatory, and narrative. Building on the foundational writing skills developed in Grade 4, students deepen their ability to craft well-organized, evidence-supported pieces across all three genres. The unit emphasizes the transition from simple transitional words to more sophisticated phrases and clauses, sharpens students' awareness of task, purpose, and audience, and culminates in a Writing Celebration where students curate a portfolio showcasing their strongest work from the unit. Each lesson includes model texts, graphic organizers, and structured practice so that students internalize the qualities of effective writing through both analysis and production.

### Prerequisite Skills (Grade 4)

- W.4.1: Writing opinion pieces with reasons supported by facts and details; using simple transitional words (for instance, because, therefore)
- W.4.2: Writing informative/explanatory texts that introduce a topic and develop it with facts, definitions, and details
- W.4.3: Writing narratives with a clear event sequence, descriptive details, and dialogue
- W.4.4: Producing writing that is appropriate to task and purpose with guidance and support
- L.4.1–L.4.3: Command of Grade 4 grammar, usage, capitalization, punctuation, and spelling conventions
- Students should be comfortable with basic paragraph structure, topic sentences, and supporting details

### Facilitation Tips

- **Model, model, model.** Fifth graders benefit enormously from watching the teacher compose in real time. Think aloud as you draft sentences on chart paper — narrate your choices about word selection, organization, and transitions.
- **Keep model texts visible.** Post annotated model texts on the wall or provide individual copies so students can refer back to them during independent practice.
- **Use graphic organizers as scaffolds, not crutches.** Teach students to use planning webs and outlines during this unit, but gradually encourage students to internalize these structures so they can plan mentally in future writing tasks.
- **Establish a writing workshop tone.** Encourage a culture of revision: first drafts are for getting ideas down, and good writing comes from rewriting. Normalize crossing things out, adding arrows, and moving paragraphs.
- **Conference strategically.** During independent writing time, circulate and hold 1–2 minute conferences with individual students. Focus each conference on one skill (e.g., transitions, evidence, sensory details) rather than trying to address everything.
- **Protect writing time.** Once students begin independent practice, minimize interruptions. Use a quiet signal or timer to mark the transition from drafting to sharing or wrap-up.
- **Celebrate effort and growth.** The final lesson is a Writing Celebration — make it feel special. Invite students to read aloud, display work on bulletin boards, and give specific praise about craft moves they have made.

### Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Reality | How to Address |
|---------------|---------|----------------|
| An opinion and a preference are the same thing | An opinion in academic writing must be supported with reasons and evidence, not just personal preference ("I like pizza" vs. "Schools should offer healthier lunch options because...") | Use the "Can you prove it?" test — if a statement can be supported with facts, it is an arguable opinion |
| Informative writing is just listing facts | Informative writing requires an organizational structure with a clear focus, grouped information, and a concluding statement — not a random collection of facts | Show students the difference between a list of facts and a well-structured informative paragraph; use the model texts in Lessons 3–4 |
| Narrative writing must be about something that really happened | Narratives can develop real OR imagined experiences; fictional narratives are equally valid | Explicitly discuss the standard's language ("real or imagined") and give examples of both memoir and fiction |
| Transitions are only single words like "first," "next," "then" | Grade 5 expectations include transitional phrases ("on the other hand," "as a result of this") and clauses ("although many people believe...", "when the evidence is considered...") | Create a class transition chart organized by type: words, phrases, and clauses; revisit it throughout the unit |
| Revision means fixing spelling and punctuation | Revision focuses on content, organization, and clarity; editing focuses on conventions | Teach revision and editing as two separate steps (Lesson 9); use different colored pencils for each |
| A conclusion just restates the introduction | A strong conclusion synthesizes, reflects, or calls to action — it should leave the reader with something new to think about | Analyze conclusions in model texts; show weak vs. strong examples side by side |

### Assessment Guidance

This unit uses multiple forms of assessment to capture student growth as writers:

**Formative Assessment (ongoing):**
- Exit tickets at the end of each lesson provide a quick snapshot of understanding
- Writing conferences during independent practice allow for individualized feedback
- Graphic organizers and planning documents reveal students' thinking processes

**Summative Assessment (Unit Assessment):**
- The unit assessment includes three writing tasks — one opinion, one informative, and one narrative — plus short-answer questions about writing craft
- Each writing task is evaluated using a 4-level rubric (Beginning / Developing / Proficient / Advanced)
- Proficiency requires a score of 3 (Proficient) or above on at least two of the three writing tasks and 70% or above on the short-answer questions

**Portfolio Assessment (Lesson 10):**
- Students select their strongest pieces, write reflections, and present to peers
- The portfolio is not formally graded but provides valuable information about student self-assessment and metacognition

### Differentiation at a Glance

**Below Grade Level:**
- Provide sentence starters and paragraph frames for each writing type
- Allow students to dictate ideas before writing or use speech-to-text tools
- Reduce the number of required paragraphs (e.g., two body paragraphs instead of three)
- Offer pre-filled graphic organizers with some sections completed as models
- Pair with a peer partner for collaborative planning before independent writing

**On Grade Level:**
- Follow lesson plans as written with the standard graphic organizers and model texts
- Encourage use of the transition chart and peer conferencing protocols
- Expect three-paragraph body sections for opinion and informative writing

**Above Grade Level:**
- Challenge students to incorporate counterarguments in opinion writing
- Encourage the use of primary sources and quotations in informative writing
- Ask narrative writers to experiment with flashback, foreshadowing, or multiple points of view
- Have students write across genres on the same topic (e.g., an opinion piece and an informative article about the same subject) to compare how purpose shapes writing

### Connection to Future Units

The writing skills developed in Unit 3 serve as the backbone for all subsequent English Language Arts work in Grade 5 and beyond. In Unit 4, students will apply these writing skills to research projects, learning to integrate information from multiple sources into coherent informative and opinion pieces. The transitional language practiced here will transfer directly to speaking and presenting, where students must organize ideas logically for a listening audience. In middle school, the three writing modes explored in this unit — argument (evolved from opinion), informative/explanatory, and narrative — remain the core genres of the ELA curriculum, with increasing expectations for evidence quality, structural sophistication, and stylistic control. The revision and self-assessment habits cultivated in Lessons 9 and 10 are particularly important: students who learn to evaluate and improve their own writing become independent, self-directed writers ready for the demands of Grades 6–8.
