# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 7

## Topic
Using Transitional Words, Phrases, and Clauses

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between transitional words, transitional phrases, and transitional clauses
- Select appropriate transitions to link opinions and reasons, connect informative ideas, and manage narrative sequence
- Revise a piece of writing to replace weak or repetitive transitions with varied and purposeful ones

## Prerequisites
- W.4.1c, W.4.2c, W.4.3c: Experience using transitional words (first, next, because, also) in all three writing modes
- Lessons 1-6: Drafts in progress for opinion, informative, and narrative writing
- Basic understanding of clauses (a group of words containing a subject and a verb)

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Students' writing drafts from Lessons 1-6
- Transition Reference Chart (created during the lesson — three columns: Words, Phrases, Clauses)
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Highlighters (three colors)
- Scissors and tape or glue (for the cut-and-sort activity, optional)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Reveal the impact of transitions by showing a paragraph with and without them.

**Activity: Missing Links**

Display the following paragraph with all transitions removed:

"Schools should have recess every day. Recess gives students time to exercise. Students who exercise regularly have stronger hearts and healthier bodies. Recess helps students focus better in class. A study found that students who had recess scored higher on afternoon tests. Some people think recess wastes learning time. Research shows that breaks actually IMPROVE learning."

Say: "Something feels wrong, right? The ideas are fine, but the paragraph reads like a list — each sentence just sits there, disconnected from the ones around it. What is missing?"

Now display the same paragraph with transitions:

"Schools should have recess every day. First and foremost, recess gives students time to exercise. As a result, students who exercise regularly have stronger hearts and healthier bodies. In addition, recess helps students focus better in class. For example, a study found that students who had recess scored higher on afternoon tests. Although some people think recess wastes learning time, research shows that breaks actually IMPROVE learning."

Say: "Transitions are the glue. They show the reader HOW ideas connect. In fourth grade, you used simple transition words like 'first,' 'next,' and 'because.' In fifth grade, we are going to upgrade to phrases and clauses — more powerful connectors that make your writing sound sophisticated and clear."

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the three levels of transitions (words, phrases, clauses) and their uses across all three writing modes.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Transitional word:** A single word that connects ideas (e.g., first, also, however, finally)
- **Transitional phrase:** A group of words (without a subject-verb pair) that connects ideas (e.g., "on the other hand," "for example," "as a result")
- **Transitional clause:** A group of words with a subject and verb that connects ideas (e.g., "although many people disagree," "when the experiment was complete," "because the evidence clearly shows")
- **Coherence:** The quality of writing that flows smoothly, with ideas logically connected

### Instruction Notes

Say: "Transitions come in three sizes — think of them as Small, Medium, and Large." Write on chart paper:

**Transition Reference Chart:**

| Type | What It Is | Opinion Examples | Informative Examples | Narrative Examples |
|------|-----------|-----------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| **Word** (Small) | A single word | also, because, therefore, however, finally | additionally, furthermore, similarly, specifically | then, suddenly, meanwhile, later, finally |
| **Phrase** (Medium) | A group of words (no subject + verb) | for this reason, on the other hand, in addition to this, most importantly | for example, as a result, in contrast, in other words | at that moment, the next morning, without warning, on the other side |
| **Clause** (Large) | A group of words with a subject + verb | although some people believe..., because the evidence shows..., since studies have proven... | when scientists examined..., as the data reveals..., while this may seem surprising... | before she could react..., as the door creaked open..., even though he had practiced a hundred times... |

Say: "Notice that the clause transitions START a sentence and need to be followed by a comma. They are the most powerful because they add new information WHILE connecting ideas."

Model with a think-aloud:

Say: "I have an opinion paragraph about homework. Watch me upgrade the transitions from words to phrases to clauses."

Write three versions on chart paper:

**Version 1 — Word transitions (Grade 4 level):**
"Also, homework helps students practice skills. However, too much homework is bad."

**Version 2 — Phrase transitions (Grade 5 upgrade):**
"In addition to classroom instruction, homework gives students valuable practice time. On the other hand, excessive homework can lead to stress and burnout."

**Version 3 — Clause transitions (Grade 5 advanced):**
"Because students need time to practice new skills independently, homework serves as a bridge between school and home learning. Although some homework is beneficial, research suggests that more than 60 minutes per night can cause unnecessary stress for elementary students."

Say: "See how each version becomes more informative and more polished? The clause transitions actually ADD information — they do not just connect; they explain."

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Display the following annotated passages showing transitions at work in all three modes:

> **Opinion (from Lesson 2 model — annotated):**
>
> **To begin with** [phrase], school gardens teach students genuine responsibility. **When students are in charge of** [clause] watering, weeding, and caring for living plants, they learn that their actions have real consequences. **Furthermore** [word], gardens bring classroom subjects to life. **Because students can measure, observe, and write about** [clause] what they grow, the garden becomes a living laboratory. **Most importantly** [phrase], school gardens improve the way students eat.

> **Informative (from Lesson 3 model — annotated):**
>
> Octopuses have a body structure unlike any other animal. **In addition to** [phrase] their unusual bodies, octopuses display remarkable intelligence. **When scientists observed octopuses** [clause] in controlled environments, they documented problem-solving abilities once thought to be unique to mammals. **Finally** [word], octopuses have survival strategies that seem almost magical.

> **Narrative (from Lesson 6 model — annotated):**
>
> **Before Aiden could respond** [clause], Mr. Hernandez leaned the crowbar against the wall. **At that moment** [phrase], the fluorescent lights above them flickered. **Then** [word] Aiden peeled the tape away carefully. **As he unfolded the paper** [clause], he realized it was not a letter at all — it was a map.

Say: "In your own writing, aim for a MIX of all three types. If every transition is a single word, your writing sounds choppy. If every transition is a clause, your writing sounds heavy. The best writers blend all three."

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students collaboratively revise a weak paragraph by upgrading its transitions.

**Activity: Transition Makeover**

Display the following paragraph with weak, repetitive transitions:

"First, students should wear uniforms because it makes getting ready easier. Next, uniforms stop bullying about clothes. Then, uniforms are cheaper than regular clothes. Also, uniforms make schools look more professional. Last, uniforms help students focus on learning."

Say: "This paragraph has five transition words, and they are all the same kind — simple sequence words. Let's give it a makeover."

Work through the paragraph together, replacing transitions one at a time:

1. "First" → Keep as is (it works fine as an opener)
2. "Next" → Upgrade to a phrase: "In addition to saving time in the morning,"
3. "Then" → Upgrade to a clause: "Because families do not have to buy expensive brand-name clothing,"
4. "Also" → Upgrade to a phrase: "Beyond the practical benefits,"
5. "Last" → Upgrade to a clause: "When students are not distracted by what their classmates are wearing,"

Read the revised paragraph aloud:

"First, students should wear uniforms because they make getting ready easier. In addition to saving time in the morning, uniforms reduce bullying related to clothing choices. Because families do not have to buy expensive brand-name clothing, uniforms are more affordable for everyone. Beyond the practical benefits, uniforms give schools a more professional appearance. When students are not distracted by what their classmates are wearing, they can focus more fully on learning."

Ask: "How does the paragraph sound now? What changed?" Guide students to notice the paragraph flows better, each sentence connects to the next, and the transitions actually add information.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students revise their own writing to improve transitions.

Choose ONE draft from your writing folder (opinion piece from Lessons 1-2, informative piece from Lessons 3-4, or narrative from Lessons 5-6).

**Task:**
1. **Highlight** every transition in your draft using three colors: one for words, one for phrases, one for clauses.
2. **Count** how many of each type you have. Record the numbers.
3. **Revise** at least three transitions — upgrade at least two words to phrases or clauses. Make sure the new transitions fit the meaning and flow of the paragraph.
4. **Read aloud** your revised paragraph to check that it sounds natural.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Highlight and revise three transitions as described. Aim for at least one phrase and one clause in the revised version.
**Stretch:** Highlight and revise five transitions. Ensure the piece uses all three types (words, phrases, clauses) and that no transition type is used more than three times in a row.
**Challenge:** Revise transitions throughout the entire piece. Then write a short reflection (3-4 sentences) explaining which transitions you changed and why the new versions are more effective.

### Interleaved Review
1. **Grammar review (L.4.1f):** Identify the clause in the following sentence: "Although the weather was cold, the students played outside." (Expected: "Although the weather was cold" is the dependent clause; "the students played outside" is the independent clause.)
2. **Opinion writing review (W.5.1b):** What is the difference between a reason and an explanation in an opinion paragraph? (Expected: A reason tells WHY you believe your opinion; an explanation tells how your evidence supports that reason.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following sentence with a weak transition. Rewrite it twice: once using a transitional phrase and once using a transitional clause.

Original: "Also, reading improves vocabulary."

**Expected Response:**
- Phrase version: "In addition to building comprehension skills, reading improves vocabulary."
- Clause version: "Because readers encounter new words in context every time they open a book, reading is one of the most effective ways to improve vocabulary."

**Mastery = Student produces at least one correct phrase transition and one correct clause transition that fit the meaning of the sentence.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the Transition Reference Chart and practice identifying words vs. phrases vs. clauses
- Provide sentence frames: "In addition to _______, [rest of sentence]." / "Because _______, [rest of sentence]."
- Have the student practice with two more simple sentences before returning to their own writing

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 8 (Writing for Task, Purpose, and Audience)
- Encourage the student to continue revising transitions in all their drafts

## Extension (Optional)
- **Transition scavenger hunt:** Find a published article (from a textbook, magazine, or approved website) and highlight every transition. Categorize them as words, phrases, or clauses. Calculate the percentage of each type.
- **Transition-free challenge:** Write a short paragraph (4-5 sentences) about your favorite hobby WITHOUT using any transitions. Then rewrite it WITH transitions. Read both versions aloud and discuss the difference.
- **Create a class Transition Wall:** On a bulletin board, post three columns (Words, Phrases, Clauses). Throughout the unit, students add new transitions they discover in their reading on sticky notes. This becomes a growing reference for the class.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is a turning point in the unit. The shift from transition words to phrases and clauses is one of the key upgrades from Grade 4 to Grade 5 writing. Spend extra time on the clause transitions, as they are the most challenging for students.
- Students often overuse transitions after this lesson, inserting them into every sentence. Remind them that transitions should appear at KEY connection points — between major ideas, between paragraphs, and at shifts in the argument or narrative. Not every sentence needs a transition.
- The phrase "In conclusion" is the most overused transition in student writing. Challenge students to find alternatives: "For all of these reasons," "When all the evidence is considered," "Ultimately," etc.
- The Transition Reference Chart created during this lesson should be posted in the classroom and remain available through the end of the unit. Encourage students to add new transitions as they encounter them in their reading.
- Some students may not yet have a clear understanding of the difference between a phrase and a clause. A quick review: a clause has a subject AND a verb ("because the scientist discovered..."); a phrase does not ("in addition to this..."). Keep this distinction simple and functional.

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Transition Variety | Only one type of transition is used (all words, or none at all) | Two types are used but one dominates heavily (e.g., five words and one phrase) | All three types (words, phrases, clauses) are used, with a reasonable balance | Transitions are varied, purposeful, and seamlessly integrated; the writer demonstrates deliberate control over transition type |
| Transition Accuracy | Transitions are used incorrectly or do not fit the meaning (e.g., "however" used where "in addition" is needed) | Some transitions fit the meaning, but others create confusion or logical errors | Transitions are used accurately and fit the logical relationship between ideas | Every transition precisely conveys the intended relationship (cause/effect, contrast, addition, sequence) and enhances coherence |
| Revision Quality | No revisions are made to transitions in the draft | One or two transitions are revised, but the changes are minor or do not improve the writing | At least three transitions are revised, with clear upgrades from words to phrases or clauses | Multiple transitions are revised thoughtfully; the student can explain WHY each revision improves the piece |
| Overall Coherence | Writing feels disconnected; ideas do not flow from one to the next | Some connections between ideas are present, but the writing is still choppy in places | Ideas flow logically from one to the next; the reader can follow the writer's thinking without confusion | The writing reads fluidly and naturally; transitions are invisible — they serve the ideas without drawing attention to themselves |
