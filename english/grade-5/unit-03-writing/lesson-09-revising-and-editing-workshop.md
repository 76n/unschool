# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 9

## Topic
Revising and Editing Workshop

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between revision (improving content, organization, and clarity) and editing (correcting grammar, spelling, and punctuation)
- Apply a revision checklist to strengthen a piece of writing for content, transitions, and word choice
- Apply an editing checklist to correct errors in capitalization, punctuation, spelling, and grammar

## Prerequisites
- Lessons 1-8: At least one complete or near-complete draft in any mode (opinion, informative, or narrative)
- L.4.1-L.4.3: Command of Grade 4 grammar, usage, capitalization, punctuation, and spelling conventions
- W.4.5: Experience with the writing process (planning, drafting, revising, editing)

## Materials Needed
- Students' writing drafts from Lessons 1-8 (at least one complete draft)
- Revision Checklist (provided below)
- Editing Checklist (provided below)
- Colored pencils: blue for revision, red for editing
- Sticky notes for peer feedback
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Peer Conference Protocol (described below)
- Timer or clock

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Establish the difference between revision and editing through a memorable analogy.

**Activity: Building a House**

Say: "Imagine you are building a house. Revision is like deciding to move a wall, add a bigger window, or redesign the kitchen — you are changing the STRUCTURE and the IDEAS. Editing is like painting the walls, fixing a leaky faucet, and making sure all the light switches work — you are polishing the SURFACE."

Display the following chart:

| Revision (Blue Pencil) | Editing (Red Pencil) |
|------------------------|---------------------|
| Does my introduction grab the reader? | Are all words spelled correctly? |
| Are my ideas in the best order? | Did I use correct punctuation? |
| Do I have enough evidence/details? | Are my sentences complete (no fragments or run-ons)? |
| Are my transitions varied and effective? | Did I capitalize proper nouns and sentence beginnings? |
| Does my conclusion leave the reader thinking? | Did I use correct grammar (subject-verb agreement, pronouns)? |
| Is my word choice precise and vivid? | Are quotation marks used correctly in dialogue? |

Say: "Today we do revision FIRST, then editing SECOND. Why? Because there is no point in fixing the punctuation in a sentence you might delete during revision. Always fix the big stuff before you fix the small stuff."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model the revision and editing process using a sample draft, and introduce the Peer Conference Protocol.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Revision:** Improving the content, organization, clarity, and word choice of a piece of writing (re-vision = "seeing again")
- **Editing:** Correcting errors in grammar, spelling, punctuation, and capitalization
- **Peer conference:** A structured conversation between two writers who give and receive specific feedback on their work
- **Constructive feedback:** Suggestions that are specific, kind, and helpful — focused on how to make the writing stronger

### Instruction Notes

**Part 1: Model Revision**

Display the following draft paragraph on chart paper:

> "I think dogs are the best pets. Dogs are really fun. They can do stuff with you. Also dogs are loyal. They will always be there. Dogs are good pets and everyone should get one."

Say: "Let's revise this paragraph using our blue pencils. I am going to think aloud as I work through the revision checklist."

Walk through each revision question:

1. **Does the introduction grab the reader?** "No — 'I think dogs are the best pets' is flat. Let me rewrite: 'Of all the animals that share our homes, dogs have earned the title of "man's best friend" for good reason.'"

2. **Are the ideas in the best order?** "The loyalty point is strongest — I should save it for last."

3. **Do I have enough evidence/details?** "'They can do stuff with you' is vague. Let me add specifics: 'Dogs can accompany their owners on hikes, play fetch in the backyard, and even be trained to perform tasks like retrieving the morning newspaper.'"

4. **Are the transitions varied?** "'Also' is weak. Let me upgrade: 'Beyond their playful nature, dogs demonstrate a loyalty that few other animals can match.'"

5. **Is the word choice precise?** "'Really fun' and 'good pets' are vague. Let me replace with 'endlessly entertaining' and 'devoted companions.'"

Display the revised version side by side with the original.

**Part 2: Model Editing**

Now switch to the red pencil and correct surface-level errors. Add a deliberate error to the revised version:

"Dogs can accompany their owners on hikes play fetch in the backyard and even be trained to perform tasks."

Say: "This sentence needs commas in a series: 'Dogs can accompany their owners on hikes, play fetch in the backyard, and even be trained to perform tasks.'"

**Part 3: Introduce the Peer Conference Protocol**

Write on the anchor chart:

**Peer Conference Protocol:**
1. **Read** your partner's writing silently.
2. **Compliment** — Name one specific thing the writer did well. ("I really liked how you used the transitional clause 'Because the evidence clearly shows...' to connect your second and third reasons.")
3. **Question** — Ask one question about something that confused you or that you want to know more about. ("In your second paragraph, I was not sure what evidence supports your reason. Can you add a specific fact?")
4. **Suggest** — Offer one specific suggestion for improvement. ("I think your conclusion could be stronger if you ended with a call to action instead of just restating your opinion.")
5. **Thank** your partner for sharing their work.

Say: "Notice that every step is SPECIFIC. 'Good job' is not helpful. 'I liked how you used the metaphor of a stone to describe Marcus's nervousness' IS helpful because the writer knows exactly what worked."

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Display a before-and-after revision of a narrative paragraph:

> **Before revision:**
>
> "Maya walked into the science fair. She was nervous. Her project was on a table. Other kids had projects too. She set up her poster. A judge came over. He asked her a question. She answered it. He said good job."

> **After revision:**
>
> "Maya's hands trembled as she pushed through the double doors of the gymnasium and scanned the rows of tables draped in white cloth. Projects lined both sides of the room — a papier-mache volcano, a robotic arm made from cardboard, a terrarium glowing with tiny LED lights. She found Table 14, carefully unrolled her poster about the effects of music on plant growth, and secured the corners with binder clips. Before she could catch her breath, a judge in a gray blazer appeared at her elbow, clipboard in hand. 'Tell me about your hypothesis,' he said. Maya swallowed hard. 'I hypothesized that plants exposed to classical music would grow taller than plants in silence,' she began, her voice steadier than she expected. The judge nodded slowly and scribbled something on his clipboard. 'Interesting approach,' he murmured. 'Very interesting indeed.'"

Ask: "What changed? What stayed the same?" Guide students to notice:
- The core events are the same, but every moment is EXPANDED with sensory details, internal thoughts, and dialogue
- Vague language ("other kids had projects too") is replaced with concrete details ("a papier-mache volcano, a robotic arm made from cardboard")
- The dialogue shows the interaction rather than summarizing it

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students practice the Peer Conference Protocol and begin revising their drafts.

**Activity: Peer Conference Round**

1. **Pair up.** Each student selects their strongest draft from the unit (opinion, informative, or narrative).

2. **Round 1 (5 minutes):** Partner A reads silently while Partner B's draft is reviewed. Partner A follows the Peer Conference Protocol: Compliment, Question, Suggest. Partner A writes feedback on a sticky note and places it on the draft.

3. **Round 2 (5 minutes):** Switch roles. Partner B reads and gives feedback on Partner A's draft.

4. **Debrief (2 minutes):** Each student reads the feedback they received and circles one revision they will make based on the suggestion.

Teacher circulates during conferences, modeling constructive feedback language and redirecting vague comments ("Make it better") to specific ones ("Add a sensory detail to the opening sentence to help the reader picture the setting").

---

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently revise and edit their draft using the checklists and peer feedback.

Using the draft that received peer feedback, complete both rounds of revision and editing:

**Round 1 — Revision (Blue Pencil, 7 minutes):**

Work through the Revision Checklist:
- [ ] My introduction grabs the reader's attention
- [ ] My ideas are in a logical, effective order
- [ ] I have enough evidence, details, or description to fully develop my ideas
- [ ] My transitions are varied (I use words, phrases, AND clauses)
- [ ] My word choice is precise and vivid (no vague words like "stuff," "things," "a lot")
- [ ] My conclusion leaves the reader with something meaningful
- [ ] I addressed at least one piece of my partner's feedback

**Round 2 — Editing (Red Pencil, 5 minutes):**

Work through the Editing Checklist:
- [ ] All sentences begin with a capital letter
- [ ] Proper nouns are capitalized
- [ ] All sentences end with correct punctuation (. ? !)
- [ ] Commas are used correctly in series and after introductory elements
- [ ] Dialogue is punctuated correctly (quotation marks, commas, new paragraphs)
- [ ] All words are spelled correctly (check uncertain words)
- [ ] Subjects and verbs agree
- [ ] No sentence fragments or run-ons

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete both checklists on one draft. Make at least three revision changes and three editing corrections.
**Stretch:** Complete both checklists and rewrite the introduction and conclusion to make them stronger. Write a brief note to your partner explaining what you changed based on their feedback.
**Challenge:** Complete both checklists on your draft AND a second draft from the unit. For each revision change, write a marginal note explaining WHY you made the change (e.g., "Changed 'also' to 'because researchers discovered...' to upgrade from a word to a clause transition").

### Interleaved Review
1. **Spelling review (L.4.2d):** Find and correct any spelling errors in the following sentence: "The goverment desided to protext the enviroment by planting more trees." (Expected: "The government decided to protect the environment by planting more trees.")
2. **Audience review (W.5.4):** You are writing a letter to a kindergarten class about fire safety. Name two adjustments you would make to your writing. (Expected: Use simple, short sentences and everyday vocabulary; include a friendly, reassuring tone rather than scary facts.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following sentence. Identify ONE revision you would make (content/clarity) and ONE editing correction (grammar/punctuation/spelling).

"their are lots of reasons that the oceen is important, and if we dont take care of it bad things could happen."

**Expected Response:**
- Revision: Replace vague language — "lots of reasons" and "bad things could happen" are vague. Revised: "The ocean is vital to life on Earth because it produces over half of the world's oxygen, regulates global temperatures, and supports millions of species — and without immediate conservation efforts, these critical systems are at risk."
- Editing: "their" should be "there"; "oceen" should be "ocean"; "dont" should be "don't."

**Mastery = Student identifies at least one meaningful content revision AND at least one surface-level editing correction.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the revision vs. editing chart from the warm-up
- Provide the sentence with editing errors already underlined — have the student focus just on content revision first, then switch to editing
- Conference one-on-one and ask guiding questions: "What is vague here? How could you make it more specific?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 10 (Writing Celebration and Portfolio)
- Encourage the student to produce a clean final draft of their revised piece for the portfolio

## Extension (Optional)
- **Revision autobiography:** Write a short reflection (5-7 sentences) describing how your writing has changed from your first draft to your revised draft. What did you add? What did you remove? What did you change?
- **Editing challenge:** Exchange your draft with a new partner (not your original conference partner). Each partner hunts for editing errors using the Editing Checklist. See who can find the most.
- **Before-and-after showcase:** Select one paragraph from your draft. Write it out twice on a sheet of paper — the original on the left and the revised version on the right. Annotate the changes you made and explain why.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The single most important habit from this lesson is the two-step process: REVISION FIRST, EDITING SECOND. Students naturally gravitate toward editing because it feels more concrete (right/wrong) than revision (better/different). Explicitly enforce the order: blue pencils out for revision, then put them away; red pencils out for editing.
- Peer conferences can go poorly if students give vague or unhelpful feedback. The Peer Conference Protocol is designed to prevent this. Model it thoroughly before releasing students to work in pairs. Consider assigning specific partnerships rather than letting students choose — strong writers can model feedback for developing writers.
- Students who are reluctant revisers often say, "But I like it the way it is." Counter with: "Professional authors revise their books DOZENS of times. Revision is not about fixing something broken — it is about making something good even better."
- Some students will over-edit and under-revise (fixing commas but leaving vague content untouched). During conferences, redirect: "Your commas look great. Now let's talk about this paragraph — is this the strongest evidence you could use?"
- Ensure students save BOTH their original drafts and their revised drafts. In Lesson 10, they will reflect on their growth, and having both versions makes that reflection concrete and meaningful.

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Revision — Content | No content revisions are made; the draft is unchanged | One or two minor content changes are made, but major issues (vague language, missing evidence, weak conclusion) remain | At least three meaningful content revisions are made, improving clarity, evidence, or word choice | The piece is substantially improved through revision; content is developed, organized, and polished to a near-final quality |
| Revision — Transitions | Transitions are not revised; original weak or repetitive transitions remain | One or two transitions are upgraded, but most remain at the word level | Transitions are revised to include a mix of words, phrases, and clauses; the piece flows more smoothly | Transitions are seamlessly integrated and precisely chosen; the writing reads with natural coherence throughout |
| Editing — Conventions | Multiple errors in spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and grammar remain uncorrected | Some errors are corrected, but several remain; the student shows awareness of conventions but incomplete follow-through | Most errors are corrected; the piece demonstrates solid command of Grade 5 conventions with only minor remaining issues | The piece is virtually error-free; spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and grammar are polished and precise |
| Peer Conference | Student does not participate in the peer conference or gives only vague feedback ("Good job") | Student participates but feedback is general; one element of the protocol is followed (compliment, question, or suggestion) | Student follows the full protocol with specific, helpful feedback — a detailed compliment, a genuine question, and a constructive suggestion | Student provides exceptionally thoughtful feedback that demonstrates deep understanding of writing craft; feedback directly helps the partner improve |
