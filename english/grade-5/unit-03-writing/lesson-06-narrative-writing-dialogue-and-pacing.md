# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 6

## Topic
Narrative Writing: Dialogue and Pacing

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write dialogue that reveals character traits, advances the plot, or shows relationships between characters
- Use correct dialogue punctuation (quotation marks, commas, end marks, new paragraphs for new speakers)
- Adjust pacing by expanding important moments with description and compressing less important ones with summary

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 5: Orienting the reader with characters, setting, and situation
- W.4.3b: Experience using dialogue and description in narratives
- L.4.2b: Correct use of quotation marks in dialogue

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Students' narrative drafts from Lesson 5
- Model narrative text with dialogue (provided below)
- Highlighters (two colors)
- Dialogue punctuation reference card (described below)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Demonstrate the power of dialogue by contrasting a passage with and without it.

**Activity: Tell vs. Show with Dialogue**

Display two versions of the same moment:

**Version A (without dialogue):**
"Mia told her friend that she was nervous about the science fair. Her friend said not to worry and that her project was really good."

**Version B (with dialogue):**
"'I can't do this,' Mia whispered, clutching her poster board so tightly it bent in the middle. 'What if the judges ask me something I don't know?'
Jada put a hand on her shoulder. 'Mia, you built a working solar oven out of a pizza box. You are going to be amazing.'"

Say: "Which version makes you FEEL something? Version B, right? Dialogue does not just tell us what characters say — it SHOWS us how they feel, how they relate to each other, and what kind of people they are. That is what we are learning today."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the craft of meaningful dialogue and the technique of pacing through expansion and compression.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Dialogue:** The exact words characters say to each other, shown in quotation marks
- **Dialogue tag:** The phrase that identifies who is speaking (e.g., "said Marcus," "she whispered")
- **Pacing:** The speed at which a story moves — writers can slow down important moments and speed up less important ones
- **Expansion:** Stretching an important moment by adding sensory details, internal thoughts, and dialogue
- **Compression:** Summarizing less important events in a sentence or two to keep the story moving

### Instruction Notes

**Part 1: Writing Meaningful Dialogue**

Say: "Dialogue in a story should do at least one of three jobs." Write on the board:

**Dialogue should:**
1. **Reveal character** — Show WHO the person is through HOW they talk
2. **Advance the plot** — Move the story forward by sharing new information or making decisions
3. **Show relationships** — Demonstrate how characters feel about each other

Say: "Watch how the same information sounds different depending on the character."

Write on chart paper:
- Confident character: "'Easy,' Jordan said, tossing the ball in the air. 'I've done this a hundred times.'"
- Nervous character: "'Are you sure this is safe?' whispered Priya, gripping the railing with both hands."
- Bossy character: "'Listen, here's what we're going to do,' Carlos announced, pointing at the map. 'Nobody moves until I say so.'"

Say: "Notice the dialogue tags: 'said,' 'whispered,' 'announced.' These help the reader hear the character's voice. Also notice the actions mixed in — tossing the ball, gripping the railing, pointing at the map. These are called action beats, and they bring dialogue to life."

**Part 2: Dialogue Punctuation Rules**

Write the punctuation rules on an anchor chart:

```
DIALOGUE PUNCTUATION RULES
1. Put quotation marks around the exact words a character says.
   "I found it!" shouted Eli.
2. Put a comma before the closing quotation mark if a dialogue tag follows.
   "I think we should go back," said Noor.
3. Start a new paragraph each time a new character speaks.
4. Put end punctuation INSIDE the quotation marks.
   "Where did you find that?" asked Mia.
5. If the dialogue tag comes first, put a comma after the tag.
   Rosa said, "Let me see."
```

**Part 3: Pacing — Expansion and Compression**

Say: "Good writers do not spend equal time on every event. They EXPAND the most important or exciting moments and COMPRESS the less important ones."

Write on chart paper:

**Compression (less important event):**
"The bus ride took twenty minutes. When they arrived at the museum, the class filed through the entrance and checked in at the front desk."

**Expansion (the important moment):**
"Dara stopped in front of the dinosaur skeleton and tilted her head back — way, way back. The Tyrannosaurus rex towered above her, its jaws frozen open, each tooth as long as her hand. Her heart hammered. She had read about this skeleton in every book she owned, memorized every measurement, drawn it a dozen times in her sketchbook. But standing here, feeling the cool museum air on her arms and hearing the echo of footsteps in the cavernous hall, she realized that no book could capture what it felt like to be this small in the shadow of something this ancient."

Say: "The bus ride got two sentences. The dinosaur moment got a whole paragraph. Why? Because the dinosaur moment MATTERS to the character. That is pacing."

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Display the following model narrative passage with dialogue and pacing:

> The storm had been building all morning, and by lunch the sky was the color of a bruise. Ms. Rivera shortened their outdoor time and herded everyone back inside. (compression)
>
> It was in the hallway, just after the lights flickered for the second time, that Elena heard it — a low, steady dripping sound coming from the supply closet.
>
> "Do you hear that?" Elena grabbed Tomás's sleeve.
>
> He pulled out one earbud. "Hear what?"
>
> "That dripping. Listen."
>
> They stood perfectly still. Drip. Drip. Drip. The sound was slow and rhythmic, like a leaky faucet, but heavier somehow — like something thick was hitting the floor.
>
> "It's probably just a pipe," Tomás said, but his voice was higher than usual, and he did not move toward the closet.
>
> Elena reached for the handle. The metal was ice-cold under her fingers. She turned it slowly, feeling the latch click, and pulled the door open. (expansion)
>
> A wave of cold, damp air washed over them. The closet was dark. Elena fumbled for the light switch on the wall and flicked it on.
>
> "Oh," she breathed.
>
> Water was trickling down the back wall from a crack in the ceiling, pooling around a cardboard box that had gone soft and dark with moisture. Inside the box, soaked but still legible, was a stack of letters tied with a faded red ribbon.
>
> "What are those?" Tomás asked, stepping closer despite himself.
>
> Elena carefully lifted the bundle. The paper was old — really old — and the handwriting was small and slanted. She read the first line aloud: "'Dear whoever finds this — I hid these letters in 1987 because I was afraid to send them.'"

Ask students to identify:
- Which part is compressed? (The storm building and Ms. Rivera herding students inside)
- Which part is expanded? (The discovery in the closet — slow, detailed, full of sensory details)
- What does the dialogue reveal about the characters? (Elena is curious and brave; Tomás is cautious but curious too)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students collaboratively write a dialogue scene with attention to pacing.

**Activity: Write a Dialogue Scene Together**

Say: "Let's continue the class narrative from Lesson 5 (Aiden and the locker). We left off when the custodian pried open the locker and they found a sealed envelope with a map inside. Let's write the dialogue scene where Aiden and the custodian react to the discovery."

Guide students through the process:

1. **Who is speaking?** Aiden and Mr. Hernandez (the custodian).
2. **What do we want the dialogue to do?** Show Aiden's curiosity, show Mr. Hernandez's surprise, and advance the plot (they decide to open the envelope).
3. **What moment should we EXPAND?** The moment Aiden opens the envelope and sees the map.

Write collaboratively on chart paper:

Sample:
"The envelope was yellowed at the edges and sealed with a strip of tape so old it had turned brown. Aiden turned it over in his hands.

'Well,' said Mr. Hernandez, leaning the crowbar against the wall, 'I've opened a lot of lockers over the years, but I've never found anything like that.'

'Can I open it?' Aiden asked. His fingers were already working at the tape.

Mr. Hernandez crossed his arms and tilted his head. 'Technically, whatever is in that locker belongs to whoever left it. But seeing as they haven't come back in a year...' He shrugged. 'Go ahead.'

Aiden peeled the tape away carefully, trying not to rip the paper. Inside was a single sheet of paper, folded into quarters. He unfolded it and held it up to the fluorescent hallway light. It was a map — hand-drawn in colored pencil — of the school grounds. A red X marked a spot near the big oak tree on the edge of the playground.

'What is it?' Mr. Hernandez stepped closer.

'I think,' Aiden said slowly, 'someone buried something under the playground.'"

Review: "Did our dialogue reveal character? Did it advance the plot? Did we expand the important moment?"

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently write a dialogue scene for their narrative from Lesson 5.

Return to the narrative you began in Lesson 5. Choose a key moment in your story where two or more characters interact. Write a scene (at least 8-10 sentences) that includes:

1. **At least four lines of dialogue** between two characters (with correct punctuation)
2. **Dialogue tags and action beats** that show how characters feel
3. **Expansion** of the most important moment (use sensory details and internal thoughts to slow it down)
4. **Compression** of at least one less important event (summarize it in 1-2 sentences)

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Write a dialogue scene with four exchanges and one expanded moment, as described above.
**Stretch:** Write a dialogue scene with six or more exchanges. Vary the dialogue tags (avoid using "said" more than twice). Include at least one moment of internal thought from the narrator.
**Challenge:** Write a full scene (12+ sentences) with dialogue, expansion, and compression. Have one character's dialogue style differ noticeably from another's (e.g., one speaks in short, clipped sentences while the other rambles). Include a shift in pacing from slow to fast (or fast to slow).

### Interleaved Review
1. **Quotation mark review (L.4.2b):** Correct the punctuation in the following dialogue: "Where are we going asked Mia. I think we should follow the path said Tomás." (Expected: "'Where are we going?' asked Mia. 'I think we should follow the path,' said Tomás.")
2. **Narrative review (W.5.3a):** Name the three things a narrative opening should establish. (Expected: The character/narrator, the setting, and the situation/problem.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Write two lines of dialogue between a student and a teacher. The dialogue should reveal that the student is excited about something. Use correct punctuation and include at least one action beat.

**Expected Response:**
Two correctly punctuated lines of dialogue with a tag or action beat, where the student's excitement is shown through word choice and actions. Example:
"'Ms. Chen, look!' Jayden burst through the classroom door, holding a trophy above his head. 'We won first place!'
Ms. Chen looked up from her desk and smiled. 'I knew you would. Tell me everything.'"

**Mastery = The dialogue is correctly punctuated, includes at least one action beat, and the character's emotion is shown (not just told).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the five dialogue punctuation rules on the anchor chart
- Practice correcting pre-written dialogue with punctuation errors
- Provide sentence frames: "'_______!' [Character] [action verb + description]. '_______,' [other character] [response]."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 7 (Transitional Words, Phrases, and Clauses)
- Encourage the student to continue developing their narrative with additional dialogue scenes

## Extension (Optional)
- **Dialogue-only story:** Write a very short story (10-12 lines) told ENTIRELY through dialogue — no narration, no description. The reader should be able to figure out what is happening just from what the characters say. Share with a partner and see if they can identify the characters, setting, and situation.
- **Pacing experiment:** Take a paragraph from your narrative and rewrite it twice — once expanded (stretching the moment with sensory details and internal thought) and once compressed (summarizing it in 1-2 sentences). Discuss which version works better for the story and why.
- **Character voice journal:** Write a short journal entry from the perspective of a character who is very different from you. Use vocabulary, sentence length, and tone that reflect that character's personality.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students commonly make two dialogue errors: (1) forgetting to start a new paragraph for each speaker, and (2) placing end punctuation outside quotation marks. The punctuation reference card should remain visible throughout the lesson and future writing sessions.
- "Said" is not a bad word. While students should learn to vary their dialogue tags, overuse of exotic tags ("exclaimed," "bellowed," "retorted") can distract from the story. Teach students that "said" is invisible to readers — it lets the dialogue itself do the work. Action beats are often more effective than fancy tags.
- Pacing is a sophisticated concept. Many fifth graders will naturally want to give every event equal weight. During conferences, ask: "What is the MOST important moment in your story?" Then ask: "How much space did you give it?" If the answer is one sentence, coach the student to expand it.
- Some students may resist dialogue because they find the punctuation intimidating. Pair them with a partner to write a dialogue scene collaboratively — hearing the conversation aloud helps them capture it on paper.

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Dialogue Quality | No dialogue is included, or dialogue is used only to state information flatly ("I am going to the store," said Tom) | Dialogue is present but does not reveal character or advance the plot; characters sound the same | Dialogue reveals character, advances the plot, or shows relationships; characters have distinct voices | Dialogue is natural, purposeful, and revealing; characters sound authentic and distinct, and every exchange serves the story |
| Dialogue Punctuation | Multiple punctuation errors; quotation marks, commas, and paragraph breaks are missing | Some punctuation is correct, but errors are frequent (e.g., missing commas, end marks outside quotes) | Dialogue is punctuated correctly with minor errors; new paragraphs for new speakers are used consistently | Dialogue punctuation is flawless, including varied tag placement (before, after, and mid-dialogue) |
| Pacing — Expansion | No important moments are expanded; the narrative moves at a flat, even pace throughout | Some expansion is attempted but is unfocused or adds detail that does not serve the story | The most important moment is clearly expanded with sensory details, internal thoughts, or description | Multiple moments are expanded with purpose; the writer demonstrates control over pacing, using expansion to build tension or emotion |
| Pacing — Compression | No compression is used; all events receive equal detail regardless of importance | Some less important events are shortened, but others are still over-developed | Less important events are efficiently summarized, keeping the story moving | The writer compresses and expands with deliberate control, creating a rhythm that enhances the reading experience |
| Action Beats / Tags | No tags or action beats are included; the reader cannot tell who is speaking | Dialogue tags are present but repetitive ("said" every time) with no action beats | Dialogue tags are varied and action beats are used to show emotion and ground the scene | Tags and action beats are skillfully woven into dialogue, adding character depth and visual clarity without interrupting the flow |
