# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 3

## Topic
Informative Writing: Introducing a Topic Clearly

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write an introduction that names the topic and provides a general observation or focus statement
- Group related information into logical categories or sections
- Distinguish between informative writing (explaining) and opinion writing (persuading)

## Prerequisites
- W.4.2: Experience writing informative/explanatory texts with facts, definitions, and details
- W.4.2a: Introducing a topic and grouping related information in paragraphs
- Lessons 1-2: Understanding of clear introductions and organizational structure from opinion writing

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Copies of the model informative text (provided below)
- Graphic organizer: Topic Web with Categories (described below)
- Highlighters (two colors)
- Sticky notes

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Help students distinguish informative writing from opinion writing and activate background knowledge.

**Activity: Opinion or Information?**

Read aloud the following opening sentences. Students hold up one finger for "opinion" and two fingers for "informative."

1. "Schools should ban junk food from cafeterias because it contributes to childhood obesity." (Opinion — takes a position)
2. "The human body has 206 bones, and each one plays a specific role in supporting movement and protecting organs." (Informative — explains a topic)
3. "Everyone should learn to play chess because it improves critical thinking skills." (Opinion)
4. "Monarch butterflies travel up to 3,000 miles during their annual migration from Canada to central Mexico." (Informative)

Say: "In opinion writing, we tried to convince the reader. In informative writing, our job is different. We are teachers. Our goal is to explain a topic so clearly that the reader truly understands it. We present facts, not arguments."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach the components of a strong informative introduction and the skill of grouping related information.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Informative/explanatory writing:** Writing that explains a topic using facts, definitions, details, and examples — without arguing a position
- **General observation:** A broad statement about the topic that gives the reader context before the details begin
- **Focus statement:** A sentence that tells the reader what specific aspect of the topic the piece will cover
- **Grouping information:** Organizing facts and details into categories or sections so the reader can follow the explanation easily

### Instruction Notes

Say: "A good informative introduction does three things." Write on the board:

**Informative Introduction Formula:**
1. **Hook** — Grab the reader's attention (interesting fact, question, or vivid image)
2. **General observation** — Give the reader a big-picture understanding of the topic
3. **Focus statement** — Narrow the topic and tell the reader what categories or subtopics the piece will cover

Say: "Watch me write an introduction about the water cycle."

Think aloud and write on chart paper:

"Hook: 'Every drop of water you drink has been recycled billions of times — the same water that filled ancient oceans now flows from your kitchen tap.' General observation: 'The water cycle is the continuous process that moves water through the earth, the atmosphere, and back again.' Focus statement: 'The three main stages of the water cycle — evaporation, condensation, and precipitation — work together in a never-ending loop that sustains all life on Earth.'"

Say: "Notice that my focus statement previews the three categories I will use to organize my body paragraphs. This is how I GROUP my information — by stage."

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Display the following model informative text:

> **The Amazing World of Octopuses**
>
> What has three hearts, blue blood, and the ability to change color in less than a second? The answer is the octopus — one of the most remarkable creatures in the ocean. Octopuses belong to a group of animals called cephalopods, which also includes squid and cuttlefish. While many people think of octopuses as simple sea creatures, they are actually among the most intelligent animals on the planet. This article explores three fascinating aspects of octopuses: their unusual body structure, their impressive intelligence, and their extraordinary survival strategies.
>
> Octopuses have a body structure unlike any other animal. They have eight flexible arms, each lined with hundreds of suction cups that can taste and grip at the same time. Unlike humans, octopuses have no bones at all, which means they can squeeze through openings as small as a quarter. Their three hearts pump blue, copper-based blood — two hearts send blood to the gills, and one pumps it to the rest of the body.
>
> In addition to their unusual bodies, octopuses display remarkable intelligence. Scientists have observed octopuses solving mazes, unscrewing jar lids, and even using coconut shells as portable shelters. In one famous experiment at the Seattle Aquarium, an octopus named Billie learned to open a childproof pill bottle in under a minute. These behaviors suggest that octopuses can learn from experience and solve problems creatively.
>
> Finally, octopuses have survival strategies that seem almost magical. They can change the color, texture, and pattern of their skin in a fraction of a second, allowing them to blend into rocks, coral, or sand to hide from predators. If camouflage fails, they can release a cloud of dark ink to confuse attackers while they jet away. Some species can even detach an arm to distract a predator — and the arm grows back later.
>
> From their boneless bodies to their problem-solving brains to their color-changing skin, octopuses are truly one of nature's most extraordinary creations. Scientists continue to study these animals, and each new discovery reveals just how much we still have to learn about the ocean's most clever residents.

Annotate with two colors:
- **Color 1:** The three parts of the introduction (hook, general observation, focus statement)
- **Color 2:** The topic sentence of each body paragraph (one per category)

Ask: "How did the writer group the information? What are the three categories?" (Body structure, intelligence, survival strategies.) "Where does the writer tell us those categories?" (In the focus statement at the end of the introduction.)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Collaboratively create an informative writing plan using the Topic Web with Categories organizer.

**Activity: Plan an Informative Piece Together**

Draw the following graphic organizer on chart paper:

```
              [TOPIC: Honeybees]
             /        |         \
    [Category 1]  [Category 2]  [Category 3]
    Body Structure  Life in the   Why Bees
    and Abilities    Hive         Matter
       |              |              |
    - 2 pairs of   - Queen, worker, - Pollinate 80%
      wings          drone roles      of flowering
    - See UV light - Honeycomb       plants
    - Carry pollen   construction  - Produce honey
      on legs      - Communicate   - Ecosystem
                     by dancing      balance
```

Guide students through the process:

1. **Name the topic:** "Honeybees"
2. **Write a hook together:** "Without honeybees, one out of every three bites of food you eat would disappear."
3. **Write a general observation:** "Honeybees are small insects that play a massive role in our world."
4. **Write a focus statement:** "From their remarkable body design to their complex social life to their critical importance for the environment, honeybees are far more fascinating than most people realize."
5. **Identify three categories** and brainstorm 2-3 facts for each (shown above).

Say: "Notice how each category will become one body paragraph. The facts under each category are the details we will develop in that paragraph."

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently plan and draft an informative introduction with grouped categories.

Choose ONE of the following topics (or propose your own, approved by the teacher):

1. A natural disaster (earthquakes, hurricanes, tornadoes, or volcanoes)
2. An animal you find fascinating
3. A country or culture you want to teach others about

**Task:**
1. Complete a Topic Web with Categories graphic organizer (topic in the center, three categories branching out, 2-3 facts under each category)
2. Draft your introductory paragraph including: a hook, a general observation, and a focus statement that previews your categories

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Complete the Topic Web and draft the introductory paragraph as described.
**Stretch:** Complete the Topic Web, draft the introduction, AND write one body paragraph that develops the first category with facts and details.
**Challenge:** Complete the Topic Web, draft the introduction, AND write two body paragraphs. Use at least one definition and one concrete detail in each paragraph.

### Interleaved Review
1. **Spelling review (L.4.2d):** Spell the following words correctly: environment, explanation, information, necessary. Check each against a dictionary or word wall.
2. **Opinion writing review (W.5.1):** In one sentence, explain what makes an opinion statement different from a focus statement. (Expected: An opinion statement takes a position and tries to persuade, while a focus statement previews what the piece will explain without arguing for or against anything.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following informative introduction. Identify and label: (a) the hook, (b) the general observation, and (c) the focus statement.

"Did you know that your brain uses 20% of all the energy your body produces? The human brain is the most complex organ in the body, controlling everything from breathing to emotions to memory. This article will examine three key parts of the brain — the cerebrum, the cerebellum, and the brain stem — and explain what each one does."

**Expected Response:**
- Hook: "Did you know that your brain uses 20% of all the energy your body produces?"
- General observation: "The human brain is the most complex organ in the body, controlling everything from breathing to emotions to memory."
- Focus statement: "This article will examine three key parts of the brain — the cerebrum, the cerebellum, and the brain stem — and explain what each one does."

**Mastery = Student correctly identifies all three components of the introduction.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the three-part introduction formula on the anchor chart
- Provide a color-coded example where each part is already highlighted
- Have the student practice labeling a second introduction with teacher support

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 4 (Developing with Facts and Details)
- Encourage the student to continue developing their informative piece during free writing time

## Extension (Optional)
- **Text structure comparison:** Read a short informative article and a short opinion article on the same topic (e.g., both about video games). Create a Venn diagram showing how the introductions are similar and different.
- **Category challenge:** Take your chosen topic and brainstorm five possible categories. Then select the best three and explain why you chose them.
- **Introduction makeover:** Rewrite the following weak introduction to make it strong: "This report is about dolphins. Dolphins are cool. I will tell you some facts." Include a hook, general observation, and focus statement.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The biggest shift for students moving from opinion to informative writing is neutrality. Students may slip into persuasive language ("Octopuses are the best animal") instead of informative language ("Octopuses are among the most intelligent ocean animals"). Gently redirect when you hear opinion language creeping in.
- The Topic Web with Categories is the key organizational tool for this lesson. Students who struggle with grouping can use a simpler approach: write all their facts on sticky notes, then physically sort the notes into piles. Each pile becomes a category.
- Remind students that the focus statement is like a roadmap — it tells the reader exactly where the piece is going. If a student's focus statement is vague ("I will tell you about volcanoes"), push for specificity ("This article explains how volcanoes form, what happens during an eruption, and where the most dangerous volcanoes are located").
- Save students' informative plans and drafts for use in Lesson 4 and the Revising and Editing Workshop (Lesson 9).

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Hook | No hook; the piece begins with a flat statement like "This report is about..." | A hook is attempted but is generic or unrelated to the topic | The hook grabs attention with an interesting fact, question, or vivid image relevant to the topic | The hook is compelling, original, and seamlessly leads into the general observation |
| General Observation | No general observation; the piece jumps into details immediately | A general observation is present but is vague or inaccurate | The general observation provides accurate big-picture context for the topic | The general observation is engaging, accurate, and demonstrates strong background knowledge |
| Focus Statement | No focus statement or the focus is unclear | A focus statement is present but does not preview specific categories | The focus statement clearly previews three logical categories or subtopics | The focus statement is polished, specific, and creates a clear roadmap that builds reader curiosity |
| Grouping / Organization | Information is not grouped; facts appear in random order | Some grouping is attempted but categories overlap or are illogical | Information is grouped into clear, distinct categories with relevant facts in each | Categories are distinct, logically ordered, and each contains well-selected, specific details |
