# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3, Lesson 4

## Topic
Informative Writing: Developing with Facts and Details

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Develop informative body paragraphs with facts, definitions, concrete details, and quotations
- Use precise language and domain-specific vocabulary appropriate to the topic
- Write a concluding statement that summarizes the key information and leaves the reader with a final insight

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 3: Writing an informative introduction with a hook, general observation, and focus statement
- W.4.2b: Experience developing a topic with facts, definitions, and details
- Understanding of the Topic Web with Categories organizer from Lesson 3

## Materials Needed
- Anchor chart paper and markers
- Students' informative plans and introductions from Lesson 3
- Lined writing paper or writing journals
- Model informative text (provided below)
- Highlighters (four colors)
- Domain-specific vocabulary chart (created during the lesson)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Distinguish between vague and specific details to build awareness of precise language.

**Activity: Vague vs. Vivid**

Display the following pairs of sentences. Students identify which sentence in each pair is more informative and explain why.

**Pair 1:**
- A: "Tornadoes are really strong and can mess things up."
- B: "An EF5 tornado can produce winds exceeding 200 miles per hour, strong enough to rip a well-built house from its foundation."

**Pair 2:**
- A: "The process by which plants convert sunlight, water, and carbon dioxide into glucose and oxygen is called photosynthesis."
- B: "Plants make food from sunlight. This is something they do every day."

Say: "In both pairs, Sentence B packs more information into fewer words by using specific facts and precise vocabulary. That is our goal today: to develop our informative writing with the kind of details that make the reader truly understand the topic."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach four types of development for informative paragraphs and introduce domain-specific vocabulary.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Concrete detail:** A specific, observable fact or piece of information (not a vague generalization)
- **Domain-specific vocabulary:** Technical or specialized words related to a particular subject (e.g., "ecosystem," "photosynthesis," "erosion")
- **Precise language:** Words chosen carefully for accuracy and clarity, avoiding vague terms like "stuff," "things," or "a lot"
- **Quotation:** The exact words of an expert or source, placed in quotation marks, used to add authority to writing

### Instruction Notes

Say: "In Lesson 3, we built the frame of our informative piece — the introduction and the categories. Today we fill in that frame with strong details. There are four tools you can use to develop your informative paragraphs."

Write on the board:

**Four Development Tools:**
1. **Facts** — Specific, verifiable pieces of information (numbers, dates, measurements)
2. **Definitions** — Clear explanations of key terms the reader might not know
3. **Concrete details** — Sensory or observable descriptions that make the topic real
4. **Quotations** — Expert words that add authority and credibility

Model with a think-aloud using the topic of coral reefs:

Say: "I am writing about why coral reefs are important. Watch how I use all four tools in one paragraph."

Write on chart paper:

"Fact: 'Coral reefs cover less than 1% of the ocean floor, yet they support approximately 25% of all marine species.' Definition: 'Coral reefs are underwater structures built by tiny animals called coral polyps, which secrete a hard calcium carbonate skeleton.' Concrete detail: 'A healthy reef teems with life — schools of neon-blue tangs dart between brain-shaped coral heads while sea turtles glide overhead.' Quotation: 'Marine biologist Dr. Sylvia Earle once said, "No water, no life. No blue, no green." Her words remind us that protecting the ocean means protecting everything that depends on it.'"

Now introduce domain-specific vocabulary:

Say: "Notice the words 'coral polyps,' 'calcium carbonate,' and 'marine species.' These are domain-specific vocabulary — they are the precise terms that experts use when discussing coral reefs. When you write about a topic, use the real vocabulary of that subject. It shows the reader you know what you are talking about."

### Worked Examples / Model Texts

Display the following model informative body paragraph and conclusion:

> **Body Paragraph (from a piece about the International Space Station):**
>
> Life aboard the International Space Station, or ISS, is nothing like life on Earth. Because the ISS orbits Earth at approximately 17,500 miles per hour, astronauts experience a condition called microgravity, which means they float freely inside the station. Eating is one of the biggest adjustments: food must be specially packaged to prevent crumbs from floating into equipment. Astronauts squeeze drinks from sealed pouches and anchor their trays with Velcro. According to NASA astronaut Scott Kelly, who spent 340 consecutive days on the ISS, "The hardest part isn't the work — it's the little things you take for granted on Earth, like sitting down to eat a meal at a table." Despite these challenges, astronauts maintain a strict schedule of scientific experiments, exercise (at least two hours a day to prevent muscle loss), and maintenance tasks to keep the station running smoothly.
>
> **Concluding Paragraph:**
>
> The International Space Station is more than a laboratory floating in space — it is a testament to what humans can accomplish when nations work together. Since its first module was launched in 1998, the ISS has hosted over 250 astronauts from 19 different countries, producing research that has led to advances in medicine, materials science, and our understanding of the human body. As space agencies plan missions to the Moon and Mars, the lessons learned aboard the ISS will serve as the foundation for the next chapter of human exploration.

Ask students to identify:
- Two facts (17,500 mph; 340 days; 250 astronauts from 19 countries)
- One definition (microgravity)
- Two concrete details (food packaged to prevent crumbs; squeeze drinks from pouches)
- One quotation (Scott Kelly)
- Three domain-specific vocabulary words (microgravity, ISS, orbit)

## Guided Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students collaboratively develop a body paragraph using all four development tools.

**Activity: Build a Paragraph Together**

Return to the class honeybee informative piece from Lesson 3. Choose one category (e.g., "Why Bees Matter") and develop a full body paragraph together.

Write on chart paper as a class, prompting students at each step:

1. **Topic sentence:** "Honeybees are essential to the global food supply and the health of ecosystems everywhere."
2. **Fact:** Ask: "Does anyone know a fact about bees and pollination?" Guide toward: "According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, honeybees pollinate approximately 80% of all flowering plants, including more than 130 types of fruits and vegetables."
3. **Definition:** Ask: "What does 'pollination' mean? Let's define it for our reader." Guide toward: "Pollination is the process by which bees transfer pollen from one flower to another, allowing the plant to produce seeds and fruit."
4. **Concrete detail:** Ask: "Can you picture a bee pollinating? Describe it." Guide toward: "As a honeybee lands on a bright yellow sunflower, tiny grains of pollen cling to the fine hairs on its legs. When the bee visits the next flower, some of that pollen rubs off, fertilizing the plant."
5. **Quotation:** Provide: "Entomologist Dr. Marla Spivak explains, 'Bees are responsible for every third bite of food we eat.'"

Read the completed paragraph aloud. Ask: "Does this teach the reader something? Could we make any language more precise?"

Create a quick domain-specific vocabulary chart for honeybees: pollination, nectar, hive, colony, entomologist, ecosystem.

## Independent Practice (10-12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Students independently draft body paragraphs and a conclusion for their informative piece.

Using your Topic Web from Lesson 3, continue developing your informative piece:

1. **Draft two body paragraphs** (one for each of two categories). Each paragraph must include at least:
   - One fact
   - One concrete detail
   - One domain-specific vocabulary word
2. **Draft a concluding paragraph** that summarizes the key information and ends with a final insight or reflection.

### Difficulty Tiers
**On-level:** Draft two body paragraphs and a conclusion as described above.
**Stretch:** Draft all three body paragraphs and a conclusion. Include at least one definition and one quotation (real or teacher-created) somewhere in the piece.
**Challenge:** Draft the complete informative piece (introduction from Lesson 3 + three body paragraphs + conclusion). Underline every domain-specific vocabulary word and ensure each term is either defined or made clear through context.

### Interleaved Review
1. **Comma review (L.4.2c):** Add commas where needed: "Coral reefs which are found in warm shallow waters support thousands of marine species." (Expected: "Coral reefs, which are found in warm shallow waters, support thousands of marine species.")
2. **Introduction review (W.5.2a):** What are the three parts of a strong informative introduction? (Expected: A hook, a general observation, and a focus statement that previews the categories.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

Read the following informative sentence. Rewrite it to make the language more precise and add one domain-specific vocabulary word.

Original: "The big storm caused a lot of damage to buildings and stuff in the area."

**Expected Response:**
A revised sentence with specific facts and precise language. Example: "The Category 4 hurricane produced sustained winds of 150 miles per hour, causing severe structural damage to more than 2,000 homes and businesses along the Gulf Coast."

**Mastery = The rewritten sentence replaces vague language with at least two specific details and includes one domain-specific term.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the "Vague vs. Vivid" warm-up pairs and identify what makes the vivid sentences stronger
- Provide a word bank of precise vocabulary related to storms (hurricane, sustained winds, Category 4, structural damage, debris)
- Have the student revise one sentence from their own draft using the same strategy

### If Mastery Is Met
- Student is ready for Lesson 5 (Narrative Writing)
- Encourage the student to revise their informative piece, replacing any vague language with precise, domain-specific terms

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Quotation quest:** Find a real quotation from a scientist, historian, or expert related to your informative topic. Write it correctly with quotation marks and an attribution (e.g., "According to..."). Add it to your draft.
- **Vocabulary glossary:** Create a mini-glossary of 5-8 domain-specific vocabulary words for your topic. Include a definition and an example sentence for each.
- **Paragraph comparison:** Write two versions of the same body paragraph — one with vague language and one with precise language. Read both aloud to a partner and discuss the difference.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students often default to vague language ("a lot," "really big," "things") because it is comfortable. The Vague vs. Vivid activity should be posted as an anchor chart and referenced throughout the unit. When conferencing, circle vague words and ask: "Can you make this more specific?"
- Quotations can be challenging for fifth graders. If students do not have access to real expert quotations, allow them to paraphrase (e.g., "Scientists say that...") or provide a bank of relevant quotations for common topics.
- Domain-specific vocabulary should be taught in context, not as a separate vocabulary list. When students encounter or use a term, they should define it briefly for the reader — this is good informative writing practice and reinforces comprehension.
- The concluding paragraph should go beyond summary. Push students to add a "so what?" — why does this information matter? What should the reader take away? This mirrors the concluding statement expectations in W.5.2e.

## Rubric / Answer Key

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| Facts and Details | No facts or details are included; paragraphs are vague or opinion-based | Some facts are included but are vague or unsupported (e.g., "Scientists say...") | Each paragraph includes at least one specific, accurate fact and one concrete detail | Paragraphs are rich with multiple specific facts, concrete details, and well-integrated quotations |
| Domain-Specific Vocabulary | No domain-specific terms are used | One or two domain-specific terms are used but not explained | Several domain-specific terms are used and made clear through definitions or context | Domain-specific vocabulary is used fluently and precisely, with terms defined naturally within the text |
| Precise Language | Language is vague throughout (e.g., "a lot," "really good," "stuff") | Language is sometimes precise but often falls back on vague terms | Language is generally precise, with specific numbers, names, and descriptive words replacing vague terms | Language is consistently precise, vivid, and carefully chosen, demonstrating strong command of word choice |
| Concluding Statement | No conclusion or the piece ends abruptly | A conclusion is present but only restates the introduction | The conclusion summarizes key information and provides a final insight or reflection | The conclusion synthesizes the information, offers a meaningful insight, and leaves the reader with a clear sense of why the topic matters |
