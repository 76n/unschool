# English Language Arts — Grade 5 — Unit 3: Writing

## Unit Assessment

### Overview

This assessment evaluates student mastery of the four core writing standards addressed in Unit 3: opinion writing (W.5.1), informative/explanatory writing (W.5.2), narrative writing (W.5.3), and writing appropriate to task, purpose, and audience (W.5.4). The assessment has two parts:

- **Part A — Writing Tasks:** Three writing prompts (one opinion, one informative, one narrative), each scored with a 4-level rubric
- **Part B — Short-Answer Questions:** Eight questions about writing craft and technique

**Time:** 60-75 minutes (recommended: 20 minutes per writing task + 15 minutes for short-answer questions)

**Mastery Criteria:** Proficient (3) or above on at least two of the three writing tasks AND 70% or above (6/8) on the short-answer questions.

---

## Part A: Writing Tasks

### Task 1 — Opinion Writing (W.5.1)

**Prompt:**

Your school is considering eliminating art and music classes to make more time for reading and math. Write an opinion piece in which you take a position on this issue. Your piece must include:

- An introduction that names the topic and clearly states your opinion
- At least two reasons, logically ordered, each supported by facts, details, or examples
- Transitional words, phrases, and/or clauses that connect your opinion to your reasons
- A concluding statement that reinforces your opinion without simply restating the introduction

Write your opinion piece on the lined paper provided. You should write at least four paragraphs (introduction, two or more body paragraphs, conclusion).

---

#### Opinion Writing Rubric

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Opinion Statement (W.5.1a)** | No clear opinion is stated, or the piece argues both sides without taking a position | An opinion is stated but is vague, buried in the text, or does not preview reasons | A clear opinion is stated in the introduction with a position and at least one reason previewed | A compelling opinion is stated early with a strong hook, clear position, and preview of logically ordered reasons |
| **Reasons and Evidence (W.5.1b)** | No reasons or evidence are provided; the piece consists only of unsupported assertions | One reason is provided with vague or missing evidence | At least two reasons are provided in logical order, each supported by at least one specific fact, detail, or example | Three or more well-chosen reasons are strategically ordered, each supported by specific, convincing evidence with clear explanation of how the evidence connects to the opinion |
| **Transitions (W.5.1c)** | No transitions are used; ideas are disconnected | Only simple transitional words are used (first, also, next), with no phrases or clauses | A mix of transitional words, phrases, and clauses is used to link opinions and reasons | Transitions are varied, precise, and seamlessly integrated; clause-level transitions add information while connecting ideas |
| **Conclusion (W.5.1d)** | No conclusion is provided, or the piece ends abruptly | The conclusion restates the introduction without adding new perspective | The conclusion reinforces the opinion using a strategy (call to action, forward-looking statement, or thought-provoking question) | The conclusion synthesizes the argument, leaves a lasting impression, and inspires the reader to think or act |
| **Conventions** | Frequent errors in spelling, punctuation, capitalization, and grammar that interfere with readability | Several errors are present; some interfere with meaning | Minor errors are present but do not interfere with meaning; the piece demonstrates solid command of Grade 5 conventions | The piece is virtually error-free and demonstrates strong command of all applicable conventions |

---

#### Sample Proficient Opinion Response

> **Keep Art and Music in Our Schools**
>
> Imagine a school day with nothing but reading worksheets and math drills — no painting, no singing, no creative expression at all. While reading and math are important, eliminating art and music classes would be a serious mistake. Schools should keep art and music because these subjects improve academic performance in other areas and because they help students develop skills that reading and math alone cannot teach.
>
> First and foremost, studies show that art and music actually boost performance in reading and math. According to a report by the Arts Education Partnership, students who participate in regular arts education score an average of 20 points higher on standardized tests than students who do not. This makes sense because music involves counting rhythms and reading patterns, which strengthens mathematical thinking, while visual art requires careful observation and analysis, skills that transfer directly to reading comprehension. Cutting art and music to improve test scores could, ironically, cause scores to drop.
>
> In addition to academic benefits, art and music develop critical skills that worksheets cannot teach. When students collaborate on a mural or rehearse for a concert, they practice teamwork, communication, and problem-solving. Because these "soft skills" are among the most valued by employers and colleges, removing the classes that build them would leave students less prepared for the future, not more. A well-rounded education includes creativity alongside computation.
>
> For all of these reasons, the school should keep art and music as core parts of the schedule. Rather than cutting these programs, administrators should look for ways to integrate the arts with reading and math — because when creativity and academics work together, every student benefits.

### Task 2 — Informative/Explanatory Writing (W.5.2)

**Prompt:**

Choose a topic you know well (an animal, a natural phenomenon, a historical event, a sport, or a hobby). Write an informative piece that teaches the reader about your topic. Your piece must include:

- An introduction with a hook, a general observation, and a focus statement that previews your categories
- At least two body paragraphs, each developing a different category with facts, definitions, concrete details, or quotations
- Precise language and at least three domain-specific vocabulary words
- Transitional words, phrases, and/or clauses that connect ideas within and between paragraphs
- A concluding statement that summarizes the key information and offers a final insight

Write your informative piece on the lined paper provided. You should write at least four paragraphs (introduction, two or more body paragraphs, conclusion).

---

#### Informative Writing Rubric

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Introduction (W.5.2a)** | No introduction, or the piece begins with "This report is about..." without a hook or focus | An introduction is present but is missing one or more elements (hook, general observation, or focus statement) | The introduction includes a hook, a general observation, and a focus statement that previews two or more categories | The introduction is engaging and polished, with a compelling hook, insightful general observation, and a clear focus statement that builds curiosity |
| **Development (W.5.2b)** | Body paragraphs contain no facts, definitions, details, or quotations; ideas are vague or opinion-based | Some facts or details are present but are vague, inaccurate, or poorly connected to the topic sentence | Each body paragraph includes at least one specific fact and one concrete detail, clearly connected to the category | Body paragraphs are richly developed with multiple facts, definitions, concrete details, and/or quotations that deepen the reader's understanding |
| **Precise Language and Vocabulary (W.5.2d)** | Language is vague throughout; no domain-specific vocabulary is used | Some precise language is present; one or two domain-specific terms are used but may not be defined | Precise language replaces vague terms; at least three domain-specific vocabulary words are used and made clear through definition or context | Language is consistently precise and vivid; domain-specific vocabulary is used fluently and contributes to the authoritative tone of the piece |
| **Transitions (W.5.2c)** | No transitions; ideas within and between paragraphs are disconnected | Simple transitional words are used (also, next) but no phrases or clauses | A mix of transitional words, phrases, and clauses links ideas within and between paragraphs | Transitions are varied, purposeful, and contribute to the coherence and flow of the piece |
| **Conclusion (W.5.2e)** | No conclusion, or the piece ends abruptly | The conclusion restates the introduction without summarizing or offering insight | The conclusion summarizes key information and provides a final insight or reflection | The conclusion synthesizes the information, offers a meaningful takeaway, and leaves the reader with a clear understanding of why the topic matters |

---

#### Sample Proficient Informative Response

> **The Secret Life of Soil**
>
> What if you were told that a single teaspoon of healthy soil contains more living organisms than there are people on Earth? Soil is far more than the dirt beneath our feet — it is a complex, living ecosystem that supports nearly all life on land. This article explores three key aspects of soil: what it is made of, the organisms that live in it, and why it matters for the future of our planet.
>
> Soil is made up of four main components: minerals, organic matter, water, and air. Minerals come from rocks that have been broken down over thousands of years by a process called weathering. Organic matter, also known as humus, is the dark, nutrient-rich material created when dead plants and animals decompose. A healthy soil is roughly 45% minerals, 25% water, 25% air, and 5% organic matter. Although organic matter makes up the smallest percentage, it is the most important ingredient for plant growth because it releases nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium — the three nutrients plants need most.
>
> In addition to its physical components, soil is home to an astonishing community of living organisms. Earthworms tunnel through the ground, aerating the soil and mixing nutrients from the surface into deeper layers. Microscopic bacteria and fungi break down dead material and convert it into forms that plant roots can absorb. Scientists estimate that a single acre of healthy farmland may contain more than one million earthworms and billions of bacteria. Because these organisms work around the clock, they are sometimes called "nature's recyclers."
>
> Understanding soil is not just an academic exercise — it is a matter of survival. Soil produces 95% of the world's food, filters and stores water, and absorbs carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Yet according to the United Nations, one-third of the world's topsoil has already been degraded by erosion, pollution, and poor farming practices. As the global population continues to grow, protecting and restoring soil health will be one of the most important challenges of the coming decades.

### Task 3 — Narrative Writing (W.5.3)

**Prompt:**

Write a narrative about a time when a character faces an unexpected challenge. The story may be based on real events or entirely imagined. Your narrative must include:

- An opening that introduces the character(s), establishes the setting, and sets up the situation
- A clear sequence of events organized logically
- At least one scene with dialogue (correctly punctuated)
- Narrative techniques: sensory details (at least three senses), description, and pacing (expand at least one important moment)
- Transitional words, phrases, and/or clauses that manage the sequence of events
- A conclusion that follows from the narrated events

Write your narrative on the lined paper provided. You should write at least five paragraphs.

---

#### Narrative Writing Rubric

| Criteria | Beginning (1) | Developing (2) | Proficient (3) | Advanced (4) |
|----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Orientation (W.5.3a)** | No character, setting, or situation is established; the reader is confused from the start | One or two elements are present but vague (e.g., a character is named but has no description; a setting is mentioned but not developed) | The opening clearly introduces the character, establishes the setting with specific details, and sets up the situation | The opening is compelling, immersive, and skillfully orients the reader; character, setting, and situation are woven together seamlessly |
| **Narrative Techniques (W.5.3b)** | No dialogue, description, or pacing techniques are used | Dialogue or description is attempted but is minimal or ineffective; pacing is flat (all events given equal weight) | Dialogue is used to reveal character or advance the plot; at least one important moment is expanded with description and sensory details | Dialogue is natural and revealing; pacing is controlled with deliberate expansion and compression; description enhances mood and tone throughout |
| **Transitions (W.5.3c)** | No transitions; the sequence of events is difficult to follow | Simple transitional words are used (then, next) but the sequence is still unclear in places | Transitional words, phrases, and clauses manage the sequence clearly; the reader can follow the timeline without confusion | Transitions are varied and sophisticated; they contribute to pacing and flow, not just sequence |
| **Concrete Words and Sensory Details (W.5.3d)** | No sensory details; language is vague and generic | One or two sensory details are present, mostly visual | Sensory details from at least three senses are woven into the narrative, using concrete, specific language | Sensory details are vivid, precise, and immersive; the reader can see, hear, feel, smell, or taste the story world |
| **Conclusion (W.5.3e)** | No conclusion; the story stops abruptly without resolution | A conclusion is present but feels rushed or disconnected from the events | The conclusion follows naturally from the narrated events and provides a sense of closure | The conclusion is satisfying and meaningful; it resolves the story and leaves the reader with something to think about |
| **Dialogue Punctuation** | Dialogue punctuation is missing or incorrect throughout | Some dialogue is punctuated correctly, but errors are frequent | Dialogue is punctuated correctly with minor errors; new paragraphs for new speakers are used | Dialogue punctuation is flawless, including varied tag placement and action beats |

---

#### Sample Proficient Narrative Response

> **The Bridge**
>
> The wooden bridge over Willow Creek had always looked solid enough from the road, but standing on it now, with the creek swollen to three times its normal size, Lena was not so sure. The spring rains had turned the quiet stream into a churning, muddy torrent. Brown water slapped against the support beams, sending a fine spray into the air that misted her face and smelled of wet earth and rotting leaves.
>
> Lena gripped the railing with one hand and her little brother Diego's wrist with the other. Their house was on the other side — she could see the yellow porch light glowing through the rain. They had walked this bridge every day after school for two years. But today, with the water roaring beneath them and the boards groaning under their feet, it felt like crossing a different bridge entirely.
>
> "I don't want to go," Diego said, pulling back. His backpack was soaked and his sneakers squelched on the wet wood.
>
> "We have to," Lena said, keeping her voice steady even though her heart was pounding. "Mom is waiting. Just hold onto me and don't look down."
>
> They took three careful steps. Then Lena felt it — a vibration under her feet, like the hum of a guitar string. She froze. One of the boards ahead of them had shifted, tilting upward on one side like a loose tooth. Muddy water seeped through the gap.
>
> "Go back," she said quickly, pulling Diego behind her. "Slowly. One step at a time."
>
> They backed off the bridge and stood on the gravel bank, rain streaming down their faces. Lena pulled out the phone their mother kept in the side pocket of her backpack for emergencies. Her fingers trembled as she dialed.
>
> "Mom? We're at the bridge. It's not safe to cross. Can you come around by the main road and pick us up?" She paused, listening to her mother's voice on the other end — worried but calm. "We're okay. We're okay."
>
> As they sat under the overhang of the old feed store waiting for their mother's headlights to appear through the rain, Diego leaned against Lena's shoulder. "You knew to go back," he said quietly. "How did you know?"
>
> Lena shrugged, but she knew the answer. She had felt it in her feet — that tiny vibration that meant something was wrong. Sometimes the bravest thing you could do was not push forward but turn around.

## Part B: Short-Answer Questions

**Directions:** Answer each question in complete sentences. Use specific examples when asked.

---

**Question 1 (W.5.1)**

What is the difference between a personal preference and a supportable opinion? Give one example of each.

**Expected Response:**
A personal preference is a statement of taste that cannot be argued with evidence (e.g., "Chocolate ice cream is the best flavor"). A supportable opinion is a position that can be backed up with reasons, facts, and evidence (e.g., "Schools should offer healthier lunch options because nutritious food improves student concentration and test scores"). The key difference is that a supportable opinion can be proven with evidence, while a preference is purely personal.

---

**Question 2 (W.5.1b)**

Read the following opinion paragraph. Identify the reason, the evidence, and the explanation.

"Schools should provide free breakfast to all students because hunger affects learning. A study by the American Dietetic Association found that students who skip breakfast are 40% more likely to struggle with concentration in the morning. This means that when schools provide breakfast, they are directly removing a barrier to learning and giving every student a fair chance to succeed."

**Expected Response:**
- Reason: Hunger affects learning.
- Evidence: A study by the American Dietetic Association found that students who skip breakfast are 40% more likely to struggle with concentration.
- Explanation: When schools provide breakfast, they remove a barrier to learning and give every student a fair chance to succeed.

---

**Question 3 (W.5.2a)**

Name the three parts of a strong informative introduction and explain the purpose of each.

**Expected Response:**
1. Hook — Grabs the reader's attention with an interesting fact, question, or vivid image.
2. General observation — Gives the reader big-picture context about the topic.
3. Focus statement — Narrows the topic and previews the specific categories or subtopics the piece will cover, acting as a roadmap for the reader.

---

**Question 4 (W.5.2d)**

Rewrite the following sentence to make it more precise. Replace at least two vague words with specific, concrete language.

"The animal was really big and could do cool things."

**Expected Response:**
(Answers will vary.) Example: "The African elephant, the largest land mammal on Earth, can uproot trees with its trunk and communicate with other elephants through low-frequency vibrations that travel through the ground." The revised sentence replaces "animal" with a specific species, "really big" with a factual comparison, and "cool things" with concrete abilities.

---

**Question 5 (W.5.3a)**

What three questions should a narrative opening answer for the reader? Write an original opening sentence (not from the unit) that answers all three.

**Expected Response:**
A narrative opening should answer: (1) WHO is in the story? (2) WHERE and WHEN does it take place? (3) WHAT is the situation or problem?

Example sentence: "On the last day of summer camp, twelve-year-old Nadia stood at the edge of the lake, trying to decide whether she was brave enough to jump from the high dive for the first time." (WHO: Nadia; WHERE/WHEN: a lake at summer camp, last day; WHAT: she is deciding whether to jump from the high dive.)

---

**Question 6 (W.5.3b-c)**

Read the following passage. Identify one example of expansion (a moment slowed down with detail) and one example of compression (an event summarized quickly).

"The bus ride to the museum took about forty-five minutes. When they arrived, Ms. Patel handed out clipboards and the class filed through the entrance. It was in the Hall of Ancient Egypt that everything changed. Jalen stopped in front of a glass case containing a small clay figure no bigger than his thumb. The figure had tiny carved eyes, a cracked smile, and what looked like a name scratched into its base. He leaned closer, his breath fogging the glass. The name was his — JALEN — spelled out in letters that looked three thousand years old."

**Expected Response:**
- Compression: "The bus ride to the museum took about forty-five minutes. When they arrived, Ms. Patel handed out clipboards and the class filed through the entrance." (Two events summarized in two sentences.)
- Expansion: "Jalen stopped in front of a glass case containing a small clay figure..." through the end. (The moment is slowed down with concrete details — the size of the figure, the carved eyes, the cracked smile, the fogging glass, the name.)

---

**Question 7 (W.5.1c, W.5.2c, W.5.3c)**

Explain the difference between a transitional word, a transitional phrase, and a transitional clause. Provide one example of each.

**Expected Response:**
- A transitional word is a single word that connects ideas (e.g., "however," "furthermore," "meanwhile").
- A transitional phrase is a group of words without a subject and verb that connects ideas (e.g., "on the other hand," "as a result," "in addition to this").
- A transitional clause is a group of words WITH a subject and verb that connects ideas (e.g., "Although many people believe that homework is unnecessary," "Because the scientist discovered a new species," "When the storm finally passed").

---

**Question 8 (W.5.4)**

A teacher gives you this assignment: "Write a letter to the city council explaining why your neighborhood needs a new playground." Identify the task, purpose, and audience. Then explain how the audience would affect your word choice.

**Expected Response:**
- Task: A letter (formal format with a greeting and closing)
- Purpose: To persuade/explain (convince the city council to build a playground)
- Audience: The city council (local government officials)

Because the audience is a group of government officials, the word choice should be formal and respectful. The writer should avoid slang and casual language (e.g., "it would be super cool") and instead use professional language (e.g., "the addition of a playground would provide significant benefits to the community"). The writer should also support claims with specific evidence (safety data, community surveys, cost estimates) rather than personal opinions, because city council members make decisions based on facts and community need.

## Scoring Guide

### Part A: Writing Tasks

Each writing task is scored using the rubric provided. Calculate the average score across all criteria for each task:

- **4.0 – 3.5:** Advanced
- **3.4 – 2.5:** Proficient
- **2.4 – 1.5:** Developing
- **1.4 – 1.0:** Beginning

**Mastery on Part A = Proficient (average of 2.5+) on at least TWO of the three writing tasks.**

### Part B: Short-Answer Questions

Each question is worth 1 point. Award full credit for responses that demonstrate understanding of the concept and provide specific, accurate examples. Award half credit for responses that show partial understanding or lack examples.

- **8/8:** Advanced
- **6-7/8:** Proficient
- **4-5/8:** Developing
- **0-3/8:** Beginning

**Mastery on Part B = 6/8 or above (75%).**

### Overall Unit Mastery

A student demonstrates overall mastery of Unit 3 if they meet BOTH of the following:
1. Proficient or above on at least two of the three writing tasks (Part A)
2. 6/8 or above on short-answer questions (Part B)

## Administration Notes

- **Timing:** Allow 60-75 minutes total. Students may work at their own pace. Recommended allocation: 20 minutes per writing task, 15 minutes for short-answer questions.
- **Reference materials:** Students may use the Transition Reference Chart from Lesson 7 and the Sensory Details Chart from Lesson 5. They should NOT use their previous drafts or graphic organizers — this assessment measures independent application.
- **Accommodations:** Students with IEPs or 504 plans should receive their documented accommodations (extended time, reduced quantity, dictation, etc.). For students who struggle with writing stamina, consider administering Part A across two sessions.
- **Scoring notes:** When scoring writing tasks, read the entire piece before assigning scores. Focus on what the student CAN do, not just what is missing. Look for evidence of the specific skills taught in the unit: opinion statements with previewed reasons, evidence with explanation, sensory details from multiple senses, varied transitions (especially phrases and clauses), and audience-appropriate tone.
