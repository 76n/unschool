# Math — Kindergarten — Unit 2, Lesson 7

## Topic
The − Sign; Mixed Addition and Subtraction

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Recognize the − sign as meaning "subtract" or "take away"
- Read and write subtraction number sentences (e.g., 5 − 2 = 3)
- Distinguish between addition (+) and subtraction (−) number sentences
- Solve a mix of addition and subtraction problems

## Prerequisites
- Solves subtraction problems within 10 with objects (Lessons 5-6)
- Reads and writes addition number sentences with + and = (Lesson 4)

## Materials Needed
- Sign cards: +, −, = (large, on index cards)
- Number cards 0-10
- Counting objects
- Ten-frame
- Pencil and paper

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review subtraction and addition.

1. "8 take away 3 leaves ___?" (Answer: 5) *(Lesson 6 review)*
2. "4 + 3 = ___?" (Answer: 7) *(Lesson 3 review — interleaved)*
3. "Read this: 2 + 5 = 7." (Student reads aloud) *(Lesson 4 review)*
4. "Write the number 10." *(Unit 1 review)*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the minus sign and subtraction number sentences. Then contrast with addition.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Minus (−):** The symbol that means "subtract" or "take away"
- **Number sentence:** Can now be addition OR subtraction (e.g., 5 − 2 = 3)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the minus sign.**
Place 5 objects on the table. Remove 2. "Five take away two leaves three. We've been saying that. Now here's how we WRITE it."

Lay out: [5] [−] [2] [=] [3].

Point to each: "Five... MINUS... two... equals... three. The minus sign means 'take away' or 'subtract.'"

**Step 2: Compare + and −.**
Hold up the + card: "This means ADD — put together — the number gets BIGGER."
Hold up the − card: "This means SUBTRACT — take away — the number gets SMALLER."

"They look different on purpose. The plus has TWO lines crossing. The minus has just ONE line."

**Step 3: Build subtraction sentences.**
Let the student watch you build each with cards, verifying with objects:

- [7] [−] [3] [=] [4] — Start with 7 objects, remove 3, count 4 left.
- [6] [−] [1] [=] [5] — Start with 6, remove 1, count 5.
- [10] [−] [4] [=] [6] — Start with 10, remove 4, count 6.

**Step 4: Write subtraction sentences.**
On paper, write: 8 − 3 = 5. "Eight minus three equals five. Can you read it with me?"

Student reads. Then the student writes one: model 6 − 2 = 4 and have them copy, then write their own.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Write ___ for "9 take away 5 leaves 4." → 9 − 5 = 4.

**Example 2:** Read "7 − 2 = 5" and verify with objects. Start with 7, remove 2, count 5. Correct!

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice reading subtraction sentences and distinguishing + from −.

**Activity 1: Read the sentence (3 minutes)**
Write these. Student reads aloud and explains:

- 6 − 2 = 4 → "Six minus two equals four. I start with 6, take away 2, and I have 4."
- 9 − 3 = 6 → "Nine minus three equals six."
- 10 − 5 = 5 → "Ten minus five equals five."

**Activity 2: Plus or minus? (4 minutes)**
Show number sentences with a blank sign. Student decides if it should be + or −:

- 3 ___ 2 = 5 → (+) "We need the number to get bigger."
- 5 ___ 1 = 4 → (−) "We need the number to get smaller."
- 4 ___ 4 = 8 → (+)
- 7 ___ 3 = 4 → (−)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student is unsure, ask: "Is the answer bigger or smaller than the first number? If bigger, we're adding. If smaller, we're taking away."*

**Activity 3: Write number sentences (3 minutes)**
Student writes:
- Show 8 objects, take away 5. "Write the number sentence." → 8 − 5 = 3
- Show 4 objects, add 3 more. "Write the number sentence." → 4 + 3 = 7

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Mixed addition and subtraction practice.

**★ On-level:**
1. 5 − 2 = ___ (3)
2. 3 + 4 = ___ (7)
3. 8 − 3 = ___ (5)
4. 6 + 2 = ___ (8)

**★★ Stretch:**
5. 10 − 7 = ___ (3)
6. 7 + 3 = ___ (10)
7. Write a subtraction number sentence that starts with 9. (e.g., 9 − 4 = 5)
8. "Is 6 − 2 = 4 true or false?" (True) "Is 5 + 1 = 7 true or false?" (False — it's 6)

**★★★ Challenge:**
9. "5 + ___ = 8. What's the missing number?" (3)
10. "9 − ___ = 6. What's the missing number?" (3)

### Interleaved Review
- "Name a pair that makes 10." *(Lesson 3 review)*
- "Count backward from 10 to 1." *(Unit 1 review)*
- Show number card 6. "Write this number." *(Unit 1 review)*

---

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess minus sign understanding and operation distinction.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Read aloud: 7 − 2 = 5. (Student reads correctly)
2. Solve: 9 − 4 = ___ (Answer: 5, using objects or drawing)
3. "What's the difference between + and −?" (Accept: plus means add/put together; minus means subtract/take away)

**Mastery = Correct on at least 2 of 3 items.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on one operation at a time if mixing them causes confusion.
- Use color coding: write + sentences in blue and − sentences in red until the student can distinguish.
- The key check: does the student know whether the answer should be bigger or smaller than the starting number?

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 8 (Story Problems).

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Fact families preview:** "If 3 + 4 = 7, then 7 − 4 = ___?" (3) and "7 − 3 = ___?" (4). Show with objects how the three numbers are related.
- **Operation sort:** Write 8 number sentences on cards (4 addition, 4 subtraction). Student sorts them into "addition" and "subtraction" piles.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Mixing operations is cognitively demanding.** For the first time, the student must decide WHICH operation to use, not just execute one they know is coming. This is a big step. If the student is overwhelmed, separate the operations: do all addition problems first, then all subtraction problems, then mix.

**The visual distinction between + and − matters.** Some children genuinely confuse the symbols. Point out: "Plus has a line going up and a line going across — it looks like a cross. Minus has only one line going across." You can also place the sign cards next to each other for comparison.

**Don't rush to abstract.** If the student needs objects to solve every problem, that is perfectly appropriate. The goal is understanding, not speed or memorization.
