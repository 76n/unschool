# Math — Kindergarten — Unit 4, Lesson 7

## Topic
Comparing Weights (Heavier, Lighter)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare two objects by weight using hands (hefting) and a balance scale
- Use the vocabulary words "heavier," "lighter," and "about the same" accurately
- Understand that size does not always determine weight (a large object can be lighter than a small object)
- Predict which of two objects is heavier, then verify using a balance scale

## Prerequisites
- Compares two objects by length and uses "longer" and "shorter" correctly (Lesson 6)
- Uses direct comparison to determine a measurable attribute (Lesson 6)
- Compares groups using "more" and "fewer" (Unit 1, Lesson 6)

## Materials Needed
- A simple balance scale (a purchased pan balance OR a homemade version: a clothes hanger with two identical cups or bags hung from each end)
- Collection of objects with varying sizes and weights:
  - Small heavy objects: a rock, a full water bottle cap, a handful of coins, a metal spoon
  - Large light objects: a cotton ball, a crumpled sheet of paper, a balloon, a sponge, a stuffed animal
  - Medium objects: a crayon, a block, a small book, an apple, a toy car
- Paper and crayons for recording predictions
- Two paper plates or small trays (for sorting)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review length comparison from Lesson 6 and activate prior knowledge.

1. Hold up two pencils of different lengths. "Which is longer? How do we check?" (Line them up at one end and compare the other end.) *(Lesson 6 review)*
2. "Put these three crayons in order from shortest to longest." (Student arranges 3 crayons correctly.) *(Lesson 6 review)*
3. "Today we are going to compare something NEW about objects. Not how LONG they are, but how HEAVY they are. Can you think of something really heavy?" (Accept any reasonable answer: a car, a refrigerator, a big rock.) "Can you think of something really light?" (A feather, a piece of paper, a leaf.)

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce weight comparison through hands-on hefting and a balance scale, and challenge the assumption that bigger always means heavier.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Heavier:** Weighs more; pushes down more
- **Lighter:** Weighs less; pushes down less
- **About the same:** Nearly equal in weight
- **Balance scale:** A tool that shows which of two objects is heavier — the heavier side goes DOWN
- **Heft:** To hold something in your hand to feel how heavy it is

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce hefting.**
Pick up a book in one hand and a cotton ball in the other. Hold your arms out like a human balance scale.

"I am going to be a BALANCE SCALE with my body! I have a book in this hand and a cotton ball in this hand. Which feels heavier?"

Let your arm with the book sink down slowly. "The book pushes my hand DOWN. It is HEAVIER. The cotton ball barely pushes at all. It is LIGHTER."

"Now YOU try. Hold out both hands." Place a rock in one hand and a crumpled piece of paper in the other. "Which hand feels heavier? Which feels lighter?"

Student responds: "The rock is heavier. The paper is lighter."

"You just COMPARED the weight! You used your hands to HEFT the objects — that means to feel how heavy they are."

**Step 2: Introduce the balance scale.**
Place the balance scale on the table. Make sure it is level to start.

"This is a BALANCE SCALE. Watch what happens when I put something on one side." Place a rock on one side. That side drops down.

"The side with the rock went DOWN. That is because the rock is heavy and it PUSHES the scale down."

Now place a cotton ball on the other side. "The rock side is STILL down. That means the rock is HEAVIER than the cotton ball. The cotton ball is LIGHTER than the rock."

Remove both objects. Place two similar blocks on each side. "Look — the scale is EVEN. That means these two blocks are ABOUT THE SAME weight. Neither is heavier."

"The rule is: the side that goes DOWN has the HEAVIER object. The side that goes UP has the LIGHTER object. If neither side moves, they are ABOUT THE SAME."

**Step 3: Challenge the assumption — bigger does not mean heavier.**
Hold up a large cotton ball (or a big sponge) and a small rock. "Which one is BIGGER?" (The cotton ball / sponge.) "So which do you think is HEAVIER?"

Many students will say the bigger one. "Let's CHECK!" Place them on the balance scale. The rock side goes down.

"SURPRISE! The little rock is HEAVIER than the big cotton ball! Being BIG does not always mean being HEAVY. A tiny rock can be heavier than a big fluffy pillow."

"That is why we always CHECK. We cannot just LOOK at something to know if it is heavier. We have to FEEL it or use a scale."

Repeat with another pair: a large balloon (or inflated bag) and a small book. "Which is bigger? Which is heavier? Let's find out!"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Compare a crayon and a metal spoon.
- "Which do you PREDICT is heavier? Hold one in each hand."
- Student hefts both: "The spoon is heavier."
- Place both on the balance scale to confirm. The spoon side goes down.
- "The spoon IS heavier. The crayon is lighter. Our hands were right!"

**Example B:** Compare a small toy car and a large piece of paper.
- "Which is bigger?" (The paper.) "Which do you predict is heavier?"
- Student hefts: "The toy car!"
- Confirm on the balance scale: the car side goes down.
- "The car is heavier, even though the paper is bigger. Size does not tell us weight."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices predicting and verifying weight comparisons.

**Activity 1: Predict, heft, and verify (5 minutes)**
Present the student with 4 pairs of objects. For each pair:
1. Student PREDICTS which is heavier.
2. Student HEFTS both objects (one in each hand).
3. Student VERIFIES on the balance scale.
4. Student says: "The ___ is heavier than the ___" or "They are about the same."

Pairs to compare:
- A block and a coin. (The block is likely heavier, depending on block size.)
- A small book and a stuffed animal. (The book is likely heavier — surprises the student.)
- Two identical blocks. (About the same.)
- A shoe and a crayon. (The shoe is heavier.)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student's prediction is wrong, celebrate the learning: "You predicted the stuffed bear would be heavier because it is bigger, but the book won! That is exactly why scientists always TEST their ideas instead of just guessing."*

**Activity 2: Sort by weight (5 minutes)**
Place a paper plate labeled "Heavier" and another labeled "Lighter" on the table. Choose one reference object (a block) and put it in the center.

"This block is our COMPARISON object. Pick up each of these items, compare it to the block, and put it on the HEAVIER plate or the LIGHTER plate."

Give the student 5-6 objects to sort: a coin, a cotton ball, a small book, a sponge, a rock, an apple.

After sorting, check 2-3 on the balance scale together. "Let's see if you were right!"

*Facilitator prompt: If the student is unsure about a close call, say: "When two things feel really close, put them both on the scale to check. If the scale barely moves, they might be ABOUT THE SAME."*

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
1. Hold a book in one hand and a feather (or cotton ball) in the other. "Which is heavier?" (The book.)
2. Look at the balance scale. The left side is down and has a rock on it. The right side is up and has a leaf on it. "Which is heavier — the rock or the leaf?" (The rock — the heavier side goes down.)
3. "Circle the heavier object: a watermelon or a grape." (The watermelon.)
4. "Is a real cat heavier or lighter than a toy cat?" (A real cat is heavier.)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. Compare a shoe and a sock using the balance scale. "Which is heavier? How do you know?" (The shoe is heavier; its side goes down on the scale.)
2. "A big balloon and a small apple. Which is heavier?" (The apple — size does not determine weight.)
3. Compare two objects that are close in weight. "Are they about the SAME, or is one heavier? How can you tell?" (Use the balance scale; if it barely tips, they are about the same.)
4. "Name something in your home that is heavier than a book." (Accept any reasonable heavy object: a chair, a pot, a gallon of milk.)
5. "Why can't we just LOOK at two things to know which is heavier?" (Because bigger things are not always heavier; we have to feel them or use a scale.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. Order three objects from lightest to heaviest using the balance scale. (Student compares pairs, then arranges all three.)
2. "A beach ball is very big. A golf ball is very small. Which is heavier?" (The golf ball.) "Why does that surprise some people?" (Because the beach ball is bigger, but it is mostly air inside.)
3. "If I put 5 cotton balls on one side of the scale and 1 rock on the other, which side do you think will go down? Let's test it." (Likely the rock, unless the rock is very small.)
4. "Find two objects that are ABOUT THE SAME weight. Prove it with the scale." (Student finds a pair and demonstrates a balanced or nearly balanced scale.)
5. "Can you make the scale balance by adding cotton balls to one side until it matches a block on the other side? How many cotton balls did you need?" (Answers vary — this previews non-standard measurement of weight.)

---

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. "Which is heavier — a rock or a cotton ball? How do you know?" (The rock; you can feel it in your hands or check on a balance scale — the rock side goes down.)
2. "On a balance scale, which side goes DOWN — the heavier side or the lighter side?" (The heavier side.)
3. "Is a bigger thing ALWAYS heavier? Give me an example." (No. A big pillow can be lighter than a small book, or a big balloon can be lighter than a small rock.)

## Teacher Notes

**Common Errors:**
- **Bigger = heavier assumption:** This is the most pervasive misconception at this age. Students naturally associate size with weight. The cotton ball vs. rock demonstration is critical. Return to this comparison throughout the lesson: "Remember — the big cotton ball was LIGHTER than the tiny rock!"
- **Confusing which side of the scale is "heavier":** Some students think the side that goes UP is heavier (perhaps because "up" feels like "more"). Consistently narrate: "Heavy things pull DOWN. Gravity pulls heavy things toward the ground. The heavier side sinks DOWN."
- **Not holding objects out at arm's length for hefting:** Students may hold objects close to their body, making it hard to sense the difference. Encourage them to extend both arms: "Hold your arms out like an airplane. Now feel which hand wants to drop."

**Tips for Success:**
- The CRA progression here is heavily Concrete. Students must hold, lift, and weigh real objects. Do NOT skip to pictures of objects on a scale — the physical experience of feeling weight is irreplaceable at this age.
- If you do not have a commercial balance scale, make one easily: hang two identical plastic cups from the ends of a clothes hanger. Hang the hanger from a doorknob or hook. Place objects in each cup. The heavier side sinks.
- Choose object pairs with OBVIOUS weight differences first (rock vs. cotton ball, book vs. feather) before moving to closer comparisons (block vs. crayon).
- Keep the mood playful. Say things like: "Let's do a WEIGHT CONTEST! Who is the heavyweight champion — the rock or the sponge?"

**Look-Fors:**
- Does the student use "heavier" and "lighter" accurately?
- Can the student interpret the balance scale (down = heavier)?
- Does the student understand that size does not determine weight?
- Can the student predict, test, and describe results?

**Connection to Future Learning:**
In Grade 1 and 2, students will use non-standard units (how many cubes does this book weigh?) and eventually standard units (grams, kilograms, ounces, pounds) to measure weight. The concept of "heavier = pushes down" extends to understanding gravitational force in science.
