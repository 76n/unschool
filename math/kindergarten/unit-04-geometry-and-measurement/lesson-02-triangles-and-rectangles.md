# Math — Kindergarten — Unit 4, Lesson 2

## Topic
Identifying Triangles and Rectangles

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify triangles by their properties (3 straight sides, 3 corners) in various orientations
- Identify rectangles by their properties (4 straight sides, 4 corners, opposite sides the same length)
- Distinguish rectangles from squares by recognizing that a square is a special kind of rectangle
- Find triangles and rectangles in everyday objects

## Prerequisites
- Identifies circles by properties: round, no straight sides, no corners (Unit 4, Lesson 1)
- Identifies squares by properties: 4 equal straight sides, 4 corners (Unit 4, Lesson 1)
- Uses the words "side" and "corner" when describing shapes (Unit 4, Lesson 1)

## Materials Needed
- Large cutout shapes: 4-5 triangles in different sizes, colors, and orientations; 4-5 rectangles in different sizes and colors; 2-3 squares for comparison
- Real-world objects: a triangular road sign or picture of one, a slice of pizza or sandwich cut diagonally, a coat hanger; a door, a book, an envelope, a phone (rectangles)
- Shape tracing templates (triangle and rectangle)
- Crayons or markers
- Paper
- Craft sticks or toothpicks for building shapes

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review circles and squares from Lesson 1 and prepare for new shapes.

1. Hold up a circle cutout. "What shape is this? Tell me one thing about it." (Answer: circle — round, no corners, no straight sides) *(Lesson 1 review)*
2. Hold up a square cutout. "What shape is this? How many sides? How many corners?" (Answer: square — 4 sides, 4 corners) *(Lesson 1 review)*
3. "Count backward from 15 to 1 with me!" Count together. *(Unit 3 review)*

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce triangles and rectangles as distinct shapes, emphasizing countable properties (sides and corners) and connecting rectangles to the already-known square.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Triangle:** A shape with 3 straight sides and 3 corners
- **Rectangle:** A shape with 4 straight sides and 4 corners; the opposite sides are the same length
- **Opposite sides:** The sides that are across from each other — not touching
- **Orientation:** The direction a shape is turned or rotated; a triangle is still a triangle even when flipped or rotated

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce the triangle.**
Hold up a large triangle cutout with one side flat on the bottom (the "standard" orientation).

"We already know circles and squares. Today we are going to learn TWO more shapes! This first one is a TRIANGLE. Say it with me: TRIANGLE."

Point to each side. "Let's count the sides: 1, 2, 3. A triangle has THREE straight sides." Run your finger along each side so the student can see them clearly.

Point to each corner. "Now let's count the corners: 1, 2, 3. A triangle has THREE corners."

"So a triangle has 3 sides and 3 corners. That's easy to remember — TRI means three! TRI-angle — three corners!"

Now rotate the triangle so a different side is on the bottom. "Look — I turned it. Is it still a triangle?" (Yes!) Flip it upside down so the point is at the bottom. "What about now?" (Still a triangle!) "That's right. No matter which way we turn it, it still has 3 sides and 3 corners, so it is STILL a triangle."

Show a tall, thin triangle and a short, wide triangle. "This triangle is tall and skinny. This triangle is short and wide. Are they both triangles?" (Yes!) "How do you know?" (Both have 3 sides and 3 corners.)

**Step 2: Introduce the rectangle.**
Hold up a large rectangle cutout (clearly longer than it is wide — not a square).

"Our second new shape is a RECTANGLE. Say it with me: RECTANGLE."

Point to each side. "Let's count: 1, 2, 3, 4. A rectangle has FOUR straight sides — just like a square!" Point to each corner. "And corners: 1, 2, 3, 4. FOUR corners — just like a square!"

"Hmm, 4 sides and 4 corners... that sounds like a square! So what makes a rectangle DIFFERENT from a square?"

Hold the rectangle next to a square cutout. Point to the rectangle. "Look at this side." (Point to a long side.) "And look at this side." (Point to a short side.) "Are they the same length?" (No — one is longer.) "In a rectangle, two sides are LONG and two sides are SHORT. The long sides are the same length as each other, and the short sides are the same length as each other. But the long sides and short sides are DIFFERENT lengths."

Point to the square. "In a square, ALL four sides are the same length. A square is actually a SPECIAL rectangle — one where all the sides happen to be equal! So every square is a rectangle, but not every rectangle is a square."

*Facilitator note: This is a subtle concept for kindergarteners. It is fine if the student simply understands that a rectangle can have sides of different lengths while a square has all sides the same. The formal subset relationship can be revisited in later grades.*

**Step 3: Show real-world examples.**
Hold up or point to a triangular object. "This yield sign is shaped like a triangle. Let's check: 1, 2, 3 sides. 1, 2, 3 corners. Triangle!"

Hold up a book or a door. "What shape is this book? Let's count: 1, 2, 3, 4 sides. Are two sides longer than the other two?" (Yes.) "It is a RECTANGLE!"

"Can you think of something shaped like a triangle?" (Slice of pizza, roof of a house, sandwich cut corner-to-corner.) "What about a rectangle?" (A door, a phone, a table top, an envelope.)

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Show a picture of a triangular road sign (yield sign). "What shape is this sign? Let's check. Count the sides with me: 1, 2, 3. Count the corners: 1, 2, 3. Three sides and three corners — it is a TRIANGLE!"

**Example B:** Hold up an envelope. "What shape is this? It has straight sides. Let's count: 1, 2, 3, 4. Four sides. Let's count corners: 1, 2, 3, 4. Four corners. Are all the sides the same?" (No — two are longer.) "So it is a RECTANGLE!"

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices identifying triangles and rectangles with facilitator support, including sorting and distinguishing from previously learned shapes.

**Activity 1: Shape identification and sorting (5 minutes)**

Spread out a mixed collection of shape cutouts: circles, squares, triangles, and rectangles (at least 3 of each, in various sizes and colors). Triangles should include different orientations.

"We now know FOUR shapes: circle, square, triangle, and rectangle. Can you sort all of these shapes into four groups?"

As the student works, ask questions:
- "How did you know that one is a triangle?" (It has 3 sides and 3 corners.)
- "This one has 4 sides. How did you decide it is a rectangle and not a square?" (The sides are not all the same — two are long and two are short.)
- "Is this triangle pointing up or pointing sideways? Is it still a triangle?" (Yes — orientation does not change the shape.)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student confuses a rectangle with a square, place them side by side and say: "Let's look carefully at the sides. In this one, are all the sides the same length?" Guide the student to notice the difference.*

*Facilitator prompt: If a student is unsure about a triangle that is "upside down" (point facing downward), say: "Let's count. How many sides? How many corners? If it has 3 and 3, it is a triangle, no matter which way it faces!"*

**Activity 2: Build shapes with craft sticks (5 minutes)**

Give the student craft sticks (or toothpicks).

"Can you build a triangle using craft sticks? How many sticks do you need?" (3.) The student builds a triangle. "Great! Count the sides and corners to check."

"Now build a rectangle. How many sticks do you need?" (4.) "Remember, a rectangle has two long sides and two short sides." If all sticks are the same length, say: "Your sticks are all the same length, so you actually built a square! That is a special rectangle. Can you try to make one where two sides are longer? You could break a stick or use two sticks for the long sides."

*Facilitator prompt: Building shapes with sticks reinforces the concrete understanding of "sides" as physical edges. Let the student experiment. If the shape does not close (sides do not connect), say: "Can you move the sticks so all the corners are touching? A shape must be closed — all the sides connect."*

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
1. The facilitator holds up a triangle cutout. "What shape is this?" (Answer: triangle)
2. The facilitator holds up a rectangle cutout. "What shape is this?" (Answer: rectangle)
3. "How many sides does a triangle have?" (Answer: 3)
4. "How many corners does a rectangle have?" (Answer: 4)
5. The facilitator places a triangle, a circle, and a square on the table. "Point to the triangle." (Answer: student points to the triangle)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. The facilitator shows a triangle rotated so the point faces down. "Is this a triangle? How do you know?" (Answer: yes — it has 3 sides and 3 corners)
2. The facilitator holds up a book. "What shape is this book? How is it different from a square?" (Answer: rectangle — it has 4 sides and 4 corners, but two sides are longer than the other two)
3. "Draw a triangle on your paper." (Answer: student draws a closed shape with 3 straight sides)
4. "Draw a rectangle on your paper." (Answer: student draws a closed shape with 4 sides, two longer than the other two)
5. The facilitator places a square and a rectangle side by side. "Which one is the rectangle? How can you tell?" (Answer: the one with sides that are not all the same length)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. "A triangle has 3 corners. A square has 4 corners. How many corners would they have all together?" (Answer: 7)
2. "Why is a square a special kind of rectangle?" (Answer: because a square has 4 sides and 4 corners like a rectangle, but all its sides are the same length)
3. "I am thinking of a shape. It has 3 sides and 3 corners, and one of its corners is pointing to the left. What shape is it?" (Answer: triangle)
4. "Draw a rectangle. Now draw a triangle on TOP of the rectangle to make a house shape." (Answer: student draws a rectangle with a triangle above it resembling a house)
5. "Can you name two things in this room that are rectangles?" (Answer: any two reasonable rectangular objects — door, book, table, screen, window, etc.)

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. The facilitator holds up a triangle cutout (in a non-standard orientation — point facing sideways). "What shape is this? How many sides does it have?" (Answer: triangle — 3 sides)
2. The facilitator holds up a rectangle cutout. "What shape is this? How is it different from a square?" (Answer: rectangle — two sides are longer than the other two, but a square has all sides the same)
3. "Name all four shapes we have learned so far." (Answer: circle, square, triangle, rectangle)

## Teacher Notes

### Common Errors
- **Thinking a rotated triangle is not a triangle:** Many young students have a fixed image of a triangle with the point on top and a flat bottom. Deliberately show triangles in multiple orientations throughout the lesson. Always redirect with: "Let's count. 3 sides and 3 corners? Then it is a triangle, no matter which way it faces."
- **Confusing rectangles and squares:** This is expected and developmentally appropriate. At this age, students often learn "square" first and may call all four-sided shapes squares. Patiently compare side lengths: "In a square, all sides are the same. In this rectangle, two sides are longer." Over time the distinction will solidify.
- **Calling any pointed shape a triangle:** Some students may see a star or arrow and call it a triangle because it has a point. Redirect by counting sides: "This shape has more than 3 sides, so it cannot be a triangle."
- **Difficulty building closed shapes with sticks:** Young students may leave gaps between sticks. Remind them: "A shape must be closed — all the sides connect at the corners, like a fence with no openings."

### Tips
- Follow the CRA progression: begin with real objects (concrete), then use cutouts and drawings (representational), then shape names alone (abstract). Spend the most time on concrete and representational stages.
- When comparing rectangles and squares, physically place one on top of the other. This makes the size difference of sides immediately visible.
- Use the word "corner" consistently rather than "vertex" at this stage. "Vertex" can be introduced in later grades. However, if a student hears "vertex" elsewhere, acknowledge it: "Vertex is a math word for corner — they mean the same thing!"
- Incorporate movement: "Stand up and make a triangle shape with your arms!" (Two arms and the top of the head form three points.) "Now make a rectangle — use your arms for the top and your legs for the sides!"

### Look-Fors
- Can the student name "triangle" and "rectangle" when shown each shape?
- Can the student state the number of sides and corners for each shape?
- Does the student recognize a triangle regardless of its orientation?
- Can the student articulate at least one difference between a rectangle and a square?
- Can the student find real-world examples of each shape?
