# Math — Kindergarten — Unit 4, Lesson 5

## Topic
Composing Shapes

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Combine two or more smaller shapes to create a new, larger shape (e.g., two triangles make a square)
- Identify the component shapes within a larger composed shape
- Create a picture or design using basic geometric shapes (circles, squares, triangles, rectangles)
- Describe a composed shape using the phrase "___ and ___ make a ___"

## Prerequisites
- Identifies and names circles, squares, triangles, and rectangles (Lessons 1-2)
- Describes shapes by attributes such as sides and corners (Lesson 3)
- Uses positional words to describe where shapes are placed (Lesson 4)

## Materials Needed
- Pattern blocks (triangles, squares, trapezoids, hexagons, rhombuses) or cardboard shape cutouts
- Pre-cut paper shapes: at least 8 small triangles, 6 small squares, 4 small rectangles, 4 circles in assorted colors
- Glue stick
- Large blank paper (at least 2 sheets)
- Crayons or markers
- A few printed or hand-drawn outline templates of larger shapes (a large square, a large rectangle, a large hexagon)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review positional words from Lesson 4 and basic shape recognition.

1. Place a triangle on the table. "What shape is this? How many sides? How many corners?" (Triangle; 3 sides; 3 corners) *(Lesson 3 review)*
2. Place a square next to the triangle. "Is the square BESIDE the triangle or ABOVE it?" (Beside) *(Lesson 4 review)*
3. Hold up a rectangle. "How is a rectangle different from a square?" (A rectangle can have two long sides and two short sides; a square has all sides the same length.) *(Lessons 2-3 review)*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the idea that smaller shapes can be put together to make bigger shapes, and that bigger shapes can be broken apart into smaller shapes.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Compose:** To put shapes together to make a new shape
- **Decompose:** To break a shape apart into smaller shapes
- **Pattern blocks:** Special shapes that fit together perfectly

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Two triangles make a square.**
Take two identical right triangles (or two equilateral triangle pattern blocks). Place them side by side on the table, separated.

"I have two triangles. Watch what happens when I slide them together." Slowly push the two triangles together along their longest sides so they form a square.

"Look! Two triangles made a SQUARE! Let's count the sides of our new shape: 1, 2, 3, 4. Four sides, all the same length. That is a square."

Separate them again. "When I pull them apart, I have two triangles again. When I push them together, I have a square. Two triangles COMPOSE a square."

**Step 2: Two squares make a rectangle.**
Take two identical small squares. Place them side by side.

"I have two squares. Let me push them together." Slide them so they share one side. "What shape did I make?"

Let the student examine it. "Let's count: 1, 2, 3, 4 sides. It has four corners. But look — these two sides are LONGER than these two sides. Is it a square?" (No.) "It is a RECTANGLE! Two squares composed a rectangle."

**Step 3: More compositions.**
"Let's try more combinations!"

- Place 4 triangles together to form a larger square. "Four triangles can make a BIGGER square!"
- Place 2 small rectangles side by side to make a larger square or a larger rectangle (depending on orientation). "Look — I can make different shapes by turning them different ways."
- Place 6 triangles around a center point to form a hexagon (if using pattern blocks). "Six triangles make a new shape called a hexagon."

"When we PUT SHAPES TOGETHER to make a new shape, that is called COMPOSING. We are shape builders!"

**Step 4: Finding shapes inside shapes.**
Draw a rectangle on paper. Draw a line down the middle. "How many shapes do you see inside this rectangle?" (Two squares, or two smaller rectangles.)

Draw a square. Draw a diagonal line from corner to corner. "How many shapes now?" (Two triangles.)

"Big shapes are made of smaller shapes hiding inside!"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Place two right triangles together with their longest sides touching.
- "What shape did we make?" (A square.)
- "How many triangles did we use?" (Two.)
- "Two triangles make one square."

**Example B:** Place three squares in a row, side by side.
- "What shape did we make?" (A long rectangle.)
- "How many squares did we use?" (Three.)
- "Three squares in a row compose a rectangle."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices composing shapes with hands-on materials.

**Activity 1: Build the target shape (5 minutes)**
Give the student pattern blocks or cut-out shapes. Show an outline of a larger shape and ask them to fill it in.

- "Use triangles to fill this square." (Student places 2 triangles inside the square outline.)
  *Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles to fit the triangles, say: "Try turning the triangle. Does it fit better this way? Slide it until the edges line up."*
- "Use squares to fill this rectangle." (Student places 2 squares side by side inside the rectangle outline.)
- "Can you fill this big triangle using smaller triangles?" (Student uses 4 small triangles to fill a large triangle outline.)

For each one, ask: "How many ___ did you use? What shape did they make together?"

**Activity 2: Free building (5 minutes)**
"Now YOU get to be the shape builder. Take any shapes you want and put them together. Tell me what new shape you made."

Let the student experiment freely. After each creation, ask:
- "What shapes did you use?"
- "What shape did you make?"
- "Can you make the SAME shape using DIFFERENT pieces?"

*Facilitator prompt: If the student places shapes randomly without composing, model one composition first: "Watch — I'll put these two triangles together. Now they make a square! Your turn. Can you put two shapes together to make something new?"*

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
1. Place two triangles together. "What shape did you make?" (A square or a rectangle, depending on triangle type.)
2. Place two squares together. "What shape did you make?" (A rectangle.)
3. Look at this rectangle (draw one with a line in the middle). "How many squares are hiding inside?" (2)
4. "Put a triangle and a square side by side. Is the new shape a circle, a rectangle, or a new shape?" (A new shape — it is a pentagon/house shape.)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. "Use 4 triangles to build a big square." (Student arranges 4 right triangles into a square.)
2. "Use 3 squares to build a rectangle. How long is your rectangle — how many squares long?" (3 squares long.)
3. Draw a square with both diagonals drawn. "How many triangles are hiding inside this square?" (4)
4. "Can you use 2 rectangles to make a square? Show me." (Student places 2 identical rectangles side by side or stacked.)
5. "I made a shape with 2 triangles. It has 4 sides and 4 corners. What shape did I make?" (A square or a rectangle.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. "Use 6 triangles to build a hexagon." (Student arranges 6 equilateral triangles around a center point.)
2. "Build a rectangle using ONLY triangles. How many triangles did you need?" (At least 2; the student may use 4.)
3. "Can you make TWO different shapes using exactly 3 triangles? Show me both." (Possible answers: a trapezoid and a different arrangement.)
4. "Build a house shape using a square and a triangle. How many sides does your house have?" (5 sides — a pentagon.)
5. "Take apart a hexagon pattern block. How many triangles fit inside?" (6)

---

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. Give the student two triangles. "Put these together to make a new shape. What shape did you make?" (A square or rectangle.)
2. Show a rectangle divided into two squares by a line. "What shapes are hiding inside this rectangle?" (Two squares.)
3. "When we put shapes together to make a new shape, what is that called?" (Composing — accept "building" or "putting together.")

---

## Answer Key

**Tier 1 (Approaching):**
1. A square (with right triangles) or a larger triangle / parallelogram (depending on arrangement)
2. A rectangle
3. 2 squares
4. A new shape (pentagon / house shape)

**Tier 2 (On Level):**
1. Student builds a large square from 4 right triangles
2. A rectangle that is 3 squares long and 1 square tall
3. 4 triangles
4. Student shows 2 identical rectangles forming a square
5. A square or a rectangle

**Tier 3 (Advanced):**
1. Student builds a hexagon from 6 equilateral triangles
2. At least 2 triangles (4 if using right triangles for a more standard rectangle)
3. Answers vary — e.g., a trapezoid and a parallelogram
4. 5 sides (a pentagon)
5. 6 triangles

**Exit Ticket:**
1. A square or rectangle (depending on triangle type)
2. Two squares
3. Composing (or "putting together" / "building")

## Teacher Notes

**Common Errors:**
- **Gaps and overlaps:** Students may leave spaces between shapes or overlap them. Encourage them to "make the edges kiss" — sides should touch perfectly with no gaps. Physically guide the student's hand to slide pieces flush together.
- **Not recognizing the new shape:** The student may push two triangles together but not realize they have made a square. Trace the outside edge of the composed shape with your finger: "Let's trace around the outside. How many sides? What shape has that many sides?"
- **Orientation confusion:** A student may not see that a rotated square is still a square, or that a "diamond" arrangement of two triangles is a square turned 45 degrees. Rotate the composed shape and confirm: "Is it still a square if I turn it? Yes!"

**Tips for Success:**
- Use pattern blocks if available — they are precision-cut to fit together perfectly, which reduces frustration. If using paper cutouts, pre-cut them carefully so edges align.
- Keep the first ten-frame / ten composition simple: 2 shapes making 1 new shape. Only after the student shows confidence should you move to 3, 4, or 6 shapes composing.
- The CRA progression in this lesson is mostly Concrete (physical blocks) and Representational (tracing outlines, drawing shapes inside shapes). The Abstract level (describing compositions verbally) is a stretch goal.

**Look-Fors:**
- Can the student fill a target outline with smaller shapes without gaps?
- Does the student name the composed shape correctly?
- Can the student describe the composition: "Two triangles make a square"?
- Does the student show interest in free exploration and try multiple combinations?

**Connection to Future Learning:**
Composing and decomposing shapes prepares students for understanding fractions (a whole can be split into parts), area (a rectangle can be tiled with unit squares), and transformational geometry (rotating and flipping shapes to make them fit).
