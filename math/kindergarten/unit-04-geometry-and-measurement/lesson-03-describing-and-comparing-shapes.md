# Math — Kindergarten — Unit 4, Lesson 3

## Topic
Describing and Comparing Shapes

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Describe shapes by their number of sides and corners
- Compare two shapes and tell how they are alike and how they are different
- Sort shapes by attributes (e.g., "has 4 sides" vs. "has 3 sides," "has corners" vs. "no corners")
- Use precise language to describe and identify a shape from a verbal description

## Prerequisites
- Identifies circles, squares, triangles, and rectangles by name (Unit 4, Lessons 1-2)
- Knows that shapes have properties: sides and corners (Unit 4, Lessons 1-2)
- Distinguishes rectangles from squares by side length (Unit 4, Lesson 2)

## Materials Needed
- Cutout shapes: at least 3 each of circles, squares, triangles, and rectangles in assorted sizes and colors
- A large piece of paper or two hula hoops for Venn diagram sorting
- Shape attribute cards (cards that say "3 sides," "4 sides," "0 corners," "4 corners," "round," "straight sides")
- Crayons or markers
- Paper
- Index cards for shape riddles

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review all four shape names and activate knowledge of shape properties.

1. Hold up shape flashcards one at a time (circle, triangle, square, rectangle) in random order. "Name this shape as fast as you can!" Student names each shape. *(Lessons 1-2 review)*
2. "How many sides does a triangle have?" (Answer: 3) "How many corners does a rectangle have?" (Answer: 4) *(Lessons 1-2 review)*
3. "What is 6 + 2?" Student may use fingers or objects. (Answer: 8) *(Unit 2 review)*

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to describe shapes using specific attributes and to compare shapes systematically by finding similarities and differences.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Attribute:** A feature or characteristic of a shape — such as the number of sides, the number of corners, or whether the sides are straight or curved
- **Describe:** To tell about a shape using words — what it looks like, how many sides and corners it has
- **Compare:** To look at two shapes and tell how they are the SAME and how they are DIFFERENT
- **Sort:** To put shapes into groups based on a shared attribute
- **Curved:** A line that bends smoothly, like the edge of a circle
- **Straight:** A line that does not bend, like the side of a square

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Model describing a shape with precise language.**
Hold up a triangle cutout. "I am going to DESCRIBE this shape using math words. Listen carefully."

"This shape has 3 straight sides. It has 3 corners. All of its sides are straight — none of them are curved. This shape is a... TRIANGLE!"

Hold up a circle. "Now let me describe this one. This shape has NO straight sides. It has NO corners. Its edge is curved — it goes round and round. This shape is a... CIRCLE!"

"When we DESCRIBE a shape, we talk about: How many SIDES does it have? How many CORNERS? Are the sides STRAIGHT or CURVED? This helps us tell shapes apart."

**Step 2: Model comparing two shapes.**
Hold up a square and a triangle side by side.

"Now let's COMPARE these two shapes. Comparing means we find what is the SAME and what is DIFFERENT."

"How are they the SAME?" Pause and let the student think. Guide if needed: "Do they both have straight sides?" (Yes!) "So one thing that is the SAME is: they both have straight sides."

"How are they DIFFERENT?" Pause. "How many sides does the square have?" (4.) "How many does the triangle have?" (3.) "So one DIFFERENCE is: the square has 4 sides and the triangle has 3."

"How many corners does the square have?" (4.) "The triangle?" (3.) "Another difference: the square has 4 corners and the triangle has 3."

Write or draw a simple comparison on the table or whiteboard:

```
Square and Triangle
SAME: Both have straight sides
DIFFERENT: Square has 4 sides, Triangle has 3 sides
           Square has 4 corners, Triangle has 3 corners
```

**Step 3: Compare a circle and a square.**
Hold up a circle and a square.

"Let's compare these two. How are they DIFFERENT?"

"The circle has NO straight sides — it is curved. The square has 4 straight sides. The circle has NO corners. The square has 4 corners. They are very different shapes!"

"Is there ANYTHING the same?" (Both are shapes. Both are flat. Both are closed — the lines connect all the way around.) "Good thinking! Even very different shapes can have SOMETHING in common."

**Step 4: Introduce sorting by attribute.**
Spread all the cutout shapes on the table.

"Now let's SORT our shapes. Sorting means putting shapes into groups. I am going to sort them by the number of SIDES."

Make three groups: shapes with 0 straight sides (circles), shapes with 3 sides (triangles), and shapes with 4 sides (squares and rectangles).

"Look — the circles are alone because they have no straight sides. The triangles are together because they all have 3 sides. And the squares AND rectangles are in the same group because they BOTH have 4 sides! Even though they look a little different, they share the same attribute: 4 sides."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Hold up a rectangle and a triangle. "Let's compare. The rectangle has 4 sides and 4 corners. The triangle has 3 sides and 3 corners. They are ALIKE because they both have straight sides. They are DIFFERENT because they have different numbers of sides and corners."

**Example B:** Hold up a circle and a triangle. "The circle has 0 straight sides and 0 corners — it is curved. The triangle has 3 straight sides and 3 corners. They are DIFFERENT in almost every way! But they are ALIKE because they are both flat shapes and both are closed."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices describing, comparing, and sorting shapes with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Venn diagram sorting (5 minutes)**

Set up a Venn diagram on the table using two overlapping circles drawn on a large sheet of paper (or two overlapping hula hoops).

Label one circle "Has corners" and the other circle "Has 4 sides."

Give the student the collection of shape cutouts. "Put each shape where it belongs."

- Circles go OUTSIDE both circles (no corners, not 4 sides).
- Triangles go in the "Has corners" circle only (has corners, but only 3 sides, not 4).
- Squares and rectangles go in the OVERLAPPING section (has corners AND has 4 sides).

*Facilitator prompt: If the student is unsure where to place a shape, ask: "Does it have corners?" If yes, it goes in the 'Has corners' circle. "Does it have 4 sides?" If yes, it also goes in the 'Has 4 sides' circle. "If it belongs in BOTH, it goes in the middle where the circles overlap."*

*Facilitator prompt: If the concept of overlapping regions is too difficult, simplify to a two-group sort: "Shapes with corners" and "Shapes without corners." The student should place circles on one side and all other shapes on the other.*

**Activity 2: Shape riddles (5 minutes)**

"I am going to give you CLUES about a shape. You guess what it is!"

Riddle 1: "I have 3 sides and 3 corners. All my sides are straight. What am I?" (Triangle!)

Riddle 2: "I have no sides and no corners. I am round. What am I?" (Circle!)

Riddle 3: "I have 4 sides and 4 corners. Two of my sides are longer than the other two. What am I?" (Rectangle!)

Riddle 4: "I have 4 sides and 4 corners. All of my sides are the same length. What am I?" (Square!)

"Now it is YOUR turn! Pick a shape and give ME clues. Don't tell me the name — just describe it, and I will guess!"

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles to create a riddle, coach them: "Tell me how many sides it has. Tell me how many corners. Are the sides straight or curved?" Celebrate any attempt at using descriptive language.*

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
1. "How many sides does a triangle have?" (Answer: 3)
2. "How many corners does a square have?" (Answer: 4)
3. "Does a circle have straight sides or curved sides?" (Answer: curved)
4. The facilitator holds up a rectangle and a triangle. "Which shape has more corners?" (Answer: the rectangle — 4 corners vs. 3)
5. The facilitator shows a group of shapes. "Put all the shapes that have 4 sides in one pile." (Answer: student separates squares and rectangles from circles and triangles)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. "Describe a circle. Tell me about its sides and corners." (Answer: A circle has no straight sides and no corners. It is round/curved.)
2. "How are a square and a rectangle ALIKE?" (Answer: Both have 4 sides and 4 corners. Both have straight sides.)
3. "How are a square and a rectangle DIFFERENT?" (Answer: A square has all sides the same length. A rectangle has two long sides and two short sides.)
4. "I have 3 corners and 3 straight sides. What shape am I?" (Answer: triangle)
5. Sort these shapes into two groups — "Has corners" and "No corners": circle, triangle, square, rectangle, circle. (Answer: "Has corners" = triangle, square, rectangle; "No corners" = circle, circle)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. "How are a triangle and a circle ALIKE? How are they DIFFERENT?" (Answer: Alike — both are shapes, both are closed/flat. Different — triangle has 3 straight sides and 3 corners; circle has no straight sides and no corners.)
2. "If I put a square, a rectangle, and a triangle in one group, what attribute do they all share?" (Answer: they all have straight sides; or they all have corners)
3. "Make up a riddle for a rectangle. Give at least 2 clues." (Answer: e.g., "I have 4 sides and 4 corners. Two of my sides are longer than the other two.")
4. "A shape has 4 corners. Could it be a triangle? Why or why not?" (Answer: No — a triangle has only 3 corners. A shape with 4 corners is a square or rectangle.)
5. "Draw a shape that has NO corners. Now draw a shape that has EXACTLY 3 corners. Label each shape." (Answer: student draws a circle and a triangle, labeling each)

---

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. "Describe a triangle using the words 'sides' and 'corners.'" (Answer: A triangle has 3 sides and 3 corners.)
2. "Tell me one way a circle and a square are DIFFERENT." (Answer: A circle has no corners and no straight sides; a square has 4 corners and 4 straight sides — or any correct difference.)
3. "I have 4 straight sides, 4 corners, and all my sides are the same length. What shape am I?" (Answer: square)

## Teacher Notes

### Common Errors
- **Describing shapes by color or size instead of geometric attributes:** A student may say "the big one" or "the red one" instead of describing sides and corners. Gently redirect: "Those are good observations, but let's use MATH words. How many sides does it have? How many corners?" Over time, the student will internalize that geometric descriptions focus on sides, corners, and curves.
- **Difficulty with Venn diagram overlap:** The overlapping region is an abstract concept. If it is too confusing, simplify to a two-category sort (shapes with corners vs. shapes without corners). You can revisit the Venn diagram in a future lesson.
- **Saying shapes are "the same" because they are the same color:** Students may focus on surface features. Redirect: "They are both blue, yes. But what about their sides and corners — are those the same?" Help the student see that shape attributes are independent of color, size, and orientation.
- **Forgetting that squares and rectangles are in the same family:** When sorting by "4 sides," students may forget to group squares with rectangles. Remind them: "Let's count. How many sides does a square have? Four! So it goes in the '4 sides' group too."

### Tips
- Use the CRA progression: start with physical shape cutouts the student can touch and move (concrete), use drawings and diagrams (representational), and finish with verbal descriptions without visuals (abstract). The riddle activity is a great bridge to the abstract level.
- The Venn diagram activity is a powerful thinking tool. Even if the overlapping concept is new, the physical act of placing shapes into regions builds classification skills that extend well beyond geometry.
- Encourage the student to use complete sentences: "A triangle has 3 sides and 3 corners" rather than just "3." This builds mathematical communication and prepares the student for explaining thinking in later grades.
- If time allows, let the student create their own Venn diagram with categories they choose (e.g., "big shapes" vs. "shapes with 3 sides"). This deepens understanding of sorting by attributes.

### Look-Fors
- Can the student describe any of the four shapes using the words "sides" and "corners"?
- When comparing two shapes, can the student name at least one similarity and one difference?
- Can the student sort shapes correctly by a given attribute (e.g., number of sides)?
- Can the student solve a shape riddle by listening to attribute clues?
- Is the student beginning to use geometric vocabulary independently (sides, corners, straight, curved)?
