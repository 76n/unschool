# Math — Kindergarten — Unit 1, Lesson 6

## Topic
Comparing Groups — More, Fewer, and the Same

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare two groups of objects (up to 10 each) and determine which has more, which has fewer, or if they are the same
- Use the words "more," "fewer" (or "less"), and "the same" correctly
- Use one-to-one matching and counting strategies to compare groups

## Prerequisites
- Can count objects up to 10 with one-to-one correspondence (Lessons 1-3)
- Can read numerals 0-10 (Lessons 4-5)
- Understands cardinality (Lesson 3)

## Materials Needed
- 20 counting objects in two different types (e.g., 10 red blocks and 10 blue blocks; or buttons and beans)
- Two small plates or bowls
- Number cards 0-10
- Paper and pencil

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Cumulative review of all Unit 1 skills.

1. Flash number cards: show 3, 7, 10, 5 in random order. Student names each. *(Review: Lessons 4-5)*
2. Place 9 objects. "Count these and tell me how many." (Answer: 9) *(Review: Lessons 2-3)*
3. "Write the number 6." *(Review: Lesson 5)*
4. "What number comes after 8?" (Answer: 9) "What number comes before 4?" (Answer: 3 — new challenge) *(Review: Lesson 1)*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce comparison of two groups using matching and counting.

### Key Vocabulary
- **More:** A larger quantity — the group with extra objects
- **Fewer:** A smaller quantity — the group with objects left over
- **The same:** Both groups have equal quantities — nothing extra, nothing missing
- **Compare:** To look at two groups and decide which has more, fewer, or the same

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce with a concrete scenario.**
Place 3 red blocks on one plate and 5 blue blocks on another plate.

Say: "I have two groups of blocks. Let's figure out which group has more. First, let me count."

Count the red blocks: "1, 2, 3. Three red blocks."
Count the blue blocks: "1, 2, 3, 4, 5. Five blue blocks."

"3 and 5. Which number is bigger? 5! So the blue group has MORE. The red group has FEWER."

**Step 2: Introduce one-to-one matching.**
Line up the 3 red blocks in a row. Line up the 5 blue blocks in a row directly below them, aligned one-to-one:

```
Red:   ■  ■  ■
Blue:  ■  ■  ■  ■  ■
```

"Look — each red block has a blue partner. But the blue group has 2 extra. The blue group has MORE because it has extras. The red group has FEWER because it runs out."

**Step 3: Show "the same."**
Place 4 red blocks and 4 blue blocks. Line them up:

```
Red:   ■  ■  ■  ■
Blue:  ■  ■  ■  ■
```

"Every red block has a blue partner. No extras. They have THE SAME amount — 4 and 4."

**Step 4: Establish two strategies.**
"We have two ways to compare:
1. **Count and compare the numbers.** Count each group and see which number is bigger.
2. **Line up and match.** Put them in rows and see which has extras."

Both work! Counting is faster once you're good at it. Matching is more visual.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Group A has 6 beans. Group B has 4 beans.
Count: "6 and 4. 6 is more than 4. Group A has more. Group B has fewer."

**Example 2:** Group A has 5 buttons. Group B has 5 buttons.
Line them up — every button matches. "They are the same!"

**Example 3:** Group A has 2 crayons. Group B has 8 crayons.
"2 and 8. 8 is more than 2. Group B has WAY more."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices comparing groups with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Which has more? (4 minutes)**
Set up pairs of groups for the student to compare:

- 4 red vs. 6 blue → "Which has more?" (blue) "Which has fewer?" (red)
- 7 red vs. 3 blue → "Which has more?" (red) "How do you know?" (Student explains: counted, or 7 is bigger than 3)
- 5 red vs. 5 blue → "Which has more?" (neither — they're the same!)

*Facilitator prompt: Always ask "How do you know?" This builds reasoning, not just guessing. Accept both counting and matching as valid strategies.*

**Activity 2: Build the group (3 minutes)**
Place 4 objects on a plate. Say: "Can you make a group that has MORE than 4?"

The student creates a group of 5, 6, 7, etc. Then: "Can you make a group that has FEWER than 4?" (1, 2, or 3.) "Can you make a group that has THE SAME as 4?" (4.)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student makes a "more" group of 5 (just one more), that's correct. If they always make much larger groups, challenge them: "Can you make a group that has just a LITTLE more?"*

**Activity 3: Number card comparison (3 minutes)**
Draw two number cards at random. The student reads both numbers and says which is more and which is fewer. Use the number line to verify: "The number farther along the number line is more."

Examples: 3 vs. 7 → 7 is more. 9 vs. 2 → 9 is more. 5 vs. 5 → the same.

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student compares groups independently.

**★ On-level:**
1. Group A: 3 objects. Group B: 6 objects. Which has more? ___. Which has fewer? ___.
2. Group A: 5 objects. Group B: 5 objects. More, fewer, or the same? ___.
3. Group A: 8 objects. Group B: 4 objects. Which has more? ___.

**★★ Stretch:**
4. Group A: 7 objects. Group B: 9 objects. Which has fewer? ___. How many fewer? ___.
5. "I have 6 blocks. Show me a group that has fewer." (Student builds a group of 1-5.)
6. Draw number cards 4 and 8. "Which number is more? Write both numbers."

**★★★ Challenge:**
7. Group A: 10 objects. Group B: 10 objects, but spread very far apart. "Which has more?" (The same — arrangement doesn't change quantity. Ties back to Lesson 3.)
8. "I have 5 objects. How many more do I need to have 8?" (Answer: 3) *(Preview of addition thinking)*

### Interleaved Review
- "Count from 1 to 10 backward." (Lesson 1 extension)
- Show the numeral 9. "Write this number." (Lesson 5 review)
- Place 7 objects in a row, then scatter them. "How many?" (Lesson 3 review)

---

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess comparison skills and vocabulary.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Group A: 4 objects. Group B: 7 objects. "Which group has more?" (Answer: Group B)
2. Group A: 6 objects. Group B: 6 objects. "Do these groups have more, fewer, or the same?" (Answer: the same)
3. Show number cards 3 and 8. "Which number is more? Which is fewer?" (Answer: 8 is more, 3 is fewer)

**Mastery = Student correctly answers all 3 items, using the words "more," "fewer," and/or "the same" appropriately.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice with very small groups first (1 vs. 3, 2 vs. 4) where the difference is visually obvious.
- Use the one-to-one matching strategy (lining up) rather than number comparison — it's more concrete.
- Check that the student isn't confusing visual spread with quantity (Lesson 3 conservation issue). If so, always line groups up before comparing.
- Practice comparison language in daily life: "You have 3 crackers, your sister has 5. Who has more?"

### If Mastery Is Met
Proceed to the Unit Assessment. The student is ready to demonstrate mastery of all Unit 1 skills.

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Estimation before counting:** Show two groups and ask "Which do you THINK has more?" before counting. Then count to check. Builds estimation skills and number intuition.
- **Comparison stories:** "There are 4 birds on a branch and 6 birds on the ground. Where are more birds?" Connects comparison to real-world scenarios.
- **Build equal groups:** "Can you make two groups that both have exactly 7?" Extends "the same" understanding.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**"Fewer" vs. "less."** In everyday English, people use "less" more often than "fewer." Both are acceptable from the student. If you want to build precise mathematical language, model "fewer" for countable objects ("fewer blocks") and "less" for continuous quantities ("less water"). But don't correct the student harshly — understanding the concept matters more than the vocabulary at this stage.

**Spatial arrangement trips up young students.** A group of 4 objects spread far apart can look like "more" than a tight group of 6. If you see this confusion, always use the line-up matching strategy. Counting both groups and comparing the numbers also helps, but the visual matching is more concrete.

**This lesson previews addition and subtraction.** When a student notices "the blue group has 2 extra," they are doing early subtraction. When they say "I need 3 more to make 8," they are doing early addition. Celebrate this thinking but don't push formal arithmetic yet.

**Language matters for all comparison activities.** Always use complete comparison sentences: "7 is more than 3" — not just "7 is more." The relational language ("more than," "fewer than") is the foundation for later work with inequality symbols (> and <).
