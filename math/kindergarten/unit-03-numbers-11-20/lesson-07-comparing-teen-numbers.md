# Math — Kindergarten — Unit 3, Lesson 7

## Topic
Comparing Teen Numbers (More and Fewer with 11-20)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare two quantities of 11-20 and identify which is more and which is fewer
- Use ten-frames to visually compare teen numbers
- Explain why one teen number is greater than another by comparing the ones (when both have one group of ten)

## Prerequisites
- Compares groups to 10 using "more" and "fewer" (Unit 1, Lesson 6)
- Understands teen numbers as 10 + ones (Unit 3, Lessons 3-4)
- Orders numbers 11-20 (Unit 3, Lesson 6)

## Materials Needed
- 20+ counting objects in two colors (e.g., 20 red and 20 blue)
- Four ten-frames (two per number being compared) and counters
- Number cards 11-20
- A number line 1-20
- Paper and pencil

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review comparing quantities to 10 and teen number structure.

1. Place 4 objects on one plate and 7 on another. "Which group has more?" (Answer: 7) "Which has fewer?" (Answer: 4) *(Unit 1 review)*
2. "What is 10 + 6?" (Answer: 16) *(Lesson 3 review)*
3. "What number comes after 18?" (Answer: 19) *(Lesson 6 review)*

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach comparison of teen numbers using ten-frames and the ones place.

### Key Vocabulary
- **More:** A bigger quantity (there are more objects in this group)
- **Fewer:** A smaller quantity (there are fewer objects in this group)
- **Greater:** A bigger number (15 is greater than 12)
- **Less:** A smaller number (12 is less than 15)
- **Compare:** To look at two numbers or groups and decide which is bigger or smaller

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Compare teen numbers with objects.**
Make two groups: 13 red objects and 16 blue objects.

"This group has 13 red objects. This group has 16 blue objects. Which group has MORE?"

If the student is unsure, count both groups. "13 and 16. Sixteen is MORE than thirteen. Thirteen is FEWER than sixteen."

**Step 2: Use ten-frames to see why.**
Set up two pairs of ten-frames side by side:

Left pair (showing 13): Full ten-frame (10) + 3 on the second ten-frame.
Right pair (showing 16): Full ten-frame (10) + 6 on the second ten-frame.

"Look at both. The first ten-frame is FULL for both numbers. They each have one group of ten. So the ten part is the SAME."

"But look at the extras! 13 has 3 extras. 16 has 6 extras. Six extras is MORE than three extras. So 16 is more than 13!"

**Step 3: State the key insight.**
"When we compare teen numbers, both have a group of ten. The tens are the same! So we just need to compare the ONES — the extras. Whichever has more extras is the bigger number."

**Step 4: Practice with another pair.**
Show 14 and 18 on ten-frames.

"Both have a full ten. Fourteen has 4 extras. Eighteen has 8 extras. Which is more?" (18) "Which is fewer?" (14)

"We can also use the number line." Point to 14 and 18 on the number line. "Numbers that are further to the right are bigger. Eighteen is to the right of fourteen, so 18 is greater."

### Worked Examples

**Example A: Compare 12 and 15.**
- 12 on ten-frames: full ten-frame + 2 extras.
- 15 on ten-frames: full ten-frame + 5 extras.
- Both have one group of 10. Compare the ones: 5 > 2.
- "Fifteen is more than twelve. Twelve is fewer than fifteen."

**Example B: Compare 17 and 13.**
- 17 on ten-frames: full ten-frame + 7 extras.
- 13 on ten-frames: full ten-frame + 3 extras.
- Both have one group of 10. Compare the ones: 7 > 3.
- "Seventeen is more than thirteen."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student compares teen numbers using ten-frames with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Ten-frame compare (5 minutes)**

Build two teen numbers on ten-frames. Student identifies which is more and which is fewer.

- Show 14 and 19. "Which is more?" (19) "Which is fewer?" (14) *Ask: "How do you know?" (19 has more extras — 9 versus 4)*
- Show 16 and 11. "Which is more?" (16) "Which is fewer?" (11) *Ask: "How many extras does each have?" (6 and 1)*
- Show 15 and 15. "Which is more?" (Neither — they are the SAME!) *Say: "When both numbers are the same, we say they are EQUAL."*

*Facilitator prompt: If the student just guesses without looking at the ten-frames, redirect: "Look at the extras. Count them for each number. Which has more extras?"*

**Activity 2: Number line compare (5 minutes)**

Give the student a number line 1-20. Say two numbers. Student points to each on the number line and says which is more.

- "13 and 17. Which is more?" (17 — it's further right.) "Which is fewer?" (13)
- "19 and 12." (19 is more; 12 is fewer.)
- "15 and 18." (18 is more; 15 is fewer.)

*Facilitator prompt: "Remember — on the number line, numbers get bigger as you go to the right. The one further right is more."*

---

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### On-Level
1. Which is more: 14 or 17? (Answer: 17)
2. Which is fewer: 19 or 13? (Answer: 13)
3. Build 12 and 16 on ten-frames. Which has more? (Answer: 16)
4. Build 18 and 15 on ten-frames. Which has fewer? (Answer: 15)

### Stretch
5. "Put these numbers in order from fewest to most: 14, 11, 18." (Answer: 11, 14, 18)
6. "I have 13 crayons. My friend has 17 crayons. Who has more? How many more?" (Answer: friend has more; 4 more)

### Challenge
7. "Think of a number that is more than 15 but fewer than 18." (Answer: 16 or 17)
8. "What is the biggest teen number?" (Answer: 19) "How do you know?" (It has the most extras — 9.)

### Interleaved Review
*(From prior units/lessons)*
- "Write the number seventeen." (Answer: 17) *(Lesson 5 review)*
- "What is 8 - 5?" (Answer: 3) *(Unit 2 review)*

---

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

1. "Which is more: 14 or 18?" (Answer: 18)
2. Build 16 and 12 on ten-frames. "Which is fewer? How do you know?" (Answer: 12 is fewer; it has only 2 extras compared to 6.)

**Scoring:**
- 2/2 correct = Mastery
- 1/2 correct = Approaching mastery
- 0/2 correct = Needs reteaching

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to comparing groups within 10 (Unit 1) and make sure that foundation is solid.
- Always build both numbers on ten-frames before comparing. The visual difference in the extras makes the comparison concrete.
- Practice with numbers that are far apart first (11 vs. 19), then move to numbers that are close (14 vs. 16).

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 8 (Counting by Tens to 100). Preview: "You know everything about numbers 11-20 now! Next time, we are going to count by TENS and go all the way to 100!"

---

## Extension (Optional)
- **Comparison stories:** "There are 12 red apples and 18 green apples. Which color has more apples? How many more?" (Green; 6 more.)
- **Greatest and least:** Give the student five number cards (e.g., 11, 14, 17, 13, 19). "Find the greatest number. Find the least number. Put them all in order."
- **Compare 20:** "Is 20 more or fewer than 17? Is 20 a teen number?" (More; no — 20 is twenty, not a teen number. It has two groups of ten.)

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Thinking a number is bigger because of the ones digit without considering the tens:** This is not an issue within 11-19 (all have one ten), but watch for it when comparing numbers like 19 and 20. The student may think 19 is bigger because "9 is bigger than 0." Use ten-frames: 20 fills two complete ten-frames, while 19 has a gap.
- **Confusing "more" and "fewer":** Some students understand which group is bigger but mix up the vocabulary. Practice with objects first: point to the bigger group and say "MORE," point to the smaller group and say "FEWER."
- **Difficulty comparing numbers that are close (e.g., 14 vs. 15):** The ten-frames make this visible. 14 has 4 extras; 15 has 5 extras. "Five is one more than four, so 15 is just a LITTLE bit more than 14."

### Pacing Advice
- Most kindergarteners will grasp the basic "which is more" comparison quickly if they already compare within 10. The new piece is comparing the ones when the tens are the same.
- Spend more time on Activity 1 (ten-frames) than Activity 2 (number line). The ten-frame comparison builds deeper understanding of why one teen number is greater than another.
- If time permits, the Stretch problem 6 ("how many more") is a valuable extension that connects comparison to subtraction thinking.
