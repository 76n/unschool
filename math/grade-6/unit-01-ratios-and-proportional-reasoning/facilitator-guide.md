# Facilitator Guide — Grade 6 Math, Unit 1: Ratios and Proportional Reasoning

**Grade:** 6
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 1 — Ratios and Proportional Reasoning
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided (facilitator leads instruction; student works independently for practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit introduces students to the foundational concepts of ratios, rates, and proportional reasoning. Students begin by understanding what a ratio is and how to express it in multiple forms, then build toward working with equivalent ratios, ratio tables, and double number lines as tools for organizing and solving problems. The second half of the unit focuses on rates and unit rates, including real-world applications such as comparing prices, speeds, and other measurable quantities. The unit culminates with multi-step word problems that require students to select and apply the most appropriate strategy.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Define a ratio and identify ratios in real-world contexts.
- Write ratios in three forms: a:b, a to b, and a/b.
- Distinguish between part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios.
- Generate equivalent ratios by multiplying or dividing both terms by the same number.
- Use ratio tables to organize equivalent ratios and find missing values.
- Represent ratios on double number lines and use them to solve problems.
- Define rate and unit rate, and calculate unit rates from real-world situations.
- Compare rates using unit rates to make decisions (e.g., best buy, fastest speed).
- Apply ratio and rate reasoning to solve multi-step word problems, including recipes, scale drawings, and proportional situations.

**Why this matters:** Proportional reasoning is one of the most important mathematical ideas students will encounter. It underpins percentage calculations, scale drawings, probability, linear functions, and virtually every branch of science. Students who develop strong ratio and rate intuition in Grade 6 are better prepared for algebraic thinking in Grades 7 and 8, and they gain practical skills they will use daily — from comparing grocery prices to understanding speed limits to scaling recipes for a family dinner.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, verify that the student can:

- [ ] Multiply whole numbers fluently (e.g., 6 x 8, 12 x 5).
- [ ] Divide whole numbers with and without remainders (e.g., 56 / 7, 45 / 6).
- [ ] Understand fractions as division (e.g., 3/4 means 3 divided by 4).
- [ ] Simplify fractions by finding common factors (e.g., 6/8 = 3/4).
- [ ] Read and interpret simple word problems, identifying the quantities involved.
- [ ] Plot values on a number line accurately.

If any prerequisite is shaky, spend a brief review session on that skill before starting Lesson 1. The interleaved review problems in each lesson also revisit Grade 5 fraction and decimal skills, which provides ongoing reinforcement.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- Whiteboard and markers (or projector/screen for digital instruction).
- Counters or small objects in two colors (at least 20 of each color) — used heavily in Lessons 1-3.
- Printed student worksheets for each lesson (Guided Practice and Independent Practice sections).
- Index cards or half-sheets of paper for Exit Tickets.
- Rulers (for double number line diagrams in Lesson 5).
- Graph paper (optional but helpful for ratio tables and number lines).
- Colored pencils or markers (two colors, for double number lines).
- Grocery flyers or printed price lists (for Lessons 7 and 8, rate and unit rate activities).
- Calculator (optional; allow for checking work on multi-step problems in Lessons 9-10, but require mental/written computation for core ratio work).
- Paper and pencil for all lessons and the unit assessment.

**Adjusting the pace:**

- If the student grasps Lessons 1 and 2 quickly, they can be combined into a single session since both focus on ratio notation.
- Lessons 3 and 5 are the most conceptually demanding in the first half; allow extra time if the student struggles with equivalent ratios or double number lines.
- Lesson 9 and 10 are application-heavy. If the student is confident with unit rates, Lesson 8 can be shortened to allow more time for word problems.
- If the student needs additional practice before the assessment, use the Extension activities from Lessons 9 and 10 as a review day.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|--------------------|---------------|------------|
| Student writes the ratio in the wrong order (e.g., 5:3 instead of 3:5 for "3 cats to 5 dogs"). | The student does not understand that the order of a ratio matters and must match the order stated in the problem. | Emphasize "the first quantity named goes first." Have the student underline or highlight each quantity in the problem and label which comes first. Practice with several examples where you swap the order and discuss how the meaning changes. |
| Student confuses part-to-part and part-to-whole ratios (e.g., says the ratio of red to total is 3:5 when there are 3 red and 2 blue). | The student is not distinguishing between comparing one part to another part versus comparing one part to the entire group. | Use physical counters. Have the student separate them into groups, count each group, and then count the total. Ask: "Are you comparing a part to another part, or a part to everything?" Reinforce with labeled diagrams. |
| Student adds the same number to both terms to make equivalent ratios (e.g., 2:3 becomes 3:4 by adding 1 to each). | The student is applying additive rather than multiplicative reasoning. This is a critical conceptual gap. | Return to concrete models. Show that 2:3 means "for every 2, there are 3." Build copies of this group (2 + 2 = 4, 3 + 3 = 6) to show that multiplying preserves the relationship while adding does not. Use ratio tables to reinforce the multiplication pattern. |
| Student divides incorrectly when finding a unit rate (e.g., divides the single unit by the other quantity instead of the reverse). | The student is unsure which quantity goes in the numerator and which in the denominator when setting up a rate as a fraction. | Teach the phrase "per one ___" and have the student always identify the "one" unit first. That unit goes in the denominator. Practice with simple examples: "12 miles in 3 hours — you want miles per ONE hour, so divide 12 by 3." |
| Student can compute but cannot interpret the result in context (e.g., finds 4.5 but cannot explain what 4.5 means in the problem). | The student is performing procedures without understanding the meaning of the quantities. | After every calculation, require the student to write a complete sentence: "This means ___." Model this consistently. Ask follow-up questions: "4.5 what? Per what?" |
| Student treats a ratio as a fraction and tries to "reduce" it even when the context requires keeping the original terms (e.g., simplifies 6:9 to 2:3 when the problem asks for the actual number of items). | The student conflates simplifying ratios with solving for actual quantities. | Clarify the difference: "Simplifying tells us the relationship, but sometimes we need the actual numbers." Practice problems where the student must find both the simplified ratio and the actual quantities. |

## Conversation Starters

Use these questions throughout the unit to promote mathematical thinking and discussion:

- "Can you think of a ratio you encounter in your everyday life? What two things are being compared?"
- "If you double a recipe, does the ratio of ingredients change? Why or why not?"
- "A store sells 6 apples for $3 and another store sells 10 apples for $4.50. How would you figure out which is a better deal? What information do you need?"
- "What is the difference between saying '3 out of 5' and '3 for every 5'? Are those the same thing or different?"
- "If you are driving 60 miles per hour, what does that really mean? How far do you go in 1 minute? In 10 minutes?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

Consider reaching out for additional support or resources if the student:

- Cannot consistently identify which quantity is which in a ratio after Lesson 2 (order confusion persists).
- Relies entirely on additive reasoning for equivalent ratios after Lesson 3 and does not respond to concrete models.
- Cannot set up a ratio table or double number line independently after Lessons 4-5.
- Struggles with basic division to the extent that unit rate calculations (Lesson 7) are inaccessible — this may indicate a gap in Grade 5 division fluency that needs targeted intervention.
- Shows frustration or avoidance during word problems (Lessons 9-10) that goes beyond normal challenge — the student may need scaffolding in reading comprehension or problem decomposition.
- Scores below 50% on the unit assessment despite reteaching attempts on individual lessons.

## What Comes Next

**Unit 2: Fractions, Decimals, and Percents** builds directly on ratio reasoning. Students will connect ratios to percentages (a ratio out of 100), convert fluently between fractions, decimals, and percents, and solve percent problems using ratio strategies learned in this unit. The ratio table and double number line tools introduced here will be reused extensively.

**Unit 3: Expressions and Equations** transitions from proportional reasoning into algebraic thinking. Students will write and evaluate expressions with variables, which extends the idea of finding unknown values in ratio problems to more general algebraic contexts.
