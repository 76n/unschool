# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 1

## Topic
What Is a Ratio?

## Learning Objectives
- Define a ratio as a comparison of two quantities.
- Identify and describe ratios in real-world situations using "for every" language.
- Determine the two quantities being compared in a given ratio scenario.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to count and compare groups of objects.
- Understanding of multiplication as repeated groups (e.g., 3 groups of 4).
- Familiarity with the concept of comparison (more than, fewer than).
- Ability to read and interpret simple word problems.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Counters or small objects in two colors (at least 20 of each color).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 6.RP.1 — Understand the concept of a ratio and use ratio language to describe a ratio relationship between two quantities.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about comparing groups and introduce the idea that we often describe how quantities relate to each other.

1. Place 4 red counters and 6 blue counters on the table (or draw them on the whiteboard). Ask: "How many red counters are there? How many blue counters?"
2. Ask: "If I wanted to describe how the red and blue counters relate to each other, what could I say?" Accept responses like "there are more blue" or "there are 2 more blue than red."
3. Say: "Today we are going to learn a new way to describe how two quantities compare — not by how much more or less, but by using a ratio."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Ratio** — A comparison of two quantities that describes how much of one thing there is compared to another.
- **Quantity** — An amount or number of something.
- **"For every"** — Language used to describe a ratio relationship (e.g., "for every 2 red, there are 3 blue").

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Connect to a concrete model.**

Place 3 red counters and 5 blue counters on the table. Say: "I have 3 red counters and 5 blue counters. The ratio of red counters to blue counters is 3 to 5. That means for every 3 red counters, there are 5 blue counters."

Ask the student to repeat the ratio using "for every" language. Then rearrange to 6 red and 10 blue (two copies of the same group). Ask: "Now how many red and blue are there? Can you still see the 'for every 3 red, there are 5 blue' pattern?" Point out the two groups of 3 red and two groups of 5 blue.

**Step 2 — Emphasize that a ratio compares two specific quantities.**

Explain: "A ratio always compares exactly two quantities. When I say 'the ratio of red to blue is 3 to 5,' I am comparing the number of red counters to the number of blue counters. I could also describe the ratio of blue to red — that would be 5 to 3. The order matters."

Demonstrate by physically pointing to the blue pile first, then the red pile, and saying "5 to 3."

**Step 3 — Identify ratios in everyday situations.**

Give several real-world examples:
- "In a class, there are 12 boys and 14 girls. The ratio of boys to girls is 12 to 14."
- "A recipe calls for 2 cups of flour for every 1 cup of sugar. The ratio of flour to sugar is 2 to 1."
- "On a sports team, there are 5 sixth graders for every 3 seventh graders. The ratio of sixth graders to seventh graders is 5 to 3."

For each example, ask: "What two quantities are being compared?" and "Can you say this using 'for every' language?"

**Step 4 — Clarify what a ratio is NOT.**

Explain: "A ratio is not the same as subtraction. If I have 3 red and 5 blue, the difference is 2, but the ratio is 3 to 5. Ratios describe a multiplicative relationship, not an additive one. We are asking 'how many of this for how many of that,' not 'how many more or fewer.'"

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Identifying a ratio from a picture.**

A bag contains 4 apples and 7 oranges.

- "What is the ratio of apples to oranges?"
- We are comparing apples (4) to oranges (7).
- The ratio of apples to oranges is 4 to 7.
- Using "for every" language: "For every 4 apples, there are 7 oranges."

**Example B — Identifying a ratio from a word problem.**

Marcus scored 6 goals in 4 soccer games.

- "What is the ratio of goals to games?"
- We are comparing goals (6) to games (4).
- The ratio of goals to games is 6 to 4.
- Using "for every" language: "For every 6 goals, there were 4 games." (Or equivalently: "For every 4 games, Marcus scored 6 goals.")

**Example C — Ratio with "for every" language given, find the quantities.**

"For every 3 dogs at the park, there are 2 cats."

- What two quantities are being compared? Dogs and cats.
- What is the ratio of dogs to cats? 3 to 2.
- What is the ratio of cats to dogs? 2 to 3.
- Notice: switching the order changes the ratio.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. For each, ask the student to identify the two quantities and state the ratio using "for every" language.

1. A pencil box has 5 pencils and 3 erasers. What is the ratio of pencils to erasers?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Which quantity is named first — pencils or erasers? So which number comes first in the ratio?"

2. A garden has 8 tomato plants and 6 pepper plants. What is the ratio of pepper plants to tomato plants?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Be careful with the order here. Which is named first in the question?"

3. There are 10 boys and 15 girls in a classroom. What is the ratio of boys to girls? State it using "for every" language.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Can you phrase this as 'for every ___ boys, there are ___ girls'?"

4. A parking lot has 9 trucks and 21 cars. What is the ratio of trucks to cars?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What two things are we comparing? Which comes first?"

5. At a pizza party, there are 2 cheese pizzas for every 3 pepperoni pizzas. If there are 6 cheese pizzas, how many pepperoni pizzas are there?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "If 2 cheese goes with 3 pepperoni, and we have 6 cheese, how many groups of 2 is that? So how many groups of 3 pepperoni?"

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### ★ On-Level

1. A fruit basket has 7 bananas and 4 strawberries. What is the ratio of bananas to strawberries?

2. A store shelf has 12 blue shirts and 8 red shirts. What is the ratio of red shirts to blue shirts?

3. On a farm there are 15 chickens and 5 cows. What is the ratio of chickens to cows? Say it using "for every" language.

4. A necklace has 6 gold beads and 10 silver beads. What is the ratio of gold beads to silver beads?

### ★★ Stretch

5. In a bag of candy, there are 9 chocolate pieces and 12 gummy pieces. What is the ratio of chocolate to gummy? What is the ratio of gummy to the total number of candies?

6. A basketball team scored 24 points in the first half and 18 points in the second half. What is the ratio of first-half points to second-half points? What is the ratio of first-half points to total points?

7. A recipe uses 3 cups of oats for every 2 cups of raisins. If you use 12 cups of oats, how many cups of raisins do you need? Explain your reasoning using "for every" language.

### ★★★ Challenge

8. At a school, the ratio of students who walk to school to students who ride the bus is 4 to 9. If 36 students ride the bus, how many walk? How many students are there in total?

9. Maria says, "The ratio of cats to dogs at the shelter is 5 to 3, so there are more cats than dogs." Kevin says, "That means there are 2 more cats than dogs." Is Maria correct? Is Kevin correct? Explain why or why not.

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

1. A jar has 8 red marbles and 5 green marbles. What is the ratio of red marbles to green marbles? State it using "for every" language.

2. In a parking lot, there are 3 motorcycles for every 7 cars. What is the ratio of cars to motorcycles?

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Use physical counters to rebuild the concept. Place two groups of objects and have the student practice stating the ratio in both orders (A to B and B to A). Focus on the "for every" phrasing until it becomes natural. Revisit Worked Examples A and B with new numbers.

### If Mastery Is Met
Have the student create three of their own ratio scenarios from things they see around them (e.g., "the ratio of windows to doors in this room is 3 to 1"). Ask them to describe each using "for every" language and also state the reverse ratio.

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Ratio Scavenger Hunt:** Walk around the room or house and find at least five pairs of objects that can be described using a ratio. Write each ratio and a sentence using "for every" language.

2. **Draw It:** Create a picture that shows the ratio 3 to 5 (e.g., 3 circles and 5 squares). Then draw a picture that shows the same ratio but with more objects (e.g., 6 circles and 10 squares). Explain how both pictures show the same ratio.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Reversing the order of the ratio.** Students may write 5 to 3 when the problem asks for the ratio of the first quantity (3) to the second (5). Reinforce that the first quantity named always corresponds to the first number in the ratio. Have the student underline or circle each quantity in the problem and label them "first" and "second."
- **Using subtraction instead of ratio language.** Students accustomed to comparison problems from earlier grades may say "there are 2 more blue than red" instead of stating a ratio. Gently redirect: "That tells us the difference. A ratio tells us something different — how many of each for a given group."
- **Thinking a ratio must be simplified.** At this point, students should simply state the ratio as given (e.g., 8 to 12, not 2 to 3). Simplification is introduced in Lesson 3.

### Pacing Advice
This lesson is intentionally introductory. If the student picks up the concept quickly, spend less time on Guided Practice and move to the Stretch and Challenge problems. If the student struggles with the warm-up (comparing groups), slow down and spend extra time on Step 1 with physical counters before moving to word problems. The most important outcome is that the student can identify two quantities and state their ratio in the correct order.

**★★ Stretch**
5. Chocolate to gummy: 9 to 12. Gummy to total: 12 to 21.

7. 8 cups of raisins. (12 / 3 = 4 groups of oats; 4 x 2 = 8 cups of raisins. "For every 3 cups of oats, there are 2 cups of raisins, so for 12 cups of oats there are 8 cups of raisins.")

**★★★ Challenge**
8. 16 students walk. (36 / 9 = 4 groups; 4 x 4 = 16.) Total students: 16 + 36 = 52.
9. Maria is correct — the ratio 5 to 3 does mean there are more cats than dogs (5 > 3 in each group). Kevin is NOT correct — we cannot say there are exactly 2 more cats than dogs. The actual difference depends on the total number of animals. If there are 5 cats and 3 dogs, the difference is 2. But if there are 10 cats and 6 dogs (same ratio), the difference is 4. A ratio describes a multiplicative relationship, not a fixed difference.
