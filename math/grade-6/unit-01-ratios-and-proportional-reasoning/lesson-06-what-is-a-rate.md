# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 6

## Topic
What Is a Rate?

## Learning Objectives
- Define a rate as a special kind of ratio that compares two quantities measured in different units.
- Identify rates in real-world situations and label each quantity with its unit.
- Set up rates from word problems and express them using correct notation (e.g., 60 miles per 2 hours).

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of ratios and equivalent ratios (Lessons 1-3).
- Ability to use ratio tables and double number lines (Lessons 4-5).
- Familiarity with units of measurement (miles, hours, dollars, pounds, etc.).
- Basic division skills.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.
- Grocery flyers or printed price lists (optional, for real-world rate examples).

**Standards:** 6.RP.2 — Understand the concept of a unit rate a/b associated with a ratio a:b with b not equal to 0, and use rate language in the context of a ratio relationship. 6.RP.3b — Solve unit rate problems.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Transition from ratios (same units) to rates (different units) by noticing that some comparisons involve different kinds of things.

1. Ask: "What is the ratio of boys to girls if there are 12 boys and 8 girls?" (12:8) "What units are we comparing?" (Boys and girls — both are people, just different groups.)
2. Ask: "A car drives 120 miles in 3 hours. What two things are being compared?" (Miles and hours.) "Are miles and hours the same kind of unit?" (No — miles measure distance, hours measure time.)
3. Say: "When a ratio compares two quantities with different units, we call it a rate. Today we will learn what rates are, where we see them, and how to set them up from word problems."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Rate** — A ratio that compares two quantities measured in different units.
- **Per** — The word used to describe a rate, meaning "for each" or "for every one" (e.g., miles per hour, dollars per pound).
- **Units** — The labels that tell us what is being measured (e.g., miles, hours, dollars, pages, gallons).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Connect to a concrete model.**

Say: "Imagine you read 40 pages in 2 hours. The ratio of pages to hours is 40:2. But this is a special kind of ratio because pages and hours are completely different units. We call this a rate."

Write on the board: **40 pages per 2 hours** or **40 pages / 2 hours**.

"The word 'per' connects the two quantities. It means 'for every.' So this rate means: for every 2 hours of reading, you read 40 pages."

**Step 2 — Identify the two quantities and their units.**

"Every rate has two parts: a quantity with its unit and another quantity with its unit. When you set up a rate, always label both numbers with their units."

Give examples and ask the student to identify both quantities:
- "A cheetah runs 70 miles in 1 hour." → 70 miles per 1 hour. Quantities: distance (miles) and time (hours).
- "A store sells 3 pounds of apples for $4.50." → 3 pounds per $4.50. Quantities: weight (pounds) and cost (dollars).
- "A factory makes 200 toys in 8 hours." → 200 toys per 8 hours. Quantities: number of toys and time (hours).

**Step 3 — Distinguish rates from ratios with the same units.**

"Not every ratio is a rate. The ratio of boys to girls (12:8) is NOT a rate because both quantities are measured in the same unit (number of people). The ratio of miles to hours (120:3) IS a rate because the units are different."

Rule of thumb: "If the two quantities have different unit labels, it is a rate."

**Step 4 — Set up rates from word problems.**

Model the process:
1. Read the problem and identify the two quantities.
2. Note the unit of each quantity.
3. Write the rate as "quantity 1 (unit) per quantity 2 (unit)."

Example: "Jasmine earns $45 for 5 hours of babysitting."
1. Quantity 1: $45 (dollars). Quantity 2: 5 hours (time).
2. Rate: $45 per 5 hours, or 45 dollars / 5 hours.

Ask: "Could we also write this as 5 hours per $45?" Yes — a rate can be written with either quantity first, but context usually makes one order more natural (dollars per hour is more common than hours per dollar).

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Identifying a rate from a sentence.**

"A runner completed 6 laps in 15 minutes."

- Quantity 1: 6 laps (count of laps).
- Quantity 2: 15 minutes (time).
- Rate: 6 laps per 15 minutes.
- The units are different (laps vs. minutes), so this IS a rate.

**Example B — Setting up a rate from a word problem.**

"A garden hose fills a pool at 24 gallons every 4 minutes."

- Quantity 1: 24 gallons (volume).
- Quantity 2: 4 minutes (time).
- Rate: 24 gallons per 4 minutes.
- Using a ratio table, we can find equivalent rates:

| Gallons | Minutes |
|---------|---------|
| 24      | 4       |
| 48      | 8       |
| 72      | 12      |
| 6       | 1       |

**Example C — Determining whether a comparison is a rate.**

"There are 14 fiction books and 6 nonfiction books on a shelf."

- Quantity 1: 14 fiction books. Quantity 2: 6 nonfiction books.
- Both are measured in the same unit (books). This is a ratio, NOT a rate.

"A printer prints 30 pages in 5 minutes."

- Quantity 1: 30 pages. Quantity 2: 5 minutes.
- Pages and minutes are different units. This IS a rate.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. "A cyclist rides 36 miles in 3 hours." Write this as a rate with units.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What two quantities are being compared? What are their units?"

2. "A store sells 5 oranges for $2." Write this as a rate. Then use a ratio table to find the cost of 20 oranges.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Set up the ratio table with oranges and dollars. What do you multiply 5 by to get 20?"

3. Decide whether each situation is a rate or a ratio (same-unit comparison):
   - (a) 150 words typed in 3 minutes.
   - (b) 7 cats and 5 dogs at a shelter.
   - (c) 80 heartbeats in 1 minute.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "For each, ask yourself: are the units the same or different?"

4. "A machine produces 250 bolts in 10 minutes." Write this as a rate. Then find how many bolts the machine produces in 30 minutes.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Set up a ratio table or use multiplication. What do you multiply 10 by to get 30?"

5. Write a rate from your own life. Identify both quantities and their units.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Think about speed, cost, or anything you do over time."

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### ★ On-Level

1. "A bus travels 180 miles in 4 hours." Write this as a rate with units.

2. "A baker uses 10 cups of flour to make 4 loaves of bread." Write this as a rate with units.

3. Decide if each is a rate or a ratio:
   - (a) 25 students to 1 teacher.
   - (b) 15 red marbles and 10 blue marbles.
   - (c) $12 for 3 pounds of meat.

4. "A faucet drips 6 ounces of water every 5 minutes." Use a ratio table to find how many ounces drip in 20 minutes.

### ★★ Stretch

5. "A train travels 340 miles in 4 hours." Write the rate. Then set up a ratio table and find how far the train travels in 10 hours.

6. Kenji says, "12 boys to 15 girls is a rate because 12 and 15 are different numbers." Is Kenji correct? Explain why or why not.

7. "At a car wash, workers wash 9 cars in 2 hours." If they keep the same rate, how many cars can they wash in 8 hours? Show your work using a ratio table or double number line.

### ★★★ Challenge

8. "A recipe calls for 2.5 cups of broth for every 3 servings." Write this as a rate. How many cups of broth do you need for 12 servings? Show your reasoning.

9. Two situations are described below. For each, write the rate. Then explain which rate tells you "more per one unit of time."
   - Situation 1: A plant grows 6 inches in 4 weeks.
   - Situation 2: A plant grows 10 inches in 8 weeks.
   (Hint: You might need to find equivalent rates to compare.)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

1. "A swimmer completes 12 laps in 8 minutes." Write this as a rate with units. Is this a rate or a same-unit ratio? How do you know?

2. "A car uses 10 gallons of gas to travel 280 miles." Write this as a rate. Use a ratio table to find how many miles the car can travel on 5 gallons.

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Review the definition of a rate with several side-by-side examples: one that IS a rate and one that is NOT. Stress the "different units" criterion. Then practice setting up rates from simple sentences: give the student five sentences and have them (a) circle the two quantities, (b) label the units, and (c) write the rate.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "In problem 2, you found that 5 gallons gets the car 140 miles. Can you figure out how far the car goes on just 1 gallon?" This previews unit rate (Lesson 7).

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Rate Scavenger Hunt:** Find at least five rates in everyday life (from signs, labels, packaging, or online). Write each as a rate with units and classify what types of quantities are being compared (speed, price, production, etc.).

2. **Create a Rate Problem:** Write your own word problem involving a rate. Include a question that requires finding an equivalent rate. Trade with a partner or have the facilitator solve it.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Forgetting to include units.** Students may write "12 per 8" without specifying "laps" and "minutes." Insist on units every time. Model the habit: "Always say what the numbers represent."
- **Confusing rates and ratios.** Some students will label every comparison a rate. Ask them to check: "Are the units different?" If both quantities count the same kind of thing (e.g., boys and girls are both people), it is a ratio, not a rate.
- **Setting up the rate in the wrong order.** Students may write "8 minutes per 12 laps" when the question asks for laps per minutes. Reinforce that the order depends on the context or the question, and that they should always label clearly.

### Pacing Advice
This lesson introduces a new concept (rate) but builds heavily on prior lessons. Most students grasp the definition quickly. Spend less time on the definition and more time on Guided Practice problems 2 and 4, which require using ratio tables with rates. This reinforces Lesson 4 skills in a rate context and prepares students for unit rate in Lesson 7.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice
1. 36 miles per 3 hours (or 36 miles / 3 hours).
2. Rate: 5 oranges per $2. Ratio table: 5:$2, 10:$4, 15:$6, 20:$8. Cost of 20 oranges = $8.
3. (a) Rate — words and minutes are different units. (b) Ratio — both are animals (same type of unit). (c) Rate — heartbeats and minutes are different units.
4. Rate: 250 bolts per 10 minutes. 10 x 3 = 30 minutes, so 250 x 3 = 750 bolts.
5. Answers will vary. Example: "I walk 4 blocks in 10 minutes" → 4 blocks per 10 minutes.

### Independent Practice

**★ On-Level**
1. 180 miles per 4 hours.
2. 10 cups of flour per 4 loaves.
3. (a) Rate — students and teachers are different roles measured differently. (b) Ratio — both are marbles. (c) Rate — dollars and pounds are different units.

**★★ Stretch**
5. Rate: 340 miles per 4 hours. Ratio table: 340:4, 680:8, 850:10. (340/4 = 85 per hour; 85 x 10 = 850.) The train travels 850 miles in 10 hours.

7. Ratio table: 9:2, 18:4, 27:6, 36:8. They can wash 36 cars in 8 hours.

**★★★ Challenge**
