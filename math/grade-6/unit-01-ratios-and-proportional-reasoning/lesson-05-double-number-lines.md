# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 5

## Topic
Double Number Lines

## Learning Objectives
- Represent a ratio relationship on a double number line diagram.
- Use a double number line to find missing values in equivalent ratio situations.
- Connect double number lines to ratio tables as two representations of the same relationship.

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of equivalent ratios (Lesson 3).
- Ability to use ratio tables to find missing values (Lesson 4).
- Comfort with plotting values on a number line.
- Multiplication and division fluency.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Rulers (for drawing straight number lines).
- Colored pencils or markers (two colors — one for each quantity).
- Graph paper (optional, for aligning tick marks neatly).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 6.RP.3a — Make tables of equivalent ratios relating quantities with whole-number measurements, find missing values in the tables, and plot the pairs of values on the coordinate plane. 6.RP.3 — Use ratio and rate reasoning to solve real-world and mathematical problems.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Connect to ratio tables (Lesson 4) and motivate a visual representation.

1. Present this ratio table:

   | Lemons | Cups of Water |
   |--------|--------------|
   | 2      | 6            |
   | 4      | 12           |
   | 6      | 18           |
   | ?      | 30           |

   Ask: "What is the missing value?" (10 lemons.)

2. Say: "We solved this using a ratio table. Today we are going to learn another visual tool — a double number line — that shows the same information in a different way. It is especially helpful for seeing the relationship between two quantities at a glance."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Double number line** — A diagram with two parallel number lines, each representing one of the two quantities in a ratio, aligned so that corresponding values line up vertically.
- **Tick marks** — The marks on a number line that show equally spaced values.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Connect to a concrete model.**

Say: "A lemonade recipe uses 2 lemons for every 6 cups of water. Let's show this on a double number line."

Draw two horizontal parallel lines on the board. Label the top line "Lemons" and the bottom line "Cups of Water." Place a tick mark at the left end of both lines and label them both 0.

"Both lines start at 0 because if we have 0 lemons, we need 0 cups of water."

Place the next tick mark. On the top line, write 2. Directly below it on the bottom line, write 6. "This shows our base ratio: 2 lemons pairs with 6 cups of water."

Continue: next tick at 4 (top) and 12 (bottom). Then 6 and 18. Then 8 and 24. Then 10 and 30.

```
Lemons:         0     2     4     6     8     10
                 |-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
                 |-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
Cups of Water:  0     6    12    18    24     30
```

"Each pair of aligned values is an equivalent ratio. 2:6, 4:12, 6:18, 8:24, 10:30 — all equivalent."

**Step 2 — Show how to find missing values.**

"Suppose I know I have 8 lemons and I need to find how many cups of water. I go to 8 on the top line and look straight down — I see 24 on the bottom line. So 8 lemons needs 24 cups of water."

"What if I have 30 cups of water and I need to find how many lemons? I find 30 on the bottom line and look straight up — I see 10. So I need 10 lemons."

**Step 3 — Explain the spacing rule.**

"Important: the tick marks on each number line must be equally spaced. On the top line, the lemons go up by 2 each time (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10). On the bottom line, the cups of water go up by 6 each time (0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30). The spacing on the top line does NOT have to match the spacing on the bottom line in actual distance — what matters is that the values that correspond to the same batch line up vertically."

**Step 4 — Connect to ratio tables.**

Draw the same data as a ratio table side by side:

| Lemons | Cups of Water |
|--------|--------------|
| 0      | 0            |
| 2      | 6            |
| 4      | 12           |
| 6      | 18           |
| 8      | 24           |
| 10     | 30           |

"A double number line and a ratio table show the same information. The double number line makes it easier to see the visual pattern. The ratio table makes it easier to compute missing values with multiplication. Both are useful tools."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Draw a double number line from a ratio.**

The ratio of red paint to blue paint in a mixture is 3:5. Draw a double number line showing five equivalent ratios.

Step 1: Draw two parallel lines. Label top "Red Paint" and bottom "Blue Paint."
Step 2: Start both at 0.
Step 3: Red goes up by 3: 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15. Blue goes up by 5: 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25.

```
Red Paint:   0     3     6     9    12    15
              |-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
              |-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
Blue Paint:  0     5    10    15    20    25
```

Each vertical pair is an equivalent ratio: 3:5, 6:10, 9:15, 12:20, 15:25.

**Example B — Find a missing value using a double number line.**

A car uses 3 gallons of gas for every 75 miles. How many miles can it travel on 12 gallons?

Step 1: Set up the double number line.

```
Gallons:  0     3     6     9    12
           |-----|-----|-----|-----|
           |-----|-----|-----|-----|
Miles:    0    75   150   225    ?
```

Step 2: The gallons go up by 3. The miles go up by 75. At 12 gallons, we have gone up 4 intervals: 75 x 4 = 300 miles.

Answer: The car can travel 300 miles on 12 gallons.

**Example C — Work backward on a double number line.**

A bakery uses 5 cups of flour for every 2 cups of butter. If the bakery used 8 cups of butter, how many cups of flour did it use?

Step 1: Set up the double number line.

```
Flour (cups):   0     5    10    15    20
                 |-----|-----|-----|-----|
                 |-----|-----|-----|-----|
Butter (cups):  0     2     4     6     8
```

Step 2: Find 8 on the butter line. Look up to the flour line: 20.

Answer: The bakery used 20 cups of flour.

Check with multiplication: 2 x 4 = 8, so 5 x 4 = 20. Confirmed.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. Draw a double number line for the ratio 4:3. Show at least four equivalent ratios.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Start both lines at 0. The top line goes up by 4, the bottom by 3. Make sure corresponding values line up."

2. Use your double number line from problem 1 to answer: If the first quantity is 16, what is the second quantity?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Find 16 on the top line. Look straight down. If you did not extend your line that far, add more tick marks."

3. A store charges $7 for every 2 pounds of deli meat. Draw a double number line and find the cost of 8 pounds.

   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What goes on each line? Dollars on one, pounds on the other. Start at 0 and count up."

4. Using the double number line from problem 3, how many pounds of deli meat can you buy for $21?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Find $21 on the dollars line and look across to the pounds line."

5. A double number line is shown. Fill in the missing values.

   ```
   Notebooks:  0     3     ?     9    12
                |-----|-----|-----|-----|
                |-----|-----|-----|-----|
   Cost ($):   0    1.50   ?    4.50   ?
   ```

   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is the pattern on each line? Notebooks go up by 3. Cost goes up by...?"

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### ★ On-Level

1. Draw a double number line for the ratio 5:8. Show four equivalent ratios beyond 0:0.

2. Use your double number line to find: if the first quantity is 20, what is the second quantity?

3. A recipe uses 4 tablespoons of cocoa for every 6 tablespoons of sugar. Draw a double number line and find how many tablespoons of sugar you need if you use 16 tablespoons of cocoa.

4. A car travels 50 miles every 2 hours. Draw a double number line and find how far it travels in 10 hours.

### ★★ Stretch

5. Draw a double number line for the ratio 6:10. Use it to find how many of the second quantity correspond to 15 of the first quantity. (Hint: you will need to go beyond your initial tick marks.)

6. A school has a ratio of teachers to students of 1:18. If there are 90 students, draw a double number line to find how many teachers there are.

7. Leo drew a double number line but made an error. The top line reads: 0, 4, 8, 12, 16. The bottom line reads: 0, 3, 6, 10, 12. Find and correct Leo's mistake.

### ★★★ Challenge

8. A double number line shows the ratio of flour to sugar. The third tick mark on the flour line is 9 and the third tick mark on the sugar line is 6. What is the ratio of flour to sugar in simplest form? What would the fifth tick mark on each line be?

9. Maya has two double number lines. The first shows 3:7 and the second shows 6:14. She says, "These are different ratios because the numbers are different." Do you agree or disagree? Use the double number lines to explain your answer.

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

1. Draw a double number line for the ratio 2:7. Show at least three equivalent ratios.

2. A printer prints 8 pages every 3 minutes. Using a double number line (or mental math), how many pages does it print in 9 minutes?

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Start with a very simple ratio like 1:2. Draw the double number line together, emphasizing the alignment of tick marks and the counting pattern on each line. Then progress to 2:5 and practice finding missing values by tracing from one line to the other. If the student is struggling with the drawing, switch to a ratio table first and then translate the ratio table into a double number line row by row.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask the student: "Can you draw a double number line where one of the values is not a whole number? For example, 3 oranges cost $2.50. Draw the double number line and find the cost of 12 oranges." This previews the concept of unit rate in Lesson 7.

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Three Representations:** Pick a ratio. Show it as (a) a ratio table, (b) a double number line, and (c) a written description with "for every" language. Explain how the three representations are connected.

2. **Double Number Line Design:** Design a double number line for a recipe of your choice. Include at least 6 pairs of values. Then write three questions that someone else could answer using your double number line.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Spacing the tick marks unequally.** Students may place tick marks inconsistently, making the diagram misleading. Encourage use of a ruler and graph paper. Remind them: "The space between tick marks on each line should be the same."
- **Misaligning the two lines.** If the tick marks on the top and bottom lines do not line up vertically, the student will read incorrect corresponding values. Stress drawing vertical dashed lines between corresponding tick marks.
- **Forgetting to start at 0.** Both lines must start at 0:0. If a student starts at the base ratio (e.g., 3:5), they lose the ability to see the full pattern of equivalent ratios.

### Pacing Advice
Drawing neat double number lines takes time, especially the first time. Budget extra time for the first practice problem and model the drawing process step by step. Once the student has drawn two or three, the process speeds up significantly. If time is short, you can pre-draw the lines on the worksheet and have the student fill in only the numbers. The key learning goal is reading the diagram to find missing values, not the act of drawing perfectly.
