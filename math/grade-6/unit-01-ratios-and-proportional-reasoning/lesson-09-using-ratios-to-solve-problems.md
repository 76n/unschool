# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Using Ratios to Solve Problems

## Learning Objectives
- Apply ratio reasoning to solve real-world problems involving scale factors, recipes, and map distances.
- Set up and solve proportions using ratio tables or equivalent ratios.
- Determine a scale factor and use it to find unknown quantities.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to generate equivalent ratios (Lesson 3).
- Fluency with ratio tables (Lesson 4).
- Understanding of unit rates (Lesson 7).
- Multiplication and division of whole numbers and decimals.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Graph paper (for scale drawing activity).
- Rulers (for measuring in the Extension activity).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 6.RP.3 — Use ratio and rate reasoning to solve real-world and mathematical problems. 6.RP.3a — Make tables of equivalent ratios relating quantities with whole-number measurements, find missing values in the tables.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of equivalent ratios and preview real-world applications.

1. "A recipe for pancakes uses 3 cups of flour and 2 eggs. If you want to use 9 cups of flour, how many eggs do you need?" (6 eggs — multiply both by 3.)
2. Ask: "How did you figure that out?" (Equivalent ratios: 3:2 = 9:6.)
3. Say: "Scaling a recipe is one example of using ratios to solve real problems. Today we will practice using ratio reasoning for recipes, maps, scale drawings, and more."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Scale factor** — The number you multiply the original quantities by to get the new quantities.
- **Proportion** — A statement that two ratios are equivalent (e.g., 3:5 = 6:10).
- **Scale** — A ratio that compares a measurement on a model, map, or drawing to the actual measurement.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Connect to a concrete model: recipes.**

"A cookie recipe makes 24 cookies and calls for:
- 2 cups of flour
- 1 cup of sugar
- 3 eggs

You want to make 72 cookies. How much of each ingredient do you need?"

"First, find the scale factor. 72 / 24 = 3. So you need to multiply every ingredient by 3."

| Ingredient | Original | x 3 | Scaled |
|-----------|----------|-----|--------|
| Flour     | 2 cups   | x 3 | 6 cups |
| Sugar     | 1 cup    | x 3 | 3 cups |
| Eggs      | 3        | x 3 | 9      |

"The scale factor is 3 because you are making 3 times as many cookies."

**Step 2 — Map distances.**

"A map has a scale of 1 inch = 50 miles. Two cities are 3.5 inches apart on the map. How far apart are they in real life?"

Set up a ratio table:

| Inches on Map | Actual Miles |
|--------------|-------------|
| 1            | 50          |
| 3.5          | ?           |

"The scale factor from 1 to 3.5 is 3.5. So 50 x 3.5 = 175 miles."

Alternatively, use the unit rate: 1 inch = 50 miles, so 3.5 inches = 3.5 x 50 = 175 miles.

**Step 3 — Finding the scale factor when it is not given directly.**

"A model car is 6 inches long. The actual car is 15 feet long. What is the scale factor?"

"We need to compare model to actual: 6 inches to 15 feet. But first, let's convert to the same units. 15 feet = 180 inches."

"Scale factor: 6 / 180 = 1/30. So the model is 1/30 the size of the real car. The scale is 1:30."

"If the model car is 2.5 inches wide, how wide is the actual car? 2.5 x 30 = 75 inches = 6.25 feet."

**Step 4 — Using ratio tables as a problem-solving tool.**

"Whenever you face a ratio problem, a ratio table is a reliable strategy. Set up the known ratio in the first row, then use multiplication or division to find the unknown."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Scaling a recipe down.**

"A punch recipe serves 20 people and uses 8 cups of juice and 5 cups of soda. How much of each do you need to serve 4 people?"

- Scale factor: 4 / 20 = 1/5 (or divide by 5).
- Juice: 8 / 5 = 1.6 cups.
- Soda: 5 / 5 = 1 cup.

Ratio table:

| People | Juice (cups) | Soda (cups) |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| 20     | 8           | 5           |
| 4      | 1.6         | 1           |

**Example B — Map scale problem.**

"On a map, 2 centimeters represents 75 kilometers. If two landmarks are 9 centimeters apart on the map, what is the actual distance?"

Ratio table:

| Map (cm) | Actual (km) |
|----------|-------------|
| 2        | 75          |
| 9        | ?           |

Strategy: Find the unit rate first. 75 / 2 = 37.5 km per cm. Then: 9 x 37.5 = 337.5 km.

**Example C — Scale drawing.**

"A blueprint shows a room that is 4 inches wide and 6 inches long. The scale is 1 inch = 3 feet. What are the actual dimensions of the room?"

- Width: 4 x 3 = 12 feet.
- Length: 6 x 3 = 18 feet.

Check: The ratio of width to length on the blueprint is 4:6 = 2:3. The ratio of actual width to length is 12:18 = 2:3. The ratio is preserved.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. A smoothie recipe uses 4 cups of fruit and 2 cups of yogurt to make 3 smoothies. How much fruit and yogurt do you need for 12 smoothies?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is the scale factor from 3 smoothies to 12 smoothies?"

2. A map scale says 1 inch = 25 miles. A park is 6 inches from a lake on the map. How far apart are they in real life?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Use the unit rate: 1 inch equals how many miles? Then multiply by 6."

3. A scale model of a building is built at a scale of 1:200. The model is 9 inches tall. How tall is the actual building in inches? Convert your answer to feet.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Scale 1:200 means the actual building is 200 times as large as the model."

4. A recipe for salad dressing uses oil and vinegar in the ratio 3:1. If you want to make 2 cups of dressing total, how much oil and how much vinegar do you need?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "The ratio 3:1 means 3 parts oil and 1 part vinegar — that is 4 parts total. How big is each part?"

5. On a map, 3 centimeters represents 120 kilometers. How many centimeters apart are two towns that are 200 kilometers apart in real life?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "This time you know the actual distance and need the map distance. Find the unit rate (km per cm) and work backward."

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### ★ On-Level

1. A lemonade recipe uses 6 lemons and 4 cups of sugar to make 8 servings. How many lemons and cups of sugar do you need for 24 servings?

2. A map scale is 1 inch = 40 miles. How far apart are two cities that are 7.5 inches apart on the map?

3. A photograph is 4 inches wide and 6 inches tall. It is enlarged so the width is 10 inches. What is the new height?

4. To make orange paint, you mix red and yellow paint in the ratio 5:3. You need 24 total cups of orange paint. How many cups of red and yellow paint do you need?

### ★★ Stretch

5. A model airplane is built at a 1:72 scale. If the real airplane has a wingspan of 108 feet, what is the wingspan of the model in inches? (Hint: convert 108 feet to inches first, then apply the scale.)

6. A recipe for bread calls for 5 cups of flour and 1.5 cups of water. Marcus only has 3 cups of flour. How much water should he use? How many "batches" of the recipe is he making?

7. Two maps show the same region. Map A uses a scale of 1 cm = 20 km. Map B uses a scale of 1 cm = 50 km. On Map A, two cities are 15 cm apart. How far apart are they on Map B?

### ★★★ Challenge

8. A garden plan uses the scale 1 inch = 4 feet. The actual garden is 28 feet long and 16 feet wide. A path runs diagonally from one corner to the opposite corner. On the plan, how long is the path? (Hint: First find the plan dimensions, then use the fact that 28-16 is not a standard Pythagorean triple — but 7-4 scaled by 4... think about the diagonal of the actual garden.)

9. A paint store says you need 1 gallon of paint for every 350 square feet of wall. A room has four walls: two are 12 feet by 8 feet and two are 10 feet by 8 feet. How many gallons of paint do you need? Round up to the nearest whole gallon.

### Interleaved Review
*(Reviewing: finding area of rectangles and triangles — Grade 5)*

10. A rectangle has a length of 12 cm and a width of 7 cm. What is its area?

11. A triangle has a base of 10 inches and a height of 6 inches. What is its area?

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

1. A recipe uses flour and butter in the ratio 5:2. If you use 15 cups of flour, how many cups of butter do you need?

2. A map has a scale of 1 inch = 30 miles. Two cities are 4.5 inches apart on the map. What is the actual distance between them?

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to ratio tables as the primary strategy. Set up every problem as a two-column table with the known ratio in the first row and the target values in the second row. Walk through finding the scale factor step by step. If the student struggles with the division to find the scale factor, use simpler numbers first (e.g., scale factors of 2, 3, or 4) before introducing non-whole-number scale factors.

### If Mastery Is Met
Present a multi-step problem: "A school is building a scale model of the solar system in the hallway. The model uses the scale 1 meter = 10 million kilometers. The distance from the Sun to Earth is 150 million kilometers. The distance from the Sun to Mars is 228 million kilometers. How far apart are Earth and Mars in the model?" (7.8 meters.)

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Scale Drawing:** Measure an object in the room (a table, a bookshelf, etc.) and create a scale drawing on graph paper. Choose an appropriate scale, label it, and calculate the scale factor.

2. **Recipe Converter:** Choose a recipe for 4 people. Convert it to serve 10 people and also to serve 1 person. Present your original recipe and both conversions in a table.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Applying the scale factor to only one quantity.** In a recipe with multiple ingredients, students may multiply one ingredient by the scale factor but forget the others. Use a table format to ensure every ingredient is scaled.
- **Confusing the map distance with the actual distance.** Students may multiply when they should divide (or vice versa). Ask: "Is the answer supposed to be bigger or smaller than what you started with?" Map distances are smaller; actual distances are larger.
- **Forgetting to convert units before applying a scale.** In problems like the model car (inches vs. feet), students must convert to the same units first. Remind them to check units before setting up the ratio.

### Pacing Advice
This lesson is application-heavy and may take more than one session. If time is limited, focus on recipe scaling (most intuitive for students) and one map/scale problem. The scale drawing problems (like the blueprint) can be saved for a second session or combined with the Extension activity. The most important skill is identifying the scale factor and applying it consistently to all quantities.
