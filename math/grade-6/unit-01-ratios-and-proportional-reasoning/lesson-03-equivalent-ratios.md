# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 1, Lesson 3

## Topic
Equivalent Ratios

## Learning Objectives
- Determine whether two ratios are equivalent by using multiplication or division.
- Generate equivalent ratios by multiplying or dividing both terms by the same nonzero number.
- Simplify a ratio to its simplest form by dividing both terms by their greatest common factor.

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of ratios and ratio notation (Lessons 1-2).
- Fluency with multiplication facts through 12 x 12.
- Ability to find factors and greatest common factor (GCF) of two numbers.
- Understanding that multiplying or dividing by 1 does not change a value.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Counters or small objects in two colors (at least 30 of each).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 6.RP.1 — Understand the concept of a ratio and use ratio language to describe a ratio relationship between two quantities. 6.RP.3a — Make tables of equivalent ratios relating quantities with whole-number measurements.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review multiplication patterns and connect to the idea that the same relationship can look different with bigger numbers.

1. Ask: "If I have 2 red counters and 3 blue counters, what is the ratio of red to blue?" (2:3)
2. Now place 4 red and 6 blue. Ask: "What is the ratio now?" (4:6) "Do you notice anything about how 2:3 and 4:6 are related?"
3. Place 6 red and 9 blue. Ask: "What about now?" (6:9) "How is this related to 2:3?"
4. Say: "All three of these ratios describe the same relationship. Today we will learn why, and what we call ratios like these."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Equivalent ratios** — Two ratios that express the same relationship between quantities. They can be found by multiplying or dividing both terms by the same nonzero number.
- **Simplest form** — A ratio in which the two terms share no common factor other than 1.
- **Greatest common factor (GCF)** — The largest number that divides evenly into both terms of a ratio.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Connect to a concrete model.**

Place 2 red and 3 blue counters on the table. Say: "This is our base group — 2 red for every 3 blue." Now make a second identical group next to it: another 2 red and 3 blue. Ask: "How many red total? How many blue total?" (4 red, 6 blue.) "The ratio is now 4:6, but we still have 2 red for every 3 blue. We just have two copies of that group."

Add a third copy. "Now we have 6 red and 9 blue. The ratio is 6:9. Same relationship — three copies of the base group."

Write on the board:
- 2:3 (x1)
- 4:6 (x2) — both terms multiplied by 2
- 6:9 (x3) — both terms multiplied by 3

Say: "These are called equivalent ratios. They represent the same comparison, just scaled up or down."

**Step 2 — Show the multiplication rule.**

"To create an equivalent ratio, multiply BOTH terms by the SAME number."

2:3 → multiply both by 4 → 8:12
2:3 → multiply both by 5 → 10:15
2:3 → multiply both by 10 → 20:30

"This works because you are making more copies of the same group. The relationship stays the same."

**Step 3 — Show how to simplify by dividing.**

"We can also go in the other direction. If we have 12:18, we can divide both terms by the same number to find a simpler equivalent ratio."

12:18 → divide both by 2 → 6:9
12:18 → divide both by 3 → 4:6
12:18 → divide both by 6 → 2:3

"Dividing both terms by their GCF gives us the simplest form. The GCF of 12 and 18 is 6, so 2:3 is the simplest form."

**Step 4 — Test whether two ratios are equivalent.**

"How do you check if two ratios are equivalent? Method 1: Simplify both to simplest form and see if they match. Method 2: Check if you can multiply both terms of one ratio by the same number to get the other."

Example: Are 6:8 and 9:12 equivalent?
- Simplify 6:8: GCF = 2, so 3:4.
- Simplify 9:12: GCF = 3, so 3:4.
- Both simplify to 3:4, so YES, they are equivalent.

**Step 5 — Address the common additive error.**

"Important warning: You CANNOT make equivalent ratios by ADDING the same number to both terms."

Show: 2:3 → add 1 to each → 3:4. "Is 3:4 equivalent to 2:3? Let's check. 2:3 simplifies to... it's already in simplest form. 3:4 is also in simplest form. 2:3 and 3:4 are NOT the same. Adding does not work."

Use counters to show: "2 red for every 3 blue is NOT the same as 3 red for every 4 blue. In the first, blue has 1 more. In the second, blue still has 1 more. But the relationship has changed — the first is 'two-thirds as many red as blue,' the second is 'three-fourths as many red as blue.' These are different."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Generate three equivalent ratios.**

Start with the ratio 5:2.

- Multiply both by 2: 10:4.
- Multiply both by 3: 15:6.
- Multiply both by 7: 35:14.

All of these are equivalent to 5:2.

**Example B — Simplify a ratio to simplest form.**

Simplify 24:36.

- Find the GCF of 24 and 36. Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24. Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. GCF = 12.
- Divide both terms by 12: 24 / 12 = 2, 36 / 12 = 3.
- Simplest form: 2:3.

**Example C — Determine if two ratios are equivalent.**

Are 8:14 and 12:21 equivalent?

- Simplify 8:14: GCF of 8 and 14 is 2. 8/2 = 4, 14/2 = 7. Simplest form: 4:7.
- Simplify 12:21: GCF of 12 and 21 is 3. 12/3 = 4, 21/3 = 7. Simplest form: 4:7.
- Both simplify to 4:7. YES, they are equivalent.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. Write three equivalent ratios for 3:5.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Pick any three numbers to multiply both terms by. Try 2, 4, and 6."

2. Simplify the ratio 18:24 to simplest form.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is the GCF of 18 and 24? List the factors of each if needed."

3. Are 10:15 and 6:9 equivalent? Show how you know.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Try simplifying both ratios to their simplest form."

4. A recipe calls for 4 cups of flour and 6 cups of water. What is the ratio of flour to water in simplest form? If you triple the recipe, what is the new ratio?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "First simplify 4:6. Then think about what happens to the ratio when you triple."

5. Liam says that 5:8 and 10:15 are equivalent because "you just double the 5 to get 10." Is Liam correct? Explain.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "If you double the first term, what should happen to the second term for the ratios to be equivalent?"

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

1. Write two equivalent ratios for 4:9.

2. Are 12:16 and 15:20 equivalent? Show your work.

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to counters. Build the ratio physically (e.g., 4 red, 9 blue) and make multiple copies side by side. Have the student count and record each new ratio. Then practice the multiplication pattern: "What did we multiply by? Did we multiply BOTH terms?" If the student used addition instead of multiplication, explicitly demonstrate why addition fails using the counter model.

### If Mastery Is Met
Challenge the student with this problem: "The ratio of cats to dogs at a shelter is equivalent to 3:7. There are between 20 and 30 cats at the shelter. How many cats and dogs could there be? Find all possible answers."

## Extension (Optional)

1. **Ratio Chain:** Start with the ratio 2:5. Generate a chain of at least 6 equivalent ratios, going both larger (multiply) and smaller (if possible by dividing). What do you notice about the chain?

2. **Error Detective:** A student wrote this on a test: "6:10 = 7:11 because I added 1 to both." Write a clear explanation of why this is wrong, using both a mathematical argument and a real-world example.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Using addition instead of multiplication.** This is the single most important misconception to address in this lesson. Students may say 3:5 → 4:6 (add 1 to each). Always circle back to the concrete model: "Did you add one more of each, or did you make another copy of the whole group?"
- **Dividing the terms by different numbers.** A student might simplify 12:18 as 6:6 (dividing 12 by 2 and 18 by 3). Stress that you must divide BOTH terms by the SAME number.
- **Finding factors but not the GCF.** A student might divide 24:36 by 2 to get 12:18 and stop. This is a valid equivalent ratio but not simplest form. Ask: "Can you divide again? What is the biggest number both terms share?"

### Pacing Advice
This lesson is one of the most important in the unit. If the student struggles, do not rush — spend a second session on equivalent ratios. The additive-vs.-multiplicative distinction is a major conceptual hurdle that, once cleared, makes the rest of the unit much smoother. If the student finds this lesson easy, push into the Challenge problems and the Extension activities, which require reasoning backward from an equivalent ratio.
