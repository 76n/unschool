# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Writing Numerical Expressions

## Learning Objectives
- Translate word phrases into numerical expressions using addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.
- Write numerical expressions that include parentheses to indicate grouping.
- Understand exponent notation as repeated multiplication and evaluate simple powers.
- Translate numerical expressions back into word phrases.

## Prerequisites
- Fluency with the four basic operations on whole numbers.
- Familiarity with parentheses in expressions (Grade 5).
- Understanding of multiplication as repeated addition.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Index cards with word phrases on one side and expressions on the other (for matching activity).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Paper and pencil.

**Standards:** 6.EE.1 — Write and evaluate numerical expressions involving whole-number exponents. 6.EE.2a — Write expressions that record operations with numbers.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of translating simple word phrases to math operations and build toward more complex expressions.

1. Write on the board: "the sum of 8 and 5." Ask: "How would you write this as a math expression?" (8 + 5)
2. Write: "12 less than 20." Ask: "What expression is this?" (20 - 12) Ask: "Why is it 20 - 12 and not 12 - 20?" Guide students to see that "less than" reverses the order.
3. Write: "the product of 6 and 7." (6 x 7)
4. Say: "Today we are going to practice turning words into math — and we are also going to learn a brand-new way to write repeated multiplication, called exponents."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Numerical expression** — A mathematical phrase that contains numbers and operation symbols but no equal sign.
- **Sum** — The result of addition.
- **Difference** — The result of subtraction.
- **Product** — The result of multiplication.
- **Quotient** — The result of division.
- **Exponent** — A small raised number that tells how many times to multiply the base by itself.
- **Base** — The number being multiplied when using an exponent.
- **Power** — An expression with a base and an exponent (e.g., 2^3 is read "2 to the third power").
- **Squared** — Raised to the second power (e.g., 5^2 = 5 squared).
- **Cubed** — Raised to the third power (e.g., 4^3 = 4 cubed).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Review key phrases for each operation.**

Display a reference chart:

| Operation | Key Phrases |
|-----------|-------------|
| Addition (+) | sum, plus, more than, increased by, added to, total |
| Subtraction (-) | difference, minus, less than, decreased by, subtracted from, fewer than |
| Multiplication (x) | product, times, multiplied by, groups of, twice, triple |
| Division (÷) | quotient, divided by, split into, per, shared equally |

Say: "These phrases are like a translation guide. When you see 'the sum of 8 and 3,' you know to write 8 + 3. When you see 'the product of 4 and 9,' you write 4 x 9."

Say: "But be careful with subtraction phrases. 'Ten less than 25' means 25 - 10, not 10 - 25. The phrase 'less than' flips the order. Think of it this way: if someone says 'I have 10 fewer dollars than you,' the subtraction starts with your amount."

**Step 2 — Multi-operation phrases and grouping.**

Write on the board: "the sum of 6 and the product of 3 and 4."

Say: "This phrase tells us two things: we need the product of 3 and 4, and then we add 6 to it. We write: 6 + (3 x 4). The parentheses show that we find the product first."

Write: "twice the difference of 10 and 3."

Say: "The difference of 10 and 3 is 10 - 3. 'Twice' means multiply by 2. So we write: 2 x (10 - 3). The parentheses are important — without them, the meaning changes."

**Step 3 — Introduce exponents as repeated multiplication.**

Write on the board: 2 x 2 x 2.

Say: "When we multiply the same number by itself several times, we can use a shortcut called an exponent. Instead of writing 2 x 2 x 2, we write 2^3. The 2 is the base — the number being multiplied. The 3 is the exponent — it tells us how many times the base appears as a factor."

Write the key examples:

- 2^3 = 2 x 2 x 2 = 8 (read: "2 to the third power" or "2 cubed")
- 5^2 = 5 x 5 = 25 (read: "5 to the second power" or "5 squared")
- 3^4 = 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 = 81 (read: "3 to the fourth power")
- 10^3 = 10 x 10 x 10 = 1,000 (read: "10 cubed")

Say: "A very common mistake is to multiply the base by the exponent. For example, some people see 3^4 and think 3 x 4 = 12. But that is wrong. The exponent tells you how many times to write the base as a factor: 3^4 = 3 x 3 x 3 x 3 = 81. Always write out the expanded form if you are unsure."

Point out the special case: "Any number to the first power is itself: 7^1 = 7. And we will learn later that any number to the zero power equals 1, but we will not worry about that today."

### Worked Examples

**Example A: Word phrase to numerical expression.**

Write the numerical expression for: "the quotient of 36 and the sum of 2 and 4."

- The sum of 2 and 4 is (2 + 4).
- The quotient of 36 and that sum is 36 ÷ (2 + 4).
- Answer: 36 ÷ (2 + 4).

**Example B: Numerical expression with exponents.**

Write the numerical expression for: "3 squared plus the product of 5 and 4."

- 3 squared = 3^2.
- The product of 5 and 4 = 5 x 4.
- The full expression: 3^2 + 5 x 4.
- Answer: 3^2 + 5 x 4.

**Example C: Expression to word phrase.**

Write a word phrase for: 2 x (8 - 3).

- (8 - 3) is "the difference of 8 and 3."
- 2 x (8 - 3) is "twice the difference of 8 and 3" or "the product of 2 and the difference of 8 and 3."
- Answer: "twice the difference of 8 and 3."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Have the student write the expression before you confirm.

1. Write a numerical expression for: "the sum of 15 and 9."
   - Answer: 15 + 9

2. Write a numerical expression for: "7 less than 20."
   - Answer: 20 - 7

3. Write a numerical expression for: "the product of 8 and the difference of 12 and 5."
   - Answer: 8 x (12 - 5)

4. Write 4 x 4 x 4 x 4 x 4 using an exponent.
   - Answer: 4^5

5. Evaluate 2^4.
   - Answer: 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 = 16

6. Write a word phrase for: 24 ÷ (3 + 5).
   - Answer: "the quotient of 24 and the sum of 3 and 5"

**Facilitator tip:** For problem 2, if the student writes 7 - 20, pause and ask: "If you have 20 apples and someone takes 7, do you start with 20 or 7?" Help the student see that "less than" means subtract from the larger number.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A: Translating Word Phrases to Expressions

Write a numerical expression for each word phrase.

1. The sum of 14 and 23
2. The difference of 50 and 18
3. 9 less than 40
4. The product of 7 and 11
5. The quotient of 72 and 8
6. Triple the sum of 6 and 4
7. The difference of 30 and the product of 3 and 8
8. The quotient of the sum of 10 and 14, and 6

### Part B: Exponents

Write each expression using an exponent, or expand the exponent and evaluate.

9. 6 x 6 x 6
10. 10 x 10 x 10 x 10
11. Evaluate: 5^3
12. Evaluate: 2^6
13. Evaluate: 9^2

### Part C: Word Problems

14. Marco says "5 cubed is 15." Is he correct? Explain why or why not.

15. Write a word phrase that matches the expression 4 x (9 + 3). Then find a different word phrase that also matches the same expression.

### Answer Key

**Part A**

1. 14 + 23
2. 50 - 18
3. 40 - 9
4. 7 x 11
5. 72 ÷ 8
6. 3 x (6 + 4)
7. 30 - (3 x 8) or 30 - 3 x 8
8. (10 + 14) ÷ 6

**Part B**

9. 6^3
10. 10^4
11. 5^3 = 5 x 5 x 5 = 125
12. 2^6 = 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 x 2 = 64
13. 9^2 = 9 x 9 = 81

**Part C**

14. Marco is incorrect. 5 cubed means 5^3 = 5 x 5 x 5 = 125, not 5 x 3 = 15. The exponent tells you how many times to use 5 as a factor, not to multiply 5 by 3.

15. Possible word phrases: "4 times the sum of 9 and 3" or "the product of 4 and the sum of 9 and 3." Another option: "the sum of 9 and 3, multiplied by 4."

## Assessment Check

Write a numerical expression for: "the sum of 6 squared and the product of 2 and 5."

**Target response:** 6^2 + 2 x 5. (The student should write the exponent correctly and place the operations in the correct order. If the student writes (6^2 + 2) x 5 or another incorrect grouping, revisit the idea that "the sum of A and B" means A + B where A = 6^2 and B = 2 x 5.)
