# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 8

## Topic
Comparing and Ordering Rational Numbers

## Learning Objectives
- Compare any mix of positive and negative fractions, decimals, and integers using inequality symbols (<, >, =).
- Convert rational numbers to a common form (common denominator or decimals) to facilitate comparison.
- Apply the rules: any positive number is greater than any negative number; between two negatives, the one closer to zero is greater.
- Order sets of 5-6 mixed rational numbers from least to greatest and greatest to least.

## Prerequisites
- Plotting rational numbers on a number line (Lesson 7).
- Understanding of absolute value (Lesson 6).
- Comparing and ordering integers (Lesson 5).
- Converting between fractions and decimals (Grade 5).
- Finding common denominators (Grade 5).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Number line handouts (horizontal, -5 to +5).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards (for a sorting activity in Guided Practice).
- Calculator (optional, for checking decimal conversions).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 6.NS.7a — Interpret statements of inequality as statements about the relative position of two numbers on a number line diagram. 6.NS.7b — Write, interpret, and explain statements of order for rational numbers in real-world contexts.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review comparing integers and connect to the need for comparing non-integer rational numbers.

1. Compare using < or >:
   - (a) -7 ___ -3
   - (b) 4 ___ -9
   - (c) -1 ___ 0

2. Ask: "How did you decide?" (On a number line, the number farther right is greater.)

3. Now present: "Which is greater: -1/2 or -2/3? This is harder because they are not integers. Today we will learn strategies for comparing and ordering any kind of rational number — fractions, decimals, integers, and mixes of all three."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Common form** — Converting numbers to the same type (all decimals or all fractions with a common denominator) so they can be compared directly.
- **Common denominator** — A shared denominator used when comparing or ordering fractions.
- **Inequality** — A mathematical statement that compares two values using <, >, or = (e.g., -3/4 > -1).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — State the two key rules.**

Write on the board:

"Rule 1: Any positive number is greater than any negative number."

"Rule 2: For two negative numbers, the one closer to zero is greater."

"These rules work for ALL rational numbers — integers, fractions, and decimals."

Example: "Is 0.1 greater than -1,000? Yes. Rule 1: any positive beats any negative, no matter how large the negative number is."

Example: "Is -1/4 greater than -3/4? Yes. Rule 2: -1/4 is closer to zero. On the number line, -1/4 is to the right of -3/4."

**Step 2 — Strategy: Convert to a common form.**

"When the numbers are in different forms — some fractions, some decimals — it is hard to compare them directly. The solution is to convert them all to the same form."

"Two options:
1. Convert everything to decimals.
2. Convert everything to fractions with a common denominator."

"Let's compare -2/5 and -0.35."

Convert -2/5 to a decimal: -2/5 = -0.4.

Now compare: -0.4 and -0.35. Both are negative. Which is closer to zero? -0.35 is closer to zero (0.35 < 0.40 in absolute value). So -0.35 > -0.4.

Therefore: -0.35 > -2/5.

**Step 3 — Strategy: Convert to fractions with a common denominator.**

"Let's compare 3/8 and 1/3."

Common denominator: LCD of 8 and 3 is 24.

3/8 = 9/24. 1/3 = 8/24.

9/24 > 8/24, so 3/8 > 1/3.

"This strategy also works for negatives. Compare -5/6 and -7/8."

-5/6 = -20/24. -7/8 = -21/24.

Compare: -20/24 and -21/24. -20/24 is closer to zero (20 < 21 in absolute value). So -20/24 > -21/24.

Therefore: -5/6 > -7/8.

**Step 4 — Ordering a mixed set.**

"To order a set of mixed rational numbers, convert everything to the same form first, then arrange them."

Order from least to greatest: 1/2, -0.75, -1/3, 0, -2.

Step 1: Convert to decimals. 1/2 = 0.5, -0.75 stays, -1/3 ≈ -0.333, 0 stays, -2 stays.

Step 2: Arrange: -2, -0.75, -0.333..., 0, 0.5.

Step 3: Write in original forms: -2, -0.75, -1/3, 0, 1/2.

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Comparing a fraction and a decimal.**

"Compare -3/4 and -0.8."

- Convert -3/4 to a decimal: -3/4 = -0.75.
- Compare -0.75 and -0.8. Both are negative.
- -0.75 is closer to zero than -0.8 (because 0.75 < 0.8).
- So -0.75 > -0.8, meaning -3/4 > -0.8.

**Example B — Ordering five rational numbers.**

"Order from least to greatest: -1 1/2, 2/3, -0.25, 1.4, -5/6."

- Convert to decimals: -1.5, 0.667, -0.25, 1.4, -0.833.
- Arrange from least to greatest: -1.5, -0.833, -0.25, 0.667, 1.4.
- In original forms: -1 1/2, -5/6, -0.25, 2/3, 1.4.

**Example C — Real-world context.**

"The temperature at midnight was -2.5°F. At dawn it was -7/4°F. Which time was colder?"

- Convert -7/4 to a decimal: -7/4 = -1.75.
- Compare: -2.5 and -1.75. Both are negative. -2.5 is farther from zero.
- -2.5 < -1.75, so -2.5°F is the colder temperature.
- Midnight was colder.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. Compare using < or >: -2/3 ___ -3/5. Show your work.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Convert both to decimals, or find a common denominator. LCD of 3 and 5 is 15."

2. Compare using < or >: -1.2 ___ -6/5.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Convert -6/5 to a decimal. What do you notice?"

3. Order from least to greatest: 3/4, -1, -0.5, 0.1, -3/8.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Start by separating positives and negatives. Then order within each group."

4. A checking account shows these transactions: -$3.50, $1.25, -$0.75, -$2, $4.50. Order the transactions from the greatest loss to the greatest gain.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Losses are negative, gains are positive. Order from least (most negative) to greatest (most positive)."

5. Sorting activity: Write each of these numbers on an index card: -2/3, 1.5, -1.75, 0, 3/8, -1, 5/4. Have the student physically arrange the cards from least to greatest.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Convert tricky ones to decimals: -2/3 ≈ -0.67, 3/8 = 0.375, 5/4 = 1.25. Then order all the decimals."
   - *If the student struggles with negative comparisons:* "For negative numbers, think about distance from zero. The farther from zero, the smaller the number."

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A: Comparing Pairs (On-Level)

1. Insert <, >, or =:
   - (a) -1/2 ___ -0.5
   - (b) -3/4 ___ -2/3
   - (c) 0.6 ___ -4
   - (d) -1.25 ___ -5/4
   - (e) -7/10 ___ -0.65

2. Which is greater: -3 1/2 or -3.4? Explain your reasoning.

3. A diver reaches -8.5 meters. A submarine is at -17/2 meters. Are they at the same depth? Show your work.

### Part B: Ordering Sets (Stretch)

4. Order from least to greatest: -2.5, 1/3, -3/4, 2.1, -1 1/6.

5. Order from greatest to least: 5/8, -0.9, -1/2, 0, -3/10, 1.

6. Six students recorded the change in their account balances this week:
   - Ava: -$1.50
   - Ben: $2.75
   - Cara: -$0.25
   - Dan: -$3/4
   - Eve: $1/2
   - Finn: -$1.80

   Order the students from the greatest loss to the greatest gain.

### Part C: Reasoning (Challenge)

7. True or false: If |a| > |b|, and both a and b are negative, then a < b. Explain with an example.

8. Find a rational number that is between -2/3 and -1/2. Prove that your number is between them by converting all three to a common form.

9. Xavier claims that -0.3 is greater than -1/4 because 0.3 > 0.25. Is his reasoning correct? Explain.

### Interleaved Review
*(Reviewing: absolute value — Lesson 6)*

10. Evaluate: |-4.5| + |3/4|.

11. Which has the greater absolute value: -2 2/3 or 2.5? By how much?

### Answer Key

**Part A**

1. (a) -1/2 = -0.5. Answer: =.
   (b) -3/4 = -0.75, -2/3 ≈ -0.667. -0.75 < -0.667, so -3/4 < -2/3.
   (c) 0.6 > -4. (Any positive > any negative.)
   (d) -1.25 = -5/4. Answer: =.
   (e) -7/10 = -0.7. -0.7 < -0.65, so -7/10 < -0.65.

2. -3 1/2 = -3.5. Compare -3.5 and -3.4. -3.4 is closer to zero, so -3.4 > -3.5. Therefore -3.4 is greater than -3 1/2.

3. -17/2 = -8.5. Yes, they are at the same depth: -8.5 = -17/2.

**Part B**

4. Convert to decimals: -2.5, 0.333, -0.75, 2.1, -1.167. Order: -2.5, -1 1/6, -3/4, 1/3, 2.1.

5. Convert to decimals: 0.625, -0.9, -0.5, 0, -0.3, 1. Order greatest to least: 1, 5/8, 0, -3/10, -1/2, -0.9.

6. Convert all to decimals: Ava = -1.50, Ben = 2.75, Cara = -0.25, Dan = -0.75, Eve = 0.50, Finn = -1.80. From greatest loss to greatest gain: Finn (-$1.80), Ava (-$1.50), Dan (-$0.75), Cara (-$0.25), Eve ($0.50), Ben ($2.75).

**Part C**

7. True. If |a| > |b| and both are negative, then a is farther from zero than b. Farther from zero in the negative direction means smaller. Example: a = -5, b = -3. |a| = 5 > |b| = 3, and -5 < -3.

8. Answers vary. Example: -7/12. Convert to common denominator (LCD = 12): -2/3 = -8/12, -1/2 = -6/12, -7/12 is between them because -8/12 < -7/12 < -6/12.

9. Xavier's reasoning is incorrect. He compared the absolute values correctly (0.3 > 0.25), but for negative numbers, greater absolute value means the number is farther from zero and therefore LESS, not greater. -0.3 < -0.25, so -0.3 < -1/4. Xavier has the comparison backward.

**Interleaved Review**

10. |-4.5| + |3/4| = 4.5 + 0.75 = 5.25.

11. |-2 2/3| = 2 2/3 ≈ 2.667. |2.5| = 2.5. 2.667 > 2.5, so -2 2/3 has the greater absolute value. Difference: 2 2/3 - 2 1/2 = 2 4/6 - 2 3/6 = 1/6 (approximately 0.167).

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket

1. Compare using < or >: -5/8 ___ -0.7. Show your work.

2. Order from least to greatest: 1.5, -2/3, -1.25, 0, 3/4.

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

**Exit Ticket Answers:**
1. -5/8 = -0.625. Compare -0.625 and -0.7. -0.625 is closer to zero, so -5/8 > -0.7.
2. Convert: 1.5, -0.667, -1.25, 0, 0.75. Order: -1.25, -2/3, 0, 3/4, 1.5.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Provide a number line from -2 to +2 with tick marks at every 0.25. Have the student plot each number on the number line first, then read the order directly from left to right. This reduces the comparison to a visual task. Once the student is comfortable with the visual approach, practice the conversion-to-decimals strategy with simpler numbers (e.g., halves and fourths only) before returning to the full set.

### If Mastery Is Met
Present: "Create a set of six rational numbers — at least one fraction, one decimal, one integer, one mixed number, and one negative — such that when ordered from least to greatest, no two consecutive numbers have the same sign (the signs alternate: negative, positive, negative, positive...). Is this always possible? Why or why not?"

## Extension

1. **Number Line Squeeze Game:** Pick two rational numbers (e.g., -1/3 and -1/4). Take turns naming a rational number that is between the two most recent numbers. The player who cannot find another number between them loses. (Spoiler: this game never ends, because between any two rational numbers there is always another one.)

2. **Financial Literacy Connection:** Find the change in stock price for five companies over one day (positive or negative values). Convert any fractional changes to decimals, then order the companies from worst performer to best performer. Explain what the ordering means in real-world terms.
