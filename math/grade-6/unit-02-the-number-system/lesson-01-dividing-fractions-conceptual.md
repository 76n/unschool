# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 2, Lesson 1

## Topic
Dividing Fractions: Conceptual Foundation

## Learning Objectives
- Interpret division of a whole number by a fraction as "how many of this fraction fit into that whole number?" (measurement model).
- Use fraction strips and number lines to model and solve whole-number-by-fraction division problems.
- Solve division problems of the form whole number ÷ unit fraction and whole number ÷ non-unit fraction using visual reasoning.
- Explain why the quotient when dividing by a fraction less than 1 is greater than the dividend.

## Prerequisites
- Multiplying fractions by whole numbers (Grade 5).
- Dividing whole numbers by unit fractions and unit fractions by whole numbers (5.NF.7).
- Understanding fractions on a number line (placement, equivalence).
- Familiarity with fraction strips or area models for representing fractions.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Fraction strip handouts (strips divided into halves, thirds, fourths, sixths, and eighths).
- Number line handouts (0 to 6, marked in thirds and sixths).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.
- Colored pencils (optional, for shading fraction strips).

**Standards:** 6.NS.1 — Interpret and compute quotients of fractions, and solve word problems involving division of fractions by fractions, e.g., by using visual fraction models and equations to represent the problem.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of division as measurement ("how many groups?") and build intuition for dividing by fractions.

1. Ask: "If you have 12 cookies and you put 3 cookies in each bag, how many bags can you fill?" (12 ÷ 3 = 4.) "How did you think about that?" Draw out the language: "I'm asking how many groups of 3 fit into 12."
2. Now ask: "What if each bag gets only 1 cookie? How many bags?" (12 ÷ 1 = 12.) "And what if each bag gets 6 cookies?" (12 ÷ 6 = 2.)
3. Say: "Notice that the smaller the amount per bag, the more bags we need. What if each serving were only half a cookie? How many half-cookie servings are in 12 cookies?" Let the student think. (12 ÷ 1/2 = 24.)
4. Say: "Today we are going to explore this idea: when you divide by a fraction, you end up with MORE groups, not fewer. We'll use fraction strips and number lines to see exactly why."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Measurement model of division** — A way of thinking about division that asks: "How many groups of this size fit into that amount?"
- **Dividend** — The number being divided (the total amount you start with).
- **Divisor** — The number you are dividing by (the size of each group).
- **Quotient** — The result of division (the number of groups).
- **Unit fraction** — A fraction with a numerator of 1 (e.g., 1/2, 1/3, 1/4).
- **Non-unit fraction** — A fraction with a numerator greater than 1 (e.g., 2/3, 3/4).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Model "How many 1/3s fit into 1?" with fraction strips.**

Take a whole fraction strip (representing 1). Below it, lay out third-strips (each representing 1/3). Ask: "How many 1/3 strips does it take to cover the whole strip?" Count together: three 1/3 strips cover exactly one whole. Write: 1 ÷ 1/3 = 3.

Say: "Division asks how many of the divisor fit into the dividend. One whole divided into groups of 1/3 each gives us 3 groups."

**Step 2 — Extend to "How many 1/3s fit into 2?"**

Place two whole fraction strips end to end. Below them, lay out 1/3 strips. Ask: "How many 1/3 pieces fit into 2 wholes?" Count: six pieces. Write: 2 ÷ 1/3 = 6.

Now show the same problem on a number line from 0 to 2. Mark every 1/3: 0, 1/3, 2/3, 1, 4/3, 5/3, 2. Count the jumps of 1/3 from 0 to 2: six jumps. Confirm: 2 ÷ 1/3 = 6.

Ask: "Is it surprising that 2 ÷ 1/3 = 6, which is bigger than 2? Why does that make sense?" Guide toward: "Because each group is very small (only 1/3), you can fit a lot of groups into 2."

**Step 3 — Move to a non-unit fraction: "How many 2/3s fit into 2?"**

Using the same number line (0 to 2, marked in thirds), now count jumps of 2/3: 0 to 2/3 (one jump), 2/3 to 4/3 (two jumps), 4/3 to 2 (three jumps). Write: 2 ÷ 2/3 = 3.

With fraction strips: lay out two whole strips. This time, group the 1/3 strips in pairs (each pair = 2/3). You had 6 third-strips; grouping in pairs gives 6 ÷ 2 = 3 groups. Confirm: 2 ÷ 2/3 = 3.

Say: "Notice the two-step reasoning: first, 2 ÷ 1/3 = 6 (how many thirds in 2), then 6 ÷ 2 = 3 (pair them up into groups of two-thirds). This two-step idea is the heart of why the 'invert and multiply' algorithm works — which we will learn in the next lesson."

**Step 4 — Extend to a larger dividend: "How many 2/3s fit into 4?"**

Draw a number line from 0 to 4, marked in thirds. Count jumps of 2/3:

- 0 → 2/3 (1)
- 2/3 → 4/3 (2)
- 4/3 → 6/3 = 2 (3)
- 2 → 8/3 (4)
- 8/3 → 10/3 (5)
- 10/3 → 12/3 = 4 (6)

Write: 4 ÷ 2/3 = 6. Confirm with the two-step reasoning: 4 ÷ 1/3 = 12 (twelve thirds in 4), then 12 ÷ 2 = 6 groups of two-thirds.

Say: "Every time we divide by a fraction less than 1, our answer is larger than the number we started with. This makes sense: if each group is a fraction of a whole, more groups fit."

**Step 5 — Address remainders/fractional quotients (preview).**

Ask: "How many 1/3s fit into 1/2?" Lay a 1/2 strip next to a 1/3 strip. The 1/3 strip fits once with some left over. The leftover portion is half of a 1/3 strip, which is 1/6. So 1/2 ÷ 1/3 = 1 1/2 (one full 1/3 piece, and the remaining portion is half of another 1/3 piece). Write: 1/2 ÷ 1/3 = 3/2.

Say: "We can also get fractional answers. We won't focus on these today, but you'll see them starting in the next lesson."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Whole number ÷ unit fraction.**

How many 1/4s fit into 3?

- Draw a number line from 0 to 3, divided into fourths.
- Count: 0 to 1/4, 1/4 to 2/4, ..., 11/4 to 12/4 = 3. That is 12 jumps.
- 3 ÷ 1/4 = 12.
- Check: 12 x 1/4 = 12/4 = 3. Correct.

**Example B — Whole number ÷ non-unit fraction.**

How many 3/4s fit into 3?

- On the same number line (0 to 3, divided into fourths), count jumps of 3/4: 0 → 3/4 (1), 3/4 → 6/4 (2), 6/4 → 9/4 (3), 9/4 → 12/4 = 3 (4).
- 3 ÷ 3/4 = 4.
- Two-step reasoning: 3 ÷ 1/4 = 12 fourths, then 12 ÷ 3 = 4 groups of three-fourths.
- Check: 4 x 3/4 = 12/4 = 3. Correct.

**Example C — Larger problem with the two-step model.**

How many 2/5s fit into 6?

- Step 1: 6 ÷ 1/5 = 30 (thirty fifths in 6).
- Step 2: 30 ÷ 2 = 15 groups of two-fifths.
- 6 ÷ 2/5 = 15.
- Check: 15 x 2/5 = 30/5 = 6. Correct.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. For each, have the student draw a number line or use fraction strips before computing.

1. How many 1/2s fit into 5?
   - *Facilitator tip:* "Draw a number line from 0 to 5. Mark every 1/2. Count the jumps."

2. How many 1/6s fit into 2?
   - *Facilitator tip:* "Use fraction strips: lay out two whole strips, then cover them with 1/6 pieces. How many do you need?"

3. How many 3/4s fit into 6?
   - *Facilitator tip:* "First, how many 1/4s are in 6? Then, group them into sets of 3."

4. How many 2/5s fit into 4?
   - *Facilitator tip:* "Use the two-step method: first divide by 1/5, then divide by 2."

5. A ribbon is 3 feet long. You need pieces that are each 1/4 of a foot. How many pieces can you cut?
   - *Facilitator tip:* "What division problem does this represent? Draw a number line if it helps."

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — Unit Fraction Divisors (Number Line or Fraction Strip)

1. How many 1/3s fit into 4? Draw a number line to show your work.

2. How many 1/8s fit into 3? Explain your reasoning.

3. A rope is 5 meters long. It is cut into pieces that are each 1/4 of a meter. How many pieces are there?

### Part B — Non-Unit Fraction Divisors (Two-Step Method)

4. How many 2/3s fit into 6? Show the two-step reasoning.

5. How many 3/8s fit into 3? Show the two-step reasoning.

6. A chef has 4 cups of sugar. Each batch of cookies requires 2/5 of a cup. How many batches can the chef make?

### Part C — Reasoning and Explanation

7. Kayla says, "6 ÷ 1/2 = 3 because half of 6 is 3." Explain why Kayla is wrong. What is the correct answer?

8. Without computing, determine which is larger: 8 ÷ 1/3 or 8 ÷ 2/3. Explain your reasoning.

9. A marathon runner completes a 2-mile segment of her run. If each stride covers 1/3 of a meter (approximately), about how many strides are in 2 meters? Now, if she groups her strides in pairs (2/3 of a meter per pair), how many pairs are in 2 meters?

### Answer Key

**Part A**

1. 4 ÷ 1/3 = 12. (Number line from 0 to 4 with jumps of 1/3; count 12 jumps.)
2. 3 ÷ 1/8 = 24. (Each whole has 8 eighths; 3 wholes have 3 x 8 = 24 eighths.)
3. 5 ÷ 1/4 = 20 pieces. (Each meter has 4 quarter-pieces; 5 meters have 5 x 4 = 20.)

**Part B**

4. 6 ÷ 2/3: Step 1: 6 ÷ 1/3 = 18. Step 2: 18 ÷ 2 = 9. Answer: 9.
5. 3 ÷ 3/8: Step 1: 3 ÷ 1/8 = 24. Step 2: 24 ÷ 3 = 8. Answer: 8.
6. 4 ÷ 2/5: Step 1: 4 ÷ 1/5 = 20. Step 2: 20 ÷ 2 = 10 batches.

**Part C**

7. Kayla confused "half of 6" (which is multiplication: 6 x 1/2 = 3) with "how many halves fit into 6" (which is division: 6 ÷ 1/2 = 12). The correct answer is 12.
8. 8 ÷ 1/3 is larger. Dividing by 1/3 creates smaller groups than dividing by 2/3, so more groups fit. Specifically, 8 ÷ 1/3 = 24 and 8 ÷ 2/3 = 12.
9. 2 ÷ 1/3 = 6 strides. 2 ÷ 2/3 = 3 pairs. (6 strides grouped in pairs of 2 gives 3 pairs.)

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket

1. How many 1/4s fit into 3? Draw a number line or fraction strip to show your answer.

2. How many 2/3s fit into 4? Use the two-step method and show your work.

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct with supporting work = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors and revisit the step where confusion occurred.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach using fraction strips with hands-on manipulation.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to fraction strips. Have the student physically place 1/3 strips on top of whole strips, counting aloud as each piece is placed. Repeat with different whole numbers (1, 2, 3) until the pattern "multiply the whole number by the denominator" becomes clear. Then rebuild the non-unit fraction step: "Now group your pieces by 2. How many groups?"

### If Mastery Is Met
Pose: "How many 3/5s fit into 3?" Challenge the student to solve it with the two-step method and verify by drawing a number line. Then ask: "Will the answer to any-whole-number ÷ any-fraction-less-than-1 always be greater than the whole number you started with? Why?"
