# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 4, Lesson 5

## Introduction to 3D Shapes and Nets

### Learning Objectives
1. Identify and name common three-dimensional shapes: rectangular prisms, triangular prisms, and pyramids.
2. Count the faces, edges, and vertices of prisms and pyramids.
3. Unfold three-dimensional shapes into two-dimensional nets.
4. Determine whether a given net will fold into a specific 3D shape.

### Prerequisites
- Identify and classify two-dimensional shapes (rectangles, triangles, squares)
- Calculate the area of rectangles and triangles (Lessons 1-2)
- Understand the difference between 2D and 3D

### Materials
- Geometric solids set (rectangular prisms, triangular prisms, square pyramids, triangular pyramids)
- Net templates on cardstock (one set per student)
- Scissors
- Tape
- Grid paper
- Colored pencils

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Display images of real-world objects:**

1. A cereal box
2. A tent (triangular prism shape)
3. The Great Pyramid

"What shape is each of these objects? Discuss with your partner."

Allow 2 minutes for discussion, then collect responses.

"These are not flat shapes — they take up space. They have length, width, and height. Today we enter the world of three-dimensional shapes."

---

## Direct Instruction (15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Three-dimensional (3D):** A shape that has length, width, and height (depth); it takes up space.
- **Face:** A flat surface on a 3D shape. Each face is a 2D shape.
- **Edge:** A line segment where two faces meet.
- **Vertex (plural: vertices):** A point where two or more edges meet; a corner.
- **Prism:** A 3D shape with two identical, parallel bases connected by rectangular faces.
- **Pyramid:** A 3D shape with one base and triangular faces that meet at a single point (the apex).
- **Net:** A 2D pattern that can be folded up to form a 3D shape.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Exploring 3D Shapes (Concrete):**

Hold up a rectangular prism (or a cereal box).

"This is a rectangular prism. Let me show you its parts."

Trace a face: "This flat surface is a face. How many faces does it have?"

Students count: 6 faces.

Run a finger along an edge: "Where two faces meet, we get an edge. How many edges?"

Students count: 12 edges.

Point to a corner: "Where edges meet at a corner, we get a vertex. How many vertices?"

Students count: 8 vertices.

Record on the board:

| Shape | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|-------|-------|-------|----------|
| Rectangular prism | 6 | 12 | 8 |

Repeat with a triangular prism:

"This is a triangular prism. The two bases are triangles, and the sides are rectangles."

| Shape | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|-------|-------|-------|----------|
| Rectangular prism | 6 | 12 | 8 |
| Triangular prism | 5 | 9 | 6 |

Repeat with a square pyramid:

"This is a square pyramid. It has one square base and four triangular faces that meet at a point — the apex."

| Shape | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|-------|-------|-------|----------|
| Rectangular prism | 6 | 12 | 8 |
| Triangular prism | 5 | 9 | 6 |
| Square pyramid | 5 | 8 | 5 |

**Step 2 — Unfolding to Nets (Representational):**

"Now I am going to do something interesting. Imagine you could cut along some edges of this cereal box and unfold it completely flat. What would you get?"

Take a cereal box that has been pre-cut along edges. Slowly unfold it.

"This flat pattern is called a net. A net is a 2D representation of a 3D shape. Every face of the 3D shape appears as a flat piece in the net."

Show the unfolded rectangular prism net on the board. Label each face.

"Count the rectangles in this net. How many?" — Six. "That matches the six faces of the rectangular prism."

**Step 3 — Nets of Different Shapes:**

Show a triangular prism net: two triangles and three rectangles.

"How many faces does a triangular prism have? Five. And the net has five pieces — two triangles and three rectangles."

Show a square pyramid net: one square and four triangles.

"How many faces does the square pyramid have? Five. The net has one square and four triangles."

**Step 4 — Valid vs. Invalid Nets:**

Show two nets — one that folds into a cube and one that does not (e.g., four squares in a row with one on each end of the same side).

"Not every arrangement of shapes is a valid net. A valid net must fold without overlapping and without gaps. Let us check: does this net fold into a cube?"

Have students try mentally folding or use the cut-out versions to test.

### Worked Example A

**Problem:** A rectangular prism has 6 faces. Describe the shapes and sizes of each face if the prism is 5 cm long, 3 cm wide, and 2 cm tall.

- Front and back faces: 5 cm x 2 cm rectangles
- Top and bottom faces: 5 cm x 3 cm rectangles
- Left and right faces: 3 cm x 2 cm rectangles

"The faces come in matching pairs — opposite faces are identical."

### Worked Example B

**Problem:** How many faces, edges, and vertices does a triangular pyramid (tetrahedron) have?

- Faces: 4 (all triangles — one base and three sides)
- Edges: 6
- Vertices: 4

"Notice: a triangular pyramid has all triangular faces. A square pyramid has one square face and four triangular faces."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

### Activity 1 — Build from Nets

"Each of you has a net template for a rectangular prism. Cut it out along the outer edges, fold along the inner lines, and tape it together."

Allow 5 minutes for cutting, folding, and taping.

"Hold up your completed shape. How many faces does it have? Touch each face and count."

Repeat with the triangular prism net if time allows.

### Activity 2 — Match the Net to the Shape

"I will show you four nets on the board. Match each net to the correct 3D shape."

| Net | Shape |
|-----|-------|
| Net 1: six rectangles arranged in a cross pattern | **Rectangular prism** |
| Net 2: two triangles and three rectangles in a row | **Triangular prism** |
| Net 3: one square with four triangles attached to its sides | **Square pyramid** |
| Net 4: four triangles arranged around a central triangle | **Triangular pyramid** |

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 — Developing

1. Name the 3D shape: two triangular bases and three rectangular faces. → **Triangular prism**
2. Name the 3D shape: six rectangular faces. → **Rectangular prism**
3. A rectangular prism has ______ faces, ______ edges, and ______ vertices. → **6, 12, 8**
4. A triangular prism has ______ faces, ______ edges, and ______ vertices. → **5, 9, 6**
5. Draw a net for a cube (a special rectangular prism where all faces are squares). → **Six squares arranged in a valid net pattern (e.g., cross shape)**

### Tier 2 — Proficient

1. A square pyramid has ______ faces, ______ edges, and ______ vertices. → **5, 8, 5**
2. Describe the shapes and sizes of each face in a triangular prism where the triangular bases have sides of 3 cm, 4 cm, and 5 cm, and the prism is 10 cm long. → **2 right triangles (3-4-5), 3 rectangles (10x3, 10x4, 10x5)**
3. A net has 2 triangles and 3 rectangles. What 3D shape does it form? → **Triangular prism**
4. A net has 1 rectangle and 4 triangles. Could this be a valid net? If so, what shape? → **No — a square pyramid needs 1 square base, not a rectangle (unless the rectangle is a square). If the rectangle is a square, then yes — square pyramid.**
5. Draw two different valid nets for the same rectangular prism that is 4 cm x 3 cm x 2 cm. → **Student draws two different unfolding arrangements of 6 rectangles.**
6. Look at this pattern of squares: four squares in a straight line with one square attached to the second square on the left. Is this a valid net for a cube? Why or why not? → **Yes/No — student must mentally fold and check for overlaps.**

### Tier 3 — Advanced

1. Is there a pattern in the number of faces, edges, and vertices of prisms? Fill in the table and look for a rule.

| Shape | Base | Faces | Edges | Vertices |
|-------|------|-------|-------|----------|
| Triangular prism | Triangle (3 sides) | 5 | 9 | 6 |
| Rectangular prism | Rectangle (4 sides) | 6 | 12 | 8 |
| Pentagonal prism | Pentagon (5 sides) | 7 | 15 | 10 |
| Hexagonal prism | Hexagon (6 sides) | 8 | 18 | 12 |

**Rule: For a prism with an n-sided base: Faces = n + 2, Edges = 3n, Vertices = 2n**

2. Euler's formula states: Faces + Vertices - Edges = 2. Verify this for a rectangular prism and a square pyramid. → **Rectangular prism: 6 + 8 - 12 = 2. Square pyramid: 5 + 5 - 8 = 2.**

3. How many different nets can you draw for a cube? (There are exactly 11.) Try to find at least 5 of them. → **Student draws at least 5 of the 11 valid cube nets.**

4. A triangular prism and a rectangular prism are sitting on a table. You can only see 3 faces of the rectangular prism and 3 faces of the triangular prism. For each shape, describe which faces are visible and which are hidden. → **Student identifies top, front, and one side as visible; bottom, back, and other side as hidden.**

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** Name a 3D shape that has exactly 5 faces, 9 edges, and 6 vertices.

**Problem 2:** Draw a net for a rectangular prism. Label each face as top, bottom, front, back, left, or right.

**Problem 3:** What is the difference between a prism and a pyramid?

---

## Answer Key

**Exit Ticket:**
1. **Triangular prism**
2. **Student draws a valid net with 6 rectangles, each labeled correctly. Opposite faces should be the same size.**
3. **A prism has two identical parallel bases connected by rectangular faces. A pyramid has one base and triangular faces that meet at a single point (apex).**

## Teacher Notes

- **Hands-on is essential:** This lesson relies heavily on concrete materials. Students need to hold, count, and fold actual 3D shapes and nets. Do not skip the building activity — it is the conceptual foundation for surface area in Lessons 6 and 7.
- **Vocabulary wall:** Add all seven vocabulary terms to the classroom vocabulary wall with diagrams. Students will use these terms throughout the rest of the unit.
- **Common confusion — faces vs. sides:** In 2D, we say "sides." In 3D, flat surfaces are "faces" and line segments are "edges." Help students transition their language.
- **Net orientation:** Students may draw valid nets in unexpected orientations. A net does not have to be in the "standard" layout — any arrangement that folds correctly without overlapping is valid. Validate creative solutions.
- **Pre-cut nets for struggling learners:** If cutting and folding is too challenging for some students, provide pre-cut nets that only need to be folded and taped.
- **Connection to surface area:** At the end of the lesson, preview the next two lessons: "Now that you can unfold a 3D shape into a flat net, imagine finding the area of every face and adding them up. That is called surface area — and that is what we do next."
- **Real-world connections:** Bring in real boxes, cans (cylinders — mention briefly as another 3D shape), and other containers. Have students identify faces, edges, and vertices on real objects.
