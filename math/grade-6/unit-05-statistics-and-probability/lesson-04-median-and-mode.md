# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 5, Lesson 4

## Topic
Median and Mode — Finding the Median in Odd and Even Data Sets, Identifying Mode

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Find the median of a data set with an odd number of values by identifying the middle value.
- Find the median of a data set with an even number of values by averaging the two middle values.
- Identify the mode (or modes) of a data set, and recognize when a data set has no mode.

## Prerequisites
- Ordering integers and decimals from least to greatest (Unit 2)
- Calculating mean by adding and dividing (Lesson 3)
- Understanding the concept of "center" of a distribution (Lesson 2)
- Adding and dividing decimals (Unit 2)

## Materials Needed
- 11 small chips, squares, or sticky notes (for physical median-finding activity)
- Number line drawn on paper or whiteboard
- Calculator (for checking work)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice ordering data and connect to the idea of "the middle" before formalizing the median.

**Activity:**
"Here are 7 students' scores on a vocabulary quiz:
85, 92, 78, 88, 91, 82, 76

Line them up from lowest to highest. Then point to the one right in the middle."

Ordered: 76, 78, 82, **88**, 91, 92
Wait — that's only 6. Let me add 85 back:
Ordered: 76, 78, 82, **85**, 88, 91, 92

"Which score is exactly in the middle? Count from each end — 3 values on each side. ... It's 85. That's the median."

**Facilitator script:**
"The median is simply the middle value when data is ordered. Three scores are below it, three are above it — perfectly balanced. Today we'll learn how to find it for any data set, including ones with an even number of values."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Develop the procedure for finding median (odd and even cases) and define mode.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Median:** The middle value of an ordered data set. When there are two middle values (even count), the median is their mean.
- **Mode:** The value that appears most frequently in a data set. A data set may have one mode, more than one mode, or no mode.
- **Ordered data set:** The data values arranged from least to greatest (required before finding median).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Odd number of values:**

"To find the median:
1. Order the data from least to greatest.
2. Count the total number of values (n).
3. The median is in position (n + 1) ÷ 2.

Example: 7 values. Position = (7 + 1) ÷ 2 = 4th value.
The 4th value in the ordered list is the median."

**Step 2 — Physical model (if chips available):**

Write 9 data values on chips. Line them up in order. Push one chip from each end toward the center simultaneously. The chip left in the middle is the median.

"9 values: I push one from each end → 7 remain. Push again → 5 remain. Push again → 3 remain. Push again → 1 remains. That's the median."

**Step 3 — Even number of values:**

"What if there's no single middle chip? With 8 values:
Push one from each end: 6 left. Push: 4 left. Push: 2 left.
Two chips remain — neither is the median alone. So we average them:

Median = (left middle value + right middle value) ÷ 2

Example: the two middle values are 14 and 18.
Median = (14 + 18) ÷ 2 = 32 ÷ 2 = **16**

Note: 16 does not appear in the data — that's fine."

**Step 4 — Mode:**

"The mode is the easiest — it's just the value that shows up most often.

- Data: 3, 5, 3, 7, 3, 9 → Mode = 3 (appears 3 times)
- Data: 2, 4, 4, 6, 6, 8 → Modes = 4 and 6 (bimodal — two modes tied for most frequent)
- Data: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 → No mode — all values appear exactly once

Mode is most useful for categorical data or when one value clearly repeats. It tells us the most common value, not necessarily the typical value."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Odd number of values:**
Number of push-ups done by 9 students:
15, 22, 18, 30, 12, 25, 20, 17, 19

Step 1: Order: 12, 15, 17, 18, **19**, 20, 22, 25, 30
Step 2: n = 9 (odd); position = (9 + 1) ÷ 2 = 5
Step 3: Count to 5th value: 12, 15, 17, 18, **19** → Median = **19**

**Example 2 — Even number of values:**
Daily steps walked over 10 days (thousands):
8, 6, 9, 7, 11, 8, 10, 6, 7, 9

Step 1: Order: 6, 6, 7, 7, **8, 8**, 9, 9, 10, 11
Step 2: n = 10 (even); two middle positions: 5th and 6th
Step 3: 5th value = 8, 6th value = 8
Median = (8 + 8) ÷ 2 = **8** (thousands steps, i.e., 8,000 steps)

**Example 3 — Mode:**
Shoe sizes for a sample of 12 middle schoolers:
7, 8, 7, 9, 8, 7, 10, 8, 7, 9, 6, 8

Tally:
- 6 appears 1 time
- 7 appears 4 times
- 8 appears 4 times
- 9 appears 2 times
- 10 appears 1 time

Mode = **7 and 8** (bimodal — both appear 4 times)

Median: Ordered: 6, 7, 7, 7, 7, **8, 8**, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10 (n = 12)
Middle positions: 6th and 7th → both are 8. Median = **8**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Problem 1:**
Points scored by a basketball player in 7 games:
18, 24, 14, 30, 22, 16, 28

(a) Order the data.
(b) Find the median.
(c) Find the mode (if any).

**Problem 2:**
Water temperatures (°F) at a beach over 8 days:
68, 72, 70, 75, 69, 73, 70, 74

(a) Order the data.
(b) Find the median. Show the two middle values clearly.
(c) Is the median one of the original data values? Does it need to be?

**Problem 3:**
Favorite pet survey results from 11 students (number of students choosing each):
```
Dog: 5 students
Cat: 3 students
Fish: 2 students
Hamster: 1 student
```

If we list each student's response as a data value, what is the mode of this data? Is median meaningful here? (Hint: this is categorical data.)

**Facilitator guidance:** Problem 3 introduces the important distinction that mode works for categorical data but median does not — you can't find the "middle" of a list of pet names.

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

Find the median and mode for each data set. Always order the data first.

1. Test scores: 88, 72, 95, 88, 84, 91, 76

2. Number of text messages sent per day: 45, 50, 38, 45, 62, 29, 50, 44

3. Heights of plants (cm) in a biology experiment: 14.2, 16.5, 15.0, 14.8, 16.5, 15.3, 14.2, 15.0, 16.0, 15.3

4. Goals scored by a soccer team in 6 games: 0, 3, 1, 2, 1, 5

5. Ages of members at a chess club: 11, 12, 11, 13, 14, 12, 11, 15, 12, 13, 11

6. Temperatures in °F for 5 days: 72, 68, 75, 80, 74

7. **Find the missing value:** The median of the data set {5, 8, 12, __, 20} is 13. What is the missing value? (Data is already ordered.)

8. **True or False:** Adding a value larger than the current median always increases the median. Give an example to support or refute this.

9. A data set has values: 3, 3, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7
   (a) Find the mean.
   (b) Find the median.
   (c) Find the mode.
   (d) Which measure — mean, median, or mode — best represents the "typical" value? Explain.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

> The weights (in pounds) of 8 puppies at a vet clinic:
> 4.2, 6.8, 5.1, 4.2, 7.3, 5.9, 6.1, 4.8
>
> (a) Find the median weight.
> (b) Find the mode weight.
> (c) The vet adds a ninth puppy weighing 4.2 pounds to the list. Without recalculating, will the median increase, decrease, or stay the same? Explain.

**Expected Answers:**

(a) Order: 4.2, 4.2, 4.8, **5.1, 5.9**, 6.1, 6.8, 7.3 (n = 8)
Middle values: 5.1 and 5.9 → Median = (5.1 + 5.9) ÷ 2 = 11.0 ÷ 2 = **5.5 pounds**

(b) Mode = **4.2** (appears twice; all others appear once)

(c) Ordered set of 9: 4.2, 4.2, 4.2, 4.8, **5.1**, 5.9, 6.1, 6.8, 7.3 → New median = 5th value = **5.1 pounds**. The median decreases from 5.5 to 5.1 because adding 4.2 (below the old median) shifted the middle value leftward.

**Mastery = correct computation for (a) and (b), and logical reasoning for (c)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Check whether the student is ordering data before finding the median. Require them to write the ordered list explicitly. Practice with a 5-value odd set until fluent, then move to a 6-value even set.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "For the original 8 puppies, is the mean equal to the median? Calculate the mean and compare." (Mean = (4.2 + 6.8 + 5.1 + 4.2 + 7.3 + 5.9 + 6.1 + 4.8) / 8 = 44.4 / 8 = 5.55; Median = 5.5. Close but not equal — a good reminder that mean ≠ median.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The most common error is failing to order the data before finding the median. Make it a non-negotiable routine: "Step 1 is always: order the data."
- Students frequently forget to average the two middle values in even data sets, instead just picking one. The physical chip-pushing activity is very effective at making both cases concrete.
- Mode is often treated as less important, but it is the only measure that applies to categorical data (colors, names, types). Reinforce this with Problem 3 in Guided Practice.
- Problem 7 (finding a missing value from the median) is an excellent algebraic reasoning problem — it requires students to work backward from the definition. Don't skip it for students who are ready.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:**
1. (a) Ordered: 14, 16, 18, **22**, 24, 28, 30
   (b) Median = **22** (4th of 7 values)
   (c) No mode — all values appear once

2. (a) Ordered: 68, 69, 70, **70, 73**, 74, 75, 72 — wait, reorder: 68, 69, 70, 70, **72, 73**, 74, 75
   Actually: 68, 69, 70, 70, 72, 73, 74, 75 — n = 8; middle positions 4th and 5th
   (b) 4th = 70, 5th = 72; Median = (70 + 72) ÷ 2 = **71°F**
   (c) 71 is NOT in the original data — and that's perfectly fine for median with an even data set

3. Mode = **Dog** (5 students); median is not meaningful for categorical data (you cannot order pet names)

**Independent Practice:**

1. Ordered: 72, 76, 84, **88**, 88, 91, 95 (n = 7); Median = **88**; Mode = **88**

2. Ordered: 29, 38, 44, **45, 45**, 50, 50, 62 (n = 8); Middle = 45 and 45; Median = **45**; Mode = **45 and 50** (bimodal)

3. Ordered: 14.2, 14.2, 14.8, 15.0, **15.0, 15.3**, 15.3, 16.0, 16.5, 16.5 (n = 10)
   Middle positions 5th and 6th: 15.0 and 15.3; Median = (15.0 + 15.3) ÷ 2 = **15.15 cm**
   Mode = **14.2, 15.0, 15.3, and 16.5** (each appears twice — all four are modes; accept any correct identification)

4. Ordered: 0, 1, **1, 2**, 3, 5 (n = 6); Middle positions 3rd and 4th: 1 and 2; Median = (1 + 2) ÷ 2 = **1.5**; Mode = **1**

5. Ordered: 11, 11, 11, 11, 12, **12**, 12, 13, 13, 14, 15 (n = 11); Median = **12** (6th value); Mode = **11** (appears 4 times)

6. Ordered: 68, 72, **74**, 75, 80 (n = 5); Median = **74°F**; No mode (all appear once)

7. Data: {5, 8, 12, __, 20} with n = 5; median is 3rd value = 13; Missing value = **13**

8. FALSE. Counterexample: {1, 2, **3**, 4, 5}, median = 3. Add value 4: {1, 2, 3, **4**, 4, 5}, median = (3 + 4)/2 = 3.5. In this case median increased. But add value 2: {1, 2, 2, **3**, 4, 5}, median = (2 + 3)/2 = 2.5. Median decreased. Adding a value larger than the current median can increase OR decrease the median depending on context.

9. (a) Mean = (3 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 + 7) / 7 = 31 / 7 ≈ **4.4**
   (b) Ordered: 3, 3, 3, **4**, 5, 6, 7; Median = **4**
   (c) Mode = **3**
   (d) The median (4) or mean (≈4.4) best represents the typical value. The mode (3) is the lowest value in the set and happens to appear most due to repetition, not because it's the typical middle value. Answers may vary — accept well-reasoned responses.
