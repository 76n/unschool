# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 5, Lesson 7

## Topic
Histograms: Creating and Interpreting Histograms, Choosing Bin Intervals, Comparing Histograms with Dot Plots

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Create a histogram from a data set by grouping data into equal-width intervals (bins) and drawing bars to represent frequency.
- Choose an appropriate bin width based on the range and size of a data set, and explain how bin width affects the appearance of a histogram.
- Interpret a histogram to describe the distribution's shape, center, and spread, and explain when a histogram is preferable to a dot plot.

## Prerequisites
- Creating and reading dot plots (Lesson 6)
- Understanding frequency, clusters, gaps, and distribution shape (Lessons 2, 6)
- Constructing and labeling bar charts (prior grades)
- Understanding intervals and inequalities on a number line (Unit 3)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Surface the limitation of dot plots with large data sets; motivate histograms.

**Prompt:**
"Imagine you surveyed 200 sixth graders and asked how many minutes they spend on screens each day. The answers range from 30 minutes to 600 minutes. If you tried to make a dot plot, what problems would you run into?"

**Facilitator script:**
"Right — you'd need a number line spanning 570 units, and some values might appear only once while others appear many times. The dot plot would be enormous, hard to read, and not particularly informative. What if instead we grouped the data into ranges — say, 0–60 minutes, 61–120 minutes, 121–180 minutes, and so on — and just counted how many students fall in each group? That's exactly what a histogram does."

## Direct Instruction (10–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define histograms, teach how to build one with appropriate bins, and develop interpretation skills.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Histogram:** A bar graph that displays the frequency (or count) of data values within equal-width intervals called bins. Bars touch each other because the intervals are continuous.
- **Bin (Class Interval):** A range of values grouped together in a histogram. Example: 60–80 is a bin of width 20.
- **Bin Width:** The size of each interval. All bins in a standard histogram have the same width.
- **Frequency:** The count of data values falling within a bin.
- **Continuous Data:** Data that can take any value within a range (height, time, temperature), as opposed to discrete data that can only take specific values.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — How a histogram differs from a bar graph and a dot plot:**

"A bar graph shows categories (like favorite sports). A dot plot shows individual values. A histogram groups continuous or large data sets into equal-width bins and shows how many values fall in each bin. Key difference: histogram bars touch because there are no gaps between intervals — every possible value belongs to exactly one bin."

**Step 2 — Constructing a histogram:**

1. Find the minimum and maximum values. Calculate the range.
2. Decide on a bin width (see Step 3). Create bin intervals that cover the full range without overlapping.
3. Use a tally to count how many values fall in each bin. Use a consistent rule: values equal to the boundary go in the HIGHER bin (e.g., a value of exactly 60 goes in the 60–79 bin, not 40–59).
4. Draw and label the horizontal axis (the variable and scale) and vertical axis (Frequency).
5. Draw bars of equal width whose heights equal the frequency for each bin. Bars must touch.
6. Add a title.

**Step 3 — Choosing bin width:**

"There is no single right answer for bin width, but these guidelines help:
- Aim for 5 to 10 bins. Fewer than 5 hides detail; more than 10 can look like a messy dot plot.
- Use "nice" numbers for bin width: 5, 10, 20, 25, 50, 100, etc.
- Rule of thumb: divide the range by 5 to get an approximate starting bin width, then round to a nice number.
- Consider what question you are answering — sometimes a wider bin makes the shape clearer."

**Step 4 — Interpreting a histogram:**

- **Shape:** Is the distribution symmetric? Skewed right (long right tail)? Skewed left (long left tail)? Bell-shaped?
- **Center:** Which bin contains the median? Roughly where does the distribution peak?
- **Spread:** How wide is the full distribution? Are values clustered in a few bins or spread across many?
- **Outliers:** A bin that stands alone far from the others suggests potential outliers.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Building a histogram:**

Ages (in years) of 20 participants at a youth science fair:
10, 11, 12, 13, 11, 10, 14, 12, 11, 13, 12, 10, 13, 14, 11, 12, 12, 11, 10, 13

Range = 14 − 10 = 4 years. With only 5 distinct values, a bin width of 1 works, giving 5 bins.

Bin frequencies:
- Age 10: 4 participants
- Age 11: 5 participants
- Age 12: 5 participants
- Age 13: 4 participants
- Age 14: 2 participants

Histogram description: Horizontal axis labeled "Age (years)" from 10 to 15. Vertical axis labeled "Frequency" from 0 to 6. Five equal-width bars, the tallest at ages 11 and 12 (height 5), flanked by bars of height 4 at ages 10 and 13, and a shorter bar of height 2 at age 14. Bars are adjacent (touching). Shape: roughly symmetric with a slight right tail (the shorter bar at 14).

Note: With only 5 unique values and n = 20, a dot plot would also work. But if ages ranged from 10 to 18, a histogram with bin width = 2 or 3 would be preferred.

**Example 2 — Larger data set with meaningful bins:**

Number of minutes 24 students spent studying for a test:
20, 45, 30, 60, 75, 40, 55, 80, 25, 35, 65, 90, 50, 70, 45, 30, 60, 85, 40, 55, 75, 20, 95, 50

Range = 95 − 20 = 75 minutes. Divide by 5 ≈ 15. Round to 20 for a nice bin width.

Bins (using 20-minute intervals starting at 20):
- 20–39 minutes: 20, 25, 30, 30, 35 → 5 students
- 40–59 minutes: 40, 40, 45, 45, 50, 50, 55, 55 → 8 students
- 60–79 minutes: 60, 60, 65, 70, 75, 75 → 6 students
- 80–99 minutes: 80, 85, 90, 95 → 4 students

Wait: let's reverify the count: 5+8+6+4 = 23. Recount. The 24th value: re-listing sorted: 20, 20, 25, 30, 30, 35, 40, 40, 45, 45, 50, 50, 55, 55, 60, 60, 65, 70, 75, 75, 80, 85, 90, 95 = 24 values.
- 20–39: 20, 20, 25, 30, 30, 35 → **6 students**
- 40–59: 40, 40, 45, 45, 50, 50, 55, 55 → **8 students**
- 60–79: 60, 60, 65, 70, 75, 75 → **6 students**
- 80–99: 80, 85, 90, 95 → **4 students**
Total: 6+8+6+4 = **24** ✓

Histogram description: Horizontal axis labeled "Study Time (minutes)" with tick marks at 20, 40, 60, 80, 100. Vertical axis labeled "Frequency" from 0 to 9. Four equal bars with heights 6, 8, 6, 4. Shape: slightly skewed right (tallest bar in second bin, with the right tail extending to the 80–99 bin).

**Example 3 — Effect of bin width:**

Using the same 24 study-time values, compare two choices:
- Bin width = 10 (9 bins): More detailed; reveals the exact shape more precisely.
- Bin width = 40 (2 bins: 20–59 and 60–99): Too coarse; hides the peak in the 40–59 range. The shape looks nearly flat (14 vs. 10 students).

Conclusion: The bin width of 20 gives the most useful picture — 4 bins showing the concentration in the 40–59 range without being too granular.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Data set:** High temperatures (°F) recorded over 20 days in July:
82, 91, 87, 76, 94, 88, 79, 85, 90, 83, 77, 96, 84, 89, 92, 78, 86, 81, 93, 88

1. Find the minimum, maximum, and range.
2. Suggest an appropriate bin width and list your bin intervals. (Hint: aim for 4–6 bins.)
3. Tally the frequencies for each bin.
4. Describe the histogram you would draw: name each bar's height and overall shape.
5. In which bin does the median fall? (You do not need to find the exact median, just identify the bin.)
6. Would a dot plot also work well for this data set? Why or why not?

For question 6: the data ranges from 76 to 96, only 21 distinct possible integer values, so a dot plot would work but would be long — a histogram is more compact.

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

**Problem 1:**
Weights of 18 backpacks (pounds) at a school weigh-in:
8, 12, 15, 9, 11, 14, 17, 10, 13, 16, 12, 8, 15, 11, 14, 9, 13, 10

(a) Find the range. Choose an appropriate bin width (aim for 4–5 bins). List your bins.
(b) Tally the frequency for each bin.
(c) Describe the histogram. What is the shape of the distribution?
(d) Identify the bin that contains the median.

**Problem 2:**
Speeds (mph) of 15 cars measured at a school zone speed trap:
22, 31, 27, 19, 25, 30, 28, 23, 35, 26, 24, 29, 21, 33, 28

(a) Create bins with a width of 5 mph starting at 15 mph (15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39).
(b) Tally frequencies for each bin.
(c) Describe the shape of the histogram. Is the distribution symmetric, skewed left, or skewed right?
(d) The speed limit is 25 mph. How many cars were recorded at or above 25 mph? What fraction of all cars does this represent?

**Problem 3:**
A dot plot displays the scores of 10 students on a 20-point vocabulary quiz, with individual dots at: 12, 14, 14, 15, 16, 16, 16, 17, 18, 19.

(a) Create a histogram using bins of width 3: 12–14, 15–17, 18–20.
(b) Compare the histogram and the dot plot. What information does the dot plot show that the histogram does not? What does the histogram do better?
(c) With only 10 students, which display is more appropriate: the dot plot or the histogram? Explain.

**Problem 4 (Stretch):**
The ages of players at a weekend basketball tournament are grouped in this histogram:

- Bin 10–11: 8 players
- Bin 12–13: 15 players
- Bin 14–15: 11 players
- Bin 16–17: 6 players

(a) How many players are at the tournament in total?
(b) What fraction of players are in the 12–13 age bin?
(c) In which bin does the median player's age fall? Explain your reasoning.
(d) Can you determine the exact mean age from this histogram? Explain why or why not. What can you estimate?

**Problem 5 (Challenge):**
The same data set of 30 students' test scores is displayed in two histograms:

Histogram A uses bin width 5: bins 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80–84, 85–89, 90–94.
Histogram B uses bin width 20: bins 50–69, 70–89, 90–109.

Histogram A frequencies: 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 6, 4, 2, 0 (total: 30)

(a) Reconstruct the frequencies for Histogram B by combining Histogram A bins.
(b) Which histogram makes it easier to see the shape of the distribution? Why?
(c) Which histogram makes it easier to identify the approximate median? Explain how you would estimate the median from each histogram.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

> The ages (years) of 16 players on a youth lacrosse team:
> 11, 12, 13, 12, 14, 11, 13, 14, 12, 13, 11, 14, 12, 13, 14, 12
>
> (a) Use bin width = 1 to create frequency counts (11, 12, 13, 14).
> (b) Which age group is the most frequent?
> (c) If you used bin width = 2 with bins 11–12 and 13–14, what would the two frequencies be?
> (d) Which bin width gives a more informative picture of this data? Explain.

**Expected Answers:**

(a) Frequencies:
  - Age 11: 3 players
  - Age 12: 5 players
  - Age 13: 4 players
  - Age 14: 4 players

(b) **Age 12** is most frequent (5 players).

(c) Bin 11–12: 3 + 5 = **8 players**; Bin 13–14: 4 + 4 = **8 players**

(d) **Bin width = 1** is more informative here because it shows that age 12 is the peak, while bin width = 2 makes both bins look identical (both 8). With only 4 distinct values and n = 16, the narrower bins reveal the shape of the distribution better. (A dot plot would also work equally well here.)

**Mastery = correct frequencies in part (a), correct merged bins in part (c), reasonable comparison in part (d).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Review the bin-assignment rule: a value at a boundary goes in the higher bin. Also clarify that bars must touch — reinforce the "continuous" concept of histograms.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "If 10 more 12-year-olds joined the team, how would that change the shape of the histogram? Would the distribution be symmetric or skewed?"

## Extension (Optional)

**Relative Frequency Histograms:**
Instead of showing the count in each bin, a relative frequency histogram shows the proportion (fraction or percentage) of the total data in each bin.

Using Example 2 study-time data (n = 24):
- 20–39: 6/24 = 0.25 (25%)
- 40–59: 8/24 ≈ 0.333 (33.3%)
- 60–79: 6/24 = 0.25 (25%)
- 80–99: 4/24 ≈ 0.167 (16.7%)

Create a relative frequency histogram. What advantage does this version have when comparing two groups of different sizes?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The most common error is students using a bar graph format (with gaps between bars) instead of touching bars. Physically demonstrate why the bars must touch: the intervals are continuous — there is no "space" between 40 and 60 when you are measuring study time.
- Students will sometimes assign a boundary value to the wrong bin. Establish and consistently enforce the rule: boundary values go in the higher (right) bin.
- Bin width choice is genuinely a judgment call. Praise students who think critically about it and try multiple widths (as in Problem 5).
- Problem 3 explicitly compares histograms and dot plots. The key distinction: dot plots preserve individual values; histograms lose individual values but handle large data sets with wide ranges more cleanly. This directly sets up Lesson 9.
- The "shape vocabulary" from Lesson 6 (symmetric, skewed right, skewed left) applies directly to histograms and should be reinforced consistently.
