# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 5, Lesson 5

## Topic
Range and Measures of Variability — Range, Interquartile Range, and Understanding Spread

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Calculate the range of a data set as the difference between the maximum and minimum values.
- Calculate the interquartile range (IQR) by finding Q1 and Q3 and subtracting.
- Explain what range and IQR tell us about the spread (variability) of a data set, and interpret a larger vs. smaller spread in context.

## Prerequisites
- Ordering data sets from least to greatest (Lessons 3–4)
- Finding the median of odd and even data sets (Lesson 4)
- Understanding the concept of spread from informal description (Lesson 2)
- Subtraction of decimals (Unit 2)

## Materials Needed
- Calculator
- Whiteboard or paper for displaying ordered data sets
- Ruler (optional, for marking quartile positions on a number line)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of spread; surface the limitation of range before introducing IQR.

**Scenario:**
"Two archers each shot 7 arrows. Here are the distances (in inches) each arrow landed from the bullseye:

Archer A: 2, 3, 1, 4, 2, 3, 2
Archer B: 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 15

Without calculating anything, who do you think is more consistent? Why?"

**Facilitator script:**
"Most of you probably said Archer A — their arrows all land within a small range. Archer B mostly shoots well, but that 15 is way off. Let's look at the range for each:

Range A = 4 − 1 = 3 inches
Range B = 15 − 1 = 14 inches

Archer B has a much larger range because of one bad shot. But is that one shot really representative of Archer B's consistency? Most of B's shots landed within 1–3 inches, just like A. Today we'll learn a better measure of spread that ignores extreme values."

## Direct Instruction (10–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce range (quick review) and interquartile range (IQR) as a more robust measure of spread.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Range:** The difference between the maximum and minimum values in a data set. Range = Maximum − Minimum.
- **Quartiles:** Values that divide an ordered data set into four equal-sized groups. Q1 is the first quartile (25th percentile), Q2 is the median (50th percentile), Q3 is the third quartile (75th percentile).
- **Interquartile Range (IQR):** The range of the middle 50% of the data. IQR = Q3 − Q1.
- **Spread / Variability:** How far apart or clustered the data values are; a measure of consistency.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Range (review and limitation):**

"Range = Max − Min. It's quick and easy but is heavily influenced by outliers, as we saw with Archer B. Range describes the total width of the data, not the typical spread."

**Step 2 — Introducing quartiles:**

"To find a better measure of spread, we split the data into four equal quarters.

Step 1: Order the data.
Step 2: Find the median (Q2). This splits the data in half.
Step 3: Find the median of the lower half → that's Q1 (first quartile).
Step 4: Find the median of the upper half → that's Q3 (third quartile).

**Important rule:** When finding Q1 and Q3, do NOT include the median (Q2) in either half if n is odd."

**Step 3 — IQR calculation:**

"IQR = Q3 − Q1

The IQR tells us the spread of the middle half of the data. It ignores the bottom 25% and top 25%, so outliers don't affect it. A small IQR means the middle half of the data is clustered tightly; a large IQR means the middle half is spread out."

**Step 4 — Connecting to context:**

"For Archer B's data (excluding the outlier of 15): most shots were between 1 and 3 inches. The IQR captures this middle consistency. The range was dominated by the one bad shot; the IQR ignores it."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Odd number of values:**
Rainfall (cm) recorded over 9 days: 3, 7, 5, 1, 8, 4, 6, 2, 9

Step 1: Order: 1, 2, 3, 4, **5**, 6, 7, 8, 9
Step 2: Q2 (median) = 5 (5th value of 9)
Step 3: Lower half (exclude Q2): 1, 2, 3, 4 → Q1 = (2 + 3) ÷ 2 = **2.5**
Step 4: Upper half (exclude Q2): 6, 7, 8, 9 → Q3 = (7 + 8) ÷ 2 = **7.5**
Range = 9 − 1 = **8**
IQR = 7.5 − 2.5 = **5**

Interpretation: The middle 50% of daily rainfall amounts spans 5 cm (from 2.5 to 7.5 cm).

**Example 2 — Even number of values:**
Points scored in 10 basketball games: 85, 92, 78, 88, 96, 84, 91, 87, 79, 93

Step 1: Order: 78, 79, 84, 85, **87, 88**, 91, 92, 93, 96
Step 2: Q2 = (87 + 88) ÷ 2 = **87.5**
Step 3: Lower half (first 5 values): 78, 79, **84**, 85, 87 → Q1 = **84**
Step 4: Upper half (last 5 values): 88, 91, **92**, 93, 96 → Q3 = **92**
Range = 96 − 78 = **18**
IQR = 92 − 84 = **8**

Interpretation: The scores typically ranged from 84 to 92 points (middle 50%), a span of 8 points. The full range was 18 points.

**Example 3 — Comparing spread:**
Team A finishing times in a relay race (seconds): 58, 61, 59, 63, 60, 62, 59, 61
Team B finishing times: 52, 65, 58, 70, 54, 68, 57, 63

Both teams have the same mean (about 60.4 seconds). But:
- Team A: Range = 63 − 58 = 5; IQR = 61.5 − 59 = 2.5
- Team B: Range = 70 − 52 = 18; IQR = 65 − 54 = 11

Team B has far more variability — their performance is much less consistent than Team A's, even though their average time is similar.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Data set:** Heights (inches) of 10 sixth-grade students:
58, 61, 63, 60, 64, 59, 65, 62, 67, 61

1. Order the data from least to greatest.
2. Find the range.
3. Find Q2 (median). Identify which values are in the lower half and which are in the upper half.
4. Find Q1 and Q3.
5. Find the IQR.
6. Interpret the IQR in context: What does it tell you about the heights of these students?

**Facilitator guidance:** For step 3, with n = 10 (even), Q2 = average of 5th and 6th values. The lower half is the first 5 values; the upper half is the last 5 values. Students must NOT include either middle value in both halves.

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

**Problem 1:**
Number of minutes students spent reading each evening for 9 days:
22, 35, 28, 45, 30, 25, 38, 32, 20

Find: (a) Range, (b) Q1, Q2, Q3, (c) IQR

**Problem 2:**
A weather station recorded maximum UV index values for 8 days:
3, 7, 5, 8, 6, 4, 9, 7

Find: (a) Range, (b) Q1, Q2, Q3, (c) IQR

**Problem 3:**
Sprint times (seconds) for two track athletes over 7 races:

Athlete Maya: 11.2, 11.8, 11.5, 11.4, 11.7, 11.3, 11.6
Athlete Jordan: 10.9, 12.4, 11.1, 13.0, 11.0, 12.8, 11.2

(a) Find the range for each athlete.
(b) Find the IQR for each athlete.
(c) Both athletes have a similar median time (~11.4–11.5 seconds). Based on range and IQR, which athlete is more consistent? Explain.
(d) A coach wants to select the most reliable athlete for a relay team. Which would you recommend and why?

**Problem 4:**
Prices (in dollars) of 7 video games at a used game store:
$12, $18, $15, $45, $14, $16, $13

(a) Find the range and IQR.
(b) Which measure better describes the "typical spread" of prices? Why?
(c) The $45 game is an outlier. How would removing it change the range? How would it change the IQR?

**Problem 5:**
A data set has Q1 = 24 and Q3 = 36. What is the IQR? If the minimum is 18 and maximum is 52, what is the range?

**Problem 6:**
Two classes took the same science test:
Class X scores: 70, 72, 74, 76, 78, 80, 82 (7 students)
Class Y scores: 60, 65, 70, 76, 82, 87, 93 (7 students)

Both classes have median = 76. Find and compare the IQR for each class. What does the difference tell you about the two classes?

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:** Problems 1–3 and 5.

**Stretch:** Problems 1–6.

**Challenge:** For Problem 6, also calculate the mean for each class. Are the means equal? Discuss: two classes with the same mean and same median can have very different IQRs. What are the implications for a teacher trying to understand their students' performance?

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

> The ages of 8 players on a youth soccer team:
> 11, 12, 11, 13, 14, 12, 13, 11
>
> (a) Find Q1, Q2 (median), and Q3.
> (b) Find the IQR.
> (c) Find the range.
> (d) Which measure — range or IQR — gives a better picture of how "spread out" the typical player ages are? Explain in one sentence.

**Expected Answers:**

Ordered: 11, 11, 11, 12, **12, 13**, 13, 14 (n = 8)
(a) Q2 = (12 + 13) ÷ 2 = **12.5**
    Lower half: 11, 11, 11, 12 → Q1 = (11 + 11) ÷ 2 = **11** (2nd and 3rd values)
    Upper half: 13, 13, 14 — wait: upper half of 8-value set is last 4: 13, 13, 14 is wrong

    Correction: n = 8; lower half = first 4: 11, 11, 11, 12; upper half = last 4: 12, 13, 13, 14
    Q1 = (11 + 11) ÷ 2 = **11**
    Q3 = (13 + 13) ÷ 2 = **13**
(b) IQR = 13 − 11 = **2 years**
(c) Range = 14 − 11 = **3 years**
(d) The IQR (2 years) gives a better picture because it focuses on the middle half of players and is not affected by the single 14-year-old; the range (3 years) is dominated by that one extreme value.

**Mastery = correct Q1, Q3, IQR, range, and reasonable comparison**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Identify the specific error: incorrect splitting of halves, wrong median of halves, or arithmetic error. The most common issue is including Q2 in one of the halves for odd-n data sets. Use the visual "four equal groups" model.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "If we added a 20-year-old to this team (perhaps a coach's child), what would happen to the range? What would happen to the IQR? Show the calculation." (Range: 20 − 11 = 9. IQR: recalculate with 9 values.)

## Extension (Optional)

**Mean Absolute Deviation Preview:**
Another measure of variability is the Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD), which is the average distance of each value from the mean.

For the data set {2, 4, 6, 8, 10}:
Mean = 6
Distances from mean: |2−6|=4, |4−6|=2, |6−6|=0, |8−6|=2, |10−6|=4
MAD = (4 + 2 + 0 + 2 + 4) / 5 = 12 / 5 = 2.4

Calculate the MAD for {1, 3, 5, 7, 9} and compare it to the IQR.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- Students often confuse Q1 with "the first value" or Q3 with "the last value." Emphasize that Q1 and Q3 are medians of sub-sets.
- The rule about whether to include Q2 in the halves varies by textbook/curriculum. This unit uses the standard: for odd n, exclude Q2 from both halves. Be consistent throughout the unit and in the assessment.
- Range is intentionally retaught briefly here. Its weakness (sensitivity to outliers) motivates IQR and directly connects to the box plot content in Lesson 8.
- Problem 3 (Maya vs. Jordan) is a high-value reasoning problem. Push students to articulate WHY consistent performance matters in a relay context.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:**
1. Ordered: 58, 59, 60, 61, 61, 62, 63, 64, 65, 67
2. Range = 67 − 58 = **9 inches**
3. Q2 = (61 + 62) ÷ 2 = **61.5 inches** (5th and 6th values); lower half: 58, 59, 60, 61, 61; upper half: 62, 63, 64, 65, 67
4. Q1 = median of {58, 59, 60, 61, 61} = **60** (3rd value); Q3 = median of {62, 63, 64, 65, 67} = **64** (3rd value)
5. IQR = 64 − 60 = **4 inches**
6. The middle 50% of student heights spans 4 inches (from 60 to 64 inches), indicating the heights are fairly consistent for most students.
