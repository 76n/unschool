# Facilitator Guide — Grade 6 Math, Unit 5: Statistics and Probability

**Grade:** 6
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 5 — Statistics and Probability
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4–5 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided

---

## Unit Overview

In this unit, students are introduced to statistical thinking as a disciplined way of asking questions about the world and using data to answer them. Students learn to distinguish between statistical and non-statistical questions, explore what it means for a data set to have a "center" and a "spread," and develop skill with three key measures of center (mean, median, mode) and two measures of variability (range and interquartile range). They also build fluency with three types of data displays — dot plots, histograms, and box plots — learning both how to construct them and how to interpret the stories they tell.

**Why it matters:** Data literacy is one of the most consequential quantitative skills students will use in everyday life. Whether reading a news article about test scores, interpreting a weather forecast, or comparing sports statistics, the concepts developed here give students a lens for evaluating claims and making sense of numerical information.

**Connection to prior learning:** This unit draws on the number line and rational number work from Unit 2, fraction and decimal computation from Units 1–2, and ratio reasoning from Unit 1. Students do not need prior experience with formal statistics.

**Connection to future learning:** In Grade 7, students extend to comparing two populations using random sampling and informal inference. In Grade 8, students study bivariate data and linear associations. High school statistics builds directly on this foundation.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, confirm that students can:

- [ ] Locate and order integers and rational numbers on a number line (Unit 2)
- [ ] Add, subtract, multiply, and divide multi-digit decimals (Unit 2)
- [ ] Simplify and evaluate numeric expressions using order of operations (Unit 3)
- [ ] Compute and interpret ratios (Unit 1)
- [ ] Read and interpret tables and basic graphs (prior grades)
- [ ] Round decimals to a specified place value (prior grades)

If students struggle with decimal division, pre-teach this skill before Lesson 3 (Mean), as it is essential for computing arithmetic averages.

## Facilitation Tips

1. **Always start with context, not formulas.** Before teaching mean or median, pose a real question ("Which soccer player had the better season?") so students feel the need for a tool. Formulas learned in context are retained far longer than formulas introduced abstractly.

2. **Make data physical when possible.** In Lessons 3–4, have students write values on sticky notes and physically sort or redistribute them. The concrete manipulation reinforces what mean and median actually do to data.

3. **Emphasize interpretation over calculation.** Students can often compute a mean correctly but struggle to explain what it means. After every calculation, ask: "What does this number tell us about the data?" Push for sentences, not just numbers.

4. **Use consistent real-world data sets across multiple lessons.** Introduce a "running data set" (e.g., a class survey of daily screen time, or a set of basketball player statistics) that reappears in Lessons 2–9. Familiarity with the context reduces cognitive load and lets students focus on the statistical concepts.

5. **Address "outlier" informally before the formal lesson.** Students will naturally notice unusual values in data sets. Name them as "outliers" when they arise and revisit the term formally in Lesson 9. Early informal exposure makes the later formal treatment easier.

6. **Use "which display should we use?" as a recurring question.** Each time a new display is introduced (Lessons 6–8), compare it to previous displays using the same data set. This side-by-side comparison builds the meta-skill of choosing an appropriate display, which is assessed in Lesson 9 and on the unit assessment.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| "The mode is always the best measure of center." | Mode is often not a useful measure of center; it tells us the most frequent value, not necessarily the middle or typical value. Some data sets have no mode or many modes. |
| "The mean is always in the middle of the data." | The mean is the balance point, not necessarily the middle. Outliers pull the mean toward them, so the mean can be far from most values. |
| "Median always equals the middle number you can see." | For even-numbered data sets, median is the average of the two middle values, which may not appear in the list at all. |
| "A histogram is just a bar graph." | Histograms show frequency for continuous intervals; there are no gaps between bars. Bar graphs show counts for separate categories with gaps between bars. |
| "The box in a box plot shows all the data." | The box shows only the middle 50% of data (IQR). The whiskers extend to the minimum and maximum. Points outside whiskers are outliers. |
| "Range tells us where the data is centered." | Range only measures spread (how far apart the extreme values are), not center. Students frequently confuse spread and center. |
| "A statistical question has one correct answer." | Statistical questions anticipate variability — there is no single "correct" answer, only a distribution of answers. |

## Assessment Guidance

**Formative:** Each lesson includes a 3-minute exit ticket. Use results to identify students who cannot yet compute the target measure or read the target display before the next lesson. Small-group re-teaching at the start of the following lesson is the recommended intervention.

**Summative:** The unit assessment (20–25 items, 60 points) includes:
- Computation items (measures of center and variability)
- Interpretation items (reading dot plots, histograms, box plots)
- Construction items (student creates a display from a data set)
- Reasoning items (justify choice of measure or display)

**Mastery threshold:** 42/60 (70%)

**Students below mastery** should receive targeted review on specific strands (computation vs. display construction vs. interpretation) rather than uniform re-teaching of all content.

## Differentiation at a Glance

| Student Need | Strategy |
|--------------|----------|
| Below grade level — struggles with decimal division | Provide a division steps reference card; allow calculator use for computation; focus energy on interpretation |
| Below grade level — number ordering difficulty | Use physical number lines; practice ordering data before computing statistics |
| On grade level | Standard lesson pacing; all difficulty tiers in independent practice |
| Above grade level / early finisher | Stretch and Challenge problems; compare multiple data sets; investigate how adding/removing a value changes mean vs. median |
| ELL students | Pre-teach vocabulary (distribution, variability, quartile) with visuals; allow native-language notes; use sentence frames for interpretation responses |
| Students with IEPs (attention/organization) | Break multi-step problems into numbered sub-steps; provide graph paper with pre-drawn axes; reduce independent practice quantity while maintaining variety |
