# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 5, Lesson 3

## Topic
Mean (Arithmetic Average) — Calculating Mean and Understanding What It Represents

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Calculate the mean of a data set by summing all values and dividing by the number of values.
- Explain the mean as the "fair share" or "balance point" of a data set.
- Recognize how outliers affect the mean and describe the impact on interpretation.

## Prerequisites
- Addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of multi-digit decimals (Unit 2)
- Order of operations: compute sum before dividing (Unit 3)
- Understanding of statistical questions and data variability (Lessons 1–2)
- Informal concept of "center" of a data distribution (Lesson 2)

## Materials Needed
- Calculator (for checking work and decimal-heavy problems)
- Sticky notes or small squares of paper (optional, for physical redistribution model)
- Whiteboard or paper for worked examples

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate the "fair share" intuition that underlies the mean before introducing the formula.

**Scenario:**
"Four friends went trick-or-treating and collected different amounts of candy:

| Friend | Pieces of Candy |
|--------|----------------|
| Amir   | 14             |
| Brianna| 8              |
| Carlos | 18             |
| Deja   | 12             |

If the friends want to share the candy equally, how many pieces should each person get?"

**Facilitator script:**
"Don't use a formula. Just think: if they pooled all the candy and split it evenly, what would each person get? ... Total: 14 + 8 + 18 + 12 = 52. Split among 4 friends: 52 ÷ 4 = 13 pieces each."

"What you just computed is the mean — also called the average. The mean is the amount each person would get if the candy were redistributed equally. That's why we call it the 'fair share' model."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Formalize the mean calculation and develop the "balance point" interpretation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Mean:** The sum of all values in a data set divided by the number of values. Also called the arithmetic average.
- **Fair share model:** A way to understand the mean — if all values were "leveled out" to be equal, each would equal the mean.
- **Balance point:** The mean is the point on a number line where the data set would balance perfectly (total distance of values below equals total distance above).
- **Outlier:** A value that is much higher or lower than the rest of the data; can pull the mean significantly.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — The formula:**

"The formula for the mean is:

    Mean = (Sum of all values) ÷ (Number of values)

Or written as a fraction:

    Mean = (x₁ + x₂ + x₃ + ... + xₙ) / n

where n is the count of values."

**Step 2 — Connect to fair share:**

"In the candy example, we had 52 pieces total and 4 people. 52 ÷ 4 = 13. If everyone started with 13, the total would still be 52. The mean is the value that makes everyone 'equal' while keeping the total the same."

**Step 3 — Physical model (if sticky notes available):**

Write values on sticky notes. Stack them in columns on a number line. "Can you physically move sticky notes from tall stacks to short stacks until every column is the same height? The height of each equal column is the mean."

**Step 4 — Balance point:**

"Here's a harder idea: the mean is also the balance point of the data. If you put the data values on a number line as weights, the mean is where the line would balance like a seesaw.

Data: 2, 4, 6
Mean = (2 + 4 + 6) / 3 = 12 / 3 = 4

From the mean (4): value 2 is 2 units to the left; value 6 is 2 units to the right. They balance. Value 4 is right on the mean. Perfectly balanced."

**Step 5 — Effect of outliers:**

"What happens to the mean when there is a very extreme value?

Data: 2, 4, 6, 30
Mean = (2 + 4 + 6 + 30) / 4 = 42 / 4 = 10.5

The mean jumped to 10.5, even though three of the four values are 6 or less. The outlier (30) pulled the mean way up. This is the main weakness of the mean."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:**
A student's test scores in science: 72, 85, 91, 78, 84, 90

Step 1: Sum = 72 + 85 + 91 + 78 + 84 + 90 = 500
Step 2: Count = 6 tests
Step 3: Mean = 500 ÷ 6 ≈ 83.3

The student's mean test score is approximately 83.3 out of 100.

**Example 2:**
Daily high temperatures (°F) for one week in April:
58, 63, 61, 67, 70, 68, 55

Step 1: Sum = 58 + 63 + 61 + 67 + 70 + 68 + 55 = 442
Step 2: Count = 7 days
Step 3: Mean = 442 ÷ 7 = 63.1°F (rounded to nearest tenth)

The average daily high that week was 63.1°F.

**Example 3 — Effect of outlier:**
Miles driven per day by a delivery driver: 45, 52, 48, 50, 47, 49, 150

Without outlier: Mean of first 6 = (45 + 52 + 48 + 50 + 47 + 49) / 6 = 291 / 6 = 48.5 miles
With outlier: Mean of all 7 = (291 + 150) / 7 = 441 / 7 = 63 miles

The outlier (150) inflates the mean from 48.5 to 63 miles. A mean of 63 miles does not accurately represent most days, because six of seven days the driver drove fewer than 53 miles.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Problem 1:**
The heights (in inches) of 8 students on a volleyball team:
62, 65, 67, 60, 68, 63, 66, 61

(a) Find the sum of the heights.
(b) Calculate the mean height.
(c) Would a player who is 70 inches tall be above or below the mean?

**Problem 2:**
Goals scored by a hockey team in their last 6 games:
3, 2, 5, 1, 4, 3

(a) Calculate the mean number of goals per game.
(b) If their next game they scored 10 goals, what would the new mean be for all 7 games?
(c) How did the outlier-ish value of 10 affect the mean?

**Problem 3:**
Monthly rainfall (inches) in a city over 5 months:
2.4, 3.1, 2.8, 3.5, 2.7

Calculate the mean monthly rainfall. Round to the nearest tenth.

**Facilitator guidance:** For Problem 2b, make sure the student adds 10 to the sum and divides by 7 (not 6). Common error: forgetting to increase the count.

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

1. Find the mean: 14, 19, 23, 11, 17, 18, 20

2. A student's homework scores for the week:
   | Day | Score |
   |-----|-------|
   | Mon | 8     |
   | Tue | 10    |
   | Wed | 7     |
   | Thu | 9     |
   | Fri | 10    |
   What is the mean score?

3. A runner's times (in minutes) for five races: 24.5, 23.8, 25.1, 24.0, 23.6
   Find the mean time. Round to the nearest hundredth.

4. Seven friends counted the number of apps on their phones: 23, 45, 31, 28, 40, 36, 29
   (a) Find the mean number of apps.
   (b) One friend later realized she miscounted — she has 85 apps, not 45. Recalculate the mean.
   (c) How much did the mean change?

5. A class of 5 students scored: 88, 92, 76, 95, 84 on a quiz.
   (a) Find the mean score.
   (b) A 6th student scored 40. Find the new mean.
   (c) The teacher wants to give an accurate picture of "typical" performance. Is the mean a good representation after the 6th student's score is included? Explain.

6. The mean of four numbers is 15. Three of the numbers are 12, 18, and 14. What is the fourth number? (Hint: work backwards from the mean formula.)

7. A data set has a mean of 30. What would happen to the mean if you added 30 to each value in the data set? What if you added 10 to each value?

8. **True or False:** The mean of a data set must be one of the values in the data set. Give an example to support your answer.

**Stretch:** Problems 1–8.

**Challenge:** Problem 7 extended: Write a general rule. If you add a constant k to every value in a data set with mean M, what is the new mean? Prove it algebraically using the mean formula with n values.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**

> A baseball player's hits per game over 6 games: 1, 3, 2, 0, 2, 4
>
> (a) Calculate the mean hits per game.
> (b) If the player had 12 hits in one exceptional game, and we include that as a 7th data point, what is the new mean?
> (c) Does the new mean accurately represent a "typical" game for this player? Explain in one sentence.

**Expected Answers:**

(a) Sum = 1 + 3 + 2 + 0 + 2 + 4 = 12; Mean = 12 / 6 = **2 hits per game**
(b) New sum = 12 + 12 = 24; New mean = 24 / 7 ≈ **3.43 hits per game**
(c) No — 3.43 is higher than 5 out of 6 of the original game totals; the exceptional game pulls the mean up, making it unrepresentative of typical performance.

**Mastery = correct computation for (a) and (b), reasonable explanation for (c)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Check whether the student is forgetting to increase the denominator when adding a new data point. Practice Problems 2b and 4b from Guided Practice again. Re-examine the formula: new value must change both numerator (sum) and denominator (count).

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask: "What value would the 7th game need to be for the mean to remain exactly 2? Set up an equation and solve." (Answer: 7 × 2 = 14 total; 14 − 12 = 2 hits needed in the 7th game.)

## Extension (Optional)

**Weighted Average Introduction (preview of middle/high school content):**
A student has grades in three subjects:
- Math: 88 (counts for 40% of the final average)
- English: 92 (counts for 35%)
- Science: 80 (counts for 25%)

A simple mean would be (88 + 92 + 80) / 3 = 86.7. But a weighted mean accounts for importance:
0.40 × 88 + 0.35 × 92 + 0.25 × 80 = 35.2 + 32.2 + 20.0 = **87.4**

Why is the weighted mean higher than the simple mean? Which subject matters most?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The most common computational error is adding correctly but dividing by the wrong count. Emphasize: the denominator is always the number of data values, not the number of different values.
- Reinforce the connection to Lesson 2: the mean is one specific way to describe the "center" of a distribution.
- Students who breeze through calculation should be pushed to work Problems 6 and 7 (inverse/algebraic reasoning). Problem 7 is particularly powerful for developing algebraic thinking.
- Decimals: many data sets produce non-integer means. Students need practice with decimal division. Use a calculator to check but have them attempt the division by hand first.
