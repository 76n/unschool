# Math — Grade 6 — Unit 5, Lesson 1

## Topic
Recognizing Statistical Questions

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between statistical questions (which anticipate variability) and non-statistical questions (which have a single definite answer).
- Explain why a question is or is not statistical by referencing the concept of variability in answers.
- Generate original statistical questions appropriate to a given real-world context.

## Prerequisites
- Understanding that some quantities can vary from person to person or situation to situation (informal, from everyday experience)
- Reading comprehension sufficient to analyze question phrasing
- Familiarity with basic survey/data collection scenarios (from prior grades)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard or display surface
- Index cards (2 per student) — one labeled "Statistical," one labeled "Non-Statistical"
- The 12-question card sort list printed or projected (provided in Warm-Up and Direct Instruction)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior thinking about questions and answers; surface the idea that some questions have many possible answers.

**Activity — Think About This:**

Display these two questions one at a time. After each, ask students to jot down what they think the answer might be.

> Question A: "How tall is the Eiffel Tower?"
> Question B: "How tall are the students in our class?"

**Facilitator script:**
"Look at Question A. If I asked ten different people this question right now, would they all give the same answer? ... Yes, pretty much — it's about 330 meters. What about Question B? If I asked ten different students, would their answers all be the same? ... No, each person has a different height. That difference — that variation — is the key idea we're exploring today."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the definition of a statistical question and the concept of variability.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Statistical question:** A question that anticipates variability in the answers — that is, you expect different people, objects, or situations to give different answers.
- **Non-statistical question:** A question with a single, fixed answer that does not vary.
- **Variability:** The tendency of data values to differ from one another; the "spread" of possible answers.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete (examples before definition):**

"Before I give you the formal definition, let's look at some examples and try to figure out the pattern."

Write on the board:

```
Non-statistical:
  - How many legs does a dog have?
  - What is 25 × 4?
  - What year was the U.S. Constitution signed?

Statistical:
  - How many pets do sixth graders at our school have?
  - How much time do students spend on homework each night?
  - How far do players hit a baseball in batting practice?
```

"What do you notice about the non-statistical questions? ... They all have one correct answer that doesn't change no matter who answers. What about the statistical ones? ... Each person might give a different answer. If you asked 30 students how many pets they have, you'd expect a range of answers."

**Step 2 — Definition:**

"A **statistical question** is one where you expect the answers to vary — different people or situations will produce different data values. A **non-statistical question** has exactly one correct answer."

**Step 3 — Key test to apply:**

"Ask yourself: 'If I collected data from many people or situations to answer this question, would I expect all the values to be the same?' If yes → non-statistical. If no (values would differ) → statistical."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:**
*"How many books did Marcus read last month?"*

Apply the test: If we asked 30 students this same question about themselves, would answers vary? Yes — some read 0, some read 3, some read 10. The word "Marcus" seems to point at one person, but notice the question is about a variable that differs by person. If the question is specifically "How many books did Marcus read?" (one person, one answer), it is non-statistical. However, if posed as "How many books do sixth graders read per month?", it becomes statistical.

Verdict: As phrased about one specific person → non-statistical (one answer). If about a group → statistical.

**Example 2:**
*"What are the typical high temperatures in Phoenix, Arizona in July?"*

Apply the test: If we looked at 31 days of July high temperatures, would they all be the same? No — there is day-to-day variation, even if they cluster around 105°F. This question is asking about a pattern across many data points.

Verdict: **Statistical** — temperatures vary from day to day.

**Example 3:**
*"How many meters are in a kilometer?"*

Apply the test: Is the answer always 1,000? Yes — this is a fixed fact with no variability.

Verdict: **Non-statistical.**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Facilitator note:** Read each question aloud. Ask the student to hold up their "Statistical" or "Non-Statistical" index card. Discuss reasoning for each.

For each question below, classify it and explain your reasoning:

1. "What is the capital of France?"
2. "How many minutes do sixth graders spend on social media per day?"
3. "What is 7 × 9?"
4. "How far do students at Lincoln Middle School travel to get to school?"
5. "What is the boiling point of water at sea level?"
6. "How many siblings do students in this class have?"

**Expected Classifications:**
1. Non-statistical (Paris — one answer)
2. Statistical (varies from student to student)
3. Non-statistical (63 — one answer)
4. Statistical (varies by student)
5. Non-statistical (100°C — one fixed answer)
6. Statistical (varies by student)

**Facilitation tip:** For #4, students sometimes say "non-statistical" because they think there's a typical distance. Push back: "Would every student give the same answer? No — data would vary."

**Instructions:** For problems 1–8, classify each question as statistical (S) or non-statistical (NS) and write one sentence explaining your reasoning.

1. "How many home runs did Babe Ruth hit in his career?"
2. "How many hours of sleep do middle school students get on school nights?"
3. "How many points did the winning team score in last night's basketball game?"
4. "How long does it take students to run one mile?"
5. "What is the area of a rectangle with length 8 cm and width 5 cm?"
6. "What is the most common shoe size among boys in Grade 6?"
7. "How much does a gallon of milk cost in the United States?"
8. "How many words per minute can students in this class type?"

**For problems 9–12, write your own statistical question for the given context.**

9. Context: A soccer team's season statistics
10. Context: Daily weather in your city
11. Context: Student backpack weights
12. Context: Amount of time spent watching TV on weekends

**On-level:** Problems 1–8 (classify) + 9–10 (write your own)

**Stretch:** Problems 1–12 (all). For problems 9–12, also explain what data you would collect to answer each question.

**Challenge:** After completing all problems, write two questions about the same topic — one statistical and one non-statistical — for three different topics of your choice. Explain what makes each version statistical or non-statistical.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
Write your answer on a slip of paper before leaving.

> Here are two questions. For each one: (a) identify it as statistical or non-statistical, and (b) write one sentence explaining your reasoning.
>
> Question 1: "How many students are enrolled in Westview Middle School this year?"
> Question 2: "How many text messages do sixth graders send per day?"

**Expected Answers:**

Question 1: Non-statistical. There is one specific enrollment number for the school this year — it does not vary across respondents.

Question 2: Statistical. Different students send different numbers of texts per day; answers will vary, producing a distribution of data.

**Mastery = correct classification AND valid reasoning for both questions**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Revisit the "key test" from Direct Instruction: "Would different people or situations give different answers?" Have the student restate the definition of variability in their own words. Try 2–3 additional examples before the next lesson.

### If Mastery Is Met
Ask the student: "Can a question become statistical just by changing a word? Give an example." (Example: "How old is my dog?" → non-statistical; "How old are dogs that visit this vet clinic?" → statistical.)

## Extension (Optional)

**Real-World Survey Design:**
Choose a topic you are curious about (sports, music, food, hobbies). Write three statistical questions you could ask your classmates. Then identify what kind of data each question would produce (whole numbers, decimals, categories). If time permits, actually collect the data from 5–10 people and list the values you gathered.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- Students often conflate "questions with numbers in the answer" with "statistical questions." Emphasize that it's variability — not the presence of numbers — that defines a statistical question.
- The hardest cases are questions about a single specific person or event ("How old is LeBron James?"). These look statistical but are non-statistical because there is one fixed answer. Use these edge cases deliberately.
- If students are struggling, return to the physical intuition: "If 30 different students each answered this question about themselves, would the answers all be the same?"
- This lesson sets the conceptual foundation for the entire unit. Take time here. Students who don't internalize variability will struggle with the purpose of measures of center and spread.

2. S — Different students spend different amounts of time on social media.

4. S — Different students live different distances from school.
5. NS — Water always boils at 100°C at sea level; fixed fact.
6. S — Different students have different numbers of siblings.

**Independent Practice:**
1. NS — Babe Ruth hit exactly 714 home runs; one fixed historical fact.
2. S — Different students sleep different amounts; hours vary.
3. NS — One specific game has one score for the winning team.
4. S — Different students run the mile in different times.
5. NS — Area = 8 × 5 = 40 cm²; one correct answer.
6. S — Shoe sizes vary across students; a distribution of sizes exists. (Note: asking for "most common" does not make it non-statistical — the data set is still variable.)
7. S — Milk prices vary by store, region, and time; different data collectors would find different prices. (Acceptable to discuss nuance: the question could be sharpened to a specific store on a specific day, making it non-statistical.)
8. S — Typing speeds vary among students.

**Student-generated statistical questions (9–12):** Answers vary. Accept any question that anticipates variability in answers. Sample answers:
9. "How many goals did each player on the team score this season?"
10. "What was the high temperature each day this week?"
11. "How much does each student's backpack weigh on a typical school day?"
12. "How many hours of TV do students watch on a Saturday?"

**Interleaved Review:**
R1. 12:8 or 3:2 or 12/8 or 3 to 2 (any three equivalent forms)
R2. 6 cups of sugar (ratio 3:2; 9 ÷ 3 = 3 groups; 3 × 2 = 6)
R3. 5 + 3 × (8 − 2) ÷ 6 = 5 + 3 × 6 ÷ 6 = 5 + 18 ÷ 6 = 5 + 3 = **8**

1. Non-statistical — one enrollment number.
2. Statistical — text counts vary from student to student.
