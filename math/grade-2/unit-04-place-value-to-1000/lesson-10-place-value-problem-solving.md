# Math — Grade 2 — Unit 4, Lesson 10

## Topic
Place Value Problem Solving

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply place value understanding to solve multi-step problems
- Decode number riddles using hundreds, tens, and ones clues
- Find missing digits in comparisons (e.g., 4_2 > 462, what could _ be?)
- Solve real-world problems involving three-digit numbers (distances, prices, quantities)

## Prerequisites
- Understanding hundreds, tens, and ones (Lessons 1-5)
- Comparing and ordering three-digit numbers (Lessons 6-7)
- Skip counting by 10s and 100s (Lesson 8)
- Understanding 1,000 as 10 hundreds (Lesson 9)

## Materials Needed
- Base-ten blocks (hundreds flats, tens rods, ones cubes)
- Paper and pencil
- Number riddle cards (optional — facilitator can read them aloud)
- Whiteboard or scratch paper for working out problems

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate all the place value skills learned in this unit with a quick review.

1. "Tell me the value of the 6 in 164." (60, or 6 tens.) "The value of the 6 in 603?" (600, or 6 hundreds.) "The value of the 6 in 436?" (6, or 6 ones.)
2. "What is 10 more than 395?" (405.) "100 less than 812?" (712.)
3. "Today we're going to use EVERYTHING you've learned about place value to solve puzzles, riddles, and real-world problems. Think of this as your place value detective day!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model how to solve different types of place value problems, building from concrete reasoning to abstract thinking.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Number riddle:** A puzzle where clues about digits lead you to a mystery number
- **Missing digit:** An unknown digit represented by a blank or question mark
- **Multi-step problem:** A problem that takes more than one step to solve
- **Reasonable answer:** An answer that makes sense for the problem

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Model a number riddle (Concrete to Abstract).**
"Here's a riddle: I am a three-digit number. I have 3 hundreds. My tens digit is double my ones digit. My ones digit is 4. What number am I?"

"Let's solve it step by step."
- "3 hundreds — so we know the hundreds digit is 3." Write: 3 _ _
- "My ones digit is 4." Write: 3 _ 4
- "My tens digit is double my ones digit. Double 4 is 8." Write: 384.
- "I am 384! Let's check: 3 hundreds? Yes. Ones digit is 4? Yes. Tens digit (8) is double ones digit (4)? Yes!"

Build 384 with blocks to verify.

**Step 2: Model a missing-digit comparison.**
"Fill in the blank: 5_7 > 567. What digits could go in the blank?"

"The blank is in the tens place. The hundreds are both 5. The ones in our mystery number are 7, same as 567. So everything depends on the tens digit."

"We need ___ > 6 (the tens digit in 567). What digits are bigger than 6?" (7, 8, or 9.)

"So the blank could be 7, 8, or 9. That gives us 577, 587, or 597 — all greater than 567."

**Step 3: Model a real-world problem.**
"A school has 475 students. Another school has 520 students. Which school has more students? How many more?"

"Which has more? Compare: 475 vs. 520. Hundreds: 4 vs. 5. 5 > 4, so 520 > 475. The second school has more."

"How many more? We need to find the difference. 520 - 475. Let's count up: 475 + 25 = 500. 500 + 20 = 520. So 25 + 20 = 45 more students." (Note: Formal subtraction with regrouping comes in a later unit; counting up is the strategy here.)

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Number riddle — "I am between 200 and 300. My ones digit is 0. My tens digit is 5. What number am I?"
- Between 200 and 300 means hundreds digit is 2. Write: 2 _ 0.
- Tens digit is 5. Write: 250.
- Answer: 250.

**Example B:** Missing digit — "3_1 < 351. What digits could go in the blank?"
- Hundreds are both 3. Ones are both 1. Compare tens: _ < 5.
- Digits less than 5: 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4.
- Possible numbers: 301, 311, 321, 331, 341. All are less than 351.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student solves problems with facilitator coaching, talking through reasoning.

**Activity 1: Number Riddles**
The facilitator reads clues. The student works through them step by step.

Riddle 1: "I have 7 hundreds. My tens digit and ones digit are the same. My tens digit is 2. What am I?"
- 7 hundreds: 7 _ _
- Tens and ones are the same, and tens is 2: 722.
- "Let's check — 7 hundreds? Yes. Tens = 2? Yes. Ones = tens? Yes!"

Riddle 2: "I am greater than 400 but less than 500. My tens digit is 3 more than my ones digit. My ones digit is 5. What am I?"
- Between 400 and 500: hundreds digit is 4. Write: 4 _ 5.
- Tens is 3 more than ones (5): 5 + 3 = 8. Write: 485.
- Check: Between 400 and 500? Yes. Ones is 5? Yes. Tens (8) is 3 more than ones (5)? Yes!

**Activity 2: Missing Digits**
- "Fill in the blank so the statement is true: 6_3 > 653. What could the blank be?" (6, 7, 8, or 9 — any digit that makes tens place at least 6, but wait: 663 > 653, so 6 works. So 6, 7, 8, 9.)
- "Fill in the blank: _25 < 425. What could the blank be?" (1, 2, or 3 — any hundreds digit less than 4.)

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
Use base-ten blocks or draw quick pictures to help.
1. Number riddle: "I have 5 hundreds, 0 tens, and 8 ones. What number am I?" (508)
2. Number riddle: "I am between 100 and 200. My tens digit is 6. My ones digit is 0. What am I?" (160)
3. What is 10 more than your answer to problem 2? (170)
4. Write >, <, or =: 508 ___ 160. (>)
5. A farmer has 312 apples and 289 oranges. Does the farmer have more apples or oranges? (More apples, because 312 > 289.)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. Number riddle: "I have 6 hundreds. My ones digit is 1 more than my tens digit. My tens digit is 3. What am I?" (634)
2. Missing digit: 7_5 > 745. What digits could go in the blank? (5, 6, 7, 8, or 9)
3. Missing digit: 2_8 < 258. What digits could go in the blank? (0, 1, 2, 3, or 4)
4. A library has 450 books on one shelf and 380 books on another. Which shelf has more books? How many more? (450 has more; 450 - 380 = 70 more.)
5. "I am 100 more than 350 and 10 less than 460. What am I?" (450 — check: 350 + 100 = 450. 460 - 10 = 450. Both match!)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. Number riddle: "I am a three-digit number. My hundreds digit is odd and less than 5. My tens digit is 3 times my hundreds digit. My ones digit is 0. What are all the numbers I could be?" (Hundreds: odd and < 5, so 1 or 3. If 1: tens = 3, number = 130. If 3: tens = 9, number = 390. Two answers: 130 and 390.)
2. Missing digit: 4_2 > 462. "List ALL digits that work, then write the smallest and largest possible numbers." (7, 8, 9. Smallest: 472. Largest: 492.)
3. "Three friends measured how far they walked. Amy walked 487 meters, Ben walked 512 meters, and Cal walked 499 meters. Put them in order from shortest to longest walk. How much farther did Ben walk than Amy?" (Order: 487, 499, 512 — Amy, Cal, Ben. Difference: 512 - 487 = 25 meters.)
4. "Write a number riddle that has exactly one answer. Give at least 3 clues. Then solve your own riddle." (Answers vary — student creates and solves a valid riddle.)

### Interleaved Review
- "Count by 100s: 250, 350, ___, ___, ___." (450, 550, 650) *(Lesson 8 review)*
- "How many hundreds are in 1,000?" (10) *(Lesson 9 review)*

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)
**Purpose:** Confirm the student can apply place value thinking to solve problems.

1. Number riddle: "I have 8 hundreds, 4 tens, and 1 one. What number am I?" (841)
2. Missing digit: 5_0 > 530. What is the smallest digit that could go in the blank? (4 — wait, 540 > 530, yes. But does 530 > 530? No, they're equal. So the smallest is 4.)

**Mastery = Both correct. Student can reason about digits and place value to solve non-routine problems.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- For number riddles: Build with blocks. "3 hundreds — put down 3 flats. 4 tens — put down 4 rods." The physical building makes each clue tangible.
- For missing digits: Write out all possible numbers in the blank (0 through 9) and check each one. This brute-force approach builds understanding before the student develops shortcuts.

### If Mastery Is Met
The student has completed the core lessons of Unit 4. Proceed to the Unit 4 Assessment.

## Teacher Notes

**This lesson is a celebration of everything the student has learned.** It's designed to be fun — riddles, puzzles, and real-world scenarios keep engagement high while reinforcing place value deeply.

**Number riddles build mathematical reasoning.** They require the student to hold multiple constraints in mind simultaneously. If a student struggles, work through one clue at a time and write partial answers (3 _ _ ) to reduce cognitive load.

**Missing-digit problems are powerful because they reverse the usual question.** Instead of "compare these two numbers," the student asks "what digits would MAKE this comparison true?" This flexible thinking is a hallmark of strong number sense.

**Encourage "check your answer" habits.** After every riddle or missing-digit problem, have the student verify: "Does my answer match ALL the clues?" This builds self-monitoring skills that transfer to all of math.

**Common errors:**
- In riddles: misreading "3 more than" as "3." Teach the student to underline key phrases like "more than," "less than," "double," "same as."
- In missing digits: forgetting that the blank could be 0. Remind: "0 is a digit too! Always check if 0 works."
- In real-world problems: not answering the full question. If the problem asks "which is more AND how many more," make sure both parts are answered.
- In multi-step problems: losing track of steps. Write each step's answer before starting the next.

**Extension for strong students:** "Write your own number riddle for a family member to solve. Make sure it has exactly one answer!" This creative task reinforces place value while developing mathematical communication.
