# Math — Grade 2 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Subtracting Within 100 (With Regrouping)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Recognize when regrouping is needed in subtraction (when the ones digit being subtracted is larger than the ones digit you have)
- Physically regroup 1 ten as 10 ones using base-ten blocks, then subtract
- Record regrouping in written (vertical) form
- Use the "think addition" strategy as an alternative (e.g., for 52 − 18, think 18 + ___ = 52)

## Prerequisites
- Subtracting within 100 without regrouping (Lesson 8)
- Subtracting tens (Lesson 7)
- Addition with regrouping (Lesson 6): understands that 10 ones = 1 ten

## Materials Needed
- Base-ten blocks (at least 9 tens rods and 20 ones cubes)
- Place value mat (paper with "Tens" and "Ones" columns)
- Paper and pencil
- Hundred chart (optional)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review no-regrouping subtraction and activate the concept of trading.

## Direct Instruction (15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach subtraction with regrouping — the hardest concept in this unit. Use many concrete examples.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Regroup (in subtraction):** Break apart 1 ten into 10 ones so you have enough ones to subtract (also called "borrowing" or "unbundling")
- **Not enough ones:** When the ones digit you're subtracting is bigger than the ones digit you have, you need to regroup

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Discover the problem.**
Write: 52 − 18 = ?

Place 52 on the place value mat: 5 tens rods, 2 ones cubes.

"I need to take away 18. Let's start with the ones. I need to take away 8 ones. How many ones do I have?" (2.)

"Can I take 8 away from 2?" (No!) "I don't have enough ones. What can I do?"

Pause and let the student think. If they suggest breaking a ten, celebrate that thinking.

**Step 2: Regroup — physically trade.**
"I need more ones. I can trade 1 ten for 10 ones!"

Pick up 1 tens rod from the Tens column. Replace it with 10 ones cubes in the Ones column.

"Now how many tens do I have?" (4 — I had 5, and I traded 1 away.)
"How many ones do I have?" (12 — I had 2, and I added 10 more.)
"Is 4 tens and 12 ones still equal to 52?" (Yes! 40 + 12 = 52.)

Write: 52 = 5 tens + 2 ones = 4 tens + 12 ones.

**Step 3: Now subtract.**
"Now I can take away 8 ones from 12 ones." Remove 8 ones cubes. "12 − 8 = 4 ones remain."

"Now take away 1 ten from 4 tens." Remove 1 tens rod. "4 − 1 = 3 tens remain."

"What's left? 3 tens and 4 ones = 34. So 52 − 18 = 34."

**Step 4: Record in writing.**
Show the vertical recording step by step:

```
  52
− 18
----
```

"Ones first: Can I do 2 − 8? No. I need to regroup."
"Cross out the 5 and write 4. Cross out the 2 and write 12."

```
  4 12
  5̶2̶
− 18
----
  34
```

"Now: 12 − 8 = 4. Write 4. Then: 4 − 1 = 3. Write 3. Answer: 34."

**Step 5: Second example with blocks.**
Write: 73 − 46 = ?

Place 7 tens and 3 ones on the mat.

"Ones: Can I subtract 6 from 3?" (No.) "Regroup!"

Trade 1 ten for 10 ones: now 6 tens and 13 ones.
73 = 6 tens + 13 ones.

Subtract ones: 13 − 6 = 7 ones.
Subtract tens: 6 − 4 = 2 tens.
Answer: 27.

Written:
```
  6 13
  7̶3̶
− 46
----
  27
```

**Step 6: Introduce "think addition" as an alternative.**
"Here's another way to think about 52 − 18. Instead of subtracting, ask: 18 + ___ = 52?"

"Start at 18. Add 2 to get to 20. Then add 32 to get to 52. 2 + 32 = 34. So 52 − 18 = 34."

"Some students find it easier to add UP to the answer rather than subtract DOWN."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** 41 − 25

Blocks: 4 tens and 1 one. Can I take 5 from 1? No. Regroup: 3 tens and 11 ones.
11 − 5 = 6 ones. 3 − 2 = 1 ten. Answer: 16.

Written:
```
  3 11
  4̶1̶
− 25
----
  16
```

Think addition: 25 + ___ = 41. 25 + 5 = 30, 30 + 11 = 41. 5 + 11 = 16. ✓

**Example 2:** 60 − 34

Blocks: 6 tens and 0 ones. Can I take 4 from 0? No. Regroup: 5 tens and 10 ones.
10 − 4 = 6 ones. 5 − 3 = 2 tens. Answer: 26.

Written:
```
  5 10
  6̶0̶
− 34
----
  26
```

Note: "When the ones digit is 0, you ALWAYS need to regroup for subtraction."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices regrouping in subtraction with scaffolded support.

**Problem 1 (with blocks): 63 − 27**
Ask: "Look at the ones. Can you take 7 from 3?" (No.)
Student regroups: 5 tens and 13 ones.
"Now subtract ones: 13 − 7 = ?" (6.) "Subtract tens: 5 − 2 = ?" (3.)
Answer: 36. ✓

**Problem 2 (with blocks and writing): 84 − 58**
Student builds 84. Checks ones: 4 < 8, must regroup. Trades 1 ten for 10 ones: 7 tens and 14 ones.
14 − 8 = 6. 7 − 5 = 2. Answer: 26. ✓
Student also records in written form with the regrouping marks.

*Facilitator prompt: If the student regroups but then forgets to reduce the tens digit, point to the blocks. "How many tens rods are on the mat now? Count them." The physical model self-corrects.*

**Problem 3 (writing, blocks available): 55 − 29**
Student writes vertically. 5 < 9, must regroup. Crosses out 5 tens, writes 4. Crosses out 5 ones, writes 15.
15 − 9 = 6. 4 − 2 = 2. Answer: 26. ✓

**Problem 4 (think addition): 72 − 45**
"Instead of subtracting, think: 45 + ___ = 72."
Student: 45 + 5 = 50, 50 + 22 = 72. 5 + 22 = 27. Answer: 27. ✓

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles with "think addition," that's okay — it's an alternative strategy, not a requirement. Return to blocks and the written method.*

**Problem 5 (student's choice of strategy): 90 − 53**
Student solves using any method. Answer: 37. ✓
Ask: "Which strategy did you use? Why?"

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build fluency with subtraction requiring regrouping.

**★ On-level:**
1. 42 − 17 = ___ (25)
2. 63 − 28 = ___ (35)
3. 81 − 45 = ___ (36)
4. 54 − 19 = ___ (35)
5. 70 − 36 = ___ (34)

**★★ Stretch:**
6. 92 − 57 = ___ (35) Show your work using the written method or break-apart strategy.
7. "Think addition: 38 + ___ = 75." (37) "So 75 − 38 = ___?" (37)
8. "Maria says 61 − 24 = 43. Is she right? If not, what mistake did she make?" (No. The correct answer is 37. Maria likely subtracted 4 − 1 in the ones column instead of regrouping. She needs to regroup: 61 = 5 tens + 11 ones. 11 − 4 = 7, 5 − 2 = 3. Answer is 37.)

**★★★ Challenge:**
9. "Find a subtraction problem where you start with a number in the 80s, subtract a number in the 40s, and get exactly 39." (e.g., 83 − 44 = 39, 82 − 43 = 39, 81 − 42 = 39)
10. "Solve this chain: Start at 95. Subtract 28. Then subtract 19 from your answer. What do you get?" (95 − 28 = 67. 67 − 19 = 48. Answer: 48.)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess subtraction with regrouping.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Solve 53 − 27. Show your work. (26)
2. Solve 80 − 46. (34)
3. "In 53 − 27, why do you need to regroup?" (Because you can't take 7 ones from 3 ones. You need to break 1 ten into 10 ones to have enough.)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct, with evidence of regrouping in the work.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student is not regrouping at all (trying to subtract 3 − 7 and getting confused): go back to the blocks. Do 5 problems purely with blocks, narrating every step. "Can I take 7 from 3? No. I need to trade."
- If the student regroups but makes arithmetic errors (e.g., 13 − 7 = 5): isolate the subtraction fact and practice it separately. The regrouping concept may be fine, but basic fact fluency needs reinforcement.
- If the student can do it with blocks but not in writing: work side-by-side. Build the problem with blocks on the mat and write the problem on paper. Each physical action (trading, removing) has a written counterpart (crossing out, writing new numbers).
- **Budget two sessions for this lesson.** Regrouping in subtraction is typically harder than regrouping in addition. Most students benefit from a second day of practice.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 10 (Word Problems Within 100). Preview: "You now know how to add and subtract any two-digit numbers. Next, we'll use these skills to solve real-world story problems!"

## Extension (Optional)
**Regrouping vs. No Regrouping Sort:** Write 10 subtraction problems on index cards. The student sorts them into two piles: "Needs Regrouping" and "No Regrouping" — WITHOUT solving them. (They just look at the ones digits.) Then solve two from each pile. This builds the important skill of recognizing when regrouping is needed.

Examples for cards:
- 67 − 23 (no) | 52 − 18 (yes) | 84 − 41 (no) | 73 − 36 (yes)
- 95 − 52 (no) | 60 − 24 (yes) | 46 − 28 (yes) | 89 − 53 (no)
- 71 − 45 (yes) | 58 − 34 (no)

**Real-world connection:** "You have 72 cents. A toy costs 48 cents. Do you have enough to buy it? How much change would you get?" (Yes. 72 − 48 = 24 cents change.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This is the hardest lesson in the unit.** Regrouping in subtraction (often called "borrowing") requires the student to understand that 52 is not just "a 5 and a 2" but "5 tens and 2 ones" — and that it can be reorganized as "4 tens and 12 ones" without changing its value. This is deep place value understanding.

**The physical trade is essential.** The moment the student picks up a tens rod, replaces it with 10 ones cubes, and sees that the total value hasn't changed — that is the moment of understanding. Do not rush past this. Do it many times. Every problem should begin with blocks until the student is clearly comfortable.

**Common errors and what they mean:**
- **Subtracting smaller from larger in each column** (e.g., 52 − 18 = 46, because 8 − 2 = 6 and 5 − 1 = 4): The student is avoiding regrouping entirely by switching the subtraction direction. The blocks prevent this because you physically cannot take 8 cubes away from a pile of 2 cubes.
- **Regrouping but not reducing the tens** (e.g., 52 − 18: writes 12 − 8 = 4 but then does 5 − 1 = 4, getting 44): The student forgot that they "used up" a ten. Point to the blocks: "You traded a tens rod — count how many tens are actually on the mat."
- **Regrouping when not needed** (e.g., regroups for 67 − 23): The student is applying the procedure mechanically. Ask: "Can you take 3 from 7? Yes! You don't need to regroup this time."

**The "think addition" strategy.** For students who find the regrouping procedure confusing, "think addition" offers an alternative. For 52 − 18, think: "18 + ___ = 52." Count up: 18 + 2 = 20, 20 + 32 = 52, so 2 + 32 = 34. This strategy avoids regrouping entirely and leverages the student's stronger addition skills. It is equally valid.

**Two sessions are normal.** Do not feel pressured to move on if the student is still shaky after one session. Regrouping in subtraction is a skill that develops over many exposures. A second session focused on practice (with blocks always available) is well worth the time.
