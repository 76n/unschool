# Math — Grade 2 — Unit 1, Lesson 4

## Topic
Adding a Two-Digit Number and a One-Digit Number (With Regrouping)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Add a two-digit number and a one-digit number when the ones digits sum to more than 9 (e.g., 37 + 5 = 42)
- Physically trade 10 ones cubes for 1 tens rod (regrouping)
- Explain why regrouping is needed: "When I have more than 9 ones, I trade 10 of them for 1 ten"
- Use the "Make a 10" strategy within the ones place to regroup mentally

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 3: Adding a two-digit number and a one-digit number without regrouping
- The "Make a 10" strategy from Grade 1 (e.g., 8 + 5: take 2 from the 5 to make 10, then 10 + 3 = 13)
- Solid understanding that 10 ones = 1 ten

## Materials Needed
- Base-ten blocks (tens rods and ones cubes) — at least 9 tens and 20 ones (you need extras for trading)
- Place value mat (paper with "Tens" and "Ones" columns)
- Paper and pencil
- Optional: 20 small objects (pennies, buttons) and rubber bands for bundling groups of 10

**Standards:** 2.NBT.5, 2.NBT.9

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge and set up the key contrast with Lesson 3.

1. 43 + 5 = ___ (No regrouping — from Lesson 3)
2. 8 + 6 = ___ (Make a 10 strategy — from Grade 1)
3. How many tens and ones in 14? ___ ten and ___ ones
4. Trade challenge: If you have 13 ones cubes, can you trade some for a tens rod? How many ones would you have left?

**Facilitator note:** Problem 4 is the key warm-up. The answer is: trade 10 ones for 1 ten, leaving 3 ones — so 13 ones = 1 ten and 3 ones. If the student understands this trade, they are ready for today's lesson.

## Direct Instruction (15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce regrouping — the most critical concept in this unit. Take your time.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Regroup:** To trade 10 ones for 1 ten (or the reverse). Sometimes called "carrying" in older textbooks, but we say "regroup" because it describes what is actually happening.
- **Trade:** Exchange 10 ones cubes for 1 tens rod — the value stays the same, but the form changes
- **Make a 10:** A strategy where you break apart a number to create a group of 10 (used within the ones place during regrouping)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Discover the problem (5 minutes)**

Write: 37 + 5

Say: "Let's build 37 with blocks." Place 3 tens rods and 7 ones cubes on the place value mat.

Say: "Now add 5 ones cubes to the ones side."

Let the student add 5 cubes. Now the mat shows: 3 tens rods and 12 ones cubes.

Ask: "How many ones do we have now?" (12)
Ask: "Is that okay? Can we have 12 in the ones place?" (No — a ones digit can only be 0 through 9.)
Say: "You are right. When we have more than 9 ones, we have a problem. We need to regroup."

**Step 2 — The trade (5 minutes)**

Say: "Watch carefully. I am going to count out 10 of these ones cubes."

Count out 10 ones cubes from the Ones column. Line them up.

Say: "10 ones cubes are the same length as 1 tens rod." (Demonstrate by placing 10 cubes next to a rod.)

Say: "So I trade these 10 ones for 1 tens rod." Remove the 10 ones cubes and place 1 tens rod into the Tens column.

Ask: "Now what do we have?"
- Tens column: 4 rods (the original 3 plus the 1 new one)
- Ones column: 2 cubes (the 12 ones minus the 10 we traded)

Say: "4 tens and 2 ones. What number is that?" (42)

Write: 37 + 5 = 42

Say: "Let me show you what happened step by step."

Write on paper:
```
  37 + 5
  7 + 5 = 12 ones    ← Too many ones!
  12 ones = 1 ten and 2 ones    ← Trade!
  3 tens + 1 new ten = 4 tens
  4 tens + 2 ones = 42
```

**Step 3 — Connect to Make a 10 (3 minutes)**

Say: "There is another way to think about this. Remember the Make a 10 strategy?"

Write: 37 + 5
Say: "37 is close to 40. How many more do I need to get from 37 to 40?" (3)
Say: "So I take 3 from the 5. That leaves 2."
Write: 37 + 3 = 40, and 2 left over.
Say: "Now I just add the leftover: 40 + 2 = 42."

Write the full strategy:
```
37 + 5
= 37 + 3 + 2      (break 5 into 3 and 2)
= 40 + 2           (37 + 3 makes the next ten)
= 42
```

Say: "Both methods give the same answer. The blocks show you *why* we regroup. The Make a 10 strategy is a shortcut for your brain."

**Step 4 — Try another together (2 minutes)**

Write: 28 + 6

Build with blocks: 2 tens, 8 ones. Add 6 ones. Now 14 ones.
Trade: 10 ones for 1 ten. Now 3 tens and 4 ones = 34.

Or with Make a 10: 28 + 2 = 30, with 4 left over. 30 + 4 = 34.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:**
Problem: 37 + 5
- Step 1: Build 37 → 3 tens, 7 ones.
- Step 2: Add 5 ones → 3 tens, 12 ones.
- Step 3: Regroup! 12 ones = 1 ten + 2 ones.
- Step 4: Move the new ten → 4 tens, 2 ones.
- Answer: 37 + 5 = 42

**Example 2:**
Problem: 46 + 8
- Step 1: Build 46 → 4 tens, 6 ones.
- Step 2: Add 8 ones → 4 tens, 14 ones.
- Step 3: Regroup! 14 ones = 1 ten + 4 ones.
- Step 4: Move the new ten → 5 tens, 4 ones.
- Answer: 46 + 8 = 54
- Make a 10 check: 46 + 4 = 50, with 4 left over. 50 + 4 = 54.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice regrouping together with facilitator support. Use base-ten blocks for every problem.

**Important:** Do not rush this section. Regrouping is the most challenging concept so far. Every problem should be built physically with blocks first.

1. 25 + 7 = ___
   - Ask: "How many ones do you have after adding? Is it more than 9? What do you do?"
   - Guide the trade: count out 10 ones, replace with 1 tens rod.
2. 48 + 4 = ___
   - Ask: "8 + 4 = ? Do you need to regroup?"
   - If the student says "12": "Yes! 12 is more than 9, so we trade."
3. 56 + 9 = ___
   - Ask: "Can you use Make a 10? How many more does 56 need to reach 60?"
   - (56 needs 4 more to reach 60. 9 − 4 = 5 left over. 60 + 5 = 65.)
4. 19 + 4 = ___
   - This one is interesting: 9 + 4 = 13. 1 ten + 1 new ten = 2 tens + 3 ones = 23.
   - Ask: "We started with 1 ten and ended with 2 tens. Where did the extra ten come from?"
5. 64 + 7 = ___
   - Say: "Try this one on your own first. Use blocks if you need them, or try it in your head."

**Facilitator note:** Watch for students who try to avoid the trade by saying "37 + 5 = 312" (writing 3 in tens, 12 in ones). This reveals the student does not yet understand that each place can hold only one digit (0-9). Go back to the physical trade and emphasize: "We cannot have 12 in the ones place. We must trade 10 of them for a ten."

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick formative assessment to gauge mastery.

### Exit Ticket

1. 38 + 6 = ___
2. 55 + 7 = ___
3. When you add 46 + 8, you get 14 ones. What do you do next?

**Mastery Criteria:** All 3 correct. For problem 3, accept any response that describes the trade/regroup (e.g., "Trade 10 ones for 1 ten" or "Regroup — 14 ones becomes 1 ten and 4 ones" or "Put the 1 ten with the other tens and keep 4 ones").

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- This is the hardest concept in the unit so far. It is completely normal for students to need a second session.
- Return to base-ten blocks. Do 5 more problems with the physical trade, narrating each step aloud:
  1. "I have ___ ones."
  2. "That is more than 9."
  3. "I count out 10 ones and trade them for 1 tens rod."
  4. "Now I have ___ tens and ___ ones."
- Try using real objects: put 10 pennies in a small bag and call it "1 dime" (1 ten). Physically seal the bag to show the trade.
- Schedule a second session on this lesson before moving to Lesson 5.

### If Mastery Is Met
Preview: "You now know how to add ones to a two-digit number — even when you need to regroup. Next, we will add two two-digit numbers together. You will add the tens AND the ones!"

## Extension (Optional)
**Purpose:** Enrichment for students who finish early or demonstrate advanced understanding.

**Regrouping Detective:** Give the student a list of addition problems and ask them to sort into two groups: "Needs Regrouping" and "No Regrouping" — WITHOUT solving them.
- 34 + 2 (No)
- 34 + 8 (Yes — 4 + 8 > 9)
- 51 + 9 (Yes — 1 + 9 = 10, which is > 9)
- 76 + 3 (No)
- 45 + 5 (Yes — 5 + 5 = 10)
- 60 + 7 (No)

Ask: "How can you tell whether a problem needs regrouping without solving it?" (Look at the ones digits. If they add to more than 9, you need to regroup.)

**Make It 50 Challenge:** Find one-digit numbers that make these equations true:
- 43 + ___ = 50 (Answer: 7)
- 46 + ___ = 50 (Answer: 4)
- 38 + ___ = 50 (Answer: 12 — wait, that is not a one-digit number! Can it be done? No — 38 + a one-digit number cannot reach 50. Discuss why.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Why This Lesson Matters
Regrouping is the gateway to all multi-digit addition. If a student understands that 10 ones can be traded for 1 ten, they will later understand that 10 tens can be traded for 1 hundred, and so on. Invest the time here — it pays off enormously in later grades.

### Common Misconceptions
- **Writing "312" instead of 42:** The student adds 3 and then 12, writing both digits. This means they do not understand that 12 ones must be regrouped. Go back to blocks and make the trade physically. Emphasize: "Each place can only hold a single digit, 0 through 9."
- **Forgetting to add the new ten:** The student regroups correctly (12 ones = 1 ten + 2 ones) but then forgets to add the new ten to the tens column. Result: 37 + 5 = 32 instead of 42. Use the place value mat: "Where does the new tens rod go? In the tens column. Count all the tens now."
- **Regrouping when it is not needed:** After learning regrouping, some students start trading on every problem — even when the ones add to less than 10. Remind them: "First, add the ones. Is it more than 9? If yes, regroup. If no, just write it down."

### Pacing Adjustments
- **Budget two sessions for this lesson if needed.** This is the most important lesson in the unit. Rushing past it will create problems in Lessons 5, 6, and beyond.
- Session 1 could cover Direct Instruction + Guided Practice. Session 2 could revisit with more guided practice + Independent Practice + Exit Ticket.
- If the student masters it quickly, the Extension activities provide excellent reinforcement.

### Discussion Questions
- "What does 'regroup' mean? Can you explain it to me like I am a younger student?"
- "When do you need to regroup? When do you not?"
- "Is 37 + 5 the same as 5 + 37? Does the order matter?" (Yes, same answer — commutative property.)
- "What would happen if we could have 12 in the ones place? Why do we only allow 0-9?"
