# Math — Grade 2 — Unit 1, Lesson 10

## Topic
Word Problems Within 100

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Solve one-step word problems using addition and subtraction within 100
- Solve two-step word problems within 100
- Identify problem types: result unknown, change unknown, start unknown, and comparison
- Draw bar models to organize information from word problems
- Write number sentences that match word problems

## Prerequisites
- All addition strategies within 100 (Lessons 2-6)
- All subtraction strategies within 100 (Lessons 7-9)
- Word problem basics (Grade 1 Unit 1, Lesson 10)

## Materials Needed
- Paper and pencil
- Base-ten blocks (available for modeling, not required for every problem)
- Colored pencils or crayons (for drawing bar models)

## Direct Instruction (15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to read word problems carefully, draw bar models, write number sentences, and solve.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Bar model:** A drawing that uses bars (rectangles) to show the parts and the whole in a problem
- **Number sentence:** A math equation that matches the word problem (e.g., 45 + 23 = 68)
- **Unknown:** The number you need to find — the answer to the problem

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Review problem types with larger numbers.**
"In first grade, we solved word problems within 20. Now we'll use the same thinking, but with bigger numbers — up to 100. The strategies are the same. The numbers are just bigger."

**Step 2: Result unknown — one-step.**
Read aloud: "A school has 38 red chairs and 45 blue chairs. How many chairs does the school have in all?"

Draw a bar model:
```
|------- Total: ? -------|
| 38 red  |  45 blue     |
```

"I know both parts. I need to find the total."
Write: 38 + 45 = ?
Solve: 8 + 5 = 13, regroup. 3 + 4 + 1 = 8. Answer: 83 chairs.

**Step 3: Change unknown — one-step.**
Read aloud: "Marcus had 62 baseball cards. He gave some to his friend and now has 35 cards. How many did he give away?"

Draw a bar model:
```
|------- 62 total -------|
| 35 left | ? gave away  |
```

"I know the total and one part. I need to find the other part."
Write: 62 − 35 = ? OR 35 + ? = 62
Solve: 62 − 35 = 27. He gave away 27 cards.

**Step 4: Start unknown — one-step.**
Read aloud: "Some children were on a playground. 28 more children came. Now there are 71 children. How many were there at first?"

Draw a bar model:
```
|------- 71 total ----------|
| ? at first  |  28 joined  |
```

"I know the total and the amount that joined. I need to find the starting amount."
Write: ? + 28 = 71, which means 71 − 28 = ?
Solve: 71 − 28. Regroup: 11 − 8 = 3, 6 − 2 = 4. Answer: 43 children.

**Step 5: Comparison.**
Read aloud: "Emma read 54 pages. Noah read 37 pages. How many more pages did Emma read than Noah?"

Draw a bar model:
```
| Emma: 54 pages            |
| Noah: 37 pages  | ? more  |
```

"I'm comparing two amounts. The difference tells me how many more."
Write: 54 − 37 = ?
Solve: Regroup. 14 − 7 = 7, 4 − 3 = 1. Answer: 17 more pages.

**Step 6: Two-step problem.**
Read aloud: "Mia had 45 stickers. She got 23 more stickers for her birthday. Then she gave 15 stickers to her sister. How many stickers does Mia have now?"

"This problem has TWO steps. Let's take it one step at a time."

Step A: 45 + 23 = 68 (stickers after birthday)
Step B: 68 − 15 = 53 (stickers after giving some away)

Draw a bar model for each step:
```
Step A: |---- ? ----|
        | 45 | 23  |
        45 + 23 = 68

Step B: |---- 68 --------|
        | ? left  | 15   |
        68 − 15 = 53
```

"Mia has 53 stickers now."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 (result unknown):** "A farmer picked 47 apples in the morning and 36 apples in the afternoon. How many apples did the farmer pick that day?"

Bar model: 47 + 36 = ?
Solve: 7 + 6 = 13, regroup. 4 + 3 + 1 = 8. Answer: 83 apples.

**Example 2 (two-step):** "There are 72 books on a shelf. A teacher takes 28 books for her class. Then a student returns 14 books. How many books are on the shelf now?"

Step A: 72 − 28 = 44.
Step B: 44 + 14 = 58.
Answer: 58 books.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices solving and modeling word problems.

**Problem 1 (result unknown):**
"A toy store has 34 stuffed animals and 49 action figures. How many toys does the store have?"
Ask: "What do you need to find?" (The total.)
"Draw a bar model and write the number sentence."
Student: 34 + 49 = 83. ✓

**Problem 2 (change unknown):**
"A jar had 75 marbles. Some fell out and now there are 48. How many fell out?"
Ask: "Do you know the total? Do you know one part?"
Student draws bar model: 75 total, 48 remain, ? fell out.
Student: 75 − 48 = 27. ✓

*Facilitator prompt: If the student writes the wrong operation, ask: "Did the number of marbles go up or down? Are we putting together or taking apart?"*

**Problem 3 (start unknown):**
"Some birds were sitting on a wire. 19 birds flew away. Now there are 34 birds. How many were there at first?"
Ask: "What don't you know?" (The starting number.)
Student: ? − 19 = 34, so 34 + 19 = 53. ✓

*Facilitator prompt: Start-unknown problems are tricky because the unknown is at the beginning. If the student gets stuck, suggest: "Think of it as adding back. If 19 flew away and 34 are left, what did you start with? 34 + 19 = ?"*

**Problem 4 (two-step):**
"A baker made 60 cookies. She sold 25 in the morning and 18 in the afternoon. How many cookies are left?"
Student: Step A: 60 − 25 = 35. Step B: 35 − 18 = 17. ✓
OR: Student adds 25 + 18 = 43, then 60 − 43 = 17. Also valid!

Ask: "Is there more than one way to solve this?" (Yes — subtract each amount one at a time, or add the amounts first and subtract once.)

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Solve a variety of word problems independently.

**★ On-level:**
1. "A parking lot has 52 cars. 24 more cars arrive. How many cars are in the lot now?" ___ Number sentence: ___ (76; 52 + 24 = 76)
2. "There are 81 crayons in a box. A student takes out 34. How many crayons are left?" ___ Number sentence: ___ (47; 81 − 34 = 47)
3. "A garden has 27 red flowers and 46 yellow flowers. How many flowers are in the garden?" ___ (73; 27 + 46 = 73)

**★★ Stretch:**
4. "Sam collected 83 rocks. He gave some to his sister and now has 55. How many did he give his sister?" ___ (28; 83 − 55 = 28)
5. "A class had some books. The teacher added 37 more books. Now there are 64 books. How many books were there before?" ___ (27; 64 − 37 = 27)
6. "Mia scored 48 points in a game. Jake scored 65 points. How many more points did Jake score than Mia?" ___ (17; 65 − 48 = 17) Draw a bar model.

**★★★ Challenge:**
7. "A bus had 45 passengers. At the first stop, 18 people got off and 12 got on. How many passengers are on the bus now?" ___ (39; 45 − 18 = 27, then 27 + 12 = 39)
8. "Ava had some stickers. She gave 24 to Ben and 19 to Cara. Now she has 32 left. How many did she start with?" ___ (75; 32 + 24 + 19 = 75, OR 24 + 19 = 43, then 43 + 32 = 75)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess word problem solving with two-digit numbers.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "A class has 36 boys and 47 girls. How many students are in the class?" Write a number sentence and solve. (83; 36 + 47 = 83)
2. "Rosa had 72 beads. She used 38 to make a bracelet. How many beads does she have left?" (34; 72 − 38 = 34)
3. "Tyler had 50 trading cards. He gave 23 to his friend and then his mom gave him 15 more. How many cards does Tyler have now?" (42; 50 − 23 = 27, then 27 + 15 = 42)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct, with matching number sentences.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student can compute but picks the wrong operation: focus on the bar model. Draw it together for every problem. Ask: "Are we putting parts together to find a total, or are we starting with a total and finding a part?"
- If the student struggles with the computation: the issue is Lessons 6 or 9, not this lesson. Return to the relevant computation lesson before reattempting word problems.
- If two-step problems are too hard: table them. Focus on one-step problems across all four types (result unknown, change unknown, start unknown, comparison). Return to two-step problems after the student is confident with one-step.

### If Mastery Is Met
The student is ready for the **Unit 1 Assessment**. Congratulations — this is the final lesson of the unit!

## Extension (Optional)
**Write Your Own:** "Write a two-step word problem that uses both addition and subtraction. The numbers should be two-digit numbers. Solve your own problem and draw a bar model." Have the student share the problem with the facilitator, who tries to solve it.

**Real-world connection:** "You have 75 cents. You want to buy a pencil that costs 38 cents and an eraser that costs 25 cents. Do you have enough money? If yes, how much change will you get? If no, how much more do you need?" (38 + 25 = 63 cents total. 75 − 63 = 12 cents change. Yes, enough money.)

**Challenge problem:** "A library shelf can hold 100 books. Right now it has 57 books. In the morning, someone returns 26 books. In the afternoon, someone checks out 14 books. How many books are on the shelf now? Will it overflow?" (57 + 26 = 83, then 83 − 14 = 69 books. No overflow — 69 is less than 100.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This lesson is about reasoning, not just computing.** The student already knows how to add and subtract within 100. The challenge here is reading a story, figuring out what operation to use, and connecting the story to a number sentence. Computation errors are computation problems (go back to Lessons 6/9). Operation errors are reasoning problems (this lesson).

**The bar model is the key tool.** A bar model (sometimes called a tape diagram) is a rectangular diagram that shows the relationship between parts and the whole. For addition problems, two parts combine to form the whole. For subtraction, the whole is split into a known part and an unknown part. For comparison, two bars of different lengths sit side by side. Teaching students to draw bar models now prepares them for more complex problem-solving in Grades 3-5.

**How to draw a bar model with the student:**
1. Read the problem aloud.
2. Ask: "What do we know? What don't we know?"
3. Draw a large rectangle for the total (or larger quantity).
4. Divide it into sections for the parts.
5. Label each section with a number or a "?".
6. The bar model tells you the operation: if "?" is the total, you add the parts; if "?" is a part, you subtract.

**Problem type difficulty (from easiest to hardest):**
1. Result unknown (add or subtract to find what's left) — most familiar
2. Change unknown (something happened, find how much changed) — requires "think backwards"
3. Comparison (find the difference between two amounts) — no physical action in the story
4. Start unknown (find the beginning amount) — most abstract
5. Two-step (combine two operations) — requires holding an intermediate result

**Two-step problems are a Grade 2 standard (2.OA.1) but they are genuinely hard.** Do not worry if the student needs significant support. The key is that they can break the problem into two manageable steps and solve each one. If the student can solve each step when told what to do but can't figure out the steps independently, that's normal — they're developing.

**Encourage multiple solution paths for two-step problems.** For example, in "sold 25 and 18, how many left from 60?", the student can either subtract 25 then 18 separately, or add 25 + 18 first and then subtract the total from 60. Both are valid, and recognizing multiple paths shows flexible thinking.
