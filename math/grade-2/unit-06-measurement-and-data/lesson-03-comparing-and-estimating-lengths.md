# Math — Grade 2 — Unit 6, Lesson 3

## Topic
Comparing and Estimating Lengths

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Estimate the length of an object using a known reference (e.g., "about 3 inches")
- Compare the lengths of two or more objects by measuring both
- Find how much longer one object is than another using subtraction
- Record and display measurement data on a simple line plot

## Prerequisites
- Measuring in inches (Lesson 1) and centimeters (Lesson 2)
- Subtraction within 100 (Unit 1)
- Vocabulary: longer, shorter, about, approximately

## Materials Needed
- Rulers (inches and centimeters)
- 5–6 classroom objects of varying lengths
- Paper strips or ribbon cut to: 1 inch, 1 foot (12 inches), and 1 meter (if available)
- Mini whiteboards
- Line plot template (number line from 1 to 10 labeled in inches)
- Sticky notes or X-marks for building the line plot

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build estimation skills through benchmarking.

**Benchmark Reference Activity:**
Show students a 1-inch paper strip. "This is 1 inch." Let each student hold it.
Ask: "About how many inches is your hand span (from pinky to thumb, spread out)? Give me a guess."
Students show estimates on whiteboards. Then measure and compare.

Repeat with: the width of your math journal.

Say: "A careful guess before measuring is called an estimate. Good mathematicians estimate first, then measure to check!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach estimation strategies and the process of comparing lengths by measuring and subtracting.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Estimate:** A thoughtful guess based on what you know; not exact
- **Compare:** To look at two measurements to find out which is more, which is less, or how different they are
- **Difference:** How much longer one object is than another; found by subtracting
- **Line plot:** A graph that uses X marks above a number line to show data

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete phase (Estimating):**
Hold up two objects. Ask: "Without measuring — which do you think is longer? By how much?" Let students handle both objects. Then measure both and find the difference.

Model: "The pencil is 7 inches. The crayon is 4 inches. How much longer is the pencil?"
Write on the board: 7 − 4 = 3. "The pencil is 3 inches longer than the crayon."

**Pictorial phase (Comparing):**
Draw two horizontal bars — one longer than the other — above a number line. Label the longer bar "7 in." and the shorter "4 in." Draw a bracket showing the difference. Connect to the subtraction equation.

**Abstract phase (Line plot):**
Explain: "When we collect lots of measurements, we can organize them on a line plot. Each X stands for one object."

Show a line plot with 6 Xs already placed above the numbers 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 5. Ask:
- "How many objects are 3 inches long?"
- "Which length is most common?"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 (Comparison):**
Rope A = 9 cm. Rope B = 5 cm.
How much longer is Rope A? 9 − 5 = 4. Rope A is 4 cm longer.

**Example 2 (Estimation):**
Show a marker. "My benchmark: a crayon is about 4 inches. This marker looks about twice as long. My estimate: about 8 inches." Measure to confirm.

**Example 3 (Line plot reading):**
A class measured 8 pencils (in inches): 5, 6, 5, 7, 6, 5, 8, 6.
Plot on the number line:
- 5 in. → XXX
- 6 in. → XXX
- 7 in. → X
- 8 in. → X

Question: "Which length was most common?" → 5 inches (and 6 inches tied). "How many pencils were measured?" → 8.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Provide 5 paper strips of different lengths (labeled A–E). Students:
1. Estimate each length in inches (before measuring).
2. Measure each strip to the nearest inch.
3. Record in the table:

| Strip | My Estimate | Actual Length |
|---|---|---|
| A | ___ in. | ___ in. |
| B | ___ in. | ___ in. |
| C | ___ in. | ___ in. |
| D | ___ in. | ___ in. |
| E | ___ in. | ___ in. |

4. Which two strips have the greatest difference in length? Find the difference.

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. A jump rope is 8 inches. A piece of string is 5 inches. How much longer is the jump rope?
   ___ − ___ = ___ inches longer

2. A caterpillar is 6 cm. An ant is 2 cm. How much shorter is the ant?
   ___ − ___ = ___ cm shorter

3. [Line plot with Xs at: 3, 3, 4, 4, 4, 5, 6, 6]
   a. How many objects were measured? ___
   b. What is the most common length? ___
   c. How many objects are 4 inches long? ___

**Stretch:**
4. Tom's pencil is 7 inches. Sara's pencil is 9 inches. Maria's pencil is 5 inches.
   a. Who has the longest pencil?
   b. How much longer is Sara's pencil than Maria's?
   c. Order the three pencils from shortest to longest.

5. A class measures 6 crayons (in cm): 8, 9, 8, 10, 9, 8. Create a line plot using this data.

**Challenge:**
6. A ribbon is 12 inches long. You need a piece that is 8 inches. If you cut off 8 inches, how long is the leftover piece? Is the leftover piece longer or shorter than 4 inches? Explain.
7. Jake estimates a stick is about 10 cm. He measures it and finds it is 14 cm. How far off was his estimate? Was his estimate too high or too low?

R1. 54 + 38 = ___ (Unit 1)
R2. 16 − 9 = ___ (Unit 2)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
1. A pencil is 8 inches. A crayon is 5 inches. How much longer is the pencil than the crayon? Write an equation. ___ − ___ = ___ inches
2. Before measuring a book, I look at it carefully and say "I think it is about 6 inches." What word describes what I am doing? _______________

**Mastery = both correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice subtraction-based comparison using linking cubes: build two towers, compare, count the difference.
- Reinforce vocabulary: "estimate" vs. "measure."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask: "Can you estimate in centimeters too? Try estimating an object and then measuring in both units."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Class Data Collection:** Measure the length of everyone's pencil (to the nearest inch). Record all data and create a class line plot together on the board. What does the data tell us?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The line plot is introduced here as a simple visual; students are not expected to create complex line plots independently yet. Lesson 9 returns to data displays with bar graphs and picture graphs.
- Estimation is a skill that requires practice and a growth mindset. Celebrate estimates that are "close" (within 1–2 inches) and avoid framing estimation as right/wrong.
- When comparing lengths, always write the subtraction equation explicitly. Do not let students stop at "which is longer" — they must find the numerical difference.
- Paper strip sets can be made from construction paper ahead of time. Suggested lengths: 2, 3, 4, 5, 7 inches.
