# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 5, Lesson 2

## Topic
Describing Patterns in Scatter Plots

## Learning Objectives
- Classify a scatter plot's association as positive, negative, or none.
- Identify an association as linear or nonlinear.
- Identify clusters and outliers in a scatter plot.
- Describe a scatter plot's pattern in words using appropriate vocabulary.

## Prerequisites
- Can construct and read a scatter plot (Unit 5, Lesson 1)
- Can describe graphs verbally using basic vocabulary (Grade 7)
- Understands that slope describes direction of a line (Grade 8, Unit 3)

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper (2 sheets)
- Pencil
- Ruler (optional, for sketching trend direction)
- Index card or half-sheet for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 8.SP.1 — Construct and interpret scatter plots for bivariate measurement data to investigate patterns of association between two quantities. Describe patterns such as clustering, outliers, positive or negative association, linear association, and nonlinear association.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review scatter plot construction and activate directional thinking.

1. True or false: In a scatter plot, you connect all the data points with a line. (False — each point is independent.)

2. Interleaved review (Unit 3 — functions): A function has the values shown below. Is it linear? How do you know?

| x | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| y | 3 | 5 | 7 | 9 | 11 |

*(Yes — y increases by 2 for every 1-unit increase in x: constant rate of change.)*

3. You plot data comparing temperature (°F) and hot chocolate sales. As temperature goes up, you expect sales to go ___? (Down — fewer people want hot chocolate when it's warm.)

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Positive association** — as x increases, y tends to increase. Points go from lower-left to upper-right.
- **Negative association** — as x increases, y tends to decrease. Points go from upper-left to lower-right.
- **No association** — x and y have no apparent relationship. Points are scattered with no direction.
- **Linear association** — the pattern follows a roughly straight line.
- **Nonlinear association** — the pattern follows a curve, not a straight line.
- **Cluster** — a group of points bunched closely together in one region of the plot.
- **Outlier** — a point that falls far from the general pattern of the other data.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Direction of association.**

Say: "The first thing to notice about a scatter plot is its direction. Ask yourself: as x increases, does y tend to go up, go down, or stay the same?"

Describe three patterns for students to sketch as small thumbnail graphs:

*Pattern A — Positive association:* "Study hours vs. test score. Students who study more tend to score higher. Points drift from lower-left to upper-right. As x increases, y increases."

*Pattern B — Negative association:* "Hours of TV watched vs. hours of sleep. Students who watch more TV tend to get less sleep. Points drift from upper-left to lower-right. As x increases, y decreases."

*Pattern C — No association:* "Shoe size vs. math quiz score. There is no reason to expect shoe size to predict quiz score. Points are scattered all over with no direction."

Say: "Direction alone doesn't say anything about cause. A positive association just means the two variables tend to move together."

**Step 2 — Form of association.**

Say: "Once we notice direction, we ask about form. Does the pattern follow a straight line, or does it curve?"

*Linear:* Points cluster around a straight line. Example: height vs. arm span — both increase together in a fairly straight-line relationship.

*Nonlinear:* Points curve. Example: speed vs. fuel efficiency in a car — as speed increases, fuel efficiency first improves then worsens, creating a curved relationship. For now, we will focus mostly on linear associations.

**Step 3 — Clusters and outliers.**

Say: "Sometimes data has sub-groups that cluster together. For example, a scatter plot of points scored vs. minutes played might show two clusters — one for guards (who play many minutes) and one for bench players (who play fewer minutes)."

Say: "An outlier is a point far from the rest. Outliers are important: they may represent a data entry error, an unusual observation, or an especially interesting case. An outlier doesn't automatically get deleted — we need to investigate it."

**Step 4 — Putting it all together.**

Model a complete description: "This scatter plot shows average weekly screen time (hours) vs. average hours of sleep per night for 10 teenagers. The data shows a moderate negative linear association — as screen time increases, hours of sleep tend to decrease. There is one possible outlier at (12 hours screen time, 9 hours sleep) — unusually high sleep for high screen time. The rest of the data clusters fairly consistently."

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

Classify each scatter plot description as (a) positive, negative, or no association; and (b) linear or nonlinear. Then write one sentence explaining the real-world meaning.

1. As the number of ads a product runs per week increases, sales tend to increase in a roughly straight-line pattern.

2. A scatter plot of students' heights vs. their test scores shows points scattered all over the graph with no apparent direction.

3. As a car's age increases, its value decreases. The drop is steep in the first few years and then levels off.

4. As outside temperature (°F) increases from 60°F to 95°F, the number of people at a local pool increases in an approximately straight-line pattern.

5. A scatter plot of a jogger's mile pace (minutes per mile) vs. distance run (miles) shows that pace gets slightly slower at longer distances, but the data curves upward rather than following a straight line.

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

6. A student says: "Since the scatter plot shows a positive association between social media use and anxiety levels, social media causes anxiety." Write a response to this student. What is correct in their statement? What is incorrect?

7. Consider this statement: "An outlier always makes a data set less reliable." Do you agree or disagree? Give a specific example to support your answer.

8. A scatter plot has two distinct clusters. Cluster A contains athletes (high fitness, low resting heart rate) and Cluster B contains non-athletes. If you described the scatter plot without knowing about the two groups, what might you say? How does knowing about the two groups change your interpretation?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

9. (Interleaved — Unit 2 linear equations) A line passes through the points (2, 55) and (8, 85). Write the equation of this line in slope-intercept form. How does the slope relate to what you'd see in a scatter plot with these two points?

10. Sketch the general shape of a scatter plot that shows a nonlinear association where: at first, as x increases, y decreases rapidly; then the rate of decrease slows and y levels off near a constant value. Describe the shape. Give a real-world example of data that might look like this.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A scatter plot shows data about hours of training per week and athletic injury rate. As training hours increase from 3 to 8 hours, injury rates generally decrease. Above 8 hours, injury rates sharply increase. Classify this association as positive, negative, no association, or nonlinear. Explain your answer.

**Problem 2:** A scatter plot of smartphone screen size (inches) vs. smartphone price ($) shows points mostly clustered between 5 and 6.5 inches, except for one point at 3 inches and $800 (a very small, expensive collector's phone). What term describes the $800 point?

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

---

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. **Positive, linear.** As the number of weekly ads increases, product sales tend to increase in an approximately straight-line pattern — more advertising, more sales.

2. **No association.** Height and test scores are unrelated — knowing a student's height tells you nothing about their test score.

3. **Negative, nonlinear.** As a car ages, its value decreases, but the relationship curves (steep drop at first, leveling off later). This is called exponential decay and is not linear.

4. **Positive, linear.** Warmer temperatures draw more people to the pool in an approximately straight-line relationship in the range described.

5. **Positive (pace increases with distance), nonlinear.** The pace slows (gets larger in value) as distance increases, but the relationship curves rather than following a straight line — a nonlinear positive association.

6. "I see a positive association in the scatter plot, which means students who use social media more tend to report higher anxiety on average. However, a scatter plot only shows association — it does not prove causation. We cannot conclude from this data alone that social media causes anxiety. There could be a third variable (like stress from school or sleep deprivation) that causes both higher social media use and higher anxiety." Accept any answer that correctly identifies association vs. causation and names a plausible third variable.

7. **Disagree.** An outlier can be meaningful and informative. Example: if most marathon runners finish in 3-5 hours but one runner finishes in 1.5 hours, that outlier might represent an elite professional athlete — a valid, interesting observation, not an error. Removing it would hide important information. The key is to investigate the outlier, not automatically delete it. Accept any well-reasoned example.

8. Without knowing the two groups, you might describe the scatter plot as having "two clusters with different ranges of x and y values, and possibly a moderately negative association" (if the non-athletes tend to have lower fitness and higher heart rates). Knowing about the two groups, you would describe it as two distinct clusters corresponding to different populations, and the association within each cluster might be weaker than the overall visual suggests. (This previews Simpson's Paradox, which is not required but may come up organically.)

9. Slope m = (85 - 55)/(8 - 2) = 30/6 = **5**. y-intercept: 55 = 5(2) + b → 55 = 10 + b → b = 45. Equation: **y = 5x + 45**. In a scatter plot, a line through these two points would slope upward to the right with a rise of 5 for every 1 unit of run — a positive association with slope 5.

10. Shape: starts high on the left, drops steeply, then gradually flattens and approaches a horizontal line on the right (like an exponential decay curve). Real-world example: the number of people who haven't heard about a new product decreases rapidly at first (word spreads fast) and then levels off as almost everyone has heard; or a cup of hot coffee cooling down — it drops in temperature fast initially, then more slowly as it approaches room temperature.

### Exit Ticket

1. **Nonlinear.** The pattern is not a straight line — injury rates decrease and then increase. It forms a U-shape (or roughly a parabola). Neither "positive" nor "negative" captures the full pattern. This is a nonlinear association.

2. The $800 point is an **outlier** — it falls far from the general pattern of the other data points.

---

## Teacher Notes

- The nonlinear vs. linear distinction is important but do not dwell on it — only linear associations will be modeled with a line of best fit (Lessons 3-5). When students see a nonlinear pattern, they should recognize it as nonlinear but not be asked to model it.
- The two-cluster data in the challenge question (Tier 2 #8) previews a sophisticated statistical concept. If the student engages deeply, it is worth a rich discussion; do not require mastery of this point.
- The Exit Ticket problem about training and injury rates is a classic nonlinear pattern (the "sweet spot" of optimal training). Use it to reinforce that not all data is linear.
- Interleaved review: Tier 3 Problem 9 revisits writing linear equations from Unit 2. This previews Lesson 5 explicitly while maintaining fluency with prior skills.
- The rainfall vs. ice cream data in Guided Practice will reappear conceptually in Lesson 6 (correlation vs. causation). You may briefly note: "This is interesting data to think about — we'll come back to why rainfall and ice cream seem connected in a few lessons."
