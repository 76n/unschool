## Section 1: Scatter Plots — Construction and Description (14 points)

**1.1.** A sports analyst records the number of minutes of sleep a college runner gets the night before a race and their finishing time (in minutes) for a 5K. Here is the data for 10 runners:

| Runner | Sleep (hours) | 5K Time (min) |
|--------|--------------|--------------|
| A      | 5            | 26.5         |
| B      | 6            | 25.0         |
| C      | 7            | 23.5         |
| D      | 8            | 22.0         |
| E      | 9            | 21.0         |
| F      | 6.5          | 24.0         |
| G      | 7.5          | 22.8         |
| H      | 8.5          | 21.5         |
| I      | 5.5          | 26.0         |
| J      | 4            | 28.0         |

(a) Plot the scatter plot on graph paper. Use the x-axis for sleep hours and the y-axis for 5K time. Label both axes clearly. **(4 points)**

| Component | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Both axes labeled with variable names and units | 1 point |
| Appropriate scale on each axis | 1 point |
| All 10 points plotted correctly | 2 points (1 point if 7-9 points are correct) |

(b) Identify the runner with the slowest (highest) 5K time. How many hours of sleep did they get? **(1 point)**

(c) A runner at (7, 23.5) does not appear to fit the overall trend as closely as the others. Is this runner an outlier, or is the point consistent with the pattern? Explain. **(1 point)**

(d) Describe the association in this scatter plot. Include: direction, form (linear or nonlinear), and whether there are any notable outliers. Write 2-3 complete sentences. **(4 points)**

| Component | Points |

(e) What does the negative association in this scatter plot mean in the context of running performance? **(1 point)**

(f) Runner K was not in the study. She slept 10 hours and ran a 5K in 24 minutes. If you added her to the scatter plot, would her point fit the pattern? Would she be an outlier? Explain. **(1 point)**

(g) Would you use a scatter plot to analyze the relationship between a runner's name and their shoe size? Explain why or why not. **(1 point)**

|-----------|--------|

| Component | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Names a plausible lurking variable | 1 point |

## Section 5: Data Reasoning — Integrated Interpretation (6 points)

**5.1.** A fitness researcher collects two types of data about teenagers: numerical data (hours of sleep vs. fitness score) and categorical data (whether they eat breakfast vs. whether they feel focused in school).

The scatter plot of sleep hours vs. fitness score shows a strong positive linear association. The two-way table for breakfast and focus shows that 80% of breakfast eaters feel focused vs. 45% of breakfast skippers.

(a) For each data type, identify the appropriate analysis tool and what it measures. **(2 points)**

| Component | Points |

If teens just sleep more and eat breakfast, they will be fitter and more focused in school." Write a paragraph evaluating this conclusion. ## Answer Key

### Section 1: Scatter Plots

**1.1a.** Scatter plot checklist:
- x-axis: "Sleep (hours)", scale from approximately 3.5 to 10 (or 0 to 10).
- y-axis: "5K Time (min)", scale from approximately 20 to 30 (or 18 to 30).
- 10 points plotted at: (5, 26.5), (6, 25.0), (7, 23.5), (8, 22.0), (9, 21.0), (6.5, 24.0), (7.5, 22.8), (8.5, 21.5), (5.5, 26.0), (4, 28.0).

**1.1b.** Runner J has the slowest 5K time of **28.0 minutes** and slept **4 hours**.

**1.1c.** Runner C at (7, 23.5) is **consistent with the pattern** — it falls close to where the trend line would predict for 7 hours of sleep. It is not an outlier; it is within the data cloud.

**1.1d.** Excellent description sample: "The scatter plot shows a moderate-to-strong negative linear association between hours of sleep and 5K finish time. As sleep hours increase, 5K finish time tends to decrease — runners who sleep more tend to run faster. The data points cluster fairly closely around an invisible straight line going from upper-left to lower-right. There are no strong outliers; all points follow the general pattern reasonably well."

**1.1e.** The negative association means runners who get more sleep tend to finish their 5K races in less time (faster). More sleep is associated with better race performance.

**1.1f.** Runner K at (10, 24.0) would be an outlier. The general trend predicts that a runner with 10 hours of sleep should run even faster (approximately 20-21 minutes based on the trend). Runner K's time of 24 minutes is much slower than the trend would predict — she falls well above the trend line. She is an outlier.

**1.1g.** No — a scatter plot is used for two numerical (quantitative) variables. "Runner's name" is categorical, not numerical; it cannot be plotted on a number scale. Scatter plots require both axes to have meaningful numerical values.

### Section 2: Line of Best Fit

**2.1a.** The line must be straight and pass through the middle of the data cloud. Award 2 points if both criteria are met, 1 point if line is straight but noticeably off-center.

**2.1b.** Two points on the line — not required to be data table values. Sample: (4, 28.0) and (9, 21.0). Students' answers will vary based on their own lines.

**2.1c.** Using sample points (4, 28.0) and (9, 21.0):
m = (21.0 - 28.0)/(9 - 4) = -7.0/5 = **-1.4 minutes per hour of sleep**.
Accept any slope in approximately the range -1.0 to -1.7 based on the student's own line.

**2.1d.** Using (4, 28.0) and m = -1.4:
28.0 = -1.4(4) + b → 28.0 = -5.6 + b → b = **33.6**.
Equation: y = -1.4x + 33.6. Accept any y-intercept consistent with the student's own slope and chosen point.

**2.1e.** y = **-1.4x + 33.6** (or equivalent consistent with student's work).

**2.1f.** "For each additional hour of sleep, a runner's 5K time changes by approximately **-1.4 minutes (1 minute and 24 seconds)**, which means runners tend to finish about 1.4 minutes faster for every additional hour of sleep the night before the race."

**2.1g.** y = -1.4(7.5) + 33.6 = -10.5 + 33.6 = **23.1 minutes**. (Accept any prediction computed correctly from the student's own equation.) **Interpolation** — 7.5 hours is within the data range of 4 to 9 hours.

**2.1h.** The researcher should NOT trust the prediction. Two reasons:
1. **Extrapolation** — 14 hours of sleep is outside the data range (data goes from 4 to 9 hours), so the model has no data to support it beyond this range.
2. **Physiological implausibility** — 14 hours of sleep per night is far above normal range for a college runner; the model predicts conditions that were not observed and may be meaningless. (Accept any two of: extrapolation, outside data range, unusual value, model may not hold, equation would give 33.6 - 1.4(14) = 33.6 - 19.6 = 14.0 minutes which is implausibly fast.)

### Section 3: Correlation vs. Causation

**3.1a.** No — the headline does not establish causation. This is an observational study (families are not randomly assigned to have books or not). Correlation between book quantity and vocabulary exists, but other factors could explain it. A controlled experiment would be needed to confirm that books themselves cause vocabulary improvement.

**3.1b.** Excellent lurking variable: **Family income / socioeconomic status**. Wealthier families tend to own more books (they can afford them) AND tend to provide better educational environments (tutoring, better schools, more reading time) that independently develop vocabulary. Both number of books and vocabulary score are driven upward by higher family income. Accept any plausible lurking variable: parental education level, time spent reading, access to quality education, etc.

**3.2a.** **Faulty reasoning.** The correlation between internet access and life expectancy is likely explained by a lurking variable — economic development. Wealthier countries have more internet access AND better healthcare, food security, and working conditions. Simply providing internet access to poorer countries without addressing other development factors would not necessarily raise life expectancy.

**3.2b.** **Correct reasoning.** This is a randomized controlled experiment, not an observational study. Students were randomly assigned to use the app or not — this controls for lurking variables (prior vocabulary knowledge, parenting, education quality) that would confound an observational study. Random assignment plus a significant outcome difference supports a causal conclusion.

**3.3.** Three explanations:
1. **A causes B** (direct causation in that direction).
2. **B causes A** (reverse causation).
3. **A third variable C causes both A and B** (common cause / lurking variable).
*(Award credit also for: coincidence/spurious correlation, if listed.)*

### Section 4: Two-Way Tables

**4.1a.** Complete table:

|                    | Happy | Not Happy | Row Total |
|--------------------|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **Athlete**        | 65    | 25        | 90        |
| **Non-Athlete**    | 50    | 40        | 90        |
| **Column Total**   | 115   | 65        | 180       |

**4.1b.** Row check: 90 + 90 = **180**. Column check: 115 + 65 = **180**. Both equal the grand total. ✓

**4.2a.** Joint relative frequencies (÷ 180):
- Athlete/Happy: 65/180 ≈ **36.1%** (≈ 36%)
- Athlete/Not Happy: 25/180 ≈ **13.9%** (≈ 14%)
- Non-Athlete/Happy: 50/180 ≈ **27.8%** (≈ 28%)
- Non-Athlete/Not Happy: 40/180 ≈ **22.2%** (≈ 22%)

**4.2b.** Row relative frequencies:
- Athletes (row total 90): Happy = 65/90 ≈ **72.2%** (≈ 72%); Not Happy = 25/90 ≈ **27.8%** (≈ 28%).
- Non-Athletes (row total 90): Happy = 50/90 ≈ **55.6%** (≈ 56%); Not Happy = 40/90 ≈ **44.4%** (≈ 44%).

**4.2c.** "There is a moderate association between sports participation and social happiness. Athletes report being happy with their social lives at a higher rate (72%) than non-athletes (56%). The two groups have noticeably different distributions — athletes are more likely to report social happiness."

**4.3.** Among students who are NOT happy (column total = 65): Athletes = 25/65 ≈ **38.5%**.
This is a **column relative frequency** (also called a conditional relative frequency — conditioned on "Not Happy").

**4.4.** No — we cannot conclude causation. This is an observational study: students self-selected into sports or non-sports. Students who choose to play sports may differ from non-athletes in many ways (social personality, family support, time management) that independently cause greater social happiness. A controlled study would be needed to establish that sports participation itself causes the higher happiness rate.

### Section 5: Data Reasoning

**5.1a.**
- Numerical bivariate data (sleep hours vs. fitness score) → **scatter plot**; measures the strength, direction, and form of the linear association, and can model the trend with a line of best fit and equation.
- Categorical bivariate data (breakfast yes/no vs. focus yes/no) → **two-way frequency table**; measures the distribution of one categorical variable within groups defined by the other, using relative frequencies to identify association.

**5.1b.** Excellent response sample: "The student's conclusion oversimplifies the data. While the scatter plot does show a positive association between sleep and fitness score, and the two-way table shows that breakfast eaters focus better, neither of these establishes causation. Both are observational studies — researchers did not randomly assign teenagers to sleep amounts or breakfast habits. Many lurking variables could explain the patterns: for example, students with high motivation or strong family support might both sleep more AND exercise more, independently driving higher fitness scores. Similarly, students with stable morning routines might both eat breakfast AND feel more focused — the breakfast itself may not be causing the focus. To strengthen these conclusions, researchers would need to conduct controlled experiments: randomly assign some teens to a specific sleep schedule or require breakfast for a randomly selected group, then compare outcomes to a control group. Only then could we more confidently claim that sleep and breakfast cause the observed improvements."

## Section-Specific Reteaching Guide

| If struggling in... | Focus on... |
|---|---|
| Section 1 (Scatter Plots) | Return to Lessons 1-2. Confirm the student can plot ordered pairs accurately on a coordinate plane — this is the foundation. Practice the vocabulary: positive/negative/no association, linear/nonlinear, cluster, outlier. Use the "direction check": draw an arrow showing where the data cloud moves from left to right. Is it going up (positive) or down (negative)? For written descriptions, use the sentence frame: "As [x-variable] increases, [y-variable] tends to [increase/decrease]. The association is [linear/nonlinear]. There [is/are / is no] outlier(s)." |
| Section 2 (Line of Best Fit) | Return to Lessons 3-5. The key skill cluster is: draw line → pick two points ON the line → compute slope → solve for y-intercept → write equation → interpret slope and y-intercept. Practice each step separately. Common errors: (1) picking data table points instead of points on the drawn line; (2) forgetting to solve for b and using a data y-value instead; (3) interpreting slope as a total rather than a rate of change per unit. For interpretation: practice saying "For each additional [x-unit], [y-variable] changes by [m] [y-unit]." |
| Section 3 (Correlation vs. Causation) | Return to Lesson 6. Review all four example scenarios (ice cream and drowning, shoe size and reading level, Nicolas Cage films, screen time and grades). The core idea: association shows that two variables move together, but it does not tell us why. Practice identifying lurking variables by asking: "What other factor is related to both X and Y?" Practice distinguishing between observational studies (cannot establish causation) and randomized controlled experiments (can establish causation). |
| Section 4 (Two-Way Tables) | Return to Lessons 7-9. First confirm the student can build a table correctly (Lesson 7). Then confirm the student can compute all three types of relative frequency using the correct denominator (Lesson 8). Final step: comparing row relative frequencies to identify association (Lesson 9). The denominator rule is critical: grand total → joint relative frequency; row total → row conditional frequency; column total → column conditional frequency. |
| Section 5 (Data Reasoning) | This section requires integration rather than a specific reteachable skill. Return to Lesson 10 (review). Practice having the student explain in words what each calculation means. The ability to write about data in context is developed through repeated practice with real data — assign new data scenarios and require written interpretations. |

## Error Analysis Guide

| Error Pattern | Example | Likely Cause | Intervention |
|--------------|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Connects data points in scatter plot | Draws a line connecting (4, 28) to (5, 26.5) to (5.5, 26.0) etc. | Confuses scatter plot with line graph | Reinforce: scatter plot = individual points, no connections. Each person is one dot, standing alone. Practice plotting 5 unconnected points and labeling each. |
| Draws line of best fit through first and last data points | Line connects (4, 28.0) to (9, 21.0) only | Does not understand the goal is to minimize overall distance from all points | Ask: "How many points are above your line? How many below?" The line must balance all points, not just start at one end and end at the other. |
| Uses data table coordinates to calculate slope instead of line coordinates | Computes slope between (5, 26.5) and (9, 21.0) from data table | Misunderstands which points to use | Explicit instruction: "Put your pencil on the line itself — not on a data dot. Find two spots on the line that are as far apart as possible." |
| Confuses joint and row relative frequencies | Divides 65 (athletes/happy) by 180 (grand total) when asked for row relative frequency | Does not distinguish the three denominator types | Denominator drill: For each question, ask "What total am I dividing into?" Write the denominator before computing. Use color coding: row totals in blue, column totals in green, grand total in red. |
| Says correlation proves causation | "The scatter plot proves sleep causes faster racing" | Does not apply the correlation ≠ causation principle | Return to Lesson 6. Practice the three-question check: (1) Is this an experiment or an observation? (2) What might be a lurking variable? (3) Could the causation run in the reverse direction? |
| Interprets slope as a total rather than a rate | Says "the slope of -1.4 means runners will run 1.4 minutes total" | Misunderstands rate of change | Clarify: slope is a rate, "per unit." For each one-unit increase in x, y changes by m. Practice: "What happens to y when x increases by 1? By 2? By 5?" |
| Row totals do not add to grand total | Leaves column totals inconsistent | Arithmetic error in table construction | Require the double-check after every table: sum of row totals = grand total; sum of column totals = same grand total. If they disagree, an error exists. |

## Facilitator Observations

- Can the student plot bivariate data accurately from a table without additional prompting?
- Does the student correctly identify the type of association (positive/negative/none, linear/nonlinear) and use precise vocabulary in describing it?
- When drawing a line of best fit, does the student use a ruler, and does the line pass through the middle of the data cloud?
- Does the student pick points ON the line of best fit (not from the data table) for slope calculations?
- Can the student correctly interpret slope and y-intercept in context — not just state the numbers but explain what they mean?
- Does the student spontaneously apply the interpolation vs. extrapolation distinction when making predictions?
- Can the student identify at least one lurking variable when presented with a correlation claim?
- Does the student correctly use the grand total, row total, or column total as the denominator for each type of relative frequency?
- Can the student identify association in a two-way table by comparing row relative frequencies?
- In the extended reasoning question (Section 5), does the student connect correlation and causation reasoning to both data types?
