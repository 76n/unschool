# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 5, Lesson 1

## Topic
Introduction to Scatter Plots

## Learning Objectives
- Define bivariate data and explain what a scatter plot displays.
- Read a two-variable data table and identify ordered pairs.
- Plot ordered pairs from a data table to construct a scatter plot.
- Read a completed scatter plot to find data values and answer questions.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to plot and read ordered pairs in all four quadrants of the coordinate plane (Grade 6-7)
- Understanding that the first coordinate in an ordered pair corresponds to the horizontal axis (Grade 6)
- Familiarity with reading data tables (Grade 6-7)

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper (2 sheets minimum)
- Ruler or straightedge
- Pencil
- Index card or half-sheet for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 8.SP.1 — Construct and interpret scatter plots for bivariate measurement data to investigate patterns of association between two quantities.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of coordinate plane plotting.

1. Plot these points on a coordinate plane: A(3, 7), B(5, 2), C(8, 9), D(1, 4). Label each point.

2. A student records data in a table about two things they measured:

| Trial | Time (seconds) | Distance (meters) |
|-------|---------------|-------------------|
| 1     | 2             | 10                |
| 2     | 4             | 20                |
| 3     | 6             | 30                |

   - Which column would you put on the x-axis? (Time, since it is the independent variable)
   - What ordered pair represents Trial 2? ((4, 20))

3. Quick mental math review (Unit 1 interleaved): Evaluate 3^2 + 4^2. (9 + 16 = 25)

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Bivariate data** — data that records two different measurements for each person or object in the study.
- **Scatter plot** — a coordinate graph that displays bivariate data as a collection of individual points.
- **Independent variable** — the variable plotted on the x-axis; often the one you measure or control.
- **Dependent variable** — the variable plotted on the y-axis; often the one you want to predict or observe.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — What is bivariate data?**

Say: "Until now, most of the data you have worked with was univariate — tracking one thing at a time, like a list of test scores or a list of temperatures. Bivariate means two variables. We are tracking two measurements for each person or object."

Example: A class records each student's height (in inches) and the amount of time they sleep each night (in hours). For each student, we have two numbers: one for height and one for sleep time. That is bivariate data.

**Step 2 — What is a scatter plot?**

Say: "A scatter plot is the graph we use to display bivariate data. Each individual in the data set becomes one point on the graph. The x-coordinate comes from the first variable; the y-coordinate comes from the second variable."

Write: "One person = one point. Each point = (x-variable value, y-variable value)."

**Step 3 — Choosing axes and scale.**

Work through this example: We have data on hours of weekly practice and free-throw percentage for 8 basketball players.

| Player | Weekly Practice (hours) | Free-Throw % |
|--------|------------------------|--------------|
| A      | 3                       | 55           |
| B      | 5                       | 60           |
| C      | 7                       | 72           |
| D      | 4                       | 58           |
| E      | 9                       | 80           |
| F      | 6                       | 68           |
| G      | 2                       | 50           |
| H      | 8                       | 75           |

Say: "Practice hours is the independent variable — it goes on the x-axis. Free-throw percentage is the dependent variable — it goes on the y-axis. Now set up the axes:
- x-axis: label 'Weekly Practice (hours)', scale from 0 to 10 by 1s.
- y-axis: label 'Free-Throw %', scale from 40 to 90 by 5s (or 0 to 100 if you have room).
Each player becomes one point. Player A is (3, 55). Player B is (5, 60). And so on."

Plot all 8 points while narrating. Emphasize: we do NOT connect the dots — each point is independent.

**Step 4 — Reading a scatter plot.**

After plotting: "We can now read information directly from the scatter plot. Which player has the most practice hours? (Player E at 9 hours.) What was Player C's free-throw percentage? (72%.) Is there a player with very few practice hours and a low free-throw percentage? (Player G, 2 hours, 50%.)"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Given this data table, plot the scatter plot.

| Student | Hours of Sleep | Math Quiz Score |
|---------|---------------|-----------------|
| 1       | 5             | 62              |
| 2       | 8             | 85              |
| 3       | 6             | 70              |
| 4       | 7             | 79              |
| 5       | 9             | 88              |
| 6       | 4             | 55              |

x-axis: Hours of Sleep (0 to 10), y-axis: Math Quiz Score (40 to 100 by 10s).
Points: (5, 62), (8, 85), (6, 70), (7, 79), (9, 88), (4, 55). Plot each point and label if desired.

**Example B:** From the completed scatter plot above, answer:
- What score did the student who slept 9 hours earn? (88)
- How many hours did the student who scored 70 sleep? (6 hours)
- Which student slept the least? (Student 6, 4 hours)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity: Constructing and Reading a Scatter Plot**

A track team records each athlete's weekly training mileage and their 5K race time (in minutes). Use this data to answer the questions below.

| Athlete | Weekly Mileage | 5K Time (min) |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| M       | 15            | 28.5           |
| N       | 30            | 22.0           |
| O       | 10            | 32.0           |
| P       | 25            | 24.5           |
| Q       | 35            | 21.0           |
| R       | 20            | 26.0           |
| S       | 5             | 36.0           |
| T       | 28            | 23.0           |

1. What goes on the x-axis? What goes on the y-axis? Explain your choice.
   *(Weekly mileage on x-axis — independent; 5K time on y-axis — dependent. Training affects race performance.)*

2. Set up axes on graph paper. Choose a scale for each axis.
   *(x: 0 to 40 by 5s; y: 18 to 40 by 2s, or similar.)*

3. Plot all 8 points on the scatter plot.

4. Which athlete trains the most? What is their 5K time?
   *(Athlete Q, 35 miles, 21.0 minutes.)*

5. Find the athlete with the slowest (highest) 5K time. How many miles do they train weekly?
   *(Athlete S, 36.0 minutes, 5 miles.)*

6. Two athletes have 5K times within 1 minute of each other. Identify them and compare their training mileage.
   *(Athlete N: 22.0 min, 30 miles; Athlete T: 23.0 min, 28 miles — about 1 minute apart, similar mileage.)*

**Facilitator tip:** Watch for students who connect the dots in order rather than leaving them as individual points. Prompt: "Each dot represents one athlete standing alone. There is no 'path' from one to the next."

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

A nutritionist records the amount of sugar (grams) and the number of calories in 7 popular snack items:

| Snack | Sugar (g) | Calories |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| 1     | 5         | 120      |
| 2     | 12        | 180      |
| 3     | 3         | 95       |
| 4     | 18        | 220      |
| 5     | 8         | 145      |
| 6     | 15        | 200      |
| 7     | 20        | 250      |

1. Identify the independent and dependent variables. Explain your choice.
2. Set up a coordinate plane on graph paper with appropriate axis labels and scale.
3. Plot all 7 data points.
4. Which snack has the most sugar? How many calories does it have?
5. Which snack has the fewest calories? How much sugar does it have?
6. A new snack has 10 grams of sugar. Based on the other data, do you expect its calorie count to be closer to 100 or 200? Explain.

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

Use the same nutritionist data above, plus these additional questions:

7. Two snacks have very similar calorie counts. Identify them. Do they also have similar sugar content?
8. If you added a snack with 0 grams of sugar, would you expect its calorie count to be 0? Explain why or why not.
9. A student argues that sugar grams should go on the y-axis and calories on the x-axis. Is this valid? What does the choice of axes assume about the relationship between the variables?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

10. Design your own bivariate data set. Choose two variables you think might be related (for example: hours spent on social media and hours of sleep, or temperature and number of people at the beach). Write 8 data values you would plausibly observe. Plot your scatter plot and write one sentence about what relationship (if any) you expect.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A researcher collects this data:

| Person | Age (years) | Reaction Time (ms) |
|--------|-------------|---------------------|
| 1      | 20          | 240                 |
| 2      | 35          | 260                 |
| 3      | 50          | 290                 |
| 4      | 65          | 320                 |

Which variable belongs on the x-axis? Write the four ordered pairs for this scatter plot.

**Problem 2:** From the scatter plot of the track team data from Guided Practice: which athlete would be plotted at the point (20, 26)?

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

---

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. **Sugar is independent** (x-axis); **calories is dependent** (y-axis). We expect calorie count to depend on sugar content — more sugar typically means more calories.

2. x-axis: "Sugar (g)", 0 to 25 by 5s (or 2s). y-axis: "Calories", 80 to 270 by 20s (or 10s). Scales may vary; credit any reasonable choice.

3. Points plotted: (5, 120), (12, 180), (3, 95), (18, 220), (8, 145), (15, 200), (20, 250). Each should be a distinct dot (not connected).

4. **Snack 7** has the most sugar (20 g) and **250 calories**.

5. **Snack 3** has the fewest calories (95) and **3 grams** of sugar.

6. **Closer to 200.** Ten grams of sugar is above the midpoint of the data range. Looking at snacks with similar sugar content (Snack 2: 12 g = 180 cal, Snack 5: 8 g = 145 cal), 10 g falls between those two, suggesting roughly 150-165 calories — but the trend overall points toward higher counts as sugar increases, so 200 is a reasonable ballpark guess. Accept any explanation that refers to the data trend.

7. **Snacks 2 and 6** are closest in calories (180 vs. 200). Their sugar content (12 g vs. 15 g) is also fairly similar — not identical, but in the same range.

8. **No.** Foods can have many calories from fat, protein, or starch even with 0 sugar. The y-intercept of this data trend would not be 0. This shows that sugar is not the only contributor to calories.

9. **This is valid — either axis placement works mathematically.** The convention is that the x-axis holds the variable we use to predict the other. If we believe calorie count can be used to predict or explain sugar content, then calories could be on the x-axis. The choice of axes reflects an assumption about which variable is "explanatory."

10. Accept any plausible bivariate data set with 8 points, appropriate axis labels, and a sentence describing the expected relationship. Check that points are plotted correctly for the student's own data.

### Exit Ticket

1. **Age belongs on the x-axis** (independent variable; reaction time is observed as age changes). Ordered pairs: **(20, 240), (35, 260), (50, 290), (65, 320).**

2. Athlete **R** is at (20, 26) — 20 miles weekly, 26.0-minute 5K time.

## Teacher Notes

- The most common error in this lesson is connecting the points as if drawing a line graph. Emphasize repeatedly: each point is an independent observation. The points are not connected because there is no "in-between" data point between them.
- Students may struggle to choose an appropriate scale for the axes. Guide them to find the minimum and maximum values in each column, then choose a scale that spaces the data across most of the graph paper. A scatter plot crammed into one corner of the paper obscures patterns.
- The independent/dependent variable discussion can be nuanced. In some data sets, either variable could reasonably go on either axis. Focus students on the question: "Which variable do we think influences the other?" This mirrors the structure they will use when they write equations in Lesson 5.
- Interleaved review: the Warm-Up includes a brief exponent problem (3^2 + 4^2 = 25) to maintain fluency with Unit 1 skills.
- Connect to Grade 7: students have read bar graphs, histograms, and line plots before. A scatter plot is a new type of graph, but the idea of representing data visually is not new.
