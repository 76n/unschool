# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 5, Lesson 8

## Topic
Two-Way Tables: Relative Frequencies

## Learning Objectives
- Define relative frequency and explain why it is useful for comparing groups of different sizes.
- Calculate joint relative frequencies (cell count / grand total).
- Calculate row relative frequencies (cell count / row total).
- Calculate column relative frequencies (cell count / column total).
- Interpret each type of relative frequency in the context of real data.

## Prerequisites
- Can construct and read a two-way frequency table (Unit 5, Lesson 7)
- Can compute percentages: (part / whole) × 100 (Grade 6-7)
- Can divide and round decimals (Grade 6-7)

## Materials Needed
- Pencil
- Calculator (recommended — students will compute many percentages)
- Index card or half-sheet for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 8.SP.4 — Use relative frequencies calculated for rows or columns to describe possible association between the two variables.

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Relative frequency** — a frequency expressed as a proportion or percentage of a total.
- **Joint relative frequency** — a cell count divided by the grand total; the proportion of all individuals who belong to both specific categories.
- **Marginal relative frequency** — a row or column total divided by the grand total; the proportion of all individuals in one category.
- **Row relative frequency (conditional)** — a cell count divided by the row total; the proportion of individuals in that row who fall in a specific column category.
- **Column relative frequency (conditional)** — a cell count divided by the column total; the proportion of individuals in that column who fall in a specific row category.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Why relative frequencies?**

Say: "Imagine two schools run the same survey about exercise habits. School A has 200 students, School B has 50 students. School A has 120 students who exercise regularly. School B has 40 students who exercise regularly. Which school has a higher exercise rate?"

Wait for answer. Then: "School A: 120/200 = 60%. School B: 40/50 = 80%. Even though School A has more students exercising in raw numbers, a higher percentage of School B students exercise. Relative frequencies let us compare groups of different sizes fairly."

**Step 2 — Three types of relative frequencies.**

Start with this frequency table and build relative frequency tables from it.

**Base table — Survey: 200 students, favorite activity (Sports vs. Gaming) and grade (Grade 7 vs. Grade 8)**

|                   | Sports | Gaming | Row Total |
|-------------------|--------|--------|-----------|
| **Grade 7**       | 60     | 40     | 100       |
| **Grade 8**       | 70     | 30     | 100       |
| **Column Total**  | 130    | 70     | 200       |

**Type 1: Joint relative frequencies (divide each cell by grand total 200)**

|                   | Sports     | Gaming     | Row Total |
|-------------------|------------|------------|-----------|
| **Grade 7**       | 60/200=30% | 40/200=20% | 50%       |
| **Grade 8**       | 70/200=35% | 30/200=15% | 50%       |
| **Column Total**  | 65%        | 35%        | 100%      |

Say: "These joint relative frequencies tell us what percentage of ALL 200 students fall into each combination. For example, 30% of all students are Grade 7 students who prefer sports."

**Type 2: Row relative frequencies (divide each cell by its row total)**

|                   | Sports      | Gaming      | Row Total |
|-------------------|-------------|-------------|-----------|
| **Grade 7**       | 60/100=60%  | 40/100=40%  | 100%      |
| **Grade 8**       | 70/100=70%  | 30/100=30%  | 100%      |

Say: "Row relative frequencies tell us: within each grade, what percentage prefers each activity? Among Grade 7 students, 60% prefer sports and 40% prefer gaming. Among Grade 8 students, 70% prefer sports and 30% prefer gaming. Each row sums to 100%."

**Type 3: Column relative frequencies (divide each cell by its column total)**

|                   | Sports       | Gaming       |
|-------------------|--------------|--------------|
| **Grade 7**       | 60/130=46.2% | 40/70=57.1%  |
| **Grade 8**       | 70/130=53.8% | 30/70=42.9%  |
| **Column Total**  | 100%         | 100%         |

Say: "Column relative frequencies tell us: among students who prefer each activity, what percentage are in each grade? Among sports-preferrers, 46.2% are Grade 7 and 53.8% are Grade 8. Each column sums to 100%."

**Step 3 — Which type to use?**

Say: "The type of relative frequency you choose depends on the question you are asking:
- 'What percentage of ALL students prefer sports?' → Joint relative frequency (use grand total).
- 'Among Grade 7 students, what percentage prefer sports?' → Row relative frequency (use row total).
- 'Among sports-preferrers, what percentage are in Grade 7?' → Column relative frequency (use column total)."

Summarize: "The denominator tells you which question you are answering."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

A fitness app collects data on 240 users. It records:
- Whether they hit their step goal: Met Goal / Did Not Meet Goal
- Time of day they are most active: Morning / Evening

**Frequency table:**

|                     | Met Goal | Did Not Meet Goal | Row Total |
|---------------------|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Morning active**  | 90       | 30                | 120       |
| **Evening active**  | 60       | 60                | 120       |
| **Column Total**    | 150      | 90                | 240       |

1. Compute the joint relative frequency for each cell (divide by grand total 240). Round to the nearest percent.

2. Complete the row relative frequency table (divide each cell by its row total). Round to the nearest percent.

3. Complete the column relative frequency table (divide each cell by its column total). Round to the nearest percent.

4. "What percentage of ALL users are morning-active people who met their goal?" Which type of relative frequency answers this? Compute it.
   *(Joint: 90/240 = 37.5%.)*

5. "Among morning-active users, what percentage met their step goal?" Which type answers this?
   *(Row: 90/120 = 75%.)*

6. "Among users who did not meet their goal, what percentage are evening-active?" Which type answers this?
   *(Column: 60/90 = 66.7%.)*

**Expected answers:**

Joint relative frequencies:
- Morning/Met: 90/240 ≈ 38%, Morning/Not: 30/240 = 13%, Evening/Met: 60/240 = 25%, Evening/Not: 60/240 = 25%.

Row relative frequencies:
- Morning: 90/120 = 75%, 30/120 = 25%.
- Evening: 60/120 = 50%, 60/120 = 50%.

Column relative frequencies:
- Met Goal: 90/150 = 60%, 60/150 = 40%.
- Did Not Meet: 30/90 = 33%, 60/90 = 67%.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

A school surveys 160 students about two things: whether they have taken a first aid course (Yes/No) and their grade level (Grade 8 / Grade 9).

**Frequency table:**

|                   | First Aid: Yes | First Aid: No | Row Total |
|-------------------|----------------|---------------|-----------|
| **Grade 8**       | 48             | 32            | 80        |
| **Grade 9**       | 36             | 44            | 80        |
| **Column Total**  | 84             | 76            | 160       |

1. Compute all joint relative frequencies. Round to the nearest percent.
2. Compute all row relative frequencies. Round to the nearest percent.
3. Compute all column relative frequencies. Round to the nearest percent.
4. What percentage of all students have taken first aid?
5. Among Grade 8 students, what percentage have taken first aid?
6. Among Grade 9 students, what percentage have taken first aid?
7. Among students who have taken first aid, what percentage are in Grade 8?

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

8. Compare your answers to #5 and #6. Do Grade 8 and Grade 9 students appear to have the same rate of first aid training? Which grade has a higher rate? What might explain this difference?

9. If both grades had the same number of students AND the same overall first aid training rate, what would the row relative frequencies look like? What if the grades had different sizes but the same training rate?

10. (Interleaved — Unit 5 correlation) Based on this two-way table data, can you conclude that being in Grade 8 causes students to take first aid courses more often? Why or why not?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

11. (Interleaved — Unit 4 Pythagorean Theorem) A ramp rises 1.2 meters vertically and extends 3.5 meters horizontally. What is the length of the ramp surface? Round to the nearest tenth.

12. A survey researcher wants to present the data in a way that makes Grade 9 students look like they have better first aid training than Grade 8. Which type of relative frequency would they use, and how would they need to frame the data? Is this misleading?

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** Using the data from Guided Practice (the step goal fitness table), a user group claims: "Morning-active people are better at meeting their step goal than evening-active people." Which type of relative frequency supports or refutes this claim? Compute the relevant frequencies and state whether the claim appears to be supported.

**Problem 2:** For the frequency table in Guided Practice, compute the marginal relative frequency for "Met Goal." Explain what this value means.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. Joint relative frequencies (divide by 160):
   - Grade 8/Yes: 48/160 = **30%**
   - Grade 8/No: 32/160 = **20%**
   - Grade 9/Yes: 36/160 = **22.5%** (≈ 23%)
   - Grade 9/No: 44/160 = **27.5%** (≈ 28%)
   - (Row totals: Grade 8 = 50%, Grade 9 = 50%. Column totals: Yes = 52.5%, No = 47.5%.)

2. Row relative frequencies:
   - Grade 8: 48/80 = **60%** Yes, 32/80 = **40%** No.
   - Grade 9: 36/80 = **45%** Yes, 44/80 = **55%** No.
   (Each row sums to 100%.)

3. Column relative frequencies:
   - First Aid Yes: 48/84 ≈ **57.1%** Grade 8, 36/84 ≈ **42.9%** Grade 9.
   - First Aid No: 32/76 ≈ **42.1%** Grade 8, 44/76 ≈ **57.9%** Grade 9.
   (Each column sums to 100%.)

4. 84/160 = **52.5%** of all students have taken first aid.

5. 48/80 = **60%** of Grade 8 students have taken first aid. (Row relative frequency.)

6. 36/80 = **45%** of Grade 9 students have taken first aid. (Row relative frequency.)

7. 48/84 ≈ **57.1%** of students who have taken first aid are in Grade 8. (Column relative frequency.)

8. **Grade 8 has a higher rate** (60% vs. 45%). Possible explanations: Grade 8 may have had first aid as a required activity, may be older students more likely to seek such training, or Grade 8 teachers may have encouraged it. Accept any reasonable explanation.

9. If both grades had the same overall first aid rate, the row relative frequencies would be identical for both rows (e.g., both rows would show 52.5% Yes and 47.5% No). The different group sizes would not affect row relative frequencies — relative frequencies normalize for group size. This is the point: row relative frequencies allow fair comparison even when group sizes differ.

10. No — this is correlation, not causation. Being in Grade 8 is associated with higher first aid training rates in this data, but we cannot conclude grade level causes the training. Possible explanations include: Grade 8 curriculum requirements, different teachers' habits, or Grade 8 students being at a life stage where they seek such certifications. The data is from an observational survey, not a controlled experiment.

11. Ramp length = hypotenuse. a = 1.2, b = 3.5. c^2 = 1.2^2 + 3.5^2 = 1.44 + 12.25 = 13.69. c = sqrt(13.69) ≈ **3.7 meters**.

12. The researcher could present **column relative frequencies** and frame the question as: "Among students who have NOT taken first aid, a higher percentage are Grade 9 students" (44/76 ≈ 57.9% vs. 32/76 ≈ 42.1%). This makes Grade 9 look more disproportionately untrained. This could be misleading because it is not the most natural comparison — normally we ask "what percentage of each grade has training" (row relative frequencies), not "among untrained students, which grade dominates." Changing the framing to make one group look better or worse is a form of misleading statistical communication.

### Exit Ticket

1. **Row relative frequencies** support or refute this claim, because we want to know: within each activity-time group, what percentage met the goal?
   - Morning: 90/120 = **75%** met their goal.
   - Evening: 60/120 = **50%** met their goal.
   The claim **is supported**: a higher percentage of morning-active users (75%) met their step goal compared to evening-active users (50%).

2. Marginal relative frequency for "Met Goal" = 150/240 = **62.5%**. This means that 62.5% of all users in the survey met their step goal, regardless of whether they were morning-active or evening-active.

## Teacher Notes

- The three types of relative frequencies are the conceptual heart of two-way table analysis. Many students blend them together. Reinforce the denominator rule at every opportunity: "Which total are you dividing by? That tells you which question you are answering."
- The most common error: students compute row totals or column totals as percentages of the grand total (marginal relative frequencies) when they are asked for conditional relative frequencies. Practice asking the question before computing: "Am I asking about all students? Then use the grand total. Am I asking about just Grade 8 students? Then use the Grade 8 row total."
- Calculator use is appropriate throughout this lesson for the division and percentage calculations. The cognitive load should be on the interpretation, not the arithmetic.
- The Tier 2 extension in #9 (what would row relative frequencies look like if both grades had the same rate?) previews the concept of statistical independence, which will be formalized in Lesson 9.
- Interleaved review: Problem 11 brings back the Pythagorean Theorem from Unit 4 in a practical context (ramp design).
