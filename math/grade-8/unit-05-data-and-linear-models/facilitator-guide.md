# Facilitator Guide — Grade 8 Math, Unit 5: Data and Linear Models

**Grade:** 8
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 5 — Data and Linear Models
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided independence (facilitator leads instruction; student works independently for practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit develops the student's ability to analyze bivariate data — data that tracks two variables at once — and to use statistical tools to describe relationships, make predictions, and reason carefully about what data can and cannot tell us. The unit opens with three lessons on scatter plots: how to construct them from a data table, how to describe the patterns they reveal (positive/negative/no association, linear vs. nonlinear, clusters, outliers), and how to draw an informal line of best fit. Lessons 4 and 5 deepen the line-of-best-fit work, moving from visual drawing to prediction and then to writing an equation in slope-intercept form with full attention to interpreting slope and y-intercept in the context of real data. Lesson 6 takes a critical-thinking turn: students examine the crucial distinction between correlation and causation, studying compelling real-world examples of spurious associations and lurking variables. The final four lessons shift to categorical data: Lessons 7, 8, and 9 build skill with two-way frequency tables — constructing them, computing relative frequencies, and using comparisons between rows and columns to identify associations in categorical data. Lesson 10 brings the entire unit together through integrated data analysis practice.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Construct a scatter plot from a data table and accurately plot ordered pairs
- Describe a scatter plot's pattern: direction (positive/negative/none), form (linear/nonlinear), and strength (clusters, outliers)
- Draw an informal line of best fit that approximately balances points above and below the line
- Use a line of best fit to make predictions by reading the graph (interpolation and extrapolation) and recognize the limitations of predictions outside the data range
- Write the equation of a line of best fit in slope-intercept form (y = mx + b) and interpret slope and y-intercept in context
- Explain the difference between correlation and causation with specific examples; identify lurking variables
- Construct a two-way frequency table from a description of categorical data
- Read and interpret a two-way table: row totals, column totals, joint frequencies
- Compute joint, marginal, and conditional relative frequencies from a two-way table
- Identify and describe associations in categorical data by comparing relative frequencies across rows or columns

**Why this matters:** Data literacy is one of the most practical mathematical skills a student can develop. Every day, people encounter graphs, statistics, and claims about relationships between variables — in news articles, health research, social media, and advertising. The tools in this unit help students move from passive consumers of data to critical analysts who can ask: What does this scatter plot actually show? Does this correlation prove that one thing causes another? What patterns appear in this table, and what might explain them? Students who master this unit are better equipped to think clearly about evidence in any domain they encounter throughout life.

**Standards:** 8.SP.1, 8.SP.2, 8.SP.3, 8.SP.4

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, confirm:

- [ ] Can plot and read ordered pairs in all four quadrants of the coordinate plane (Grade 6-7; reinforced in Unit 4)
- [ ] Can compute the slope of a line given two points: m = (y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1) (Grade 8, Unit 3)
- [ ] Can write a linear equation in slope-intercept form y = mx + b (Grade 8, Unit 2-3)
- [ ] Can substitute values into an equation and solve (Grade 8, Unit 2)
- [ ] Understands the concepts of mean, median, mode, and range (Grade 7 statistics)
- [ ] Has experience reading and interpreting data displays: bar graphs, line graphs, histograms (Grade 6-7)
- [ ] Can compute percentages: part/whole x 100 (Grade 6-7)
- [ ] Can interpret the meaning of ratios and proportions (Grade 7)

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Graph paper** (for scatter plots in every lesson — at least 2-3 sheets per lesson)
- **Ruler or straightedge** (for drawing lines of best fit in Lessons 3-5)
- **Pencil** (recommended over pen so students can adjust lines of best fit)
- **Whiteboard and markers** (or projector for instructor demonstrations)
- **Calculator** (recommended for Lesson 5 slope calculations and Lessons 7-9 percentage computations)
- **Index cards or half-sheets** (for Exit Tickets)
- **Colored pencils** (optional, for distinguishing data sets or highlighting trends on scatter plots)
- **Student worksheets** (Guided Practice and Independent Practice for each lesson)

**Note on scatter plot construction:** This unit uses text-based data tables rather than pre-printed scatter plots. Students construct plots on their own graph paper from coordinate pairs provided in the lesson. This is a deliberate instructional choice — the act of plotting reinforces the connection between a data table and its visual representation.

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 1 (scatter plot basics) is accessible if the student can plot points. The primary skill being learned is how to read a data table and translate it into a scatter plot. One session should suffice.
- Lessons 2-3 (describing patterns, line of best fit) form a natural pair. Students often find drawing the line more intuitive than they expect — what takes time is the descriptive vocabulary (positive/negative association, clustering, outliers). Allow two sessions for Lesson 3 if the student struggles with the informal fitting concept.
- Lesson 4 (predictions) is typically quick if Lesson 3 is solid — it is essentially applying what was already learned.
- Lesson 5 (writing equations) is the algebraic heart of the unit. Students must connect their Unit 3 linear equations work to real-world data. Allow two sessions if slope calculation or y-intercept interpretation is shaky.
- Lesson 6 (correlation vs. causation) is conceptual and discussion-heavy. One session is usually enough; the examples are engaging for teenagers. Encourage genuine discussion — this lesson is as much critical-thinking practice as mathematics.
- Lessons 7-9 (two-way tables) form a tight sequence. Do not skip the construction step in Lesson 7 even if the student seems impatient — the ability to build a table from scratch cements understanding of what the cells mean. Lesson 8 (relative frequencies) is the most computationally intensive; allow two sessions if percentage calculations are slow.
- Lesson 10 (review) can be shortened to one session for strong students or extended to two for students who need consolidation.

## Lesson Summaries

| Lesson | Summary |
|--------|---------|
| 1 | Introduces scatter plots as a tool for displaying bivariate data. Students learn to read a two-variable data table, identify ordered pairs, and plot them on a coordinate plane. They practice reading a completed scatter plot to answer questions about individual data points. |
| 2 | Develops the vocabulary for describing scatter plot patterns: positive association, negative association, no association, linear, nonlinear, clusters, and outliers. Students practice categorizing scatter plots and explaining patterns in context. |
| 3 | Introduces the informal line of best fit as a straight line that models a linear trend in bivariate data. Students learn to draw lines that balance points above and below, identify the line's approximate y-intercept and points to calculate slope, and explain what the line represents. |
| 4 | Applies the line of best fit to make predictions. Students practice interpolation (reading values within the data range) and extrapolation (predicting outside the data range), and critically evaluate which predictions are more reliable. |
| 5 | Connects the line of best fit to slope-intercept form (y = mx + b). Students calculate slope from two points on the line, identify the y-intercept, write the equation, and interpret slope and y-intercept in the context of the real-world data. |
| 6 | Examines the difference between correlation and causation. Through multiple compelling real-world examples — ice cream sales and drowning rates, shoe size and reading level, Nicolas Cage films and pool drownings — students learn to identify spurious correlations, propose lurking variables, and reason carefully about what data can and cannot prove. |
| 7 | Introduces two-way frequency tables for organizing categorical data. Students learn to read the table structure (rows, columns, joint frequencies, marginal totals), construct a two-way table from a verbal description, and interpret what each cell means. |
| 8 | Develops relative frequency calculations for two-way tables. Students compute joint relative frequencies (cell/grand total), row relative frequencies (cell/row total), and column relative frequencies (cell/column total), and interpret each type of percentage in context. |
| 9 | Applies two-way table analysis to identify associations in categorical data. Students compare conditional relative frequencies across rows or columns, describe whether an association appears to exist, and explain the association in context. |
| 10 | Comprehensive review integrating all unit skills: scatter plot construction and description, line of best fit, equation writing, prediction, correlation vs. causation reasoning, and two-way table analysis. Students work through a multi-context data problem set. |

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Bivariate data** | Data that tracks two variables for each individual in a data set. |
| **Scatter plot** | A graph that displays bivariate data as a collection of points, with one variable on the x-axis and one on the y-axis. |
| **Positive association** | A relationship in a scatter plot where, as the x-variable increases, the y-variable tends to increase. |
| **Negative association** | A relationship in a scatter plot where, as the x-variable increases, the y-variable tends to decrease. |
| **No association** | A scatter plot pattern in which there is no apparent relationship between the two variables. |
| **Linear association** | A scatter plot pattern where the points cluster roughly around a straight line. |
| **Nonlinear association** | A scatter plot pattern where the points follow a curve rather than a straight line. |
| **Cluster** | A group of data points that are closely grouped together in one region of a scatter plot. |
| **Outlier** | A data point that falls far from the general pattern of the other points. |
| **Line of best fit** | A straight line drawn through a scatter plot that best approximates the linear trend in the data; also called a trend line. |
| **Interpolation** | Using a line of best fit to predict a value for a point within the range of the data. |
| **Extrapolation** | Using a line of best fit to predict a value for a point outside the range of the data. |
| **Slope (in context)** | The rate of change of the y-variable per one-unit increase in the x-variable; interpreted with units (e.g., "For each additional hour of study, the score increases by about 4 points"). |
| **y-intercept (in context)** | The predicted value of y when x = 0; may or may not have a meaningful real-world interpretation depending on the context. |
| **Correlation** | A statistical relationship or association between two variables. |
| **Causation** | A relationship in which one variable directly causes a change in another variable. |
| **Lurking variable** | A hidden third variable that is related to both of the variables being studied, creating the appearance of a direct relationship. |
| **Two-way frequency table** | A table that displays the frequencies of two categorical variables simultaneously, with one variable as rows and the other as columns. |
| **Joint frequency** | The count in a cell of a two-way table, representing individuals in both category A (a specific row) and category B (a specific column). |
| **Marginal frequency** | The row or column total in a two-way table; the total count for one category regardless of the other. |
| **Relative frequency** | A frequency expressed as a fraction or percentage of a total. |
| **Conditional relative frequency** | A relative frequency calculated using a row total or column total as the denominator (rather than the grand total); shows the frequency of one variable given a specific value of the other. |
| **Association (categorical)** | A pattern in a two-way table where the distribution of one categorical variable differs depending on the value of the other categorical variable. |

## Differentiation Strategies

### If the Student Needs More Support

- **Pre-plotting scaffold for scatter plots:** In Lessons 1-3, provide a coordinate grid that is already labeled with the appropriate axis titles and scale. Students who struggle with setting up axes from scratch should begin on a pre-labeled grid, then transition to blank graph paper once plotting is comfortable.
- **Ordered pair checklist:** Some students plot (y, x) instead of (x, y). Before Lessons 1-2, practice reading: "The first number is always the horizontal (x) value; the second number is always the vertical (y) value." Use color coding: x-value in blue, y-value in red, matching the axes.
- **Guided line-fitting procedure:** In Lesson 3, give explicit steps for drawing a line of best fit: (1) Look at the overall direction of the data. (2) Draw a line that goes through the middle of the data cloud, not just connecting the first and last points. (3) Count points above and below the line — they should be roughly equal. (4) Adjust if needed. Post this procedure at the student's workspace.
- **Equation-writing template:** In Lesson 5, provide a template: "Step 1: Identify two points on my line: (__, __) and (__, __). Step 2: Calculate slope: m = (y2 - y1)/(x2 - x1) = ___. Step 3: Identify y-intercept b = ___. Step 4: Write equation: y = ___x + ___. Step 5: Interpret slope: For each one-unit increase in ___, the ___ increases/decreases by ___."
- **Two-way table vocabulary support:** In Lessons 7-9, use color coding: shade all row total cells in one color, all column total cells in another color, and the grand total in a third color. This visual scaffold helps the student distinguish joint, marginal, and relative frequencies.
- **Percentage scaffolding:** If percentage calculations slow the student down in Lessons 8-9, allow a calculator throughout and focus instruction on the interpretation (what does this percentage mean?) rather than the arithmetic.

### If the Student Needs More Challenge

- **Least-squares method:** In Lesson 3 or 5, introduce the concept of minimizing squared residuals. Ask: "If we measured the vertical distance from each point to the line, squared each distance, and added them up, how would we know which line is 'best'?" This previews the formal definition of the line of best fit used in high school statistics.
- **Residual analysis:** After Lesson 4, introduce residuals (actual y-value minus predicted y-value). Ask the student to compute residuals for several points and plot them on a residual plot. Discuss: what does it mean if residuals show a pattern vs. appearing random?
- **Designing studies:** After Lesson 6, challenge the student to design a real investigation that could establish causation vs. one that could only establish correlation. What is the difference between an observational study and a controlled experiment?
- **Chi-square reasoning (informal):** After Lesson 9, ask: "If there were no association between the two variables, what would the conditional relative frequencies look like?" Help the student reason about what "no association" means in a two-way table.
- **Finding equations using specific points:** In Lesson 5, after finding an informal best-fit line, ask the student to find the exact equation by choosing two specific data points (not necessarily on the line) and computing the least-squares line using the formula, comparing it to their informal line.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student plots (y, x) instead of (x, y) | Reverses the order of coordinates | Reinforce the convention: the first coordinate in an ordered pair always corresponds to the horizontal (x) axis. Practice reading coordinates aloud: "x goes across, y goes up." |
| Student draws line of best fit through the first and last data points | Treats line of best fit as a connect-the-dots exercise | Emphasize: the line of best fit is not connecting specific data points. It is a straight-line model of the overall trend. The goal is roughly equal numbers of points above and below the line. |
| Student draws the line through the origin always | Assumes y = mx (no y-intercept) as default | Remind the student to read the actual y-intercept from the graph. Not all linear relationships pass through the origin. Show a counterexample: a line of best fit for height vs. arm span in centimeters will have a y-intercept far from zero. |
| Student says "the scatter plot proves X causes Y" | Conflates correlation with causation | Lesson 6 addresses this directly. Use the lurking variable framework: "What other variable could be related to both X and Y and explain this pattern?" Practice identifying lurking variables for several examples. |
| Student confuses joint and marginal frequencies | Reads the wrong cell or uses the wrong total | Use the color-coding scaffold: joint frequencies are inside the table (the actual cell counts); marginal frequencies are on the edges (row and column totals). Practice pointing to each type of frequency before calculating. |
| Student uses the grand total for all relative frequency calculations | Computes joint relative frequencies even when asked for conditional | Explicitly distinguish the three types of denominators: (1) grand total -> joint relative frequency; (2) row total -> row conditional frequency; (3) column total -> column conditional frequency. Ask: "What is the denominator?" before each computation. |
| Student reports an association in a two-way table without comparing relative frequencies | Uses raw counts instead of relative frequencies to conclude association | Explain: raw counts are affected by sample size. If Group A has 100 people and Group B has 20, Group A will almost always have higher raw counts in every cell. Relative frequencies (percentages) allow fair comparison. Always convert to percentages to assess association. |
| Student extrapolates far outside the data range without flagging the uncertainty | Does not recognize that predictions far from the data are unreliable | Revisit the extrapolation discussion from Lesson 4. Use a compelling example: if a scatter plot shows a positive relationship between a child's age (5-12 years) and height, extrapolating to age 100 would predict an impossible height. The model is only valid near the data. |
| Student thinks a stronger correlation means causation is more likely | Equates strength of association with direction of causation | Lesson 6 addresses this. A very strong correlation can still be entirely due to a lurking variable. The Nicolas Cage example (strong correlation, obvious absurdity) is useful here. |

## Assessment Guidance

### Formative Assessment (During the Unit)
- Each lesson includes an Exit Ticket. Use these to gauge daily understanding before the next lesson.
- Pay special attention to three key transition points in this unit:
  - **Lesson 2 to 3:** Does the student describe patterns fluently? Students who cannot describe a scatter plot verbally will struggle to know what their line of best fit should look like.
  - **Lesson 4 to 5:** Does the student understand what the line represents before writing its equation? Students who treat Lesson 5 as purely algebraic often produce equations that contradict their own scatter plot.
  - **Lesson 7 to 8:** Does the student understand what each cell in the table represents before computing relative frequencies? Computational fluency in Lesson 8 depends on conceptual understanding from Lesson 7.
- During Guided Practice, watch whether the student can interpret answers in context, not just compute them. The goal is always meaning, not just procedure.

### Summative Assessment (End of Unit)
The unit assessment contains 5 sections totaling 60 points:
- Section 1: Scatter Plots — constructing, reading, describing (14 points)
- Section 2: Line of Best Fit — drawing, predicting, equation writing (16 points)
- Section 3: Correlation vs. Causation (10 points)
- Section 4: Two-Way Tables (14 points)
- Section 5: Data Reasoning — integrated interpretation (6 points)

**Mastery threshold:** 42 of 60 points (70%) AND at least 50% in each section.

### What to Do If the Student Does Not Meet Mastery
- Identify the weakest section(s) and reteach using the section-specific guidance in the unit assessment file.
- If the student struggles with Sections 1-2, determine whether the issue is with scatter plot construction (go back to Lesson 1) or with the line of best fit (Lesson 3).
- If the student struggles with Section 3, spend additional time on Lesson 6, working through more examples of spurious correlations and practicing the lurking-variable identification process.
- If the student struggles with Sections 4-5, determine whether the issue is table construction, relative frequency computation, or interpretation. These are distinct skills and may need separate reteaching.
- Students who score below 50% across three or more sections should be checked for prerequisite gaps: coordinate plotting, slope calculation from Unit 3, and percentage computation.

## Conversation Starters

- "If you track two different things about the same group of people — like how many hours they sleep and how many days they get sick — what do you think the scatter plot would look like? Why?"
- "Imagine you draw the 'best' line through a scatter plot. What does 'best' mean? How would you decide?"
- "In weather forecasting, meteorologists use data from past years to predict future temperatures. Do you think their predictions are more reliable for tomorrow or for the same date 20 years from now? Why?"
- "If I told you that people who eat more ice cream are more likely to drown, would you conclude that eating ice cream causes drowning? What else might explain that pattern?"
- "Survey data shows that teenagers who watch more YouTube also have higher grades. Does that mean YouTube helps students learn? What other explanations might there be?"
- "When you look at a survey, why do you think researchers report percentages instead of raw counts? What problem does reporting raw counts create?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

Consider seeking additional support if:

- The student cannot reliably plot ordered pairs on a coordinate plane — every lesson in this unit depends on this skill
- The student cannot calculate slope from two points (a Grade 8 Unit 3 skill) — this is essential for Lesson 5
- The student cannot compute percentages (part/whole x 100) — the relative frequency lessons require this
- The student's conceptual reasoning about data is very weak (struggles to describe what a graph means in words) — the entire unit emphasizes interpretation over calculation; if language and reasoning are severely limited, additional scaffolding is needed
- After reteaching, the student scores below 50% on the unit assessment in more than two sections

## Connection to Future Mathematics

This unit provides foundation for high school statistics and data analysis:

- **High school Statistics:** The informal line of best fit introduced here becomes the formal least-squares regression line in a statistics course. Students who understand the concept intuitively (balancing distances above and below the line, interpreting slope and intercept in context) will find the formal mathematical version much more accessible.
- **Two-way tables and chi-square:** The association analysis in Lessons 7-9 is the conceptual foundation for the chi-square test of independence, which is a standard topic in AP Statistics.
- **Correlation coefficient:** Students who understand positive vs. negative association and its strength are prepared to learn the formal Pearson correlation coefficient r in a future statistics course.
- **Algebra II and functions:** The idea of using a linear model to make predictions is an early version of regression, and the extension to nonlinear models (exponential, quadratic) studied in Algebra II builds directly on this unit.
- **Critical thinking across all subjects:** The correlation vs. causation distinction (Lesson 6) is arguably the most broadly applicable mathematical idea in the entire K-8 curriculum. Students who internalize this lesson will think more clearly about evidence in science, social studies, journalism, and everyday life.
