# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 5, Lesson 6

## Topic
Correlation vs. Causation

## Learning Objectives
- Explain the difference between correlation (association) and causation.
- Identify that a scatter plot or statistical association does not prove causation.
- Define "lurking variable" and identify lurking variables in given scenarios.
- Evaluate claims about data with appropriate skepticism and identify flawed reasoning.

## Prerequisites
- Understands positive, negative, and no association in scatter plots (Unit 5, Lesson 2)
- Can read scatter plot patterns and describe them in context (Lessons 1-2)
- Prior exposure to the idea that data can be misused or misinterpreted (Grade 7 statistics)

## Materials Needed
- No graph paper needed for this lesson
- Pencil and lined paper (for written responses)
- Index card or half-sheet for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 8.SP.1 — Investigate patterns of association between two quantities; reason about whether association implies causation.

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Correlation** — a statistical association between two variables: when one changes, the other tends to change in a consistent direction. Also called association.
- **Causation** — a direct cause-and-effect relationship: changing one variable directly causes a change in the other.
- **Lurking variable** — a third variable that is related to both variables being studied; its presence can create the appearance of a direct relationship between those two variables even when no such relationship exists.
- **Spurious correlation** — a correlation that appears statistically real but has no causal or meaningful connection; often the result of a lurking variable or coincidence.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — The core idea.**

Say: "So far, we have been describing associations in scatter plots. A positive association means as x goes up, y tends to go up. A negative association means as x goes up, y tends to go down. But here is the critical question: does the association mean one thing is causing the other?"

Write on the board: **"Correlation does not imply causation."**

Say: "This is one of the most important ideas in statistics, science, and everyday reasoning. Just because two things are associated does not mean one is causing the other. There are several other explanations for why two variables might be correlated."

**Step 2 — Example 1: Ice cream and drowning.**

Say: "Imagine a researcher collects data over 12 months. She finds a strong positive association: in months with high ice cream sales, there are also more drowning deaths. She plots the data and draws a line of best fit. The correlation is strong and statistically real."

Ask: "Does eating ice cream cause people to drown? What is going on here?"

Let the student think, then explain: "The lurking variable is temperature (or summer). In hot summer months, both ice cream sales increase AND more people swim — leading to more drowning accidents. Ice cream and drowning are both caused by summer heat. They move together because of the season, not because of any real connection between ice cream and drowning."

Draw this diagram:
```
Hot summer weather
     ↓           ↓
Ice cream sales  Drowning rate
        (positive correlation, but no causation)
```

**Step 3 — Example 2: Shoe size and reading ability.**

Say: "A study of children ages 5 to 15 finds a strong positive association between shoe size and reading ability. Kids with bigger feet tend to be better readers. Does having big feet help you read?"

Answer: "No. The lurking variable is age. Older children have both bigger feet (they've grown) and better reading skills (they've been in school longer). Shoe size and reading ability both increase with age, but neither one causes the other."

```
Age
 ↓        ↓
Shoe size  Reading ability
     (positive correlation, but no causation)
```

**Step 4 — Example 3: Nicolas Cage films and pool drownings.**

Say: "This one is real. Someone found that the number of Nicholas Cage films released per year and the number of people who drowned in swimming pools are strongly correlated from 1999 to 2009. The data lines up almost perfectly."

Pause for reaction. Then: "This is called a spurious correlation. It is a coincidence with no possible causal mechanism. Both variables happen to have fluctuated in a similar pattern over those years. There is no lurking variable — it is just a statistical fluke. In large data sets with many variables, you will always find some coincidental correlations just by chance."

**Step 5 — Example 4: Screen time and grades (ambiguous causation).**

Say: "A study finds students who spend more time on screens get lower grades. Someone concludes: 'Screen time hurts academic performance.' What problems do you see?"

Point out multiple possibilities:
1. **Screen time causes lower grades:** Maybe excessive entertainment use reduces study time. (Possible.)
2. **Lower grades cause more screen time:** Students struggling academically may turn to entertainment as an escape. (Reverse causation.)
3. **Lurking variable:** Stress, family instability, or learning difficulties might cause both lower grades and higher screen time.
4. **Both:** Multiple mechanisms may operate simultaneously.

Say: "Association alone cannot tell us the direction or mechanism of causation. That requires carefully designed experiments."

**Step 6 — How to establish causation.**

Say: "Scientists establish causation through randomized controlled experiments: randomly assign people to groups (treatment vs. control), expose one group to the variable of interest, and measure the outcome. This controls for lurking variables. Most data in everyday life comes from observational studies — we just observe what people do. Observational studies can establish correlation but not causation."

**Step 7 — The three explanations for a correlation.**

Write:
1. **A causes B.** (Possible — needs experimental evidence.)
2. **B causes A.** (Reverse causation — possible.)
3. **C causes both A and B.** (Lurking variable — often the most common explanation for correlations in the real world.)
4. **Coincidence.** (Pure statistical chance, especially with many variables.)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

For each scenario, (a) identify whether the claim of causation is justified or not, and (b) propose a possible lurking variable or alternative explanation if one exists.

**Scenario 1:** A data set shows a positive correlation between the number of firefighters sent to a fire and the amount of fire damage. A city manager concludes: "Firefighters cause more damage — we should send fewer of them."

*(Not justified. Lurking variable: size of the fire. Bigger fires cause more damage AND require more firefighters. Fewer firefighters would likely cause even more damage.)*

**Scenario 2:** A study finds that people who drink more coffee are less likely to develop certain diseases. A nutritionist says: "Drinking coffee prevents disease."

*(Not necessarily justified. This is an observational study. Possible lurking variables: coffee drinkers may have other healthy habits (exercise, diet), or socioeconomic factors that provide better healthcare access. Reverse causation is less likely here. Further studies (randomized trials) would be needed to confirm causation.)*

**Scenario 3:** A scatter plot shows that cities with more libraries also have lower crime rates. A mayor says: "We should build more libraries to reduce crime."

*(Not necessarily justified. Possible lurking variable: wealth or investment level of the city. Wealthier cities tend to have more public resources (including libraries) and also tend to have different crime patterns. Building libraries alone might not reduce crime without addressing underlying factors.)*

**Scenario 4:** A positive correlation is found between the number of storks in a European region and the regional birth rate. Someone concludes: "Storks bring babies."

*(Not justified — this is a spurious correlation. The lurking variable may be rural vs. urban areas: rural regions tend to have more storks (farmland, open land) and also higher birth rates (different population characteristics). The stork-birth correlation is famous as a joke correlation.)*

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

For each claim, state whether you agree or disagree that causation has been established, and explain why. Identify any lurking variable.

1. "Data shows that countries with more televisions per household have higher life expectancy. Television must help people live longer."

2. "Students who own more books tend to score higher on reading tests. Therefore, owning more books causes better reading."

3. "Athletes who warm up before games have fewer injuries. Therefore, warming up prevents injuries."

4. "A scatter plot of ice sales and sunscreen sales shows a strong positive correlation. Buying ice causes people to buy sunscreen."

5. "Research shows a negative correlation between the number of doctors in a region and the death rate. Therefore, having more doctors causes lower death rates."

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

6. Explain in your own words: what is the difference between an observational study and a randomized controlled experiment? Why does this distinction matter for claims about causation?

7. A tech company claims: "Users who engage with our productivity app for more than 30 minutes per day earn 15% more than users who do not use the app." Design a question a skeptical reporter should ask about this claim.

8. A student says: "Correlation doesn't imply causation, so correlations are useless." Do you agree or disagree? What can correlations still tell us, even if they don't prove causation?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

9. (Interleaved — Unit 4 geometry/similarity) Two advertising posters are similar rectangles. The small poster is 8 inches wide and 11 inches tall. The large poster is 24 inches wide. What is the height of the large poster?

10. The correlation between two variables A and B is strong and positive. You identify a lurking variable C that is strongly positively correlated with both A and B. Draw a causal diagram showing this relationship (using arrows to show C → A and C → B) and explain why this means we cannot conclude A causes B from the A-B correlation alone.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A researcher finds that schools with more vending machines have higher average student test scores. A news article concludes: "Vending machines improve academic performance." Explain why this conclusion is not justified. Propose one possible lurking variable.

**Problem 2:** State the three main alternative explanations for why two variables X and Y might show a strong correlation. (You do not need to use a specific example — just state the three general explanations.)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. **Disagree — causation not established.** Television ownership is strongly correlated with wealth. Wealthier countries have more TVs and also better healthcare, food security, and working conditions that lead to higher life expectancy. The lurking variable is economic development level. Televisions do not directly extend lives.

2. **Disagree — causation not clearly established** (though it may contribute). Possible lurking variable: family income and education level. Families with higher income and education tend to buy more books AND provide better educational environments. Additionally, children who enjoy reading may seek out both more books and more reading practice — potential reverse causation. However, randomized studies have shown that access to books does have a direct positive effect on reading, so there may be a real causal pathway here too. Strong answers will note that it is complex.

3. **This is more plausible as causation.** Athletes who warm up are engaging in deliberate practice that physically prepares muscles and joints, which has a biological mechanism for reducing injury. However, possible lurking variable: more disciplined or experienced athletes may warm up consistently AND use better technique, making them less injury-prone for multiple reasons. Correlation is consistent with causation, but a controlled study would be more definitive.

4. **Not causation — spurious or common-cause correlation.** The lurking variable is summer/hot weather. In summer, people buy both more ice and more sunscreen. Ice purchases do not cause sunscreen purchases.

5. **This one is tricky.** It may appear to be reverse causation (healthier regions attract more doctors) or a lurking variable (wealthier regions have more doctors AND more healthcare resources). However, there is also a plausible direct causal mechanism: more doctors → more medical care → lower death rates. Many well-designed studies support this interpretation, though the correlation alone does not prove it. Excellent answers will note that causation is plausible here but requires further study.

6. **Observational study:** The researcher observes and records data without intervening or controlling any variables. Individuals choose their own behaviors. **Randomized controlled experiment:** The researcher randomly assigns participants to conditions (treatment vs. control), controls all other factors, and measures outcomes. Random assignment ensures that lurking variables are balanced across groups. **Why it matters:** In observational studies, lurking variables can explain correlations. In randomized experiments, random assignment eliminates the effect of most lurking variables, allowing causal conclusions.

7. Skeptical questions include: Are app users and non-users different to begin with? (Higher earners may be more likely to use productivity apps.) Is this an observational study or experiment? What other differences exist between the two groups (education, job type, experience)? Did the company define "engagement" before collecting data? What time frame was used?

8. **Disagree with "correlations are useless."** Even without proving causation, correlations are highly valuable: (1) They identify patterns worth investigating. (2) They allow prediction, even without causal understanding (e.g., predicting weather, stock prices, medical risk). (3) They suggest hypotheses for controlled studies. (4) In many cases, the correlation is the first evidence that a causal relationship might exist, motivating deeper research.

9. Scale factor = 24/8 = 3. Height of large poster = 11 × 3 = **33 inches**.

10. The causal diagram shows: C → A and C → B. This means C causes both A and B to increase/decrease together. When we observe a correlation between A and B, we cannot distinguish between: (1) A directly causes B, (2) B directly causes A, or (3) C causes both A and B. Without a controlled experiment that manipulates A independently of C, we cannot isolate the A → B pathway. The correlation is real — C genuinely causes them to move together — but the A-B correlation has no direct causal meaning.

### Exit Ticket

1. **Causation not established.** This is an observational association, not a controlled experiment. A plausible lurking variable is **school funding** (or socioeconomic level of the school community): wealthier schools tend to have both more money for vending machines and more resources for strong academic programs (better teachers, more materials, better facilities), leading to higher test scores. The vending machines are not causing the higher scores — both are caused by greater school investment.

2. Three general explanations for a correlation between X and Y:
   1. **X causes Y** (direct causation).
   2. **Y causes X** (reverse causation).
   3. **A third variable Z causes both X and Y** (lurking variable / common cause).
   *(Accept also: coincidence/spurious correlation as a fourth explanation — award full credit for any three of the four.)*

## Teacher Notes

- This is a discussion-heavy lesson. Budget time for genuine back-and-forth on the examples. Teenagers are often genuinely surprised by the ice cream/drowning example and the Nicolas Cage example — these provoke curiosity and engagement.
- The firefighter example (Guided Practice #1) is particularly effective because the "naive" causal claim seems almost reasonable at first glance. The lurking variable (fire size) is not immediately obvious. Let the student think before offering the explanation.
- Many students come to this lesson thinking that "correlation doesn't equal causation" means correlations are not useful at all. Challenge this — correlations are extremely useful for prediction, identification of patterns, and generating hypotheses. The lesson is about being careful about causal claims, not about dismissing data.
- The warming-up before exercise example (Independent Practice #3) is deliberately ambiguous — there are plausible causal mechanisms but also lurking variables. This is realistic: in science and medicine, many important relationships are supported by both correlational evidence and plausible mechanisms. Praise nuanced answers that acknowledge both.
- Interleaved review: Tier 3 #9 brings back similarity from Unit 4.
