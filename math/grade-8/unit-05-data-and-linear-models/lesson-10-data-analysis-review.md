# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 5, Lesson 10

## Topic
Data Analysis Review

## Learning Objectives
- Construct and interpret a scatter plot for bivariate numerical data.
- Draw a line of best fit and write its equation in slope-intercept form.
- Make predictions using the equation and evaluate interpolation vs. extrapolation.
- Reason carefully about correlation vs. causation.
- Construct and interpret a two-way frequency table including relative frequencies.
- Identify associations in both scatter plots and two-way tables.
- Apply all unit skills in integrated, multi-step data analysis problems.

## Prerequisites
- All Lessons 1-9 of Unit 5

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper (3-4 sheets)
- Ruler
- Pencil
- Calculator
- Index card or half-sheet for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 8.SP.1, 8.SP.2, 8.SP.3, 8.SP.4 (comprehensive review)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick-fire review of all unit vocabulary.

Match each term to its description (facilitator reads aloud or writes on board):

1. Positive association → *(As x increases, y tends to increase)*
2. Line of best fit → *(Straight line drawn through a scatter plot to model a linear trend)*
3. Extrapolation → *(Predicting beyond the data range)*
4. Lurking variable → *(A third variable related to both variables being studied)*
5. Joint relative frequency → *(Cell count divided by the grand total)*
6. Conditional relative frequency → *(Cell count divided by a row or column total)*
7. No association (in a two-way table) → *(Row relative frequencies are the same in every row)*

## Direct Instruction (5-10 minutes)

### Review Connections Across the Unit

Say: "Today is a review and integration lesson. Instead of learning new content, we will practice connecting everything from Unit 5 into cohesive data analysis."

Draw this unit map on the board:

```
NUMERICAL DATA (Bivariate)          CATEGORICAL DATA (Bivariate)
         ↓                                        ↓
   Scatter Plot                          Two-Way Table
         ↓                                        ↓
  Describe Pattern                    Compute Frequencies
  (direction, form,                   (joint, row, column)
   clusters, outliers)                             ↓
         ↓                              Identify Association
 Line of Best Fit                    (compare row rel. freq.)
         ↓
  Write Equation (y=mx+b)
         ↓
  Make Predictions
  (interpolation/extrapolation)
         ↓
  Correlation ≠ Causation
```

Say: "Both halves — scatter plots and two-way tables — are asking the same fundamental question: is there a pattern between two variables? The tools differ, but the reasoning is the same."

Common errors to review before practice:
1. Connecting dots in a scatter plot (don't — each point is independent).
2. Drawing line of best fit through specific data points rather than through the middle of the data cloud.
3. Using data table coordinates for slope instead of coordinates ON the line of best fit.
4. Confusing joint relative frequency (÷ grand total) with conditional relative frequency (÷ row or column total).
5. Concluding causation from correlation.

## Integrated Practice (25-30 minutes)

This lesson uses a single extended scenario to practice all five major skills of the unit. Work through each section in sequence.

---

### Context: The School Health and Technology Study

A researcher studies 200 middle school students (Grade 7 and Grade 8) across two schools. The researcher measures:
- Daily phone screen time (hours)
- Sleep duration per night (hours)
- Whether the student exercises regularly: Yes / No
- Whether the student reports low energy: Yes / No

---

### Part A: Scatter Plot and Line of Best Fit

The data below records daily screen time (hours) and sleep duration (hours) for a sample of 12 students.

| Student | Screen Time (hrs) | Sleep (hrs) |
|---------|------------------|-------------|
| 1       | 1                | 9.5         |
| 2       | 2                | 9.0         |
| 3       | 3                | 8.5         |
| 4       | 4                | 8.0         |
| 5       | 5                | 7.5         |
| 6       | 6                | 7.0         |
| 7       | 7                | 6.5         |
| 8       | 8                | 6.0         |
| 9       | 9                | 5.5         |
| 10      | 3                | 7.8         |
| 11      | 6                | 8.0         |
| 12      | 8                | 5.8         |

**A1.** Plot this data on graph paper. Label axes: x = "Screen Time (hrs)", y = "Sleep (hrs)." Use x-axis 0-10 and y-axis 5-10.

**A2.** Describe the association: direction, form, any outliers.
*(Expected: moderate-to-strong negative linear association. No obvious outliers; students 10 and 11 are slightly above the trend — Student 11 has 6 hours screen time but 8.0 hours sleep, which is above average for that x-value.)*

**A3.** Draw a line of best fit. Choose two points ON your line and record them.
*(Sample: (1, 9.4) and (9, 5.4) are approximate. Accept any two points on the student's line.)*

**A4.** Calculate the slope. Show your work and include units.
*(Using (1, 9.4) and (9, 5.4): m = (5.4 - 9.4)/(9 - 1) = -4.0/8 = -0.5 hours of sleep per hour of screen time.)*

**A5.** Find the y-intercept and write the equation of the line of best fit.
*(Using (1, 9.4) and m = -0.5: 9.4 = -0.5(1) + b → b = 9.9. Equation: y = -0.5x + 9.9. Answers will vary based on individual line placement.)*

**A6.** Interpret the slope and y-intercept in context.
*(Slope: For each additional hour of daily screen time, a student's sleep duration decreases by about 0.5 hours. y-intercept: A student with 0 hours of screen time would be predicted to sleep about 9.9 hours. This is extrapolation but plausible as a baseline.)*

**A7.** Use the equation to predict how much sleep a student gets if they have 4.5 hours of screen time. Is this interpolation or extrapolation?
*(y = -0.5(4.5) + 9.9 = -2.25 + 9.9 = 7.65 hours ≈ 7.7 hours. Interpolation — 4.5 is within the data range.)*

**A8.** Predict sleep duration for a student with 15 hours of screen time. Should you trust this prediction? Why or why not?
*(y = -0.5(15) + 9.9 = -7.5 + 9.9 = 2.4 hours. Extrapolation — far outside the data range. Likely unreliable; 15 hours of screen time per day would leave very little time for anything else, and a prediction of 2.4 hours of sleep is physiologically implausible for a teenager. Do not trust this prediction.)*

---

### Part B: Correlation vs. Causation

**B1.** The scatter plot shows a negative correlation between screen time and sleep duration. A student says: "This proves that using your phone makes you sleep less." Evaluate this claim. Is the student correct? What other explanations could there be?

*(Not proven — this is correlation, not causation. Possible explanations: (1) Screen time may cause less sleep directly (light exposure, mental stimulation keeping students awake). (2) Students who sleep less for other reasons (stress, busy schedules) may turn to phones to fill time. (3) A lurking variable such as parental supervision — families with less parental oversight may have both higher screen time AND less regulated sleep schedules. The data is from an observational study, not a controlled experiment.)*

**B2.** Propose a lurking variable that could explain the screen time / sleep correlation. Draw a diagram (C → A and C → B) showing the relationship.
*(Accept any plausible lurking variable. Common examples: stress level, parental rules, after-school activities, time of day phone is used.)*

---

### Part C: Two-Way Tables

The same 200 students were asked about exercise (Regular / Not Regular) and low energy (Yes / No).

|                         | Low Energy: Yes | Low Energy: No | Row Total |
|-------------------------|-----------------|----------------|-----------|
| **Regular Exercise**    | 30              | 70             | 100       |
| **Not Regular Exercise**| 80              | 20             | 100       |
| **Column Total**        | 110             | 90             | 200       |

**C1.** Compute the row relative frequencies for both rows.
*(Regular Exercise: 30/100 = 30% low energy, 70/100 = 70% not low energy. Not Regular: 80/100 = 80% low energy, 20/100 = 20% not low energy.)*

**C2.** Is there an association between exercise habits and energy level? Describe it using specific numbers.
*(Yes, strong association. Students who exercise regularly report low energy only 30% of the time, compared to 80% for non-exercisers. Regular exercisers report low energy far less often.)*

**C3.** Compute the joint relative frequency for the cell (Not Regular Exercise, Low Energy: Yes).
*(80/200 = 40% of all students are non-exercisers who report low energy.)*

**C4.** Among students who report low energy, what percentage exercise regularly? Which type of relative frequency is this?
*(Column relative frequency: 30/110 ≈ 27.3%. Among low-energy students, about 27% exercise regularly.)*

**C5.** Can we conclude that not exercising causes low energy? Explain.
*(No — this is an observational study. The association is strong, but other factors could explain it: sleep habits (linked to screen time in Part A), diet, academic stress, or other health conditions. Causation would require a controlled study.)*

---

### Part D: Connecting the Parts

**D1.** This study found that screen time is negatively associated with sleep, and exercise habits are associated with energy levels. A student suggests: "High screen time → low sleep → low energy → less exercise. Everything is connected." Evaluate this proposed chain of causation.

*(This is an interesting hypothesis but not proven by this data. The data shows: screen time ↔ sleep (negative association), exercise ↔ energy (association). The causal chain is plausible, but each link would need to be studied in a controlled experiment to establish causation. Observational data can suggest the chain but cannot prove it. Additionally, the direction of each causal arrow could be reversed (e.g., low energy might cause less exercise AND more screen time, rather than screen time causing the chain).)*

---

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** From Part A, a student's line of best fit has equation y = -0.5x + 10. A classmate says: "My line gives y = -0.6x + 10.5." Neither student made arithmetic errors. Explain how two students can have different equations for the same data.

**Problem 2:** In Part C, the row relative frequencies are 30% and 80%. If there were no association between exercise and energy, what would both percentages be? (Hint: what is the overall percentage of all 200 students who report low energy?)

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

---

## Answer Key

### Part A
Answers provided in-line above. Key checkpoints:
- A2: Negative linear association — full credit for direction + linear form + noting any outliers.
- A4: Slope should be negative and approximately -0.4 to -0.6 depending on line placement. Award full credit for slopes in this range with correct work shown.
- A5: y-intercept approximately 9.5-10.2 depending on line. Award full credit for correct setup.
- A7: Interpolation; approximately 7.5-7.9 hours — accept any value computed correctly from the student's own equation.
- A8: The value is less important than the judgment: extrapolation, outside data range, physiologically implausible, do not trust.

### Part B
B1 and B2: Award full credit for: (1) correctly identifying this as correlation not causation, (2) identifying at least one alternative explanation or lurking variable, (3) noting this is an observational study. Award partial credit for identifying correlation ≠ causation without elaborating.

### Part C
C1: Regular Exercise row: 30%, 70%. Not Regular row: 80%, 20%. Each row sums to 100%.
C2: Strong association. Accept any description that compares the two rows and uses specific percentages (30% vs. 80%).
C3: 80/200 = **40%**.
C4: 30/110 ≈ **27.3%**. Column relative frequency.
C5: No — observational study, association only. Accept any answer that correctly identifies the limitation and names a potential alternative explanation.

### Part D
D1: Award full credit for: (1) acknowledging the hypothesis is interesting and plausible, (2) correctly identifying that this is speculation — not proven by the data, (3) noting that each causal arrow needs independent experimental evidence, and/or (4) noting that reverse causation or lurking variables could explain the pattern.

### Exit Ticket

1. Two students can have different line of best fit equations because drawing the line of best fit is an informal, visual process — different people will draw slightly different lines that both reasonably balance the data points. The line of best fit is an approximation, not a unique answer. Both equations can be valid if both lines reasonably fit the data. (In high school, students will learn the precise least-squares formula that gives a unique equation.)

2. If there were no association, the row relative frequencies would equal the overall (marginal) relative frequency for low energy.
   Overall low energy rate: 110/200 = **55%**.
   If no association: both rows would show **55% low energy and 45% not low energy**. The fact that the actual percentages (30% and 80%) differ dramatically from 55% is evidence of a strong association.

## Teacher Notes

- This lesson is deliberately integrated and scenario-based. Students should feel the connection between all parts of the unit. Resist the temptation to treat each section in isolation.
- The scenario chain (Part D) is the most sophisticated reasoning in the unit. Encourage the student to think carefully rather than giving a quick answer. There is no single "right" answer — the quality of reasoning matters.
- If the student needs more time, prioritize Parts A and C (the computational skills) over Part B and D (the conceptual reasoning). However, if the student finds Parts A and C straightforward, lean heavily into the discussion in B and D.
- The Exit Ticket question 2 — asking what the row relative frequencies would be with no association — is an excellent pre-assessment for the unit summative. If the student gets this correctly without hesitation, they have deeply understood the logic of two-way table association analysis.
- Interleaved review: This lesson naturally reviews all of Units 1-4 through slope calculation (Unit 3), equation writing (Unit 2), and the physical geometry of scatter plot axes (Unit 4 coordinate plane). No separate interleaved problems are needed.
