# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Scientific Notation

## Learning Objectives
- Write numbers in scientific notation (a x 10^n, where 1 <= a < 10 and n is an integer).
- Convert numbers from scientific notation to standard form.
- Recognize when scientific notation is useful (very large or very small numbers).

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of powers of 10 (10^1 = 10, 10^2 = 100, etc.) (Grade 5-6)
- Understanding of negative exponents (10^(-1) = 0.1, 10^(-2) = 0.01, etc.) (Lesson 8)
- Ability to multiply and divide by powers of 10
- Comfort with decimal place value

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Calculator (for verification).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 8.EE.3 — Use numbers expressed in the form of a single digit times an integer power of 10 to estimate very large or very small quantities, and to express how many times as much one is than the other.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of powers of 10 and connect to real-world large and small numbers.

1. Evaluate: 10^1, 10^3, 10^6, 10^9. (10; 1,000; 1,000,000; 1,000,000,000)
2. Evaluate: 10^(-1), 10^(-3), 10^(-6). (0.1; 0.001; 0.000001)
3. Write on the board: "The distance from Earth to the Sun is about 150,000,000,000 meters." Ask: "Is this number easy to work with? What if there were a shorter way to write it?"

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Scientific notation** — a way of writing numbers as the product of a coefficient and a power of 10. The form is a x 10^n, where 1 <= a < 10 and n is an integer.
- **Coefficient** — the number a in scientific notation. It must be at least 1 and less than 10 (one nonzero digit before the decimal point).
- **Standard form** — the normal way of writing a number with all its digits (e.g., 4,500,000 or 0.00032).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Introduce the problem that scientific notation solves.**
Write these numbers on the board:
- The mass of Earth: 5,972,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 kg
- The mass of a proton: 0.000000000000000000000000001672 kg

Say: "These numbers are hard to read, hard to write, and easy to make mistakes with. Scientists needed a better system."

**Step 2 — Define scientific notation.**
Say: "In scientific notation, every number is written as: a x 10^n, where a is between 1 and 10 (including 1, but not 10), and n is an integer."

Write the rules:
- Exactly one nonzero digit appears before the decimal point.
- The power of 10 tells you how many places the decimal moved.

**Step 3 — Convert large numbers from standard form to scientific notation.**
Example: Write 4,500,000 in scientific notation.
1. Place the decimal after the first nonzero digit: 4.500000.
2. Count how many places the decimal moved: it moved 6 places to the left.
3. Write: 4.5 x 10^6.

Example: Write 93,000,000 in scientific notation.
1. Place the decimal: 9.3000000.
2. The decimal moved 7 places to the left.
3. Write: 9.3 x 10^7.

**Step 4 — Convert small numbers from standard form to scientific notation.**
Example: Write 0.00032 in scientific notation.
1. Place the decimal after the first nonzero digit: 3.2.
2. Count how many places the decimal moved: it moved 4 places to the right.
3. Since we moved right (the number is less than 1), the exponent is negative: 3.2 x 10^(-4).

Example: Write 0.0000071 in scientific notation.
1. Place the decimal: 7.1.
2. The decimal moved 6 places to the right.
3. Write: 7.1 x 10^(-6).

**Step 5 — Convert from scientific notation back to standard form.**
Example: Write 6.02 x 10^23 in standard form.
1. The exponent is 23 (positive), so move the decimal 23 places to the right.
2. 602,000,000,000,000,000,000,000.

Example: Write 8.5 x 10^(-3) in standard form.
1. The exponent is -3 (negative), so move the decimal 3 places to the left.
2. 0.0085.

Memory aid: Positive exponent = large number (decimal moves right when converting to standard form). Negative exponent = small number (decimal moves left when converting to standard form).

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Large number to scientific notation**
Write 278,000,000 in scientific notation.
- Move the decimal: 2.78000000. Moved 8 places left.
- 278,000,000 = 2.78 x 10^8.
- Check: 2.78 x 100,000,000 = 278,000,000. Correct.

**Example B — Small number to scientific notation**
Write 0.0000509 in scientific notation.
- Move the decimal: 5.09. Moved 5 places right.
- 0.0000509 = 5.09 x 10^(-5).
- Check: 5.09 x 0.00001 = 0.0000509. Correct.

**Example C — Scientific notation to standard form**
Write 3.7 x 10^5 in standard form.
- Exponent is 5 (positive). Move decimal 5 places to the right.
- 3.70000 becomes 370,000.
- 3.7 x 10^5 = 370,000.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. The student attempts each problem first, then discusses.

1. Write 62,000 in scientific notation.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Where does the decimal go? How many places did it move?"

2. Write 0.0047 in scientific notation.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Put the decimal after the 4. How many places? Is the exponent positive or negative?"

3. Convert 9.1 x 10^(-7) to standard form.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Negative exponent means a very small number. Move the decimal left — how many places?"

4. Convert 5.28 x 10^4 to standard form.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Positive exponent means a large number. Move the decimal right 4 places."

5. Which is larger: 3.2 x 10^5 or 8.7 x 10^4? Explain.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Compare the exponents first. Which power of 10 is bigger?"

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket
**Problem 1:** Write 0.00000803 in scientific notation.
**Problem 2:** Convert 4.16 x 10^9 to standard form.

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on the direction of decimal movement. Use a physical number line or written arrows to show the decimal moving.
- Provide a mnemonic: "Left for Large (positive exponent, large number, decimal in standard form is to the right of where we put it), Right for Really small (negative exponent, small number, decimal in standard form is to the left)."
- Practice five more conversions in each direction before moving to Lesson 10.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Research project: Find three real-world quantities expressed in scientific notation (from science textbooks, news, or online sources) and convert each to standard form.
- Preview Lesson 10: "Now that you can write numbers in scientific notation, we need to learn how to add, subtract, multiply, and divide them."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Scale of the Universe:** Research the approximate sizes or distances of the following and express each in scientific notation: diameter of an atom, diameter of a cell, height of a person, diameter of Earth, distance to the nearest star.
- **Powers of 10 Video:** Watch the classic "Powers of Ten" film (by Charles and Ray Eames) and write a paragraph about how scale changes as you zoom in or out by factors of 10.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Coefficient is not between 1 and 10.** Students may write 45 x 10^3 instead of 4.5 x 10^4. Remind them: the coefficient must have exactly one nonzero digit before the decimal point.
- **Wrong sign on the exponent.** Students may write 0.005 as 5 x 10^3 instead of 5 x 10^(-3). Reinforce: numbers less than 1 always have negative exponents; numbers greater than 10 always have positive exponents.
- **Counting decimal places incorrectly.** Off-by-one errors are common. Encourage the student to count places carefully and check by converting back.
- **Confusing direction of decimal movement.** When converting TO scientific notation, the decimal moves in one direction. When converting FROM scientific notation, it moves in the opposite direction. Always verify by checking reasonableness.

### Pacing Advice
- Steps 3 and 4 (converting to scientific notation) are the core of the lesson. Spend the most time here, especially on small numbers (negative exponents), which students find harder.
- If the student is solid on positive exponents but shaky on negative exponents, spend extra time on Step 4 with additional examples (0.1, 0.01, 0.025, 0.000003).
- The comparison question (Guided Practice 5) previews a key skill for Lesson 10. If time permits, do more comparison examples.
