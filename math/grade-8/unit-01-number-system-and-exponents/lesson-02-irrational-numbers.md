# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 1, Lesson 2

## Topic
Irrational Numbers

## Learning Objectives
- Define irrational numbers as numbers that cannot be expressed as a/b where a and b are integers.
- Identify the key property of irrational numbers: their decimal expansions are non-terminating and non-repeating.
- Classify given numbers as rational or irrational and justify the classification.

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of rational numbers and their decimal representations (Lesson 1)
- Ability to convert fractions to decimals using long division
- Familiarity with perfect squares (1, 4, 9, 16, 25)
- Basic understanding of square roots (e.g., sqrt(9) = 3)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Calculator (for exploring decimal expansions).
- Graph paper (for drawing a geometric proof of sqrt(2)).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 8.NS.1 — Know that numbers that are not rational are called irrational. Understand informally that every number has a decimal expansion; for rational numbers show that the decimal expansion repeats eventually, and convert a decimal expansion which repeats eventually into a rational number.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review the rational number definition from Lesson 1 and set up the key question for today.

1. Ask: "What makes a number rational?" (It can be written as a fraction a/b with integers a and b, b not zero.)
2. Write these decimals on the board: 0.75, 0.333..., 0.101001000100001...
3. Ask: "Which of these are rational? How do you know?" (0.75 terminates — rational. 0.333... repeats — rational. The third one does not terminate and does not repeat — this is the question for today.)
4. Tell the student: "Today we discover that there are numbers that CAN'T be written as fractions — no matter how hard you try."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Irrational number** — a number that cannot be expressed as a fraction a/b where a and b are integers. Its decimal expansion goes on forever without repeating.
- **Non-terminating** — a decimal that never ends.
- **Non-repeating** — a decimal in which no block of digits repeats in a predictable pattern forever.
- **Square root** — a number that, when multiplied by itself, gives the original number. For example, sqrt(25) = 5 because 5 x 5 = 25.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Introduce the idea with a geometric puzzle.**
Draw a square on graph paper with side length 1 unit. Ask: "What is the area of this square?" (1 square unit.) Now draw the diagonal. Say: "How long is this diagonal?"

Use the Pythagorean relationship (preview — students will study this formally later): the diagonal of a 1-by-1 square has length sqrt(1^2 + 1^2) = sqrt(2).

Say: "Let's try to figure out what sqrt(2) is as a decimal."

**Step 2 — Explore sqrt(2) with a calculator.**
Type sqrt(2) into a calculator. Display: 1.41421356237...

Say: "Notice — this decimal keeps going. But does it repeat? Look carefully. There is no repeating block. Mathematicians have computed millions of digits of sqrt(2), and no repeating pattern has ever been found — and it has been proven that one never will."

**Step 3 — Explain WHY sqrt(2) is irrational (intuitive proof).**
Say: "Here is the key idea. If sqrt(2) WERE rational, we could write it as a fraction p/q in simplest form. Then (p/q)^2 = 2, which means p^2 = 2q^2. This tells us p^2 is even, so p must be even. But if p is even, then p^2 is divisible by 4, which means 2q^2 is divisible by 4, so q^2 is even, so q is even. But wait — we said p/q was in simplest form, meaning they share no common factors. Yet we just showed both are even — a contradiction. So our assumption must be wrong: sqrt(2) cannot be written as a fraction."

Note to facilitator: This proof is presented at an intuitive level. The student does not need to reproduce it formally, but should understand the punchline — no fraction, when squared, gives exactly 2.

**Step 4 — Introduce other famous irrational numbers.**
Write on the board:
- sqrt(2) = 1.41421356... (diagonal of a unit square)
- sqrt(3) = 1.73205080... (height of an equilateral triangle with side 2)
- pi = 3.14159265... (ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter)

Say: "These numbers show up everywhere in geometry and science. They are real numbers — they have locations on the number line — but they cannot be captured by any fraction."

**Step 5 — Establish the classification system.**
Draw a diagram:
- Real Numbers divide into Rational Numbers and Irrational Numbers.
- Rational Numbers include Integers, which include Whole Numbers, which include Natural Numbers.

Emphasize: "Every real number is either rational OR irrational — never both, never neither."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Classifying sqrt(9)**
Is sqrt(9) rational or irrational?
- sqrt(9) = 3, because 3 x 3 = 9.
- 3 is an integer, and all integers are rational (3 = 3/1).
- sqrt(9) is **rational**.
- Key point: Not every square root is irrational. Square roots of perfect squares are rational.

**Example B — Classifying sqrt(7)**
Is sqrt(7) rational or irrational?
- 7 is not a perfect square (2^2 = 4, 3^2 = 9 — nothing squared gives 7).
- Calculator: sqrt(7) = 2.6457513...
- The decimal does not terminate and does not repeat.
- sqrt(7) is **irrational**.

**Example C — Classifying 0.123123123...**
Is 0.123123123... rational or irrational?
- The decimal repeats the block "123" forever.
- Any repeating decimal is rational. We can convert it:
  - Let x = 0.123123...
  - 1000x = 123.123123...
  - 999x = 123
  - x = 123/999 = 41/333
- 0.123123123... is **rational**.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. The student attempts each problem first, then discusses.

1. Classify: sqrt(16). Rational or irrational? Explain.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What number times itself gives 16?"

2. Classify: sqrt(5). Rational or irrational? Explain.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Is 5 a perfect square? What does the calculator show for sqrt(5)?"

3. Classify: 0.101001000100001... (the number of zeros increases each time). Rational or irrational?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Does this decimal terminate? Does it repeat a fixed block of digits?"

4. Classify: -sqrt(49). Rational or irrational? Explain.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "First find sqrt(49). Then apply the negative sign. Is the result an integer?"

5. A student says, "Pi equals 22/7, so pi is rational." What is wrong with this reasoning?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Is 22/7 exactly equal to pi, or is it an approximation?"

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### ★ On-Level
1. Classify each number as rational or irrational: 0.75, sqrt(10), -3, sqrt(36), pi
2. True or false: Every integer is a rational number. Explain.
3. True or false: Every square root is an irrational number. Explain and give a counterexample if false.
4. Give an example of an irrational number between 4 and 5. (Hint: Think about square roots.)

### ★★ Stretch
5. Between which two consecutive integers does sqrt(30) fall? Is sqrt(30) rational or irrational?
6. A student writes: "0.12112111211112... is rational because it has a pattern." Do you agree? Explain.
7. Classify sqrt(1/4). Rational or irrational? (Hint: What is sqrt(1/4) as a decimal or fraction?)

### ★★★ Challenge
8. Explain why sqrt(2) + 1 is irrational. (Hint: If sqrt(2) + 1 were rational, what could you say about sqrt(2)?)
9. Is the product of two irrational numbers always irrational? Find a counterexample to prove your answer.

### Interleaved Review
*(Reviewing: percent problems from Grade 7)*
10. A shirt originally costs $40. It is on sale for 25% off. What is the sale price?
11. After a 15% tip, a meal costs $34.50. What was the original price of the meal?

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket
**Problem 1:** Classify sqrt(15) as rational or irrational. Explain your reasoning in one sentence.
**Problem 2:** Give one example of a rational number and one example of an irrational number. For each, explain why it fits that category.

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the classification system diagram (rational vs. irrational). Have the student sort 10 numbers into two columns, using a calculator to check decimal expansions.
- Focus on the concrete test: "Can you write it as a fraction? If yes, rational. If the decimal never ends and never repeats, irrational."
- Provide additional examples of perfect vs. non-perfect square roots to reinforce that sqrt(perfect square) = rational.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask the student to research one more famous irrational number (e.g., e = 2.71828..., the golden ratio phi = 1.61803...) and write one sentence about where it appears in the real world.
- Preview Lesson 3: "Now that we know irrational numbers exist, how do we figure out approximately where they sit on the number line?"

## Extension (Optional)
- **Spiral of Theodorus:** Draw a right triangle with legs 1 and 1 (hypotenuse = sqrt(2)). Then use that hypotenuse as one leg of a new right triangle with the other leg = 1 (new hypotenuse = sqrt(3)). Continue to build a spiral. Measure each hypotenuse and compare to the calculator value.
- **Debate:** "Are there more rational numbers or more irrational numbers?" Research and present a brief argument. (Spoiler: there are more irrationals, but this is a deep idea from set theory.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Assuming all square roots are irrational.** Remind students that sqrt(4) = 2, sqrt(9) = 3, sqrt(100) = 10 — square roots of perfect squares are rational. The test is whether the number under the square root is a perfect square.
- **Confusing "long decimal" with "irrational."** A student may think 1/7 = 0.142857142857... is irrational because the decimal is so long. Emphasize: length does not matter — what matters is whether a block of digits repeats. 1/7 repeats "142857" forever, so it is rational.
- **Thinking pi = 3.14.** Students often memorize the approximation and believe it is exact. Clarify that 3.14 is a rounded value. The true decimal expansion of pi never ends and never repeats.

### Pacing Advice
- The intuitive proof that sqrt(2) is irrational (Step 3) is optional for students who find it overwhelming. The essential takeaway is the classification rule, not the proof. Spend at most 3 minutes on the proof.
- If the student grasps the classification quickly, use the extra time for the Guided Practice discussion questions, especially question 5 (the pi = 22/7 misconception), which deepens conceptual understanding.
- This lesson is more conceptual than computational. Encourage discussion and questions rather than rushing through problems.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice
1. sqrt(16) = 4, which is an integer. Rational.
2. sqrt(5) = 2.2360679... The decimal does not terminate or repeat. 5 is not a perfect square. Irrational.
3. The decimal 0.101001000100001... does not terminate, and the pattern of zeros increases each time (it is not a fixed repeating block). Irrational.
4. -sqrt(49) = -7, which is an integer. Rational.
5. 22/7 is an approximation of pi, not its exact value. 22/7 = 3.142857142857... (repeating), while pi = 3.14159265... (non-repeating). They are close but not equal. Pi is irrational.

### Independent Practice

**★ On-Level**
1. 0.75 = rational (terminating decimal = 3/4); sqrt(10) = irrational (10 is not a perfect square); -3 = rational (integer = -3/1); sqrt(36) = 6 = rational; pi = irrational (non-terminating, non-repeating)
2. True. Every integer n can be written as n/1, which is a fraction of two integers with a nonzero denominator.
3. False. Counterexample: sqrt(25) = 5, which is rational. Only square roots of non-perfect-squares are irrational.
4. sqrt(17), sqrt(18), sqrt(19), sqrt(20), sqrt(21), sqrt(22), sqrt(23), or sqrt(24) — any of these is between 4 and 5 because 4^2 = 16 and 5^2 = 25.

**★★ Stretch**
5. 5^2 = 25 and 6^2 = 36, so sqrt(30) is between 5 and 6. Since 30 is not a perfect square, sqrt(30) is irrational.
6. Disagree. Having a pattern is not the same as having a repeating block. In a repeating decimal, the same finite block of digits repeats forever (like 0.121212...). In 0.12112111211112..., the number of 1s keeps growing — there is no fixed block that repeats. This number is irrational.
7. sqrt(1/4) = sqrt(1)/sqrt(4) = 1/2 = 0.5. This is rational (it is a fraction of two integers).

**★★★ Challenge**
8. Suppose sqrt(2) + 1 were rational. Then sqrt(2) + 1 = p/q for some integers p and q. Subtracting 1 from both sides: sqrt(2) = p/q - 1 = (p - q)/q. This would mean sqrt(2) is rational — but we know it is irrational. Contradiction. Therefore sqrt(2) + 1 must be irrational.
9. No. Counterexample: sqrt(2) x sqrt(2) = 2. Both sqrt(2) values are irrational, but their product is the rational number 2.

1. sqrt(15) is irrational because 15 is not a perfect square, so its square root produces a non-terminating, non-repeating decimal (sqrt(15) = 3.87298...).
2. Answers will vary. Example: 2/3 is rational because it is a fraction of two integers. sqrt(3) is irrational because 3 is not a perfect square and its decimal expansion (1.73205...) never terminates and never repeats.
