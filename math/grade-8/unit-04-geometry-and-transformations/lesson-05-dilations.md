# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 5

## Topic
Dilations

## Learning Objectives
- Define a dilation as a transformation that enlarges or reduces a figure from a center point by a scale factor.
- Apply the coordinate rule (x, y) -> (kx, ky) for dilations centered at the origin.
- Distinguish between enlargements (scale factor k > 1) and reductions (0 < k < 1).
- Verify that dilations preserve shape and angle measure but change side lengths proportionally.

## Prerequisites
- Translations, reflections, and rotations (Lessons 1-3)
- Understanding of proportional relationships and ratios (Grade 7)
- Multiplication of integers and fractions (Grade 7)
- Plotting ordered pairs in all four quadrants (Grade 6-7)

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper (at least two sheets per student)
- Ruler or straightedge
- Colored pencils (two colors: one for pre-image, one for image)
- Index card or half-sheet for Exit Ticket
- Calculator (optional, for checking decimal computations)

**Standards:** 8.G.3 — Describe the effect of dilations, translations, rotations, and reflections on two-dimensional figures using coordinates; 8.G.4 — Understand that a two-dimensional figure is similar to another if the second can be obtained from the first by a sequence of rotations, reflections, translations, and dilations.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review proportional thinking and connect to scaling.

1. A recipe calls for 2 cups of flour and 3 cups of sugar. If you want to double the recipe, how much flour and sugar do you need? (4 cups flour, 6 cups sugar.)
2. If you want to make half the recipe, how much? (1 cup flour, 1.5 cups sugar.)
3. Multiply: 3 x (4, 2) = ? (Think of it as multiplying each number by 3.) (12, 6.)
4. Multiply: 0.5 x (6, -4) = ? (3, -2.)

Say: "Today we learn a transformation that works exactly like scaling a recipe — every measurement gets multiplied by the same factor."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Dilation** — a transformation that enlarges or reduces a figure by a scale factor from a center point.
- **Scale factor (k)** — the number by which every distance from the center of dilation is multiplied.
- **Center of dilation** — the fixed point from which the figure is enlarged or reduced. In this lesson, the center is always the origin (0, 0).
- **Enlargement** — a dilation with scale factor k > 1 (the figure gets bigger).
- **Reduction** — a dilation with scale factor 0 < k < 1 (the figure gets smaller).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Introduce dilations with a physical analogy.**

Say: "Imagine you have a photograph and you zoom in on your phone. The picture gets bigger, but the shapes in the picture do not change — people still look like people, circles still look like circles. Everything just gets scaled up by the same factor. That is an enlargement. If you zoom out, everything gets smaller by the same factor — that is a reduction. Both are dilations."

**Step 2 — Define the coordinate rule.**

Write on the board: "Dilation centered at the origin with scale factor k: (x, y) -> (kx, ky)"

Say: "To dilate a point, multiply BOTH coordinates by the scale factor k. If k = 2, every point moves twice as far from the origin. If k = 1/2, every point moves half as far from the origin."

**Step 3 — Demonstrate an enlargement (k > 1).**

Triangle with vertices A(1, 1), B(3, 1), C(2, 3). Scale factor k = 2.
- A(1, 1) -> A'(2, 2)
- B(3, 1) -> B'(6, 2)
- C(2, 3) -> C'(4, 6)

Say: "Plot both triangles. The image triangle is bigger — every side is twice as long. But the shape is exactly the same. The angles have not changed. This is the key property of a dilation: shape is preserved, but size changes."

Check side lengths:
- AB = 3 - 1 = 2 units. A'B' = 6 - 2 = 4 units. Ratio: 4/2 = 2. Matches scale factor.
- The image is 2 times larger in every direction.

**Step 4 — Demonstrate a reduction (0 < k < 1).**

Same triangle: A(1, 1), B(3, 1), C(2, 3). Scale factor k = 1/2.
- A(1, 1) -> A'(0.5, 0.5)
- B(3, 1) -> B'(1.5, 0.5)
- C(2, 3) -> C'(1, 1.5)

Say: "The image triangle is smaller — every side is half as long. The shape is still the same. A reduction shrinks the figure toward the center of dilation."

**Step 5 — Important distinctions from rigid transformations.**

Say: "Translations, reflections, and rotations are called rigid transformations because they preserve distance — the figure stays the same size. A dilation is NOT a rigid transformation because it changes the size. However, a dilation does preserve the SHAPE: all angles stay the same, and all sides change by the same factor. This idea of same shape but different size is called SIMILARITY, which we will study in Lesson 7."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Dilate the point (-4, 6) by scale factor k = 3 from the origin.
- (-4 x 3, 6 x 3) = **(-12, 18)**

**Example B:** Dilate triangle with vertices P(0, 0), Q(8, 0), R(4, 6) by scale factor k = 1/4.
- P(0, 0) -> P'(0, 0) — the origin stays at the origin for any dilation centered there.
- Q(8, 0) -> Q'(2, 0)
- R(4, 6) -> R'(1, 1.5)
- Check: PQ = 8. P'Q' = 2. Ratio = 2/8 = 1/4. Matches scale factor.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity 1: Dilating Points**

1. Dilate the point (3, -5) by scale factor k = 2 from the origin.
   - "Multiply both coordinates by 2."
   - Image: **(6, -10)**

2. Dilate the point (10, -4) by scale factor k = 1/2 from the origin.
   - Image: **(5, -2)**

3. A point A(2, 3) is dilated to A'(6, 9). What is the scale factor?
   - "How do we find the scale factor?" (Divide image coordinate by original coordinate.)
   - 6/2 = 3, and 9/3 = 3. Scale factor: **k = 3**

4. A point B(8, -12) is dilated to B'(2, -3). What is the scale factor?
   - 2/8 = 1/4, and -3/-12 = 1/4. Scale factor: **k = 1/4**

**Activity 2: Dilating Figures**

5. Rectangle with vertices A(2, 1), B(6, 1), C(6, 3), D(2, 3). Dilate by k = 2.
   - A'(4, 2), B'(12, 2), C'(12, 6), D'(4, 6)
   - Ask: "What happened to the side lengths?" Original: width = 4, height = 2. Image: width = 8, height = 4. Everything doubled.
   - Ask: "Is the image still a rectangle?" (Yes — shape preserved.)

6. Triangle with vertices (3, 0), (9, 0), (6, 6). Dilate by k = 1/3.
   - (1, 0), (3, 0), (2, 2)
   - Ask: "Are the angles of the image the same as the original?" (Yes.)

**Facilitator tip:** If the student confuses dilation with translation, emphasize: translation ADDS to coordinates (shifting position), while dilation MULTIPLIES coordinates (changing size). Have the student write "x" for dilation and "+" for translation as a reminder.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. Dilate the point (4, -3) by scale factor k = 5 from the origin.
2. Dilate the point (-6, 8) by scale factor k = 1/2 from the origin.
3. Triangle with vertices (1, 2), (3, 2), (2, 5). Dilate by k = 3. List the image vertices.
4. A point is dilated from (10, 15) to (2, 3). What is the scale factor?
5. True or false: A dilation changes the angles of a figure. Explain.

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

6. A square has vertices at (0, 0), (4, 0), (4, 4), (0, 4). Its area is 16 square units. After a dilation with k = 3, what is the area of the image? (Hint: Find the new side length first.)
7. A triangle is dilated by k = 2 and then dilated again by k = 3. What single scale factor would produce the same result?
8. A point P(5, -2) is dilated by k = 1/2 and then translated by (x + 3, y - 1). What is the final image?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

9. A dilation has scale factor k = -1. Apply it to the point (3, 4). Where does the point end up? What other transformation produces the same result?
10. The area of a triangle is 12 square units. After a dilation with scale factor k, the area of the image is 48 square units. What is k?

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** Dilate the point (-3, 4) by scale factor k = 2 from the origin. State the image.

**Problem 2:** A triangle with vertices (2, 1), (6, 1), (4, 5) is dilated by k = 1/2 from the origin. List the image vertices. Is the image larger or smaller than the original?

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. (4 x 5, -3 x 5) = **(20, -15)**
2. (-6 x 0.5, 8 x 0.5) = **(-3, 4)**
3. (1 x 3, 2 x 3) = (3, 6); (3 x 3, 2 x 3) = (9, 6); (2 x 3, 5 x 3) = (6, 15). Image: **(3, 6), (9, 6), (6, 15)**.
4. 2/10 = 1/5, 3/15 = 1/5. Scale factor: **k = 1/5**.
5. **False.** A dilation preserves all angle measures. The shape stays the same; only the size changes.
6. Original side = 4. After k = 3: new side = 12. New area = 12 x 12 = **144 square units**. (The area is multiplied by k^2 = 9.)
7. k = 2 then k = 3: combined scale factor = 2 x 3 = **6**. (Scale factors multiply.)
8. Dilate (5, -2) by k = 1/2: (2.5, -1). Translate: (2.5 + 3, -1 - 1) = **(5.5, -2)**. (Note: coincidentally close to the original, but the path was different.)
9. k = -1: (3 x -1, 4 x -1) = **(-3, -4)**. This is the same result as a **180-degree rotation** about the origin. A negative scale factor reflects through the center of dilation.
10. Area scale factor = k^2. So k^2 = 48/12 = 4. Therefore k = **2**. (Scale factor is 2 because area scales by the square of the linear scale factor.)

### Exit Ticket

1. (-3 x 2, 4 x 2) = **(-6, 8)**
2. (2 x 0.5, 1 x 0.5) = (1, 0.5); (6 x 0.5, 1 x 0.5) = (3, 0.5); (4 x 0.5, 5 x 0.5) = (2, 2.5). Image: **(1, 0.5), (3, 0.5), (2, 2.5)**. The image is **smaller** (scale factor is less than 1).

## Teacher Notes

- Dilations are conceptually different from the rigid transformations in Lessons 1-3. Emphasize this distinction: rigid transformations preserve size, dilations change size.
- The most common error is applying the scale factor to only one coordinate: writing (kx, y) instead of (kx, ky). Reinforce that BOTH coordinates are multiplied by k.
- Students sometimes confuse scale factor with the amount added. A scale factor of 2 does NOT mean "add 2 to each coordinate" — it means "multiply each coordinate by 2." Watch for this confusion and address it immediately.
- The relationship between scale factor and area (area scales by k^2) is a powerful concept that connects to proportional reasoning. Even though it is not a core standard at this level, it appears in the Tier 2 and Tier 3 problems as enrichment.
- When the scale factor is between 0 and 1, students may call it a "negative scale factor." Clarify: 1/2 is a positive number less than 1, not a negative number. A truly negative scale factor (like -1) would reflect the figure through the center and is introduced only in the Challenge tier.
- Connect to real life: maps (scale factor tells you how the map relates to real distances), architectural blueprints, model building, photo enlargement and reduction.
