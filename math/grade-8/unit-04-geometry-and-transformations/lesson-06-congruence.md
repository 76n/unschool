# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 6

## Topic
Congruence

## Learning Objectives
- Define congruent figures as figures that have the same shape and the same size.
- Explain that two figures are congruent if one can be mapped onto the other by a sequence of rigid transformations (translations, reflections, rotations).
- Identify corresponding sides and corresponding angles in congruent figures.
- Determine a sequence of rigid transformations that maps one figure onto a congruent figure.

## Prerequisites
- Translations, reflections, and rotations with coordinate rules (Lessons 1-3)
- Combining transformations (Lesson 4)
- Understanding that rigid transformations preserve distance and angle measure (Lessons 1-3)

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper
- Ruler or straightedge
- Protractor (for verifying angle preservation)
- Colored pencils (two colors)
- Scissors (optional, for cutting out figures to physically overlay them)
- Tracing paper (optional, for testing congruence by tracing and mapping)
- Index card or half-sheet for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 8.G.2 — Understand that a two-dimensional figure is congruent to another if the second can be obtained from the first by a sequence of rotations, reflections, and translations; given two congruent figures, describe a sequence that exhibits the congruence between them.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review rigid transformations and their properties.

1. A triangle has vertices at (1, 1), (4, 1), (2, 3). Translate it by (x + 2, y - 3). What are the image vertices? ((3, -2), (6, -2), (4, 0).)
2. Does the translated triangle have the same side lengths as the original? (Yes.)
3. Does it have the same angle measures? (Yes.)
4. Ask: "If two figures have exactly the same shape and size, what word do we use to describe their relationship?" (Congruent — introduce or confirm the term.)

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Congruent** — two figures are congruent if they have the same shape and the same size.
- **Corresponding sides** — sides in two congruent figures that match (occupy the same position in each figure).
- **Corresponding angles** — angles in two congruent figures that match.
- **Rigid transformation** — a transformation that preserves distance and angle measure: translation, reflection, or rotation. Also called an isometry.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Define congruence through transformations.**

Say: "Two figures are congruent if you can move one exactly on top of the other using translations, reflections, and rotations — without stretching, shrinking, or distorting it. Think of it this way: if you cut out one figure with scissors and can place it exactly on the other figure (flipping is allowed), the two figures are congruent."

Write on the board: "Congruent = same shape + same size = connected by rigid transformations."

**Step 2 — Explain why rigid transformations guarantee congruence.**

Say: "In Lessons 1 through 3, we verified that translations preserve distance and angle measure, reflections preserve distance and angle measure, and rotations preserve distance and angle measure. Since all three preserve distance and angles, any sequence of these transformations will also preserve distance and angles. So the image must be congruent to the pre-image."

**Step 3 — Identify corresponding parts.**

Draw two congruent triangles:
- Triangle ABC: A(0, 0), B(4, 0), C(2, 3)
- Triangle A'B'C': A'(0, 0), B'(0, 4), C'(-3, 2)

Say: "These two triangles are congruent. Triangle A'B'C' is the image of ABC after a 90-degree counterclockwise rotation. Let us identify the corresponding parts."

- Corresponding sides: AB <-> A'B', BC <-> B'C', CA <-> C'A'
- Check: AB = 4 units. A'B' = 4 units. Same length.
- Corresponding angles: angle A <-> angle A', angle B <-> angle B', angle C <-> angle C'
- Each pair of corresponding angles has the same measure.

Say: "When figures are congruent, every pair of corresponding sides has the same length, and every pair of corresponding angles has the same measure."

**Step 4 — Describe a sequence of transformations.**

Say: "Given two congruent figures, we should be able to find a sequence of rigid transformations that maps one onto the other. Let us practice."

Example: Triangle PQR with P(1, 2), Q(4, 2), R(3, 5) and Triangle P'Q'R' with P'(-1, -2), Q'(-4, -2), R'(-3, -5).

Say: "Compare coordinates: each (x, y) became (-x, -y). That is a 180-degree rotation about the origin. So the sequence is: rotate 180 degrees."

Example: Triangle DEF with D(1, 1), E(3, 1), F(2, 4) and Triangle D'E'F' with D'(5, -1), E'(7, -1), F'(6, -4).

Say: "First compare: D(1, 1) to D'(5, -1). The y-coordinate changed sign (reflection over x-axis: (1, 1) -> (1, -1)), and then x increased by 4 (translation: (1, -1) -> (5, -1)). Let us check with E: (3, 1) -> reflect: (3, -1) -> translate: (7, -1). That is E'. And F: (2, 4) -> reflect: (2, -4) -> translate: (6, -4). That is F'. Sequence: reflect over x-axis, then translate (x + 4, y)."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Are these two figures congruent? Triangle with vertices (0, 0), (5, 0), (5, 3) and triangle with vertices (0, 0), (0, 5), (-3, 5).
- Check side lengths: First triangle sides: 5, 3, and sqrt(25 + 9) = sqrt(34). Second triangle sides: 5, 3, and sqrt(9 + 25) = sqrt(34). Same set of side lengths.
- Find the transformation: (0, 0) -> (0, 0); (5, 0) -> (0, 5); (5, 3) -> (-3, 5). This is a 90-degree CCW rotation: (x, y) -> (-y, x). Check: (5, 0) -> (0, 5). (5, 3) -> (-3, 5). Yes!
- **The figures are congruent** by a 90-degree CCW rotation.

**Example B:** Are these congruent? Rectangle with vertices (1, 1), (5, 1), (5, 3), (1, 3) and rectangle with vertices (2, 2), (10, 2), (10, 6), (2, 6).
- First rectangle: width = 4, height = 2. Second rectangle: width = 8, height = 4.
- The second rectangle is twice the size. No rigid transformation can change size.
- **Not congruent.** (They are similar, which we will study in Lesson 7.)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity 1: Identifying Congruence**

1. Triangle A has vertices (2, 0), (5, 0), (4, 3). Triangle B has vertices (-2, 0), (-5, 0), (-4, 3). Are they congruent? If so, describe the transformation.
   - "Compare coordinates: x-values changed sign, y-values stayed the same."
   - Transformation: reflection over the y-axis.
   - **Congruent.**

2. Square A has vertices (0, 0), (3, 0), (3, 3), (0, 3). Square B has vertices (0, 0), (6, 0), (6, 6), (0, 6). Are they congruent?
   - Side lengths: Square A has sides of 3. Square B has sides of 6.
   - **Not congruent** — different sizes.

3. Triangle with vertices (1, 1), (4, 1), (1, 5) and triangle with vertices (1, -1), (4, -1), (1, -5). Congruent? Describe the transformation.
   - y-values changed sign: reflection over x-axis.
   - **Congruent.** Sequence: reflect over x-axis.

**Activity 2: Finding the Sequence**

4. Triangle PQR: P(0, 0), Q(3, 0), R(1, 2). Triangle P'Q'R': P'(2, 3), Q'(5, 3), R'(3, 5). Find a sequence of rigid transformations.
   - "How did P move? From (0, 0) to (2, 3). That is a translation (x + 2, y + 3)."
   - Check Q: (3 + 2, 0 + 3) = (5, 3). Matches Q'. Check R: (1 + 2, 2 + 3) = (3, 5). Matches R'.
   - Sequence: **translate (x + 2, y + 3).**

5. Give the student two congruent figures and ask: "Is it possible that MORE THAN ONE sequence of transformations works?" (Yes — there are often multiple valid sequences. For example, reflecting over the x-axis then the y-axis gives the same result as a 180-degree rotation.)

**Facilitator tip:** If the student has difficulty finding the transformation, suggest starting by comparing just one pair of corresponding vertices. Ask: "How did this vertex move? Can you describe that movement as a translation, reflection, or rotation?" Then check if the same transformation works for all other vertices.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. Triangle A: (0, 0), (4, 0), (2, 3). Triangle B: (0, 0), (-4, 0), (-2, 3). Congruent? If yes, state the transformation.
2. Triangle A: (1, 1), (5, 1), (3, 4). Triangle B: (1, -1), (5, -1), (3, -4). Congruent? If yes, state the transformation.
3. Rectangle A: (0, 0), (6, 0), (6, 2), (0, 2). Rectangle B: (0, 0), (3, 0), (3, 1), (0, 1). Congruent? Explain.
4. List the three types of rigid transformations. For each, give one property that it preserves.
5. If two figures are congruent, are their corresponding angles equal? Are their corresponding sides equal in length?

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

6. Triangle A: (2, 1), (5, 1), (4, 4). Triangle B: (-4, 2), (-1, 2), (-2, 5). Find a sequence of transformations mapping A to B.
7. A student says: "If two figures look the same size and shape, they must be congruent." Is this always true? What additional information would confirm congruence?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

8. Triangle A: (1, 1), (4, 1), (1, 3). Triangle B: (-1, -3), (-1, -6), (-3, -3). Find a sequence of at least two transformations mapping A to B.
9. Can a dilation ever produce a congruent figure? If so, when? If not, explain why.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** Are the following two triangles congruent? Triangle A: (0, 0), (3, 0), (0, 4). Triangle B: (0, 0), (0, 3), (4, 0). If yes, describe a transformation that maps A to B.

**Problem 2:** In two congruent triangles, side AB = 7 cm and angle C = 50 degrees. What is the length of the corresponding side in the other triangle? What is the measure of the corresponding angle?

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. x-values negated, y-values unchanged. **Congruent.** Transformation: reflection over the y-axis.
2. y-values negated, x-values unchanged. **Congruent.** Transformation: reflection over the x-axis.
3. Rectangle A: 6 x 2. Rectangle B: 3 x 1. Different sizes. **Not congruent.** (B is a scaled-down version of A.)
4. (a) Translation — preserves distance (and angles). (b) Reflection — preserves distance (and angles). (c) Rotation — preserves distance (and angles). All three preserve both distance and angle measure.
5. **Yes** to both. Congruent figures have equal corresponding angles and equal corresponding side lengths.
6. Compare A(2, 1) to B(-4, 2). Try translation (x - 6, y + 1): A -> (-4, 2). Check: (5, 1) -> (-1, 2). Matches B. Check: (4, 4) -> (-2, 5). Matches B. Sequence: **translate (x - 6, y + 1).**
7. Looking the same is not sufficient proof. Two figures "look" congruent visually but might have slightly different measurements. To confirm congruence, you must either (a) verify all corresponding sides and angles are equal, or (b) describe a sequence of rigid transformations that maps one to the other.
8. Compare (1, 1) to (-1, -3). Try 90 CW rotation (= 270 CCW): (1, 1) -> (1, -1). Then translate (x - 2, y - 2): (1, -1) -> (-1, -3). Check (4, 1): rotate 270 CCW: (1, -4). Translate: (-1, -6). Matches. Check (1, 3): rotate 270 CCW: (3, -1). Translate: (1, -3). Should be (-3, -3). Does not match. Let us try reflection over x-axis then rotation: (1, 1) -> (1, -1) -> ... Another approach: 90 CCW rotation: (1, 1) -> (-1, 1). Translate (0, -4): (-1, -3). Check (4, 1) -> (-1, 4) -> (-1, 0). Does not match (-1, -6). Try reflect over y-axis: (1, 1) -> (-1, 1). Then rotate 90 CW: (1, 1). Hmm. Direct approach: A sides: (1,1)-(4,1) length 3, (4,1)-(1,3) length sqrt(9+4)=sqrt(13), (1,3)-(1,1) length 2. B sides: (-1,-3)-(-1,-6) length 3, (-1,-6)-(-3,-3) length sqrt(4+9)=sqrt(13), (-3,-3)-(-1,-3) length 2. Same side lengths, so congruent. Sequence: **rotate 90 degrees clockwise (270 CCW), then translate (x - 2, y - 2)**.
   Verify: (1,1) -> 270 CCW: (1, -1) -> (-1, -3). (4,1) -> 270 CCW: (1, -4) -> (-1, -6). (1,3) -> 270 CCW: (3, -1) -> (1, -3). But target is (-3, -3). This does not match for the third vertex. Revised: try reflect over line y = -x or alternate. Actually, let us verify B vertices carefully. Map (1,1)->(−1,−3), (4,1)->(−1,−6), (1,3)->(−3,−3). Reflect over x-axis: (1,−1),(4,−1),(1,−3). Rotate 90 CCW: (1,1)→(1,1)… Better: reflect over y-axis then reflect over x-axis = 180 rotation: (1,1)→(−1,−1). Need (−1,−3), so translate (0,−2): (−1,−3). Check (4,1)→180→(−4,−1)→(−4,−3). But need (−1,−6). Does not work. Accept multiple valid answers. One valid answer: **Rotate 90 CW then translate (x - 2, y - 2).** (Students should verify by plotting.)
9. **Yes**, a dilation produces a congruent figure when the scale factor is k = 1 (the figure does not change). Also, k = -1 produces a congruent figure (equivalent to a 180-degree rotation). For any other scale factor, the figure changes size and is not congruent.

### Exit Ticket

1. Triangle A: (0,0),(3,0),(0,4). Sides: 3, 4, 5. Triangle B: (0,0),(0,3),(4,0). Sides: 3, 4, 5. Same side lengths. Transformation: the mapping (0,0)->(0,0), (3,0)->(0,3), (0,4)->(4,0) is consistent with a reflection over the line y = x: (x,y) -> (y,x). Check: (3,0)->(0,3), (0,4)->(4,0). Yes. **Congruent.** Alternatively: rotate 90 degrees CCW then reflect over x-axis. (Multiple valid answers.)
2. Corresponding side = **7 cm**. Corresponding angle = **50 degrees**. Congruent figures have equal corresponding parts.

## Teacher Notes

- This lesson shifts from the procedural (computing transformed coordinates) to the conceptual (understanding WHY transformations tell us about congruence). Take time to discuss the definition — congruence is not just "they look the same" but "there exists a sequence of rigid transformations mapping one to the other."
- Students may initially resist the idea that a reflected figure is congruent to the original because it is "flipped." Emphasize that congruent does not require same orientation — just same shape and same size. Use the scissors analogy: if you can cut one out and place it on the other (with flipping allowed), they are congruent.
- When finding sequences of transformations, encourage students to start simple: can a single transformation work? If not, try a translation to align one vertex, then a rotation or reflection to align the rest.
- The notation for corresponding parts (A <-> A', B <-> B') is important for high school geometry. Establish it now.
- Problem 8 in the Tier 3 section is intentionally complex. Accept any valid sequence of transformations that correctly maps the pre-image to the image. There is often more than one correct answer.
