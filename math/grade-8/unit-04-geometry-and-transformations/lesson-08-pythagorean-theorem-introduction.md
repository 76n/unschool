# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 4, Lesson 8

## Topic
Pythagorean Theorem Introduction

## Learning Objectives
- State the Pythagorean Theorem: in a right triangle, a^2 + b^2 = c^2, where c is the hypotenuse and a, b are the legs.
- Identify the hypotenuse and legs of a right triangle.
- Understand a visual proof of the Pythagorean Theorem using area.
- Use the theorem to find a missing side length in a right triangle.

## Prerequisites
- Identifying right triangles and right angles (Grade 7)
- Computing squares and square roots of perfect squares (Grade 8, Unit 1)
- Understanding of area of squares (Grade 6-7)
- Simplifying expressions involving exponents (Grade 8, Unit 1)

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper
- Ruler or straightedge
- Calculator (optional, for checking square roots)
- Colored pencils (for the area proof diagram)
- Index card or half-sheet for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 8.G.6 — Explain a proof of the Pythagorean Theorem and its converse; 8.G.7 — Apply the Pythagorean Theorem to determine unknown side lengths in right triangles in real-world and mathematical problems.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of squares, square roots, and right triangles.

1. Compute: 3^2 = ? (9). 5^2 = ? (25). 12^2 = ? (144).
2. Compute: sqrt(49) = ? (7). sqrt(100) = ? (10). sqrt(64) = ? (8).
3. A right triangle has a 90-degree angle. Which side is opposite the right angle — one of the shorter sides or the longest side? (The longest side.)
4. Does 9 + 16 = 25? (Yes.) Does 3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2? (Yes.) Interesting...

Say: "That pattern in Problem 4 is not a coincidence. Today we will learn one of the most famous theorems in all of mathematics."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Right triangle** — a triangle with exactly one 90-degree angle.
- **Hypotenuse** — the side of a right triangle opposite the right angle. It is always the longest side.
- **Leg** — either of the two sides of a right triangle that form the right angle.
- **Pythagorean Theorem** — in any right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c: a^2 + b^2 = c^2.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Identify parts of a right triangle.**

Say: "Before we state the theorem, let us make sure we know the vocabulary. Draw a right triangle. Mark the right angle with a small square. The side across from the right angle — the longest side — is called the hypotenuse. The two shorter sides that form the right angle are called the legs."

Say: "We label the legs a and b, and the hypotenuse c. It does not matter which leg is a and which is b, but c is ALWAYS the hypotenuse."

**Step 2 — State the Pythagorean Theorem.**

Write on the board in large letters: **a^2 + b^2 = c^2**

Say: "The Pythagorean Theorem says: in any right triangle, if you square the length of each leg and add those squares together, the result equals the square of the hypotenuse. This works for EVERY right triangle, no matter how big or small."

**Step 3 — Visual proof by area.**

Say: "Let me show you WHY this theorem is true using areas."

Describe the proof:

"Imagine a right triangle with legs a and b and hypotenuse c. Now build a square on each side of the triangle: a square with side a (area = a^2), a square with side b (area = b^2), and a square with side c (area = c^2)."

"Here is the key insight: arrange four copies of the same right triangle around a square with side c. The four triangles fit inside a larger square with side (a + b). The area of the large square is (a + b)^2. The area is also equal to the area of the four triangles plus the area of the inner square (side c)."

"Let us compute:"
- Large square area: (a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2
- Four triangles: 4 x (1/2 x a x b) = 2ab
- Inner square: c^2
- So: a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = 2ab + c^2
- Subtract 2ab from both sides: a^2 + b^2 = c^2

Say: "And there it is. The algebra confirms what the picture shows: the sum of the squares of the legs equals the square of the hypotenuse."

**Step 4 — Using the theorem to find a missing side.**

Say: "The Pythagorean Theorem lets us find a missing side when we know the other two."

**Finding the hypotenuse:** If the legs are 3 and 4:
- a^2 + b^2 = c^2
- 3^2 + 4^2 = c^2
- 9 + 16 = c^2
- 25 = c^2
- c = sqrt(25) = 5

**Finding a leg:** If the hypotenuse is 13 and one leg is 5:
- a^2 + b^2 = c^2
- 5^2 + b^2 = 13^2
- 25 + b^2 = 169
- b^2 = 144
- b = sqrt(144) = 12

Say: "Always start by identifying which side is the hypotenuse (the longest side, opposite the right angle). Then substitute into a^2 + b^2 = c^2 and solve."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** A right triangle has legs of length 6 and 8. Find the hypotenuse.
- 6^2 + 8^2 = c^2
- 36 + 64 = c^2
- 100 = c^2
- c = sqrt(100) = **10**

**Example B:** A right triangle has a hypotenuse of 17 and one leg of 15. Find the other leg.
- a^2 + 15^2 = 17^2
- a^2 + 225 = 289
- a^2 = 64
- a = sqrt(64) = **8**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity 1: Identifying Parts and Finding the Hypotenuse**

1. A right triangle has legs 5 and 12. Find the hypotenuse.
   - "Which value goes in for c?" (The unknown — c is what we are finding.)
   - 5^2 + 12^2 = c^2 -> 25 + 144 = c^2 -> 169 = c^2 -> c = **13**

2. A right triangle has legs 8 and 15. Find the hypotenuse.
   - 8^2 + 15^2 = c^2 -> 64 + 225 = c^2 -> 289 = c^2 -> c = **17**

3. A right triangle has legs 7 and 24. Find the hypotenuse.
   - 7^2 + 24^2 = c^2 -> 49 + 576 = c^2 -> 625 = c^2 -> c = **25**

**Activity 2: Finding a Missing Leg**

4. A right triangle has hypotenuse 10 and one leg 6. Find the other leg.
   - "Which value is c?" (10 — the hypotenuse.)
   - 6^2 + b^2 = 10^2 -> 36 + b^2 = 100 -> b^2 = 64 -> b = **8**

5. A right triangle has hypotenuse 15 and one leg 9. Find the other leg.
   - 9^2 + b^2 = 15^2 -> 81 + b^2 = 225 -> b^2 = 144 -> b = **12**

6. Ask: "Can the hypotenuse ever be shorter than one of the legs?" (No — the hypotenuse is always the longest side. If your answer for c is shorter than a or b, you have made an error.)

**Facilitator tip:** If the student mistakenly adds a leg and hypotenuse (writing a^2 + c^2 = b^2 or similar), always redirect them to start by labeling: "Which side is opposite the right angle? That is c. The other two are a and b. Now write a^2 + b^2 = c^2." This labeling step prevents most setup errors.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. A right triangle has legs 9 and 12. Find the hypotenuse.
2. A right triangle has hypotenuse 26 and one leg 10. Find the other leg.
3. A right triangle has legs 20 and 21. Find the hypotenuse.
4. A right triangle has hypotenuse 25 and one leg 7. Find the other leg.
5. Label the right triangle below: the sides are 5, 12, and 13. Which is the hypotenuse? Which are the legs?

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

6. A right triangle has legs of length 1 and 1. Find the exact length of the hypotenuse. (Leave your answer as a square root if needed.)
7. A right triangle has a hypotenuse of 10 and one leg of 5. Find the other leg. (The answer will not be a whole number — leave as a simplified square root.)
8. Which of these are valid side lengths for a right triangle? Test each using the Pythagorean Theorem.
   - (a) 6, 8, 10
   - (b) 5, 7, 9
   - (c) 9, 40, 41

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

9. A right triangle has legs of length a and 2a. Express the hypotenuse in terms of a.
10. The three sides of a right triangle are consecutive integers (three whole numbers in a row, like n, n+1, n+2). Find all three side lengths.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** State the Pythagorean Theorem in words and as an equation.

**Problem 2:** A right triangle has legs 9 and 40. Find the hypotenuse.

**Mastery = 2 out of 2 correct.**

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. 9^2 + 12^2 = c^2 -> 81 + 144 = c^2 -> 225 = c^2 -> c = **15**
2. 10^2 + b^2 = 26^2 -> 100 + b^2 = 676 -> b^2 = 576 -> b = **24**
3. 20^2 + 21^2 = c^2 -> 400 + 441 = c^2 -> 841 = c^2 -> c = **29**
4. 7^2 + b^2 = 25^2 -> 49 + b^2 = 625 -> b^2 = 576 -> b = **24**
5. Hypotenuse = **13** (the longest side, opposite the right angle). Legs = **5 and 12** (the two shorter sides that form the right angle).
6. 1^2 + 1^2 = c^2 -> 2 = c^2 -> c = **sqrt(2)** (approximately 1.414).
7. 5^2 + b^2 = 10^2 -> 25 + b^2 = 100 -> b^2 = 75 -> b = sqrt(75) = **5*sqrt(3)** (approximately 8.66).
8. (a) 6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2. **Yes, right triangle.** (b) 5^2 + 7^2 = 25 + 49 = 74. But 9^2 = 81. 74 ≠ 81. **Not a right triangle.** (c) 9^2 + 40^2 = 81 + 1600 = 1681 = 41^2. **Yes, right triangle.**
9. a^2 + (2a)^2 = c^2 -> a^2 + 4a^2 = c^2 -> 5a^2 = c^2 -> c = **a*sqrt(5)**.
10. Let the sides be n, n + 1, n + 2 with hypotenuse n + 2. Then n^2 + (n + 1)^2 = (n + 2)^2. Expand: n^2 + n^2 + 2n + 1 = n^2 + 4n + 4. Simplify: 2n^2 + 2n + 1 = n^2 + 4n + 4. So n^2 - 2n - 3 = 0. Factor: (n - 3)(n + 1) = 0. Since n must be positive: n = 3. Sides: **3, 4, 5**.

### Exit Ticket

1. In words: "In a right triangle, the sum of the squares of the two legs equals the square of the hypotenuse." Equation: **a^2 + b^2 = c^2**, where a and b are legs and c is the hypotenuse.
2. 9^2 + 40^2 = c^2 -> 81 + 1600 = c^2 -> 1681 = c^2 -> c = **41**.

## Teacher Notes

- The Pythagorean Theorem is arguably the most important single theorem students encounter before high school. Take time to build understanding — do not rush past the proof to get to computation.
- The area proof described in Step 3 is the most accessible visual proof. If possible, draw or display the diagram. Many students find the visual argument compelling and memorable.
- The most common errors: (1) Adding a leg and the hypotenuse instead of the two legs (writing a^2 + c^2 = b^2). Always start by labeling c. (2) Forgetting to take the square root at the end (writing c^2 = 25 and then saying c = 25 instead of c = 5). (3) Using the formula as a + b = c instead of a^2 + b^2 = c^2.
- Perfect-square answers build confidence. The first several problems use Pythagorean triples (3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25, 9-40-41) so students get whole-number answers. Non-perfect-square answers are introduced in Tier 2 to prepare for Lesson 9.
- Students should recognize common Pythagorean triples: (3, 4, 5) and its multiples (6, 8, 10), (9, 12, 15), etc.; (5, 12, 13); (8, 15, 17); (7, 24, 25). These appear frequently in problems.
- The Challenge problem about consecutive integers is a beautiful application that connects geometry to algebra. It shows that (3, 4, 5) is the only right triangle with consecutive integer sides — a satisfying and surprising result.
