# Facilitator Guide — Grade 8 Math, Unit 3: Functions

**Grade:** 8
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 3 — Functions
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided (facilitator leads instruction; student works independently for practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit introduces the concept of a function and develops the student's ability to work with functions in multiple representations. The unit begins by defining a function as a rule that assigns exactly one output to each input, using input/output tables, mapping diagrams, and the vertical line test. Students then learn function notation, f(x), and practice evaluating functions. The middle lessons focus on linear functions: identifying them from tables and graphs, understanding slope as a rate of change, and mastering the slope-intercept form y = mx + b. Students learn to write equations from graphs, tables, and pairs of points. The unit concludes with comparing functions represented in different ways, an introduction to nonlinear functions, and a comprehensive review with real-world applications.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Define a function and determine whether a relation is a function using tables, mapping diagrams, and the vertical line test
- Use function notation f(x) to evaluate functions and interpret inputs and outputs
- Represent functions using tables, graphs, equations, and verbal descriptions, and translate between these representations
- Identify linear functions by recognizing a constant rate of change in tables and a straight-line graph
- Calculate slope from two points, a table, or a graph, and interpret slope as a rate of change
- Write and graph linear equations in slope-intercept form y = mx + b
- Write linear equations given a graph, a table, or two points
- Compare properties of two functions represented in different ways (e.g., one as a table and one as a graph)
- Distinguish between linear and nonlinear functions and describe qualitative features of a graph (increasing, decreasing, linear, nonlinear)

**Why this matters:** Functions are the central organizing concept of algebra and all higher mathematics. Every equation the student solved in Unit 2 can be reframed as finding where a function takes a particular value. Function thinking — viewing inputs, outputs, and the rule connecting them — is essential for modeling real-world situations in science, economics, engineering, and everyday life. This unit provides the conceptual bridge from equation-solving to the broader world of mathematical modeling.

| 4 | Linear Functions | 1 |

| 9 | Nonlinear Functions | 1 |
| 10 | Review and Problem Solving | 1-2 |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 1 is foundational and conceptual. If the student grasps the definition quickly, one session is sufficient. If the student struggles with distinguishing functions from non-functions, allow extra time with mapping diagrams before moving on.
- Lessons 5-7 (slope, slope-intercept form, writing equations) form the densest cluster in the unit. Plan for up to two sessions each. Slope is a concept that students often understand procedurally but not conceptually — take time to build meaning before drilling calculation.
- Lesson 3 (representing functions) can be expanded to two sessions if the student needs more practice translating between representations. This skill is foundational for Lesson 8 (comparing functions).
- Lesson 8 (comparing functions) is conceptually demanding because it requires fluency with all representations. If needed, provide the representations and ask comparison questions rather than having the student generate all representations from scratch.
- Lesson 9 (nonlinear functions) is an introduction, not a mastery lesson. One session is typically sufficient. The goal is recognition and qualitative description, not computation.
- Lesson 10 (review) may require two sessions if the student needs additional practice on specific skills before the unit assessment.

## Differentiation Strategies

### If the Student Needs More Support
- **Mapping diagrams first:** In Lesson 1, spend extra time with mapping diagrams before introducing the vertical line test. Physical objects (e.g., linking input cards to output cards with string) make the one-to-one mapping concrete.
- **Color-coded representations:** When translating between tables, graphs, and equations (Lessons 3-4), use a consistent color scheme: one color for inputs (x-values), another for outputs (y-values). This visual cue helps students track which values go where.
- **Slope as movement:** In Lesson 5, use the "walk the line" metaphor. Starting at a point on the graph, the slope tells you how to walk: rise (up or down) then run (right). Physically tracing the movement with a finger reinforces the concept.
- **Structured equation writing:** In Lesson 7, provide a step-by-step template: (1) find the slope, (2) write y = mx + b with the slope filled in, (3) substitute a known point, (4) solve for b, (5) write the final equation.
- **Reduce cognitive load in comparisons:** In Lesson 8, provide both functions already converted to the same representation (e.g., both as tables) so the student can focus on the comparison skill rather than the conversion skill.

### If the Student Needs More Challenge
- **Function composition preview:** After Lesson 2, introduce the idea of composing two functions: if f(x) = 2x + 1 and g(x) = x - 3, what is f(g(4))?
- **Slope from equations not in slope-intercept form:** In Lessons 5-6, present equations in standard form (e.g., 3x + 2y = 12) and ask the student to determine the slope without graphing.
- **Three-representation comparison:** In Lesson 8, give one function as a table, a second as a graph, and a third as an equation. Ask the student to rank all three by rate of change.
- **Real-world nonlinear modeling:** In Lesson 9, present a real dataset (e.g., population growth, ball height over time) and ask the student to argue whether a linear or nonlinear model is more appropriate.
- **Create-your-own problems:** Throughout the unit, ask the student to create real-world scenarios that produce functions with specific properties (e.g., "Create a scenario with a negative slope and a y-intercept of 200").

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student says a table with repeated outputs is "not a function" | Confuses "each input has one output" with "each output has one input." A function CAN have two inputs that give the same output. | Clarify: the rule is about inputs, not outputs. The input 3 and the input 5 can both produce the output 7. What is NOT allowed is the input 3 producing BOTH 7 and 12. |
| Student writes f(3) = 3f or treats f(x) as f times x | Does not understand function notation — reads f(x) as multiplication | Explicitly state: f(x) does NOT mean f multiplied by x. The parentheses here mean "the function f evaluated at the input x." Compare to how we read "Room 204" — the number 204 identifies the room, not a multiplication. |
| Student calculates slope as run/rise instead of rise/run | Reverses the formula | Reinforce: slope = rise/run = change in y / change in x. The y-change (vertical) is always on top. Use the mnemonic: "rise is up, so it goes on top of the fraction." |
| Student writes slope as a positive number when the line goes down | Does not track signs in the slope formula | Emphasize: when a line goes down from left to right, the slope is negative. In the formula, if y2 < y1 (you go down), the numerator is negative. Always subtract in the SAME order: (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1). |
| Student confuses slope and y-intercept in y = mx + b | Does not remember which letter represents which | Consistent verbal reinforcement: m = slope (the multiplier of x, the "movement"), b = y-intercept (where the line "begins" on the y-axis). Practice identifying both from at least 10 equations before graphing. |
| Student reads the y-intercept as a point but writes just the number | Writes "the y-intercept is 3" instead of (0, 3) | Clarify: the y-intercept is a point on the y-axis. Its coordinates are always (0, b). When graphing, plot it as a point at (0, b), not just anywhere along y = 3. |
| Student graphs a line by plotting only one point and guessing the direction | Does not use slope to find a second point | Require at least two plotted points (ideally three) for every graphed line. Demonstrate: start at the y-intercept, use the slope to find the next point, verify with a third point. |
| Student cannot compare functions in different representations | Overwhelmed by needing to convert or interpret multiple forms simultaneously | Break the comparison into steps: (1) find the slope/rate of change of each function, (2) find the y-intercept/initial value of each function, (3) answer the comparison question using those values. |

## Assessment Guidance

### Formative Assessment (During the Unit)
- Each lesson includes an Assessment Check (Exit Ticket-style). Use these to gauge daily understanding.
- During Guided Practice, observe whether the student can set up each step independently or needs prompting.
- Key transition points to watch: Lesson 1 to 2 (concept of function to notation), Lesson 4 to 5 (recognizing linear functions to computing slope), and Lesson 6 to 7 (reading equations to writing equations). These transitions increase in abstraction.

### Summative Assessment (End of Unit)
The unit assessment contains 5 sections totaling 50 points:
- Section 1: Understanding Functions (10 points) — identifying functions, domain and range
- Section 2: Linear Functions and Slope (12 points) — calculating slope, identifying linear vs. nonlinear
- Section 3: Slope-Intercept Form (12 points) — graphing, writing equations from slope-intercept form
- Section 4: Comparing and Analyzing Functions (10 points) — comparing functions in different representations
- Section 5: Word Problems (6 points) — applying functions to real-world contexts

**Mastery threshold:** 40 of 50 points (80%) AND at least 50% in each section.

### What to Do If the Student Does Not Meet Mastery
- Identify the weakest section(s) and reteach using the section-specific guidance in the unit assessment file.
- Reassess the weak section(s) with parallel problems before moving to Unit 4.
- If the student scores below 50% across three or more sections, revisit prerequisite skills (plotting points, evaluating expressions, solving equations) before reteaching.

## Conversation Starters

- "If a vending machine gives you a snack when you press a button, is that a function? What would make it NOT a function?"
- "Can two different inputs to a function give the same output? Can one input give two different outputs? Why does the difference matter?"
- "If a car is driving at 60 miles per hour, what does the slope of the distance-time graph look like? What would it mean if the slope changed?"
- "When you see y = 3x + 5, what story could you tell about what the 3 and the 5 mean in real life?"
- "If I show you a table and a graph of two different functions, how can you tell which one is growing faster without converting them to the same form?"

---

## When to Seek Additional Help

Consider seeking additional support if:

- The student cannot plot ordered pairs on a coordinate plane — this is required for Lessons 3-9 and the unit assessment
- The student cannot evaluate simple expressions like 2(5) + 3 — function evaluation in Lesson 2 will be inaccessible
- The student does not understand what "equals" means in an equation, or cannot solve basic one-step equations — the slope formula and equation writing in Lessons 5-7 require this skill
- The student cannot distinguish between positive and negative numbers on a number line — slope work requires signed arithmetic
- After reteaching, the student scores below 50% on the unit assessment in more than two sections
