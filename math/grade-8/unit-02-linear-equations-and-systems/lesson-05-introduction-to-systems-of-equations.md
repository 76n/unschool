# Math — Grade 8 — Unit 2, Lesson 5

## Topic
Introduction to Systems of Equations

## Learning Objectives
- Define a system of equations as two or more equations with the same variables.
- Understand that a solution to a system is an ordered pair (x, y) that satisfies both equations simultaneously.
- Determine whether a given ordered pair is a solution to a system by substituting into both equations.
- Interpret systems of equations in real-world contexts.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to evaluate expressions by substituting values for variables
- Familiarity with ordered pairs and the coordinate plane (Grade 6-7)
- Ability to solve one-variable equations (Lessons 1-4)
- Understanding of linear equations in two variables (e.g., y = 2x + 3)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Graph paper and ruler.
- Coordinate plane handout (pre-printed, axes labeled from -10 to 10).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 8.EE.8 — Analyze and solve pairs of simultaneous linear equations. 8.EE.8a — Understand that solutions to a system of two linear equations in two variables correspond to points of intersection of their graphs, because points of intersection satisfy both equations simultaneously.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review substituting values into equations with two variables and activate thinking about when two conditions can be true at the same time.

1. If y = 3x + 1, find y when x = 2. (y = 7)
2. If y = -x + 9, find y when x = 2. (y = 7)
3. Ask: "Interesting — both equations give y = 7 when x = 2. What does the ordered pair (2, 7) represent for these two equations?"
4. Ask: "Could you find another ordered pair that works in BOTH equations at the same time?"

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **System of equations** — a set of two or more equations that share the same variables. We are looking for values that make ALL the equations true at the same time.
- **Solution to a system** — an ordered pair (x, y) that satisfies every equation in the system. When substituted into each equation, both sides are equal.
- **Simultaneous equations** — another name for a system of equations, emphasizing that the equations must be satisfied at the same time.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Introduce the concept with a story.**
Say: "Imagine you and a friend each have a different piggy bank. You start with $10 and save $5 per week. Your friend starts with $25 and saves $2 per week. When will you both have the same amount of money?"

Write the two equations:
- Your savings: y = 5x + 10
- Friend's savings: y = 2x + 25

Say: "These two equations together form a SYSTEM of equations. We want to find the week (x) and the dollar amount (y) where both equations are true — where you both have the same savings."

**Step 2 — Define the solution to a system.**
Say: "A solution to a system of equations is an ordered pair (x, y) that makes BOTH equations true simultaneously. Not just one — both."

Write on the board: "To check if (x, y) is a solution, substitute into BOTH equations. If both check out, it is a solution. If even one fails, it is NOT a solution."

**Step 3 — Demonstrate checking a point.**
System:
- y = 3x + 1
- y = -x + 9

Check (2, 7):
- Equation 1: 7 = 3(2) + 1 = 7. True.
- Equation 2: 7 = -(2) + 9 = 7. True.
- Since (2, 7) satisfies BOTH equations, it IS a solution to the system.

Check (1, 4):
- Equation 1: 4 = 3(1) + 1 = 4. True.
- Equation 2: 4 = -(1) + 9 = 8. False (4 does not equal 8).
- Since (1, 4) does NOT satisfy both equations, it is NOT a solution.

Say: "Notice that (1, 4) works in the first equation but not the second. To be a solution to the SYSTEM, it must work in both."

**Step 4 — Return to the savings story.**
System:
- y = 5x + 10
- y = 2x + 25

Check (5, 35):
- Equation 1: 35 = 5(5) + 10 = 35. True.
- Equation 2: 35 = 2(5) + 25 = 35. True.
- (5, 35) is the solution. After 5 weeks, both people have $35.

**Step 5 — How many solutions can a system have?**
Say: "Most systems of two linear equations have exactly one solution — one point where the two lines cross. But just like with single equations, there are special cases."
- **One solution:** The two lines intersect at exactly one point. (Most common.)
- **No solution:** The two lines are parallel and never intersect.
- **Infinitely many solutions:** The two equations describe the same line.

Say: "We will explore solving systems in detail over the next several lessons. For now, focus on understanding what a system is and how to check if a point is a solution."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Checking a solution (true)**
System: x + y = 10 and x - y = 4.
Is (7, 3) a solution?
- Equation 1: 7 + 3 = 10. True.
- Equation 2: 7 - 3 = 4. True.
- Yes, (7, 3) is a solution to this system.

**Example B — Checking a solution (false)**
System: 2x + y = 11 and x - y = 1.
Is (3, 5) a solution?
- Equation 1: 2(3) + 5 = 11. True.
- Equation 2: 3 - 5 = -2. False (-2 does not equal 1).
- No, (3, 5) is NOT a solution.

**Example C — Finding the solution by testing**
System: y = 2x - 1 and y = -x + 8.
Test (3, 5):
- Equation 1: 5 = 2(3) - 1 = 5. True.
- Equation 2: 5 = -(3) + 8 = 5. True.
- (3, 5) is the solution.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. The student attempts each problem first, then discusses.

1. System: y = x + 4 and y = 2x + 1. Is (3, 7) a solution?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Substitute x = 3 and y = 7 into both equations. Do both check out?"

2. System: x + y = 8 and 2x - y = 1. Is (3, 5) a solution?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Check both equations. Remember, both must be true."

3. System: y = 4x - 3 and y = x + 9. Is (4, 13) a solution?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Substitute into the first equation, then the second."

4. System: 3x + y = 14 and x - y = 2. Find the solution by testing (4, 2).
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "If it works, great. If not, what adjustment would you try?"

5. Write a real-world situation that could be modeled by a system of two equations. (Example: Two runners start at different positions and run at different speeds. When do they meet?)
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Think of two things that change at different rates. When do they become equal?"

**Facilitator tip:** Students sometimes check only one equation and declare the point a solution. Consistently reinforce: "Did you check the OTHER equation too?" Make this a habit before moving to solving methods.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — Checking Solutions
Determine whether the given ordered pair is a solution to the system.

1. System: y = 3x - 2 and y = x + 4. Is (3, 7) a solution?
2. System: x + y = 12 and x - y = 2. Is (7, 5) a solution?
3. System: 2x + 3y = 19 and x - y = 3. Is (4, 1) a solution?
4. System: y = -2x + 10 and y = x + 1. Is (3, 4) a solution?

### Part B — Finding Solutions by Testing
For each system, test the given points to find which one is the solution.

5. System: y = 2x + 1 and y = -x + 7. Test: (1, 3), (2, 5), (3, 4).
6. System: x + y = 9 and 2x - y = 0. Test: (3, 6), (4, 5), (2, 7).

### Part C — Reasoning and Application

7. System: y = 5x and y = 3x + 8. Without solving algebraically, can you figure out the solution by testing x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5? Show your work.
8. A gym charges $30 per month with no sign-up fee. A second gym charges $20 per month with a $50 sign-up fee. Write a system of equations for the total cost y after x months at each gym. Find the number of months where both gyms cost the same by testing values.
9. Explain in your own words: Why must a solution to a system satisfy BOTH equations, not just one?
10. True or false: The ordered pair (0, 0) is a solution to every system of equations. Explain.

### Answer Key

**Part A**
1. Eq 1: 7 = 3(3) - 2 = 7. True. Eq 2: 7 = 3 + 4 = 7. True. **Yes, (3, 7) is a solution.**
2. Eq 1: 7 + 5 = 12. True. Eq 2: 7 - 5 = 2. True. **Yes, (7, 5) is a solution.**
3. Eq 1: 2(4) + 3(1) = 11. False (11 does not equal 19). **No, (4, 1) is NOT a solution.** (No need to check Eq 2.)
4. Eq 1: 4 = -2(3) + 10 = 4. True. Eq 2: 4 = 3 + 1 = 4. True. **Yes, (3, 4) is a solution.**

**Part B**
5. Test (2, 5): Eq 1: 5 = 2(2) + 1 = 5. True. Eq 2: 5 = -(2) + 7 = 5. True. **(2, 5) is the solution.** (The other points fail at least one equation.)
6. Test (3, 6): Eq 1: 3 + 6 = 9. True. Eq 2: 2(3) - 6 = 0. True. **(3, 6) is the solution.**

**Part C**
7. Testing values: x = 1: y = 5, y = 11 (not equal). x = 2: y = 10, y = 14 (not equal). x = 3: y = 15, y = 17 (not equal). x = 4: y = 20, y = 20 (equal!). **The solution is (4, 20).**
8. Gym 1: y = 30x. Gym 2: y = 20x + 50. Test x = 5: 150 vs 150. Equal. **After 5 months, both gyms cost $150.** The solution is (5, 150).
9. Answers will vary. A system represents two conditions that must both be true at the same time. A point that satisfies only one equation meets only one condition. We need a point that meets BOTH conditions simultaneously.
10. False. (0, 0) is only a solution if substituting x = 0 and y = 0 makes both equations true. For example, in the system y = x + 1 and y = 2x + 3, substituting gives 0 = 1 (false). So (0, 0) does not work for every system.

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket
**Problem 1:** Is (5, 2) a solution to the system x + y = 7 and 2x - y = 8? Show your work.
**Problem 2:** Is (1, 6) a solution to the system y = 4x + 2 and y = -x + 7? Show your work.

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct (with work shown for both equations) = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

**Exit Ticket Answer Key:**
1. Eq 1: 5 + 2 = 7. True. Eq 2: 2(5) - 2 = 8. True. **Yes, (5, 2) is a solution.**
2. Eq 1: 6 = 4(1) + 2 = 6. True. Eq 2: 6 = -(1) + 7 = 6. True. **Yes, (1, 6) is a solution.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review substitution into single equations first. Make sure the student can evaluate y = 3x + 1 when x = 4 confidently.
- Use a T-chart approach: make a table with columns for x, y, Left Side of Eq 1, Right Side of Eq 1, Left Side of Eq 2, Right Side of Eq 2, and "Both True?"
- Provide three additional systems with scaffolded checking (the first substitution is done; the student completes the second).

### If Mastery Is Met
- Challenge: "Given the system y = 2x + 1 and y = -x + 10, can you find the solution WITHOUT being given points to test? What strategy could you use?" (Set the two expressions for y equal to each other: 2x + 1 = -x + 10 — preview of substitution method.)
- Ask: "Could a system of two equations have exactly two solutions? Why or why not?" (Not for linear equations — two lines can intersect at most once.)

## Extension

- **Graphing Preview:** Plot both equations from the Warm-Up (y = 3x + 1 and y = -x + 9) on graph paper. Where do the lines cross? Does the intersection match the solution you found algebraically?
- **Three Equations:** Can a system have three equations and two variables? What would that look like? Create an example and find a point that satisfies all three. (This is possible if all three lines pass through the same point.)
