# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 8

## Topic

Multiplying 2-Digit x 2-Digit: Partial Products

## Learning Objectives

- Write the four partial products of a two-digit by two-digit multiplication problem as a vertical list.
- Add the partial products to find the total product.
- Connect the partial-products method to the area model from Lesson 7.
- Use estimation to verify that the final answer is reasonable.

## Prerequisites

- Place-value decomposition of two-digit numbers (tens and ones).
- Multiplying a two-digit number by a one-digit number (Lessons 3-4).
- The area model for two-digit by two-digit multiplication (Lesson 7).
- Estimating products by rounding (Lesson 6).

## Materials Needed

- Whiteboard and markers (teacher).
- Personal whiteboards or lined paper (students).
- Partial-Products Recording Sheet (one per student, optional).
- Area-model reference poster from Lesson 7 (displayed).
- Colored pencils (optional, for color-coding partial products).

**Standards:** 4.NBT.5

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Bridge from the area model to the written partial-products method by reviewing decomposition.

1. "Use the area model to find 14 x 12. What are the four partial products?" (100, 20, 40, 8)
2. "Now, instead of drawing a rectangle, I want you to write those four partial products as a list, one beneath the other, and add them up." (100 + 20 + 40 + 8 = 168)
3. "Today we will learn to write partial products without drawing the rectangle every time."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary

- **Partial product** — the result of multiplying part of one factor by part of the other factor.
- **Decompose** — to break a number into parts by place value.
- **Sum** — the result of adding numbers together.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Set up the problem.**
Write on the board:
```
    23
  x 15
  ----
```
- "We are going to find 23 x 15 by listing every partial product."

**Step 2 — Decompose both factors.**
- "23 = 20 + 3 and 15 = 10 + 5."
- "We need to multiply every part of 23 by every part of 15."

**Step 3 — List the partial products.**
Write them one at a time:
```
    23
  x 15
  ----
   200    (20 x 10)
   100    (20 x 5)
    30    (3 x 10)
  + 15    (3 x 5)
  ----
   345
```
- "20 x 10 = 200. Write 200."
- "20 x 5 = 100. Write 100 below."
- "3 x 10 = 30. Write 30 below."
- "3 x 5 = 15. Write 15 below."
- "Now add all four: 200 + 100 + 30 + 15 = 345."

**Step 4 — Connect to the area model.**
- Point to the Lesson 7 area-model poster: "Notice these are the exact same four numbers that appeared in the four sections of our rectangle. The partial-products method is the area model written as a list!"

**Step 5 — Estimate to check.**
- "23 rounds to 20, 15 rounds to 20. 20 x 20 = 400. Our answer of 345 is close — reasonable!"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** 14 x 12
```
    14
  x 12
  ----
   100    (10 x 10)
    20    (10 x 2)
    40    (4 x 10)
  +  8    (4 x 2)
  ----
   168
```

**Example B:** 36 x 21
```
    36
  x 21
  ----
   600    (30 x 20)
    30    (30 x 1)
   120    (6 x 20)
  +  6    (6 x 1)
  ----
   756
```

**Example C:** 25 x 18
```
    25
  x 18
  ----
   200    (20 x 10)
   160    (20 x 8)
    50    (5 x 10)
  + 40    (5 x 8)
  ----
   450
```

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Students solve on whiteboards using the partial-products method. Compare answers as a class after each problem.

1. Find 22 x 13 using partial products.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Decompose each factor first. Write all four partial products in a list, then add."

2. Find 31 x 24 using partial products.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is 30 x 20? What is 30 x 4? Keep going — you have four partial products."

3. Find 17 x 26 using partial products. Then estimate to check.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "After you find your answer, round each factor and multiply. Is your exact answer close to your estimate?"

4. Deshawn listed these partial products for 28 x 13: 200, 80, 30, 24. Is he correct?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What are the parts of 28? What are the parts of 13? Multiply each combination and compare to Deshawn's list."

2. Use partial products to solve 32 x 21.
3. Use partial products to solve 19 x 15.

### Stretch

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** Use partial products to find 27 x 14. Show all four partial products and the sum.

**Problem 2:** How is the partial-products method the same as the area model? How is it different? Write one or two sentences.

**Scoring:**
- Problem 1: All four partial products correct AND sum correct = Mastery of computation.
- Problem 2: Identifies that both methods use the same four partial products, but the area model uses a rectangle while partial products uses a list = Mastery of understanding.
- Both mastery = Full mastery.
- One of two = Approaching.
- Neither = Not yet.

### If Mastery Is Not Met

- Have the student draw the area model side-by-side with the partial-products list for the same problem, matching each section of the rectangle to a line in the list.
- Provide a structured template where the decomposition is pre-filled and the student only needs to compute each multiplication.
- Practice two simpler problems (e.g., 12 x 11, 13 x 11) during the next warm-up with direct support.

### If Mastery Is Met

- Student moves to Extension activity.
- Student may try a three-digit by one-digit problem written as partial products (e.g., 213 x 4 = 800 + 40 + 12 = 852).

## Extension (Optional)

**Partial-Products Puzzles:** Give students partial-products lists with one blank and ask them to figure out the missing partial product and the total.

- **Puzzle 1:** Partial products for a mystery problem are 400, 80, 60, and ___. The problem is 23 x 24. What is the missing partial product, and what is the total?
  - 23 x 24: 20x20=400, 20x4=80, 3x20=60, 3x4=12. Missing product: 12. Total: 400+80+60+12 = 552.

- **Puzzle 2:** Partial products are 600, ___, 120, 6. What is the missing partial product? What two-digit numbers are being multiplied?
  - The problem is 36 x 21: 30x20=600, 30x1=30, 6x20=120, 6x1=6. Missing product: 30. Total: 756.

Students can also create their own puzzles for classmates to solve.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors

- **Missing a partial product.** Students may write only two or three partial products instead of four. Encourage a systematic approach: "tens x tens, tens x ones, ones x tens, ones x ones" — always four steps.
- **Place-value mistakes.** Students write 2 x 1 = 2 instead of 20 x 10 = 200. Stress that the digit 2 in 23 represents 20, not 2. Have them write the expanded form (20 + 3) alongside the problem.
- **Addition errors.** When adding four numbers, students sometimes skip a number or miscalculate. Encourage them to add in pairs: first add the two largest, then add the two smallest, then combine.
- **Confusing the order of operations.** Some students try to add before they have all four partial products. Remind them: "Multiply first to get all four parts, then add."

### Pacing Advice

- Students who mastered the area model in Lesson 7 will transition quickly. Spend more time on the connection between the two methods rather than re-teaching decomposition.
- Guided Practice problem 4 (Deshawn's error) is the most important discussion problem — do not skip it.
- If students finish Independent Practice early, have them verify their answers by drawing the area model and comparing.
- The Extension puzzle activity may take longer than expected. It is best used as a homework challenge or a station activity rather than a timed in-class task.

4. Deshawn listed 200, 80, 30, 24 for 28 x 13. Check: 20x10=200 (correct), 20x3=60 (Deshawn wrote 80 — incorrect), 8x10=80 (Deshawn wrote 30 — incorrect), 8x3=24 (correct). Deshawn swapped the middle two partial products. The values should be 200, 60, 80, 24. The sum is still 200+60+80+24 = 364 either way (addition is commutative), so his total would be correct if he added properly. However, his labels for which multiplication produced each number are swapped. Correct total: 364.
