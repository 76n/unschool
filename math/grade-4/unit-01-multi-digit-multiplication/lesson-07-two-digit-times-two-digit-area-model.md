# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 1, Lesson 7

## Topic

Multiplying 2-Digit x 2-Digit: Area Model

## Learning Objectives

- Decompose each factor into tens and ones to set up a four-part area model.
- Calculate and label each partial area inside the model.
- Add the four partial products to find the total product.
- Explain how the area model represents the distributive property.

## Prerequisites

- Place-value understanding: tens and ones (Grade 3).
- Multiplying a two-digit number by a one-digit number (Lessons 3-4).
- Estimating products by rounding (Lesson 6).
- Fluency with basic multiplication facts through 9 x 9.

## Materials Needed

- Grid/graph paper (1-cm squares, one sheet per student).
- Colored pencils or crayons (at least 2 colors per student).
- Whiteboard and markers (teacher).
- Blank Area-Model Recording Sheet (one per student).
- Scissors (optional, for cutting out rectangles).

**Standards:** 4.NBT.5

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review place-value decomposition and build toward the area model by connecting multiplication to area.

1. "Break apart these numbers into tens and ones."
   - 23 = 20 + 3
   - 15 = 10 + 5
   - 47 = 40 + 7
2. Quick review: "What is the area of a rectangle that is 20 units long and 10 units wide?" (200 square units)
3. "What is the area of a rectangle that is 3 units long and 5 units wide?" (15 square units)

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary

- **Area model** — a rectangle divided into smaller sections to show partial products.
- **Decompose** — to break a number into parts (e.g., 23 = 20 + 3).
- **Partial product** — the result of multiplying one part of a factor by one part of the other factor.
- **Distributive property** — multiplying a sum by a number is the same as multiplying each addend separately and then adding the results.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Pose the problem and estimate first.**
Write on the board: 23 x 15.
- "Before we find the exact answer, let's estimate. 23 rounds to 20 and 15 rounds to 20. 20 x 20 = 400. Another estimate: 20 x 15 = 300. So we expect an answer somewhere around 300 to 400."

**Step 2 — Draw the area model.**
- Draw a large rectangle on the board.
- Label one side with the decomposition of 23: split into 20 and 3.
- Label the other side with the decomposition of 15: split into 10 and 5.
- "We now have four smaller rectangles inside our big rectangle."

**Step 3 — Fill in each section.**
- Top-left: 20 x 10 = 200
- Top-right: 20 x 5 = 100
- Bottom-left: 3 x 10 = 30
- Bottom-right: 3 x 5 = 15
- "Each small rectangle is one partial product."

**Step 4 — Add the partial products.**
- 200 + 100 + 30 + 15 = 345
- "So 23 x 15 = 345. Is that close to our estimate of 300-400? Yes!"

**Step 5 — Connect to the distributive property.**
- "We are really computing (20 + 3) x (10 + 5). The area model shows every combination of parts multiplied together."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** 14 x 12

|       | 10  | 2   |
|-------|-----|-----|
| **10**| 100 | 20  |
| **4** | 40  | 8   |

Partial products: 100 + 20 + 40 + 8 = 168.

**Example B:** 36 x 21

|       | 20  | 1   |
|-------|-----|-----|
| **30**| 600 | 30  |
| **6** | 120 | 6   |

Partial products: 600 + 30 + 120 + 6 = 756.

**Example C:** 25 x 18

|       | 10  | 8   |
|-------|-----|-----|
| **20**| 200 | 160 |
| **5** | 50  | 40  |

Partial products: 200 + 160 + 50 + 40 = 450.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Students draw area models on grid paper or recording sheets. Work each problem, then compare as a class.

1. Use an area model to find 22 x 13.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "How do you decompose 22? How do you decompose 13? Draw your rectangle and label the sides."

2. Use an area model to find 31 x 24.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "How many sections will your rectangle have? Fill in each section and add them up."

3. Use an area model to find 16 x 35.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Before you start, estimate the product. After you finish, check — is your answer close to your estimate?"

4. Kayla drew an area model for 27 x 14 and got four partial products: 200, 70, 80, 28. She says the answer is 368. Is she right? Explain what went wrong if not.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Check each partial product. What is 20 x 10? What is 20 x 4? What is 7 x 10? What is 7 x 4?"

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### On-level

1. Draw an area model to solve 24 x 13.
2. Draw an area model to solve 32 x 21.
3. Draw an area model to solve 18 x 14.

### Stretch

4. Draw an area model to solve 45 x 23. Then check your answer with an estimate.
5. A school cafeteria has 26 tables with 15 seats each. Use an area model to find the total number of seats.

### Challenge

6. Without drawing the model, list the four partial products for 38 x 27, then find the total.
7. Marcus says 42 x 35 = 1,__(fill in the blank). He knows three of the four partial products are 1,200, 60, and 100. What is the missing partial product? What is the final answer?

### Interleaved Review

8. (From Lesson 5) Solve: 324 x 3 = ?
9. (From Lesson 6) Estimate 67 x 8 by rounding. Is the exact answer greater or less than your estimate?

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** Draw an area model to find 26 x 14. Show all four partial products and the total.

**Problem 2:** Explain in one or two sentences why the area model works for multiplication.

**Scoring:**
- Problem 1 correct with all four partial products shown and labeled = Mastery of computation.
- Problem 2 references breaking numbers into parts and multiplying each combination = Mastery of understanding.
- Both mastery = Full mastery.
- One of two = Approaching.
- Neither = Not yet.

### If Mastery Is Not Met

- Have the student use grid paper to physically shade a rectangle for a simpler problem such as 12 x 11, counting squares to verify the area matches the partial products.
- Provide a pre-drawn area-model template with the grid lines already in place so the student can focus on the multiplication rather than the drawing.
- Re-teach place-value decomposition if the student cannot break numbers into tens and ones.

### If Mastery Is Met

- Student moves to Extension activity.
- Student may attempt three-digit by one-digit area models (e.g., 123 x 4 as a three-section model) for enrichment.

## Extension (Optional)

**Design a Garden:** Students design a rectangular garden that is 17 meters by 24 meters on grid paper. They divide the garden into four sections using the area model, label each section with its dimensions and area, and calculate the total area. They can then "plant" different crops in each section and describe how much space each crop gets.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors

- **Incorrect decomposition.** A student writes 23 as 2 + 3 instead of 20 + 3. Reinforce that the tens digit represents groups of ten.
- **Missing a section.** Students sometimes compute only two or three of the four partial products. Encourage them to always draw the grid lines and systematically fill in every cell.
- **Addition errors when combining partial products.** Have students line up the partial products vertically to add them, using place-value columns.
- **Swapping dimensions.** The orientation of the rectangle does not matter (20 on top or on the side), but students should be consistent. Model a standard layout each time.

### Pacing Advice

- The grid paper drawing takes longer than expected for many students. Have templates ready for students who struggle with the drawing so they can focus on the math.
- If the class is moving quickly, have students try Example C during Direct Instruction on their own before you reveal the answer.
- Guided Practice problem 4 (Kayla's error) is valuable for deepening understanding — allocate at least 2 minutes for discussion.
- Save Challenge problems for early finishers or homework if time runs short.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice

1. 22 x 13: Decompose 22 = 20 + 2, 13 = 10 + 3. Partial products: 20x10=200, 20x3=60, 2x10=20, 2x3=6. Total: 200+60+20+6 = 286.
2. 31 x 24: Decompose 31 = 30 + 1, 24 = 20 + 4. Partial products: 30x20=600, 30x4=120, 1x20=20, 1x4=4. Total: 600+120+20+4 = 744.
3. 16 x 35: Decompose 16 = 10 + 6, 35 = 30 + 5. Partial products: 10x30=300, 10x5=50, 6x30=180, 6x5=30. Total: 300+50+180+30 = 560. Estimate: 20 x 40 = 800 or 20 x 35 = 700. The answer 560 is in a reasonable range.
4. Kayla's partial products should be: 20x10=200, 20x4=80, 7x10=70, 7x4=28. She swapped 70 and 80. The correct sum is 200+80+70+28 = 378, not 368. Kayla is incorrect.

### Independent Practice

1. 24 x 13: 20x10=200, 20x3=60, 4x10=40, 4x3=12. Total: 200+60+40+12 = 312.
2. 32 x 21: 30x20=600, 30x1=30, 2x20=40, 2x1=2. Total: 600+30+40+2 = 672.
3. 18 x 14: 10x10=100, 10x4=40, 8x10=80, 8x4=32. Total: 100+40+80+32 = 252.
4. 45 x 23: 40x20=800, 40x3=120, 5x20=100, 5x3=15. Total: 800+120+100+15 = 1,035. Estimate: 50 x 20 = 1,000. Reasonable.
5. 26 x 15: 20x10=200, 20x5=100, 6x10=60, 6x5=30. Total: 200+100+60+30 = 390 seats.
6. 38 x 27: 30x20=600, 30x7=210, 8x20=160, 8x7=56. Total: 600+210+160+56 = 1,026.
7. Marcus: 42 x 35. Partial products: 40x30=1,200, 40x5=200, 2x30=60, 2x5=10. The three he knows are 1,200, 60, and 100 — but 40x5=200, not 100, and 2x5=10. If his three known products are 1,200, 60, and 100, then one of those is incorrect. Re-interpreting: perhaps he has 1,200, 60, and 10, and is missing 200. Missing partial product: 200. Total: 1,200+200+60+10 = 1,470. So 42 x 35 = 1,470.

### Interleaved Review

8. 324 x 3 = 972.
9. 67 rounds to 70. 70 x 8 = 560. Exact: 67 x 8 = 536. The exact answer (536) is less than the estimate (560) because we rounded up.

### Exit Ticket

1. 26 x 14: Decompose 26 = 20 + 6, 14 = 10 + 4. Partial products: 20x10=200, 20x4=80, 6x10=60, 6x4=24. Total: 200+80+60+24 = 364.
2. Sample response: "The area model works because we break each number into tens and ones and multiply every part by every other part. Then we add all the pieces together to get the total, just like finding the area of a rectangle."
