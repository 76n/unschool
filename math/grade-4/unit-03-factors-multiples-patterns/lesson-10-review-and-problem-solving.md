# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 10

## Topic
Review and Problem Solving — Factors, Multiples, and Patterns

## Learning Objectives
- Review all key concepts from the unit: factors, factor pairs, prime numbers, composite numbers, multiples, GCF, LCM, and patterns
- Apply these concepts to solve real-world and mathematical problems
- Distinguish between factors and multiples in context
- Synthesize multiple concepts within a single problem

## Prerequisites
- All concepts from Lessons 1-9 of this unit
- Factor pairs and the rainbow method (Lessons 1-2)
- Prime and composite numbers (Lessons 3-4)
- Multiples and the factor-multiple relationship (Lesson 5)
- GCF and LCM (Lessons 6-7)
- Arithmetic patterns and pattern relationships (Lessons 8-9)

## Materials Needed
- Student dry-erase boards or scratch paper
- Pencil and paper
- Hundred chart (optional reference)
- All reference notes from the unit

**Standards:** 4.OA.4 — Find all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1-100. Recognize that a whole number is a multiple of each of its factors. Determine whether a given whole number in the range 1-100 is prime or composite. 4.OA.5 — Generate a number or shape pattern that follows a given rule.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Quick review of key vocabulary and concepts from the entire unit.

1. Rapid-fire review. Students hold up fingers or write answers on boards:
   - "A number that has exactly 2 factors is called ___." (prime)
   - "A number that has more than 2 factors is called ___." (composite)
   - "The number 1 is ___ prime nor composite." (neither)
   - "If 6 is a factor of 42, then 42 is a ___ of 6." (multiple)
   - "The largest factor shared by two numbers is called the ___." (GCF, or greatest common factor)
   - "The smallest positive multiple shared by two numbers is called the ___." (LCM, or least common multiple)
2. Say: "Today is our review day. We will practice everything from this unit and solve some challenging problems that combine multiple ideas."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Factor (review):** A number that divides evenly into another number.
- **Factor pair (review):** Two numbers that multiply to give a product.
- **Prime number (review):** A whole number greater than 1 with exactly two factors.
- **Composite number (review):** A whole number greater than 1 with more than two factors.
- **Multiple (review):** The product of a number and any whole number.
- **GCF (review):** Greatest common factor — the largest factor two numbers share.
- **LCM (review):** Least common multiple — the smallest positive multiple two numbers share.
- **Pattern rule (review):** The operation that takes you from one term to the next.

### Instruction Notes
**Step 1 — Concept map review.**
Say: "Let's build a concept map on the board that connects everything we have learned."

Draw connections on the board:
- FACTORS: go into a number. Found using division. Come in pairs.
- MULTIPLES: come out of a number. Found using multiplication. Go on forever.
- Connection: If A is a factor of B, then B is a multiple of A.
- PRIME: exactly 2 factors.
- COMPOSITE: more than 2 factors.
- GCF: biggest shared factor.
- LCM: smallest shared multiple.
- PATTERNS: follow a rule. Can be compared using corresponding terms.

**Step 2 — Common confusion review.**
Say: "Let's clear up the most common mistakes before the assessment."

Write on the board and discuss:
- "1 is NOT prime. It has only 1 factor."
- "2 IS prime. It is the only even prime."
- "Factors FIT IN. Multiples MOVE OUT."
- "To prove composite, find just ONE extra factor. To prove prime, check ALL possible factors."

**Step 3 — Strategy review for each problem type.**
Say: "Let me remind you of the strategies we use for each type of problem."

- Finding factor pairs: Use the rainbow method (T-chart). Start at 1, test each number, stop when the number exceeds the quotient.
- Prime or composite: Find factor pairs. 2 factors = prime. More = composite.
- Listing multiples: Skip count or multiply by 1, 2, 3, 4, ...
- GCF: List all factors of both numbers, find the biggest one in common.
- LCM: List multiples of both numbers until you find the first match.
- Pattern rules: Look at the difference (or ratio) between consecutive terms.
- Pattern relationships: Compare corresponding terms across two patterns.

### Worked Examples
**Example A:** Mixed concept problem.
The number 36: (a) List all factor pairs. (b) Is it prime or composite? (c) List the first 5 multiples. (d) Find the GCF of 36 and 24.

- (a) Factor pairs of 36: (1, 36), (2, 18), (3, 12), (4, 9), (6, 6). Factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.
- (b) 36 has 9 factors. More than 2, so it is composite.
- (c) First 5 multiples of 36: 36, 72, 108, 144, 180.
- (d) Factors of 36: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36. Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24. Common factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. GCF = 12.

**Example B:** Real-world GCF problem.
A florist has 48 red roses and 32 white roses. She wants to make identical bouquets using all the flowers. What is the greatest number of bouquets she can make? How many of each color will be in each bouquet?
- GCF of 48 and 32: Factors of 48: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48. Factors of 32: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Common: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16. GCF = 16.
- She can make 16 bouquets: 48/16 = 3 red roses and 32/16 = 2 white roses each.

**Example C:** Pattern problem with connection to factors.
Pattern: Start at 0, rule: add 6. Terms: 0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, ...
Are the terms of this pattern (other than 0) all multiples of 6? (Yes!) Are any of them prime? (No — all terms from 12 onward have at least 3 factors. The term 6 itself is composite: 1, 2, 3, 6.)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these mixed-concept problems as a class.

1. **Find all factor pairs of 56. Is 56 prime or composite?**
   Answer: Factor pairs: (1, 56), (2, 28), (4, 14), (7, 8). Factors: 1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 14, 28, 56. 56 is composite (8 factors).

2. **Find the GCF and LCM of 12 and 18.**
   Answer: Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18. GCF = 6. Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36. Multiples of 18: 18, 36. LCM = 36.

3. **What is the rule? 5, 14, 23, 32, 41, ... What are the next 2 terms?**
   Answer: Rule: add 9. Next terms: 50, 59.

4. **List all prime numbers between 20 and 40.**
   Answer: 23, 29, 31, 37.

5. **A swimming pool is open every 3 days. A library has story time every 4 days. If both happen today, in how many days will they both happen again?**
   Answer: LCM of 3 and 4 = 12 days.

**Facilitator tip:** Use this guided practice to identify which concepts students still struggle with. Note any areas that need additional review before the assessment.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
### Part A: Factors and Factor Pairs
1. Find all factor pairs of 42.
2. Find all factor pairs of 64.
3. How many factors does 50 have?

### Part B: Prime and Composite
4. Classify each number as prime, composite, or neither: 1, 17, 24, 47, 51, 63, 89.
5. List all prime numbers between 60 and 80.

### Part C: Multiples and GCF/LCM
6. Is 84 a multiple of 7? Explain.
7. Find the GCF of 20 and 35.
8. Find the LCM of 6 and 8.
9. Find the GCF and LCM of 15 and 20.

### Part D: Patterns
10. Identify the rule and write the next 3 terms: 7, 15, 23, 31, 39, ___, ___, ___
11. Generate the first 6 terms: Start at 0, rule: add 4. Then generate: Start at 0, rule: add 12. What is the relationship between corresponding terms?

### Part E: Word Problems
12. A teacher has 30 pencils and 42 erasers to put into student supply bags. Each bag must have the same number of pencils and the same number of erasers, with nothing left over. What is the most bags she can make? How many pencils and erasers per bag?

13. Two neon signs flash at different rates. Sign A flashes every 5 seconds. Sign B flashes every 8 seconds. If they both flash at the same time, how many seconds until they flash together again?

14. Derek says that 57 is prime because it is odd and does not end in 0 or 5. Is Derek correct? Show why or why not.

### Answer Key

**Part A:**
1. Factor pairs of 42: (1, 42), (2, 21), (3, 14), (6, 7). Factors: 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 14, 21, 42.
2. Factor pairs of 64: (1, 64), (2, 32), (4, 16), (8, 8). Factors: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64.
3. Factors of 50: 1, 2, 5, 10, 25, 50. That is 6 factors.

**Part B:**
4. 1: Neither. 17: Prime. 24: Composite (2 x 12 = 24). 47: Prime. 51: Composite (3 x 17 = 51). 63: Composite (7 x 9 = 63). 89: Prime.
5. Prime numbers between 60 and 80: 61, 67, 71, 73, 79.

**Part C:**
6. 84 / 7 = 12, no remainder. Yes, 84 is a multiple of 7 (7 x 12 = 84).
7. Factors of 20: 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20. Factors of 35: 1, 5, 7, 35. Common: 1, 5. GCF = 5.
8. Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24. Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24. LCM = 24.
9. Factors of 15: 1, 3, 5, 15. Factors of 20: 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20. GCF = 5. Multiples of 15: 15, 30, 45, 60. Multiples of 20: 20, 40, 60. LCM = 60.

**Part D:**
10. Rule: add 8. Next terms: 47, 55, 63.
11. Pattern A: 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20. Pattern B: 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60. Each term in Pattern B is 3 times the corresponding term in Pattern A (because 12/4 = 3).

**Part E:**
12. GCF of 30 and 42: Factors of 30: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30. Factors of 42: 1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 14, 21, 42. Common: 1, 2, 3, 6. GCF = 6. She can make 6 bags: 30/6 = 5 pencils and 42/6 = 7 erasers per bag.

13. LCM of 5 and 8: Multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40. Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40. LCM = 40. They will flash together again in 40 seconds.

14. Derek is NOT correct. 57 / 3 = 19, so 57 = 3 x 19. Since 57 has factors besides 1 and 57, it is composite, not prime. Being odd and not ending in 0 or 5 only tells us the number is not divisible by 2 or 5 — there are other factors to check!

## Assessment Check
1. Find all factor pairs of 40, classify it as prime or composite, and list the first 3 multiples.
2. Find the GCF and LCM of 8 and 12.

**Target response:**
1. Factor pairs of 40: (1, 40), (2, 20), (4, 10), (5, 8). 40 is composite (8 factors). First 3 multiples of 40: 40, 80, 120.
2. Factors of 8: 1, 2, 4, 8. Factors of 12: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Common: 1, 2, 4. GCF = 4. Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24. Multiples of 12: 12, 24. LCM = 24.

## Extension
- **Number mystery:** I am a two-digit composite number. My digits add up to 10. I am a multiple of 7. What number am I? (Answer: 28. Digits: 2+8=10. 28 = 4 x 7, composite. Another possibility: 91 has digits 9+1=10, and 91 = 7 x 13, composite. But 91 is not typically expected — 28 is the intended answer for a two-digit number with digit sum 10 that is a multiple of 7. Actually, 91 also works. Students who find both should be praised!)
- **Unit preview:** The next unit focuses on fractions. Understanding factors and multiples is crucial for finding common denominators and simplifying fractions. Think about how GCF helps you simplify and LCM helps you find common denominators!
