# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 1

## Topic
Understanding Factors

## Learning Objectives
- Define what a factor is and explain it in their own words
- Find factor pairs of a number by building arrays with tiles or drawings
- Write multiplication sentences to represent factor pairs
- Understand that every number has at least one factor pair (1 and itself)

## Prerequisites
- Fluency with single-digit multiplication facts
- Understanding of arrays from Grade 3
- Ability to determine if a division results in a whole number (no remainder)

## Materials Needed
- Color tiles or counters (at least 40 per student)
- Grid paper
- Student dry-erase boards or scratch paper
- Pencil and paper

**Standards:** 4.OA.4 — Find all factor pairs for a whole number in the range 1-100. Recognize that a whole number is a multiple of each of its factors.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of multiplication facts and arrays.

1. Display these multiplication facts. Students write products on their boards:
   - 3 x 4 = ? (12)
   - 2 x 6 = ? (12)
   - 1 x 12 = ? (12)
2. Ask: "What do you notice about all three of these problems?" (They all equal 12.)
3. Ask: "Can you think of any other multiplication fact that equals 12?" (4 x 3, 6 x 2, 12 x 1 — which are the same pairs reversed)

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Factor:** A number that divides evenly into another number. For example, 3 is a factor of 12 because 12 / 3 = 4 with no remainder.
- **Factor pair:** Two numbers that multiply together to make a given product. For example, 3 and 4 are a factor pair of 12.
- **Product:** The result of multiplying two numbers.
- **Array:** Objects arranged in equal rows and columns.
- **Divides evenly:** When you divide and get a whole number with no remainder.

### Instruction Notes
**Step 1 — Introduce factors through arrays.**
Give each student 12 tiles.
Say: "Using all 12 tiles, build a rectangle. How many rows do you have? How many tiles in each row?"
Allow students to explore. They may build 1x12, 2x6, 3x4, 4x3, 6x2, or 12x1.

Say: "Each rectangle you built shows a factor pair of 12. The number of rows and the number of columns are both factors of 12. When we multiply them together, we get 12."

**Step 2 — Define factor formally.**
Say: "A factor is a number that divides evenly into another number. When we say 3 is a factor of 12, we mean that 12 divided by 3 equals a whole number — in this case, 4. There is no remainder."

Write on the board:
- 12 / 1 = 12 (no remainder) --> 1 is a factor of 12
- 12 / 2 = 6 (no remainder) --> 2 is a factor of 12
- 12 / 3 = 4 (no remainder) --> 3 is a factor of 12
- 12 / 4 = 3 (no remainder) --> 4 is a factor of 12
- 12 / 5 = 2 R2 (remainder!) --> 5 is NOT a factor of 12
- 12 / 6 = 2 (no remainder) --> 6 is a factor of 12

**Step 3 — Introduce factor pairs.**
Say: "Factors come in pairs. If 3 is a factor of 12, then we know 12 / 3 = 4, so 4 is also a factor. We call 3 and 4 a factor pair of 12. Written as a multiplication sentence: 3 x 4 = 12."

Write the factor pairs of 12:
- 1 and 12 (1 x 12 = 12)
- 2 and 6 (2 x 6 = 12)
- 3 and 4 (3 x 4 = 12)

Say: "Every whole number has at least one factor pair: 1 and the number itself. The number 1 is always a factor, and the number itself is always a factor."

**Step 4 — Try another number together.**
Say: "Let's find the factor pairs of 18. Take 18 tiles and build as many different rectangles as you can."

Guide students through:
- 1 x 18 = 18
- 2 x 9 = 18
- 3 x 6 = 18

Say: "The factors of 18 are: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, and 18. We found three factor pairs."

### Worked Examples
**Example A:** Find all factor pairs of 10.
- Can I make a 1-row rectangle? 1 x 10 = 10. Yes! Factor pair: (1, 10)
- Can I make a 2-row rectangle? 2 x 5 = 10. Yes! Factor pair: (2, 5)
- Can I make a 3-row rectangle? 10 / 3 = 3 R1. No.
- Can I make a 4-row rectangle? 10 / 4 = 2 R2. No.
- Can I make a 5-row rectangle? 5 x 2 = 10 — but we already have (2, 5). We can stop!
- Factor pairs of 10: (1, 10) and (2, 5)
- All factors of 10: 1, 2, 5, 10

**Example B:** Find all factor pairs of 16.
- 1 x 16 = 16. Factor pair: (1, 16)
- 2 x 8 = 16. Factor pair: (2, 8)
- 16 / 3 = 5 R1. Not a factor.
- 4 x 4 = 16. Factor pair: (4, 4)
- When we reach 5, we check: 16 / 5 = 3 R1. Not a factor. And 5 > 4, which was the last new factor we found — we can stop.
- Factor pairs of 16: (1, 16), (2, 8), (4, 4)
- All factors of 16: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16

**Example C:** Find all factor pairs of 7.
- 1 x 7 = 7. Factor pair: (1, 7)
- 7 / 2 = 3 R1. Not a factor.
- 7 / 3 = 2 R1. Not a factor.
- We've reached a point where the next number to test (4) is greater than 3 (the quotient from our last test). We can stop.
- Factor pair of 7: (1, 7)
- All factors of 7: 1, 7
- Note: 7 has only one factor pair. We will learn a special name for numbers like this in Lesson 3!

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these problems together. Students build arrays with tiles or draw them on grid paper.

1. **Find all factor pairs of 8.**
   Facilitator prompt: "Start with 1. What is 1 x 8?" (8) "Now try 2. What is 2 x ?" (4) "Try 3: does 8 / 3 give a whole number?" (No, 8 / 3 = 2 R2) "Try 4: 4 x ? = 8." (4 x 2 = 8, but we already have 2 x 4.) "We can stop."
   Answer: (1, 8) and (2, 4). Factors: 1, 2, 4, 8.

2. **Find all factor pairs of 15.**
   Facilitator prompt: "Start at 1." (1 x 15) "Try 2." (15 / 2 = 7 R1, no) "Try 3." (3 x 5 = 15, yes!) "Try 4." (15 / 4 = 3 R3, no) "Try 5 — we already have it from the pair with 3."
   Answer: (1, 15) and (3, 5). Factors: 1, 3, 5, 15.

3. **Find all factor pairs of 20.**
   Answer: (1, 20), (2, 10), (4, 5). Factors: 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20.

4. **Find all factor pairs of 9.**
   Answer: (1, 9) and (3, 3). Factors: 1, 3, 9.

5. **Is 5 a factor of 30? How do you know?**
   Answer: Yes, because 30 / 5 = 6 with no remainder. The factor pair is (5, 6).

**Facilitator tip:** If a student is unsure whether a number is a factor, encourage them to divide: "Does it divide evenly? Is there a remainder?"

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
### Part A: Find All Factor Pairs
Find all factor pairs for each number.

1. Factor pairs of 6: ___
2. Factor pairs of 14: ___
3. Factor pairs of 24: ___
4. Factor pairs of 11: ___
5. Factor pairs of 36: ___
6. Factor pairs of 25: ___
7. Factor pairs of 30: ___
8. Factor pairs of 13: ___

### Part B: True or False
Write T (true) or F (false). If false, explain why.

1. 4 is a factor of 20. ___
2. 3 is a factor of 16. ___
3. 7 is a factor of 21. ___
4. The number 1 is a factor of every number. ___

### Part C: Word Problems
1. Maria is arranging 24 chairs into equal rows for a class event. List all the different ways she could arrange the chairs into equal rows. (Each arrangement is a factor pair.)

2. A baker has 18 cupcakes. He wants to arrange them on plates so that each plate has the same number of cupcakes with none left over. What are all the possible numbers of cupcakes per plate?

### Answer Key

**Part A:**
1. Factor pairs of 6: (1, 6), (2, 3). Factors: 1, 2, 3, 6.
2. Factor pairs of 14: (1, 14), (2, 7). Factors: 1, 2, 7, 14.
3. Factor pairs of 24: (1, 24), (2, 12), (3, 8), (4, 6). Factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24.
4. Factor pairs of 11: (1, 11). Factors: 1, 11.
5. Factor pairs of 36: (1, 36), (2, 18), (3, 12), (4, 9), (6, 6). Factors: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 9, 12, 18, 36.
6. Factor pairs of 25: (1, 25), (5, 5). Factors: 1, 5, 25.
7. Factor pairs of 30: (1, 30), (2, 15), (3, 10), (5, 6). Factors: 1, 2, 3, 5, 6, 10, 15, 30.
8. Factor pairs of 13: (1, 13). Factors: 1, 13.

**Part B:**
1. T — 20 / 4 = 5, no remainder.
2. F — 16 / 3 = 5 R1. There is a remainder, so 3 is not a factor of 16.
3. T — 21 / 7 = 3, no remainder.
4. T — 1 divides evenly into every whole number.

**Part C:**
1. Factor pairs of 24: (1, 24), (2, 12), (3, 8), (4, 6). So Maria could arrange: 1 row of 24, 2 rows of 12, 3 rows of 8, 4 rows of 6, 6 rows of 4, 8 rows of 3, 12 rows of 2, or 24 rows of 1.
2. Factor pairs of 18: (1, 18), (2, 9), (3, 6). Possible cupcakes per plate: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, or 18.

## Assessment Check
1. Find all factor pairs of 28.
2. Is 6 a factor of 42? Explain how you know.

**Target response:**
1. Factor pairs of 28: (1, 28), (2, 14), (4, 7). Factors: 1, 2, 4, 7, 14, 28.
2. Yes, 6 is a factor of 42 because 42 / 6 = 7 with no remainder.

## Extension
- **Square arrays:** Find all the numbers between 1 and 50 that have a factor pair where both factors are the same (e.g., 4 x 4 = 16). These are called perfect squares. How many can you find? (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49)
- **Preview of Lesson 2:** Look at your factor pair lists. Which numbers had only one factor pair? Which had the most? In the next lesson, we will learn a system to make sure we never miss a factor pair.
