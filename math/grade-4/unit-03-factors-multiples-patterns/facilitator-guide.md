# Facilitator Guide — Grade 4 Math, Unit 3: Factors, Multiples, and Patterns

**Grade:** 4
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 3 — Factors, Multiples, and Patterns
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided (facilitator leads instruction; student works independently for practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit introduces students to the foundational number theory concepts of factors, multiples, prime numbers, and composite numbers. Students learn to find all factor pairs for whole numbers up to 100, identify prime and composite numbers, and generate and analyze arithmetic patterns. These concepts build on the multiplication and division skills developed in Units 1 and 2 and lay critical groundwork for fraction work in later grades.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Find all factor pairs for any whole number in the range 1-100
- Determine whether a given whole number in the range 1-100 is prime or composite
- Explain why 1 is neither prime nor composite
- Recognize that a whole number is a multiple of each of its factors
- List multiples of a given number
- Find common factors and the greatest common factor (GCF) of two numbers
- Find common multiples and the least common multiple (LCM) of two numbers
- Generate a number pattern that follows a given rule
- Identify relationships between two related number patterns

**Why this matters:** Understanding factors and multiples is essential for simplifying fractions, finding common denominators, and working with ratios. Pattern recognition develops algebraic thinking and prepares students for formal algebra. These concepts appear constantly in everyday contexts — scheduling, packaging, tiling, and problem solving.

## Lesson Summaries and Facilitation Notes

### Lesson 1: Understanding Factors

**Goal:** Students understand that factors are numbers that divide evenly into a given number. They find factor pairs using arrays and multiplication.

**Key moves:**
- Begin with physical arrays (tiles or counters) to make factors concrete
- Connect arrays to multiplication sentences (3 rows of 4 = 12, so 3 and 4 are factors of 12)
- Emphasize that factors always come in pairs

**Watch for:** Students who confuse factors with multiples. Reinforce: "Factors go INTO a number. Multiples come OUT of a number."

### Lesson 2: Factor Pairs — Systematic Method

**Goal:** Students learn the rainbow method for finding ALL factor pairs of a number, ensuring no pairs are missed.

**Key moves:**
- Model starting at 1 and testing each number in order
- Show students how to stop searching (when the factor pair starts repeating)
- Practice organizing factor pairs in order

**Watch for:** Students who forget that 1 and the number itself are always a factor pair. Students who stop too early and miss factor pairs.

### Lesson 3: Prime Numbers

**Goal:** Students learn the definition of prime numbers, identify primes up to 50, and understand why 1 is not prime.

**Key moves:**
- Use the Sieve of Eratosthenes on a hundred chart — this is a highly engaging, visual activity
- Build the definition from prior knowledge: a prime number has exactly 2 factors (1 and itself)
- Address the special case of 1 explicitly (only 1 factor, so not prime)

**Watch for:** Students who think 1 is prime (it has only one factor, not two). Students who think all odd numbers are prime (9, 15, 21, etc. are odd but not prime).

### Lesson 4: Composite Numbers

**Goal:** Students define composite numbers, classify numbers 1-100 as prime or composite, and see 1 as neither.

**Key moves:**
- Build on prime definition: composite = more than 2 factors
- Practice classifying numbers using factor pair knowledge from Lessons 1-2
- Create a reference chart of primes and composites to 50

**Watch for:** Students who classify 2 as composite (it is the only even prime). Students who think 0 is prime or composite (it is neither — clarify if it comes up, but the standard focuses on 1-100).

### Lesson 5: Multiples

**Goal:** Students list multiples of a number, connect multiples to skip counting, and recognize the factor-multiple relationship.

**Key moves:**
- Explicitly connect: "If 4 is a factor of 20, then 20 is a multiple of 4"
- Use skip counting as the bridge to listing multiples
- Emphasize that multiples go on forever — there is no largest multiple

**Watch for:** Students confusing factors and multiples. Use consistent language: "Factors divide INTO. Multiples are products OF."

### Lesson 6: Common Factors and GCF

**Goal:** Students find common factors of two numbers and identify the greatest common factor.

**Key moves:**
- List all factors of each number, then circle the shared ones
- Introduce the term "greatest common factor" — the biggest number in both lists
- Use Venn diagrams as a visual organizer

**Watch for:** Students who find only some common factors and declare the greatest one they found, rather than checking all factors first.

### Lesson 7: Common Multiples and LCM

**Goal:** Students find common multiples of two numbers and identify the least common multiple.

**Key moves:**
- List multiples of each number until you find common ones
- Introduce "least common multiple" — the smallest number (other than 0) in both lists
- Connect to real-world scheduling problems

**Watch for:** Students who say 0 is the LCM (since 0 is a multiple of every number). Clarify: LCM means the smallest positive common multiple.

### Lesson 8: Arithmetic Patterns — Rules

**Goal:** Students identify the rule governing a number pattern and generate patterns from a given rule.

**Key moves:**
- Start with simple patterns (add 3, subtract 2, multiply by 2)
- Distinguish between the rule and the starting number
- Have students create patterns for a partner to solve

**Watch for:** Students who identify the rule between consecutive terms but cannot extend the pattern. Students who confuse "add" and "multiply" rules.

### Lesson 9: Pattern Relationships

**Goal:** Students compare two related patterns and describe the relationship between corresponding terms.

**Key moves:**
- Generate two patterns with the same starting point but different rules
- Line up corresponding terms in a table and look for relationships
- Guide students to describe the relationship in words and numbers

**Watch for:** Students who compare sequential terms within one pattern instead of corresponding terms across two patterns.

### Lesson 10: Review and Problem Solving

**Goal:** Comprehensive review of all unit concepts with real-world application problems.

**Key moves:**
- Use a variety of problem types (multiple choice, fill-in, word problems)
- Include problems that combine concepts (e.g., "Is this number prime or composite? How do you know?")
- Allow students to work in pairs for part of the review

**Watch for:** This lesson serves as a diagnostic before the unit assessment. Note which concept areas need additional review.

## Differentiation Strategies

### Struggling Learners

- **Provide manipulatives:** Use color tiles to build arrays for factor pair work. Physical objects make the abstract concrete.
- **Use a hundred chart:** Keep a highlighted hundred chart available for identifying primes, composites, and multiples.
- **Reduce the range:** For factor pair problems, start with numbers under 30 before moving to larger numbers.
- **Sentence frames:** Provide sentence frames for explanations: "___ is a factor of ___ because ___ x ___ = ___."
- **Factor pair organizer:** Provide a T-chart template for systematic factor pair finding.

### Advanced Learners

- **Extend the range:** Challenge students to find factor pairs for numbers beyond 100.
- **Explore square numbers:** Why do some numbers have an odd number of factors? (They are perfect squares.)
- **Goldbach's Conjecture:** Introduce the idea that every even number greater than 2 can be written as the sum of two primes. Have students test this for even numbers up to 50.
- **Pattern complexity:** Give multi-step rules (e.g., "multiply by 2, then add 1") or geometric patterns alongside arithmetic patterns.
- **Prime factorization preview:** Introduce factor trees as a way to break numbers into prime factors.

## Common Errors and How to Address Them

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student says 1 is prime | Misunderstands the definition (exactly 2 factors) | "How many factors does 1 have? Just one: itself. Prime numbers must have exactly 2 factors." |
| Student says 2 is not prime because "all primes are odd" | Overgeneralization | "Let's list the factors of 2: 1 and 2. That's exactly 2 factors, so 2 is prime. It's the only even prime!" |
| Misses factor pairs (e.g., finds 1x24 and 2x12 but skips 3x8) | Not testing systematically | Reteach the rainbow method: start at 1 and test every number in order until the pairs start repeating. |
| Confuses factors and multiples | Vocabulary mix-up | Use the anchor phrase: "Factors Fit IN, Multiples Move OUT." Post it on the wall. |
| Lists 0 as the LCM | Technically 0 is a multiple of every number | Clarify: "When we find the LCM, we look for the smallest positive common multiple — a multiple greater than 0." |
| Applies the wrong operation in a pattern rule | Misidentifies the rule | "Look at the difference between each pair of consecutive terms. Is the change the same each time? What operation gives that change?" |
| Student thinks all odd numbers are prime | Only tested divisibility by 2 | "Is 9 odd? Yes. Is 9 prime? 3 x 3 = 9, so 9 has factors other than 1 and itself. Odd does not mean prime." |

## Assessment Guidance

The Unit Assessment (see `unit-assessment.md`) is a 50-point written test covering all five major concept areas. No calculators are permitted.

**Mastery Criteria:**
- **Overall:** 40 out of 50 points (80%) to demonstrate mastery
- **Section minimums:** Students should achieve at least 50% in each section to confirm balanced understanding

**Mastery levels:**

| Level | Score Range | Next Steps |
|-------|-----------|------------|
| Mastered | 40-50 (80-100%) | Advance to Unit 4. |
| Approaching | 30-39 (60-79%) | Target reteaching in weak areas; reassess those sections. |
| Developing | Below 30 (<60%) | Reteach core concepts (Lessons 1-5 especially); check prerequisite fluency. |

**Section-specific reteaching:**

| If struggling in... | Focus on... |
|---|---|
| Section 1 (Factors/Factor Pairs) | Return to arrays with manipulatives (Lessons 1-2). Practice the systematic rainbow method. |
| Section 2 (Prime/Composite) | Redo the Sieve of Eratosthenes activity (Lesson 3). Build factor lists for each number in question. |
| Section 3 (Multiples/GCF/LCM) | Reconnect to skip counting. Use number lines for multiples. List-and-compare method for GCF and LCM. |
| Section 4 (Patterns) | Start with simple "add N" rules before advancing. Use tables to organize pattern work. |
| Section 5 (Word Problems) | Identify the operation first — is the problem asking about factors or multiples? Use graphic organizers. |
