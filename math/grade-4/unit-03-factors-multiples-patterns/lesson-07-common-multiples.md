# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 3, Lesson 7

## Topic
Common Multiples and Least Common Multiple (LCM)

## Learning Objectives
- List multiples of two numbers and identify multiples they share (common multiples)
- Define and find the least common multiple (LCM) of two numbers
- Understand that common multiples repeat in a pattern
- Apply LCM to real-world scheduling and grouping problems

## Prerequisites
- Listing multiples of a number (Lesson 5)
- Understanding of common factors and GCF (Lesson 6)
- Skip counting fluency
- Multiplication facts through 12

## Materials Needed
- Student dry-erase boards or scratch paper
- Pencil and paper
- Hundred chart (optional)
- Number line (optional, for visual support)
- Colored pencils

**Standards:** 4.OA.4 — Recognize that a whole number is a multiple of each of its factors.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review listing multiples and motivate the concept of common multiples.

1. Ask students to list the first 12 multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27, 30, 33, 36
2. Ask students to list the first 12 multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 44, 48
3. Ask: "Do you see any numbers that appear on BOTH lists?" (12, 24, 36)
4. Say: "In Lesson 6, we found common factors — factors shared by two numbers. Today we are looking at common multiples — multiples shared by two numbers."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
### Key Vocabulary
- **Common multiple:** A number that is a multiple of two (or more) numbers. For example, 12 is a common multiple of 3 and 4 because 12 appears in the multiples list of both 3 and 4.
- **Least common multiple (LCM):** The smallest positive number that is a multiple of two (or more) numbers. The LCM of 3 and 4 is 12.

### Instruction Notes
**Step 1 — Define common multiples.**
Say: "A common multiple is a multiple that two numbers share. For example, let's look at 4 and 6."

Write on the board:
- Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40, 44, 48, ...
- Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, ...

Say: "Which numbers appear in both lists?" Circle: 12, 24, 36, 48, ...

Say: "These are the common multiples of 4 and 6. Notice that the common multiples go on forever, just like regular multiples. There are infinitely many common multiples."

**Step 2 — Introduce the least common multiple.**
Say: "The least common multiple, or LCM, is the SMALLEST positive common multiple. Looking at our list, what is the smallest number that is a multiple of both 4 and 6?" (12)

Write: LCM of 4 and 6 = 12.

Say: "Notice something interesting: 4 x 6 = 24, but the LCM is 12, which is less than 24. The LCM is not always the product of the two numbers — sometimes it is smaller."

**Step 3 — Show the pattern of common multiples.**
Say: "Here is a cool pattern: all the common multiples of 4 and 6 are multiples of the LCM (12). Look: 12, 24, 36, 48 — those are 12 x 1, 12 x 2, 12 x 3, 12 x 4. Once you find the LCM, you can find all the other common multiples by listing multiples of the LCM!"

**Step 4 — When the LCM equals the product.**
Say: "Sometimes the LCM IS the product of the two numbers. Let's try 3 and 5."

Write:
- Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27, 30, ...
- Multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, ...

Say: "The first common multiple is 15. And 3 x 5 = 15. The LCM equals the product! This happens when the two numbers share no common factors other than 1." (Connect to Lesson 6: the GCF of 3 and 5 is 1.)

**Step 5 — Real-world connection.**
Say: "The LCM helps us solve scheduling problems. Imagine that Event A happens every 4 days and Event B happens every 6 days. If they both happen today, when is the next day they will happen at the same time?" (In 12 days — the LCM of 4 and 6.)

### Worked Examples
**Example A:** Find the LCM of 6 and 8.
- Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 42, 48, ...
- Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, ...
- Common multiples: 24, 48, ...
- LCM = 24

**Example B:** Find the LCM of 5 and 7.
- Multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, ...
- Multiples of 7: 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, ...
- First common multiple: 35
- LCM = 35
- Note: 5 x 7 = 35. Since 5 and 7 share no common factor other than 1, the LCM is their product.

**Example C:** Find the LCM of 4 and 12.
- Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, ...
- Multiples of 12: 12, 24, 36, ...
- First common multiple: 12
- LCM = 12
- Note: When one number is a multiple of the other, the LCM is simply the larger number!

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Find the common multiples and LCM for each pair. Students work on boards.

1. **Find the LCM of 3 and 5.**
   Facilitator prompt: "List multiples of 3 and multiples of 5 until you find a match."
   Answer: Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15. Multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15. LCM = 15.

2. **Find the LCM of 6 and 9.**
   Facilitator prompt: "List multiples of each. Look for the first match."
   Answer: Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18. Multiples of 9: 9, 18. LCM = 18.

3. **Find the LCM of 4 and 10.**
   Answer: Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20. Multiples of 10: 10, 20. LCM = 20.

4. **Find the LCM of 3 and 7.**
   Answer: Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21. Multiples of 7: 7, 14, 21. LCM = 21.

5. **Find the LCM of 5 and 10.**
   Facilitator prompt: "Is one of these numbers a multiple of the other?"
   Answer: 10 is a multiple of 5. LCM = 10.

6. **List the first three common multiples of 4 and 5.**
   Answer: LCM = 20. Common multiples: 20, 40, 60.

**Facilitator tip:** Encourage students to list multiples of the larger number first, since those are spaced further apart and easier to check against. For example, for LCM of 6 and 9, list multiples of 9 (9, 18, 27...) and check each one: is 9 a multiple of 6? No. Is 18 a multiple of 6? Yes! LCM = 18.

### Part C: Word Problems
1. Liam gets a haircut every 6 weeks. His brother gets a haircut every 8 weeks. They both got haircuts today. In how many weeks will they both get haircuts on the same day again?

2. Hot dogs come in packs of 8. Hot dog buns come in packs of 6. What is the fewest number of hot dogs and buns you need to buy so that you have exactly the same number of each (no leftovers)?

3. Bus Route A arrives at the bus stop every 10 minutes. Bus Route B arrives every 15 minutes. If both buses arrive at 9:00 AM, when is the next time both buses will arrive at the same time?

## Assessment Check
1. Find the LCM of 8 and 10.
2. Explain the difference between common factors and common multiples.

**Target response:**
1. Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40. Multiples of 10: 10, 20, 30, 40. LCM = 40.
2. Common factors are factors (divisors) that two numbers share — they are smaller than or equal to both numbers, and there is a finite number of them. Common multiples are multiples that two numbers share — they are larger than or equal to both numbers, and there are infinitely many. The GCF is the greatest common factor, and the LCM is the least common multiple.

## Extension
- **LCM of three numbers:** Find the LCM of 3, 4, and 6. (Hint: find the LCM of 3 and 4 first, then find the LCM of that result and 6.) Answer: LCM of 3 and 4 = 12. LCM of 12 and 6 = 12. So LCM of 3, 4, and 6 = 12.
- **GCF and LCM connection:** For several number pairs, find both the GCF and LCM. Then multiply GCF x LCM and compare to the product of the two original numbers. What do you notice? (GCF x LCM = product of the two numbers. For example: GCF(6,8)=2, LCM(6,8)=24, and 2 x 24 = 48 = 6 x 8.)
