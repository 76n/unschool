# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 4, Lesson 1

## Topic
Equivalent Fractions with Visual Models

## Learning Objectives
- Recognize that different fractions can name the same amount
- Use area models, fraction strips, and number lines to identify equivalent fractions
- Explain why two fractions are equivalent by referring to a visual model
- Generate at least one equivalent fraction for a given fraction using visual reasoning

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of fractions as parts of a whole (Grade 3)
- Ability to read and write fractions using numerator and denominator (Grade 3)
- Ability to represent fractions on a number line (Grade 3)

## Materials Needed
- Fraction strips (halves, thirds, fourths, sixths, eighths, twelfths)
- Grid paper
- Colored pencils or crayons
- Student dry-erase boards or scratch paper
- Pencil and paper

**Standards:** 4.NF.1 — Explain why a fraction a/b is equivalent to (n x a)/(n x b) using visual fraction models.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of fraction concepts from Grade 3.

1. Draw a rectangle on the board divided into 4 equal parts with 1 part shaded. Ask: "What fraction is shaded?" (1/4)
2. Draw another rectangle the same size, divided into 2 equal parts with 1 part shaded. Ask: "What fraction is shaded?" (1/2)
3. Ask: "Which is greater, 1/2 or 1/4? How do you know?" (1/2 is greater — the shaded area is larger, or you can reason that halves are bigger pieces than fourths.)

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce the concept that the same amount of a whole can be named by more than one fraction.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Equivalent fractions:** Fractions that name the same amount. They look different but represent the same part of the whole. For example, 1/2 and 2/4 are equivalent fractions.
- **Area model:** A shape (usually a rectangle or circle) divided into equal parts, where some parts are shaded to show a fraction.
- **Fraction strip:** A paper strip divided into equal sections, used to compare fractions by lining them up.
- **Number line:** A line marked with numbers at equal intervals, used to show where fractions fall.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Discover equivalence with fraction strips.**
Give each student fraction strips for halves, fourths, and eighths.

Say: "Take your halves strip and fold to show 1/2. Now take your fourths strip and line it up underneath. How many fourths does it take to match 1/2?" (2 fourths)

Say: "So 1/2 and 2/4 cover the same amount. They are equivalent fractions — different names for the same size."

Now say: "Line up the eighths strip. How many eighths match 1/2?" (4 eighths)

Write on the board: 1/2 = 2/4 = 4/8

Say: "Three different fractions, all naming the exact same amount. The pieces are different sizes, but the total shaded area is the same."

**Step 2 — Confirm with area models.**
Draw two identical rectangles on the board.
- Rectangle 1: Divide into 3 equal columns. Shade 2 columns. Label: 2/3
- Rectangle 2: Divide into 6 equal columns. Shade 4 columns. Label: 4/6

Say: "Look at the shaded areas. Are they the same size?" (Yes.) "So 2/3 and 4/6 are equivalent fractions. The second rectangle has more pieces, but I shaded more of them — the total shaded amount is the same."

**Step 3 — Show on a number line.**
Draw a number line from 0 to 1, marked in thirds (0, 1/3, 2/3, 1).
Below it, draw a number line from 0 to 1, marked in sixths (0, 1/6, 2/6, 3/6, 4/6, 5/6, 1).

Say: "Where is 2/3 on the top number line?" (Point to it.) "Where is 4/6 on the bottom number line?" (Point to it.) "They are at the same spot! This confirms that 2/3 and 4/6 are equivalent."

**Step 4 — Key idea.**
Say: "When we cut each piece into smaller equal pieces, we get more pieces but each piece is smaller. The total amount stays the same. That is why equivalent fractions exist — different ways to name the same amount."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Show that 1/3 and 2/6 are equivalent using an area model.
- Draw a rectangle divided into 3 equal parts. Shade 1 part. This shows 1/3.
- Draw an identical rectangle divided into 6 equal parts. Shade 2 parts. This shows 2/6.
- The shaded areas are the same size, so 1/3 = 2/6.
- Notice: each third was cut into 2 equal pieces, giving 6 pieces total. The 1 shaded third became 2 shaded sixths.

**Example B:** Use a number line to find a fraction equivalent to 3/4.
- Draw a number line from 0 to 1, marked in fourths: 0, 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, 1.
- Below it, draw a number line from 0 to 1, marked in eighths: 0, 1/8, 2/8, 3/8, 4/8, 5/8, 6/8, 7/8, 1.
- Find 3/4 on the top line. Look directly below on the bottom line: it aligns with 6/8.
- So 3/4 = 6/8.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice finding equivalent fractions using visual models with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Fraction Strip Matching**
Using fraction strips, find an equivalent fraction for each. Line up strips to verify.

1. Find a fraction equivalent to 1/2 using fourths.
   Facilitator prompt: "Line up fourths under your 1/2 strip. How many fourths match?" (2/4)

2. Find a fraction equivalent to 2/3 using sixths.
   Facilitator prompt: "How many sixths match 2/3?" (4/6)

3. Find a fraction equivalent to 3/4 using twelfths.
   Facilitator prompt: "Each fourth equals how many twelfths?" (3 twelfths, so 3/4 = 9/12)

**Activity 2: Area Model Drawing**
Draw area models to show each pair of equivalent fractions.

4. Show that 1/4 = 2/8 by drawing two rectangles.
   Facilitator prompt: "Divide the first rectangle into 4 parts and shade 1. Divide the second into 8 parts and shade 2. Do they cover the same amount?"

5. Show that 2/4 = 4/8 by drawing two rectangles.
   Facilitator prompt: "If the student struggles, ask: What happens if you cut each fourth in half?"

**Facilitator tip:** If a student says two fractions are equivalent, always ask "How do you know?" Push for a visual or verbal explanation, not just a number answer.

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1: On-Level
Use fraction strips, area models, or number lines to find the equivalent fraction.

1. 1/2 = ?/6 (Answer: 3/6)
2. 2/4 = ?/8 (Answer: 4/8)
3. 1/3 = ?/12 (Answer: 4/12)
4. 3/6 = ?/2 (Answer: 1/2)
5. Draw an area model to show that 2/3 = 4/6.

### Tier 2: Scaffolded
Fraction strips are provided. Match each fraction on the left to its equivalent on the right.

1. 1/2 --> (a) 2/6, (b) 4/8, (c) 3/12
   Answer: (b) 4/8
2. 1/3 --> (a) 3/6, (b) 2/8, (c) 2/6
   Answer: (c) 2/6
3. 3/4 --> (a) 6/8, (b) 4/6, (c) 5/8
   Answer: (a) 6/8
4. Use fraction strips to verify each answer.

### Tier 3: Challenge
1. Find THREE different fractions equivalent to 1/2. (Possible: 2/4, 3/6, 4/8, 5/10, 6/12)
2. Find TWO different fractions equivalent to 3/4. (Possible: 6/8, 9/12)
3. Are 4/6 and 6/9 equivalent? Draw area models to find out. (Yes — both equal 2/3.)
4. Name a fraction equivalent to 2/3 that has a denominator greater than 12. (e.g., 8/12... wait, that is 12. Try 10/15, 12/18, etc.)
5. True or false: Every fraction has infinitely many equivalent fractions. Explain your reasoning. (True — you can always multiply both parts by another number to get a new equivalent fraction.)

---

## Exit Ticket

1. Use a visual model (drawing) to show that 2/4 = 1/2.
2. Name one fraction that is equivalent to 3/6.
3. How do you know that two fractions are equivalent?

**Mastery threshold:** 2 out of 3 correct.

---

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:**
1. 1/2 = 2/4
2. 2/3 = 4/6
3. 3/4 = 9/12
4. Area model showing 1/4 = 2/8 (one of four parts shaded vs. two of eight parts shaded, same total area)
5. Area model showing 2/4 = 4/8

**Independent Practice — Tier 1:**
1. 1/2 = 3/6
2. 2/4 = 4/8
3. 1/3 = 4/12
4. 3/6 = 1/2
5. Drawing: rectangle in thirds with 2 shaded = rectangle in sixths with 4 shaded

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Drawing showing that 2 out of 4 equal parts covers the same area as 1 out of 2 equal parts.
2. Any correct equivalent: 1/2, 6/12, etc. (accept any valid equivalent fraction).
3. Two fractions are equivalent when they represent the same amount of the whole — visually, they cover the same area or land on the same point on a number line.

---

## Teacher Notes

- This lesson is almost entirely visual and concrete. Resist the urge to introduce the "multiply top and bottom by the same number" rule yet — that comes in Lesson 2. Let students discover the pattern naturally.
- If students notice "I multiplied 1 by 2 and 2 by 2 to get 2/4 from 1/2," celebrate the observation but save the formal rule for next lesson.
- Some students will need extra time with fraction strips. It is better to spend two sessions on this lesson than to rush through without conceptual understanding.
- Keep fraction strips available for the rest of the unit — many students will refer back to them as a checking tool.
