# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 4, Lesson 6

## Topic
Adding Fractions with Like Denominators

## Learning Objectives
- Add two fractions with the same denominator
- Explain why we add numerators but keep the denominator the same
- Simplify the sum to its simplest form when needed
- Represent fraction addition using visual models and equations

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of fractions as parts of a whole (Grade 3)
- Equivalent fractions and simplifying (Lessons 1-3)
- Understanding that the denominator names the size of the pieces

## Materials Needed
- Fraction strips
- Grid paper or area model templates
- Colored pencils
- Student dry-erase boards or scratch paper
- Pencil and paper

**Standards:** 4.NF.3a — Understand addition of fractions as joining parts referring to the same whole. 4.NF.3b — Decompose a fraction into a sum of fractions with the same denominator.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of addition concepts and connect to counting like objects.

1. Solve: 3 apples + 2 apples = ? (5 apples)
2. Solve: 3 tens + 2 tens = ? (5 tens)
3. Solve: 3 eighths + 2 eighths = ? (5 eighths)
4. Ask: "What pattern do you notice?" (When we add things with the same name, we add the count and keep the name.)
5. Say: "Fractions work the same way! Today we will add fractions that have the same denominator — the same 'name' for the pieces."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach addition of fractions with like denominators using concrete, representational, and abstract approaches.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Like denominators:** Fractions that have the same denominator (same-sized pieces). For example, 2/5 and 1/5 have like denominators.
- **Sum:** The result of adding two or more numbers.
- **Decompose:** To break a fraction apart into a sum of smaller fractions. For example, 3/4 = 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete: Use fraction strips.**
Place a 1/8 strip and a 3/8 strip end-to-end.

Say: "I have 1 eighth and 3 eighths. When I put them together, how many eighths do I have?" (4 eighths, or 4/8.)

Place the combined strips next to a 1/2 strip to confirm: "4/8 = 1/2. So 1/8 + 3/8 = 4/8, which simplifies to 1/2."

**Step 2 — Representational: Use an area model.**
Draw a rectangle divided into 6 equal parts.
- Shade 2 parts in blue. Label: 2/6.
- Shade 3 more parts in red. Label: 3/6.
- Total shaded: 5 out of 6 parts. Label: 5/6.

Write: 2/6 + 3/6 = 5/6.

Say: "The pieces are all sixths — the same size. I have 2 of them plus 3 of them, which gives me 5 of them. The denominator stays 6 because the size of each piece did not change."

**Step 3 — Abstract: State the rule.**
Write on the board:

When adding fractions with the same denominator:
- Add the numerators.
- Keep the denominator the same.
- Simplify if possible.

a/d + b/d = (a + b)/d

Say: "We add the numerators because we are counting how many same-sized pieces we have in total. We keep the denominator because the size of each piece does not change."

**Step 4 — The most common mistake.**
Write: 1/4 + 2/4 = ?

Say: "A common mistake is to add both the tops AND the bottoms: 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/8. But that is wrong!"

Draw two rectangles to show: "1/4 + 2/4 covers 3 out of 4 parts, not 3 out of 8 parts. The answer is 3/4."

**Step 5 — Simplify when needed.**
Write: 3/8 + 1/8 = 4/8.

Say: "4/8 is correct, but can we simplify? GCF of 4 and 8 is 4. So 4/8 = 1/2. Always check if your answer can be simplified."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** 2/5 + 1/5.
- Same denominator (fifths). Add numerators: 2 + 1 = 3.
- Keep denominator: 3/5.
- Simplify? GCF of 3 and 5 is 1. Already in simplest form.
- Answer: 2/5 + 1/5 = 3/5.

**Example B:** 3/10 + 5/10.
- Same denominator (tenths). Add numerators: 3 + 5 = 8.
- Keep denominator: 8/10.
- Simplify? GCF of 8 and 10 is 2. 8/10 = 4/5.
- Answer: 3/10 + 5/10 = 8/10 = 4/5.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice fraction addition with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Add and Simplify**

1. 1/6 + 3/6 = ?
   Facilitator prompt: "Add the numerators. What do you get?" (4/6) "Can you simplify?" (Yes — GCF is 2. 4/6 = 2/3.)

2. 2/8 + 3/8 = ?
   Facilitator prompt: "Add the tops, keep the bottom." (5/8) "Simplify?" (GCF of 5 and 8 is 1. Already simplest form.)

3. 4/10 + 4/10 = ?
   Facilitator prompt: "What is 4 + 4?" (8) "So 8/10. Simplify?" (8/10 = 4/5)

**Activity 2: Show with a Model**

4. Draw an area model to show 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4.
   Facilitator prompt: "Divide a rectangle into 4 parts. Shade 1 part in one color, then 2 more in another. How many total parts are shaded?"

5. Write a number sentence for this picture: a strip divided into 6 parts, with 2 parts shaded blue and 1 part shaded red.
   Answer: 2/6 + 1/6 = 3/6 = 1/2.

**Facilitator tip:** If a student adds the denominators, return immediately to the concrete model: "Show me with fraction strips. Put 1/6 and 3/6 together. Are you looking at twelfths? No — they are still sixths."

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1: On-Level
Add. Simplify your answer if possible.

1. 1/5 + 3/5 = ? (Answer: 4/5)
2. 2/8 + 4/8 = ? (Answer: 6/8 = 3/4)
3. 3/12 + 5/12 = ? (Answer: 8/12 = 2/3)
4. 1/6 + 1/6 = ? (Answer: 2/6 = 1/3)
5. 5/10 + 3/10 = ? (Answer: 8/10 = 4/5)

### Tier 2: Scaffolded
Fill in the blanks.

1. 1/4 + 2/4 = (1 + 2)/4 = ___/4 (Answer: 3/4)
2. 3/8 + 2/8 = (3 + ___)/8 = ___/8 (Answer: 5/8)
3. 2/6 + 2/6 = ___/___ = ___/___ (simplified) (Answer: 4/6 = 2/3)
4. Draw a picture to show 1/3 + 1/3 = 2/3.

### Tier 3: Challenge
1. 5/12 + 7/12 = ? (Answer: 12/12 = 1. A whole!)
2. 3/8 + 5/8 = ? (Answer: 8/8 = 1)
3. 7/10 + 5/10 = ? (Answer: 12/10 — more than one whole! We will learn about this in Lesson 8.)
4. Decompose 5/6 into two different addition problems with like denominators. (Example: 1/6 + 4/6, or 2/6 + 3/6)
5. Maria ate 2/8 of a pizza and then 3/8 more. How much pizza did she eat in total? Is the result in simplest form? (2/8 + 3/8 = 5/8. GCF of 5 and 8 is 1, so 5/8 is already in simplest form.)

## Exit Ticket

1. Solve: 3/10 + 4/10 = ?. Simplify if possible.
2. Explain why we do NOT add the denominators when adding fractions.

**Mastery threshold:** Both correct.

---

## Answer Key

**Exit Ticket:**
1. 3/10 + 4/10 = 7/10. GCF of 7 and 10 is 1, so 7/10 is already in simplest form.
2. The denominator tells the size of each piece. When we add fractions with the same denominator, the size of the pieces does not change — we are just counting how many pieces we have total. Adding the denominators would change the size of the pieces, which would give a wrong answer.

---

## Teacher Notes

- The "adding like objects" warm-up (apples, tens, eighths) is a critical conceptual bridge. If students understand that 3 apples + 2 apples = 5 apples, they already understand fraction addition — they just need to see eighths as another "thing" being counted.
- The most dangerous misconception in this lesson is adding denominators. Address it immediately and repeatedly with visual models. Once a student has "proven" with fraction strips that 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4 (not 3/8), the correct rule sticks better.
- Some students may get sums greater than 1 (like 7/10 + 5/10 = 12/10). Acknowledge this and tell them they will learn about improper fractions in Lesson 8. For now, they can leave such answers as improper fractions.
- Simplifying after adding should become automatic. Remind students: "Add first, then check if you can simplify."
