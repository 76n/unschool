# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 4, Lesson 4

## Topic
Comparing Fractions with Unlike Denominators

## Learning Objectives
- Compare two fractions with unlike denominators by finding a common denominator
- Compare two fractions using the benchmark fraction 1/2
- Use the symbols >, <, and = to record fraction comparisons
- Justify comparisons using visual models or numerical reasoning

## Prerequisites
- Generating equivalent fractions by multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number (Lesson 2)
- Simplifying fractions (Lesson 3)
- Comparing fractions with the same denominator (Grade 3)

## Materials Needed
- Fraction strips
- Number line templates (0 to 1)
- Student dry-erase boards or scratch paper
- Pencil and paper

**Standards:** 4.NF.2 — Compare two fractions with different numerators and different denominators by creating common denominators or numerators, or by comparing to a benchmark fraction such as 1/2.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review same-denominator comparisons and introduce the challenge of unlike denominators.

1. Compare: 3/8 ___ 5/8. (3/8 < 5/8 — same denominator, so compare numerators.)
2. Compare: 7/10 ___ 4/10. (7/10 > 4/10)
3. Now try: 2/3 ___ 3/4. Ask: "Can you compare the numerators directly?" (No — the denominators are different, so the pieces are different sizes.)
4. Say: "When fractions have different denominators, we need a strategy to compare them fairly. Today we will learn two strategies."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach two strategies for comparing fractions with unlike denominators.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Common denominator:** A denominator that is the same for two or more fractions. We create a common denominator so we can compare fractions fairly.
- **Benchmark fraction:** A well-known fraction used as a reference point. The most useful benchmark is 1/2.
- **Unlike denominators:** Fractions that have different bottom numbers (e.g., 2/3 and 3/5 have unlike denominators).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Strategy 1: Find a common denominator.**
Say: "When fractions have unlike denominators, the pieces are different sizes, so we cannot compare directly. But if we rewrite both fractions with the same denominator, then the pieces are the same size and we can compare numerators."

Write: Compare 2/3 and 3/4.
- "I need a number that is a multiple of both 3 and 4. What about 12?"
- 2/3 = (2 x 4)/(3 x 4) = 8/12
- 3/4 = (3 x 3)/(4 x 3) = 9/12
- "Now compare: 8/12 ___ 9/12. Since 8 < 9, we know 8/12 < 9/12."
- "So 2/3 < 3/4."

Say: "I used the least common multiple (LCM) of 3 and 4, which is 12. Any common multiple works, but the LCM keeps the numbers small."

**Step 2 — Strategy 2: Use the benchmark 1/2.**
Say: "Sometimes we can compare fractions quickly by thinking about 1/2."

Write: Compare 3/8 and 5/6.
- "Is 3/8 more or less than 1/2? Half of 8 is 4, and 3 < 4, so 3/8 < 1/2."
- "Is 5/6 more or less than 1/2? Half of 6 is 3, and 5 > 3, so 5/6 > 1/2."
- "Since 3/8 < 1/2 and 5/6 > 1/2, we know 3/8 < 5/6."

Say: "The benchmark strategy is quick when one fraction is clearly less than 1/2 and the other is clearly greater than 1/2. When both fractions are on the same side of 1/2, we need the common denominator strategy."

**Step 3 — When to use which strategy.**
Say: "Use the benchmark strategy for a quick check. Use the common denominator strategy when you need precision, or when both fractions are on the same side of 1/2."

**Step 4 — Important reminder.**
Say: "Comparisons only make sense when both fractions refer to the same whole. If I eat 1/2 of a small pizza and you eat 1/4 of a giant pizza, we cannot just compare the fractions — the wholes are different!"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Compare 3/5 and 2/3. Use the common denominator method.
- LCM of 5 and 3 is 15.
- 3/5 = (3 x 3)/(5 x 3) = 9/15
- 2/3 = (2 x 5)/(3 x 5) = 10/15
- 9/15 < 10/15
- So 3/5 < 2/3.

**Example B:** Compare 5/8 and 3/10. Use the benchmark 1/2.
- 5/8: half of 8 is 4, and 5 > 4, so 5/8 > 1/2.
- 3/10: half of 10 is 5, and 3 < 5, so 3/10 < 1/2.
- Since 5/8 > 1/2 and 3/10 < 1/2, we have 5/8 > 3/10.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice comparing with facilitator support using both strategies.

**Activity 1: Common Denominator Comparisons**

1. Compare 1/3 and 2/5.
   Facilitator prompt: "What is a common multiple of 3 and 5?" (15) "Rewrite both fractions."
   Answer: 1/3 = 5/15, 2/5 = 6/15. Since 5/15 < 6/15, we have 1/3 < 2/5.

2. Compare 3/4 and 5/6.
   Facilitator prompt: "Common multiple of 4 and 6?" (12) "Rewrite both."
   Answer: 3/4 = 9/12, 5/6 = 10/12. 9/12 < 10/12, so 3/4 < 5/6.

**Activity 2: Benchmark Comparisons**

3. Compare 2/8 and 7/12.
   Facilitator prompt: "Is 2/8 more or less than 1/2?" (Less — half of 8 is 4, and 2 < 4.) "Is 7/12 more or less than 1/2?" (More — half of 12 is 6, and 7 > 6.) "So which is greater?"
   Answer: 2/8 < 7/12.

4. Compare 4/6 and 5/8.
   Facilitator prompt: "Both are greater than 1/2, so the benchmark does not help us decide. Use common denominators."
   Answer: LCM of 6 and 8 is 24. 4/6 = 16/24, 5/8 = 15/24. 16/24 > 15/24, so 4/6 > 5/8.

**Facilitator tip:** After each comparison, ask: "Does your answer make sense? Can you verify with fraction strips?" Using multiple representations builds confidence and catches errors.

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1: On-Level
Compare using >, <, or =. Show your work.

1. 2/5 ___ 3/10 (Answer: 2/5 = 4/10 > 3/10, so 2/5 > 3/10)
2. 1/3 ___ 3/8 (Answer: 1/3 = 8/24, 3/8 = 9/24, so 1/3 < 3/8)
3. 5/6 ___ 7/8 (Answer: 5/6 = 20/24, 7/8 = 21/24, so 5/6 < 7/8)
4. 2/4 ___ 3/6 (Answer: 2/4 = 1/2, 3/6 = 1/2, so 2/4 = 3/6)
5. 4/5 ___ 3/4 (Answer: 4/5 = 16/20, 3/4 = 15/20, so 4/5 > 3/4)

### Tier 2: Scaffolded
Compare. The common denominator is given.

1. 1/2 ___ 2/5 (Use denominator 10.) 1/2 = ?/10, 2/5 = ?/10. (Answer: 5/10 > 4/10, so 1/2 > 2/5)
2. 1/4 ___ 1/3 (Use denominator 12.) (Answer: 3/12 < 4/12, so 1/4 < 1/3)
3. 3/4 ___ 2/3 (Use denominator 12.) (Answer: 9/12 > 8/12, so 3/4 > 2/3)
4. Use the benchmark 1/2 to compare: 2/6 ___ 5/8. (Answer: 2/6 < 1/2, 5/8 > 1/2, so 2/6 < 5/8)

### Tier 3: Challenge
1. Compare 7/12 and 5/8. (Answer: 7/12 = 14/24, 5/8 = 15/24, so 7/12 < 5/8)
2. Compare 11/15 and 3/4. (Answer: 11/15 = 44/60, 3/4 = 45/60, so 11/15 < 3/4)
3. Find a fraction between 1/3 and 1/2. Prove it is between them. (Example: 5/12. 1/3 = 4/12, 1/2 = 6/12, and 4/12 < 5/12 < 6/12.)
4. Without finding a common denominator, explain why 99/100 > 98/99. (Both are one piece away from a whole. 99/100 is missing 1/100 while 98/99 is missing 1/99. Since 1/100 < 1/99, 99/100 is closer to 1, so 99/100 > 98/99.)
5. True or false: If the numerator of a fraction is more than half the denominator, the fraction is greater than 1/2. Explain. (True — if numerator > denominator/2, then the fraction > 1/2.)

---

## Exit Ticket

1. Compare 2/3 and 5/8. Show your work.
2. Use the benchmark 1/2 to compare 3/10 and 7/12. Explain your reasoning.

**Mastery threshold:** Both correct with reasoning shown.

---

## Answer Key

**Exit Ticket:**
1. LCM of 3 and 8 is 24. 2/3 = 16/24, 5/8 = 15/24. Since 16/24 > 15/24, 2/3 > 5/8.
2. 3/10 < 1/2 (half of 10 is 5, and 3 < 5). 7/12 > 1/2 (half of 12 is 6, and 7 > 6). So 3/10 < 7/12.

---

## Teacher Notes

- The benchmark strategy is powerful for quick mental comparisons, but students must understand its limitation: it only helps when fractions are on different sides of 1/2. When both fractions are greater than 1/2 (or both less), a common denominator is needed.
- Students often ask "Why can't I just compare 2 and 5 and 3 and 8?" Redirect: "The numerator only tells you how many pieces. The denominator tells you the size of the pieces. You cannot compare counts of different-sized things."
- If students struggle to find a common denominator, remind them of the LCM work from Unit 3, Lesson 7. The simplest approach: multiply the two denominators together (this always works, though it may not give the LCM).
- Fraction strips remain an excellent checking tool. After students compute an answer, have them verify with strips to build confidence.
