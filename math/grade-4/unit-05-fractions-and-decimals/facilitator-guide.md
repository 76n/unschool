# Facilitator Guide — Grade 4 Math, Unit 5: Fractions and Decimals

**Grade:** 4
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 5 — Fractions and Decimals
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4–5 weeks at 2–3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided (facilitator leads instruction; student works independently for practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit bridges the fraction work of Unit 4 with the new concept of decimal notation. Students learn that fractions with denominators of 10 and 100 have a special shorthand — decimal notation — and that the two representations are interchangeable. The unit moves systematically from the meaning of tenths (fractions and visual models) to hundredths, through the symbolic decimal system, and culminates in comparing decimals and solving real-world problems involving money and measurement.

The central idea is **connection**: a decimal is not a new kind of number — it is a fraction, written in a place-value shorthand. This realization, built through 10×10 grids, number lines, and money contexts, prevents the common misconception that decimals and fractions are separate topics.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Express fractions with denominator 10 or 100 using decimal notation
- Recognize that fractions with denominator 10 can be rewritten with denominator 100 (and vice versa) to create equivalent fractions
- Convert fluently between fraction form and decimal form for tenths and hundredths
- Locate decimals to the hundredths place on a number line
- Compare two decimal numbers to the hundredths place using place value reasoning, fraction equivalents, and number line placement
- Add fractions with denominators of 10 and 100 by creating equivalent fractions (4.NF.5)
- Apply decimal and fraction notation in real-world contexts including money and measurement

**Standards addressed:**
- **4.NF.5** — Express a fraction with denominator 10 as an equivalent fraction with denominator 100, and use this technique to add two fractions with respective denominators 10 and 100.
- **4.NF.6** — Use decimal notation for fractions with denominators 10 or 100.
- **4.NF.7** — Compare two decimals to hundredths by reasoning about their size. Recognize that comparisons are valid only when the two decimals refer to the same whole. Record the results of comparisons with the symbols >, =, or <, and justify the conclusions using a visual model.

**Why this matters:** Decimals permeate daily life — every price tag, every measurement in science, every sports statistic uses decimal notation. Grade 4 is the gateway to this system. Students who understand decimals as fractions have a conceptual anchor that prevents place-value errors (e.g., thinking 0.8 < 0.75 because 8 < 75) and prepares them for decimal operations in Grade 5, percentages in Grade 6, and rational numbers throughout middle school.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, confirm the student can:

- [ ] Read and write fractions (numerator/denominator) and explain their meaning as parts of a whole (Grade 3 / Grade 4 Unit 4)
- [ ] Represent fractions on a number line between 0 and 1 (Grade 4, Unit 4)
- [ ] Generate equivalent fractions using multiplication and visual models (Grade 4, Unit 4)
- [ ] Compare fractions with unlike denominators using reasoning or common denominators (Grade 4, Unit 4)
- [ ] Understand place value for whole numbers (ones, tens, hundreds) (Grade 3 / Grade 4 Unit 1)

If the student cannot generate equivalent fractions (e.g., 3/10 = 30/100), spend one session reviewing equivalent fraction procedures from Unit 4 before beginning Lesson 3. This is the single most critical prerequisite for this unit.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **10×10 grids** (printed; at least 3–5 per lesson for shading activities)
- **Tenths strips** (a single strip divided into 10 equal parts; can be cut from grid paper)
- **Number line templates** (0 to 1, marked in tenths; 0 to 1, marked in hundredths)
- **Base-ten blocks or drawings** (ones flat = 1 whole; tens rod = one tenth; unit cube = one hundredth)
- **Play money** (dimes and pennies, or printed cutouts; dime = 1/10 dollar, penny = 1/100 dollar)
- **Colored pencils or crayons** (two contrasting colors for shading grids)
- **Grid paper and plain paper**
- **Student dry-erase boards or scratch paper**
- **Calculator** (optional; for checking only, not during instruction)
- **Pencil**

## Lesson Sequence Rationale

The sequence is deliberately cumulative. Lessons 1–2 build meaning for tenths and hundredths as fractions before any decimal symbol is introduced. Lesson 3 establishes the equivalence bridge (3/10 = 30/100) that underpins all decimal notation. Lessons 4–5 introduce the decimal symbol as shorthand for what students already understand. Lesson 6 consolidates the fraction ↔ decimal connection. Lessons 7–8 extend to the number line and comparison. Lesson 9 applies the 10/100 equivalence to addition. Lesson 10 integrates all skills in context.

**Do not skip Lessons 1–3.** Students who jump straight to decimal notation without the fraction foundation consistently develop misconceptions about place value (e.g., that more digits after the decimal point means larger value).

## Facilitation Tips

### Working with 10×10 Grids
The 10×10 grid is the single most important manipulative in this unit. Use it consistently:
- 1 full row (or column) of 10 cells = 1/10 of the whole grid
- 1 individual cell = 1/100 of the whole grid
- When working with tenths, shade full rows. When working with hundredths, shade individual cells.
- When showing equivalence (3/10 = 30/100), shade 3 full rows, then count the individual cells shaded (30) to "see" the equivalence.

### The Money Context
Money is the most accessible real-world context for hundredths. Use it regularly from Lesson 5 onward:
- 1 dollar = 1 whole
- 1 dime = 1/10 = 0.1 of a dollar
- 1 penny = 1/100 = 0.01 of a dollar
- $0.35 = 35/100 of a dollar = 35 pennies

Students almost always have intuitive knowledge of money that can be harnessed to build decimal sense before formal procedures.

### Language to Reinforce
- Say "3 tenths" not "zero point three" when reading 0.3 — this reinforces the fraction connection.
- Say "25 hundredths" not "zero point two five" for 0.25.
- After students are comfortable, transition to both: "0.3, which we read as three tenths."
- Consistently ask: "Can you write that as a fraction? Can you write that as a decimal?"

### Pacing Caution
Lessons 4 and 5 (introducing decimal notation) often feel fast to facilitators but slow for students. The decimal point is a new symbol and students need time to process it. Watch for the student writing "0.10" when they mean "1/10" — this is normal and indicates they are still anchoring to the fraction form. Celebrate it as a sign of thinking, then help them see the correct decimal form.

## Common Misconceptions to Watch For

| Misconception | What It Looks Like | Correction Strategy |
|---------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| More digits = larger decimal | Student says 0.75 > 0.8 because "75 is bigger than 8" | Return to 10×10 grid: shade 0.8 (80 cells) vs. 0.75 (75 cells). Which is more? |
| Decimals and fractions are different things | Student can do fractions but is confused when asked to write the decimal | Explicitly state and show: "3/10 and 0.3 are two names for the same number." Use the grid to show both labels at once. |
| 0.10 = 10 tenths | Student reads 0.10 as "zero point ten" | Clarify that 0.10 = 10/100 = 1/10 = 0.1. Use equivalent fraction reasoning. |
| The decimal point separates two whole numbers | Student treats 1.3 as "1 and 3" | Emphasize that digits after the decimal point represent parts less than 1. Use the number line to show 1.3 is between 1 and 2. |
| Hundredths are always larger than tenths | Student thinks 0.09 > 0.9 because "hundredths are smaller pieces" confusion | Return to the grid: 0.9 = 90 cells, 0.09 = 9 cells. |
| Adding tenths and hundredths without converting | Student writes 3/10 + 4/100 = 7/110 | Ask: "Can we add sevenths and fifths directly? What do we need first?" Connect to equivalent fraction work. |

## Assessment Guidance

### Formative Assessment (Each Lesson)
Each lesson includes an exit ticket with a clear mastery criterion (typically 2 of 2 or 3 of 4 correct). Use these consistently. If a student misses the exit ticket on Lessons 1, 2, or 3, address the gap before continuing — these are load-bearing lessons.

### Mid-Unit Check
After Lesson 5, informally review the student's work on Lessons 4–5. Can they:
- Write any tenth as a decimal (e.g., 7/10 → 0.7)?
- Write any hundredth as a decimal (e.g., 53/100 → 0.53)?
- Reverse the process (e.g., 0.4 → 4/10)?

If not, revisit before proceeding.

### Summative Assessment
The unit assessment at the end covers all 10 lessons. It is designed for approximately 40–50 minutes. It includes:
- Section A: Fraction ↔ decimal conversion (tenths and hundredths)
- Section B: Locating and comparing decimals
- Section C: Adding fractions with denominators 10 and 100
- Section D: Real-world application (money and measurement)
- Section E: Challenge extension (for students working above grade level)

**Mastery threshold:** 80% on Sections A–D (Challenge section is unscored for mastery determination).

## Differentiation

### For Students Who Need Additional Support
- Spend extra time on the 10×10 grid in Lessons 1–3. Do not rush to symbolic notation.
- Provide pre-drawn grids with rows already divided so the student only needs to shade, not draw.
- Use physical play money (dimes and pennies) extensively in Lessons 5–10.
- When comparing decimals, always return to the grid or number line rather than working purely symbolically.
- Reduce the volume of independent practice problems, focusing on accuracy over quantity.

### For Students Working at Grade Level
Follow the lesson sequence as written. Use all three tiers of practice (on-level, stretch, challenge) as appropriate. Ensure the student can explain their reasoning — not just compute answers — before moving forward.

### For Students Ready for Acceleration
- Explore thousandths: What would 1/1000 look like? How would you write it as a decimal?
- Investigate patterns: What happens when you multiply 0.1 × 10? 0.01 × 10? 0.01 × 100?
- Connect to percentages: 0.75 = 75/100 = 75%. How does this work?
- Challenge problems in each lesson provide enrichment without accelerating to Grade 5 content.

## Cross-Curricular Connections

- **Science:** Measurements in science (length in centimeters, mass in grams) routinely involve decimals. If the student is doing any science work, connect decimal readings from rulers or scales to this unit.
- **Real life — money:** Every purchase, price comparison, and change calculation reinforces hundredths. Involve the student in real shopping tasks during this unit.
- **Real life — sports:** Batting averages, race times, and many other sports statistics use decimal notation to the thousandths — a natural extension conversation.
