# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 5, Lesson 1

## Topic
Introduction to Tenths

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Divide a whole into 10 equal parts and name each part as one tenth (1/10)
- Shade a 10×10 grid and a tenths strip to represent any fraction with denominator 10
- Place fractions with denominator 10 on a number line from 0 to 1
- Write the fraction form for a given number of tenths (e.g., 7 tenths = 7/10)

## Prerequisites
- Understands fractions as equal parts of a whole (numerator/denominator meaning)
- Can place unit fractions (1/2, 1/4) on a number line
- Can generate equivalent fractions (Grade 4 Unit 4)

## Materials Needed
- 10×10 grid paper (at least 4 grids printed or drawn)
- A tenths strip (a long rectangle divided into 10 equal sections — can be cut from grid paper)
- Number line template: 0 to 1, with 10 equal intervals marked
- Colored pencils or crayons (one color)
- Pencil and plain paper

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of fractions and equal partitioning.

Ask the student:
1. "If I cut a pizza into 4 equal slices and eat 3, what fraction did I eat?" (3/4)
2. "Draw a rectangle and shade 2/5 of it."
3. "What does the denominator of a fraction tell us?" (How many equal parts the whole is divided into)

Review the student's drawing. Confirm that the rectangle is divided into 5 equal parts with 2 shaded. If the parts are unequal, reinforce: "The parts must be exactly equal — that is what makes it a fraction."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build the concept of tenths through concrete and pictorial models before introducing the notation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Tenth:** One of ten equal parts of a whole. Written as 1/10.
- **Denominator:** The bottom number of a fraction; tells how many equal parts the whole is divided into.
- **Numerator:** The top number of a fraction; tells how many of those parts are being counted.
- **Tenths strip:** A rectangle divided into exactly 10 equal parts.

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete phase (tenths strip):**
Place the tenths strip in front of the student. Count the sections together: "1, 2, 3, … 10. This strip is divided into 10 equal parts. Each part is called one tenth. I can write one tenth as the fraction 1/10."

Point to one section: "This is 1/10 of the whole strip." Point to three sections: "If I cover 3 sections, that is 3/10 of the strip."

Have the student shade sections of the strip to represent 2/10, 5/10, and 9/10 as you name them.

**Representational phase (10×10 grid):**
Show the student a 10×10 grid. "This grid is our whole. It has 100 small squares. But look at the rows — there are 10 rows, and each row has 10 squares. So one whole row is 1/10 of the entire grid."

Shade 1 full row (10 cells): "This shaded row is 1/10." Shade 3 full rows: "Now I've shaded 3/10."

Ask: "If I shade 7 full rows, what fraction is shaded?" (7/10)

**Number line phase:**
On the 0-to-1 number line, show the 10 equal intervals. "Each interval is 1/10. Starting at 0 and jumping 1 tenth at a time: 1/10, 2/10, 3/10, …, 10/10 = 1."

Plot 4/10 together: count 4 intervals from 0. Ask the student to plot 7/10 independently.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Write the fraction for "6 tenths."
- "6 tenths means 6 out of 10 equal parts."
- Write: 6/10
- On the tenths strip: shade 6 sections.
- On the 10×10 grid: shade 6 complete rows.

**Example 2:** A number line goes from 0 to 1 with 10 equal marks. A point is placed at the 4th mark. What fraction does the point represent?
- Count the marks from 0: the 4th mark is 4 intervals from 0.
- Each interval = 1/10.
- Answer: 4/10

**Example 3:** Maria ate 3 slices of a licorice stick that was cut into 10 equal pieces. What fraction did she eat?
- 10 equal pieces → denominator is 10.
- 3 pieces eaten → numerator is 3.
- Answer: 3/10

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together, with the facilitator asking questions and the student doing the shading/labeling:

1. Shade the 10×10 grid to show 5/10. How many complete rows did you shade?
2. On a tenths strip, which fraction is halfway between 0 and 1? Shade it.
3. Look at this 10×10 grid: [2 rows shaded]. What fraction is shaded? What fraction is NOT shaded?
4. Place these fractions on the number line: 1/10, 6/10, 9/10.
5. A juice bottle is divided into 10 equal servings. If 4 servings have been poured out, what fraction of the bottle has been poured? What fraction remains?

**Facilitation questions:**
- "How do you know the parts are equal?"
- "How does the denominator tell you how to draw the model?"
- "Where on the number line is 5/10? What other name do we know for 5/10?" (1/2 — connect to prior knowledge)

---

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Shade a 10×10 grid to represent each fraction: a) 2/10  b) 8/10  c) 1/10  d) 10/10
2. Write the fraction (denominator 10) for each description:
   a) 3 tenths     b) 7 tenths     c) 9 tenths     d) 10 tenths
3. Place each fraction on a 0-to-1 number line divided into tenths: 3/10, 6/10, 1/10, 9/10
4. A garden has 10 rows. 4 rows are planted with tomatoes. Write the fraction of the garden planted with tomatoes.

**Stretch:**
5. On a 10×10 grid, shade 6/10. Then write: what fraction of the grid is NOT shaded?
6. Order these fractions from least to greatest: 7/10, 2/10, 10/10, 4/10, 1/10
7. A ribbon is 10 inches long. Kezia cuts off 3 inches. What fraction of the ribbon is left?

**Challenge:**
8. Can 5/10 be written as a simpler fraction? How do you know? (Connect to Unit 4 equivalent fractions)
9. I am thinking of a fraction with denominator 10. It is greater than 4/10 but less than 6/10. What is it?
10. How many different fractions with denominator 10 are there between 0 and 1 (not including 0 and 1)? List them all.

### Interleaved Review
R1. *(Unit 4 — Equivalent Fractions)* Write two fractions equivalent to 2/4.
R2. *(Unit 3 — Multiples)* List the first five multiples of 6.
R3. *(Unit 1 — Multiplication)* Find: 23 × 4 = ___

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Shade a 10×10 grid to show 8/10.
2. A recipe uses 10 equally-sized cups of flour. Dani has poured in 5 cups so far. Write the fraction of flour that has been added.
3. Place 3/10 on this number line: [0 ——————————— 1, with 10 marks]

**Mastery = all 3 correct (shading accurate and fraction notation correct)**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the tenths strip. Have the student physically count and shade sections while saying the fraction aloud.
- Ask: "How many equal parts does the whole have? So what is the denominator?" Build up to writing the fraction symbol from spoken language.
- Repeat the "grid rows" activity with different numbers: "Shade 4 rows. What fraction is that? Write it."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask the bonus question: "Is 5/10 equal to 1/2? How could you prove it using the grid?"
- Preview Lesson 2: "Tomorrow we'll break each tenth into 10 even smaller pieces — and name those."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Real-world tenths hunt:** Have the student find at least 3 real-world examples of things divided into 10 parts (a dime ruler, egg carton with 10 slots, a 10-frame in a math book, 10 fingers). For each, describe what "1/10" would mean in that context.

**Pattern exploration:** Fill in the pattern on a number line and notice: 1/10, 2/10, 3/10, … Is there a pattern in the numerators? What happens at 10/10?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- Today is entirely about fraction meaning — no decimal notation yet. Resist introducing the "0.1" symbol even if the student asks. Say: "We'll get to that way of writing it in a few lessons. For now, let's make sure we really understand what tenths are."
- The most common error today is drawing unequal parts. Reinforce that equal parts are essential.
- 5/10 = 1/2 is a natural connection — validate it if the student notices it, but do not require it.
- If the student has strong fraction recall from Unit 4, this lesson may move quickly. That is fine — the real conceptual work begins in Lesson 2 when hundredths are introduced.
