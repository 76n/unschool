# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 5, Lesson 3

## Topic
Equivalent Fractions with Denominators 10 and 100

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Express any fraction with denominator 10 as an equivalent fraction with denominator 100 (e.g., 3/10 = 30/100)
- Express any fraction with denominator 100, when possible, as an equivalent fraction with denominator 10
- Explain why these fractions are equivalent using grid models and multiplication reasoning
- Identify which fractions with denominator 100 cannot be simplified to tenths

## Prerequisites
- Can represent tenths and hundredths on a 10×10 grid (Lessons 1–2)
- Can generate equivalent fractions by multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number (Unit 4)
- Knows multiplication facts for 10

## Materials Needed
- 10×10 grid paper (at least 4 grids)
- Colored pencils (two contrasting colors)
- Pencil and paper

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review equivalent fractions procedure from Unit 4 and connect to today's work.

1. "What does it mean for two fractions to be equivalent?" (They name the same amount; they represent the same part of the whole)
2. "Find an equivalent fraction for 1/2 by multiplying numerator and denominator by 2." (2/4)
3. "Find an equivalent fraction for 2/3 by multiplying numerator and denominator by 3." (6/9)
4. Quick review: "What is 4 × 10?" (40)  "What is 7 × 10?" (70)  "What is 10 × 10?" (100)

Tell the student: "Today we'll use the same equivalent fraction strategy — but specifically between fractions with denominator 10 and fractions with denominator 100."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Establish the rule that multiplying or dividing both numerator and denominator by 10 converts between tenths and hundredths, and demonstrate this visually on the 10×10 grid.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Equivalent fractions:** Two fractions that represent the same portion of a whole (same point on the number line, same area of a model).
- **10-to-100 conversion:** Multiplying numerator and denominator of a tenths fraction by 10 gives the equivalent hundredths fraction.

### Instruction Notes

**Visual demonstration:**
Shade 3 full rows on a 10×10 grid (one color). "This is 3/10."
Now, without adding more shading, look at individual cells: "How many cells are shaded?" (30) "So the same amount of grid is also 30/100."
Write: 3/10 = 30/100.

"What happened to the numerator? 3 → 30 (multiplied by 10). What happened to the denominator? 10 → 100 (multiplied by 10). We multiplied both by the same number — 10 — so the value didn't change."

**The rule — tenths to hundredths:**
> To convert a fraction with denominator 10 to an equivalent fraction with denominator 100: multiply both the numerator and denominator by 10.
> Example: 4/10 × (10/10) = 40/100

**The reverse — hundredths to tenths:**
"Can we go the other direction?" Show 20/100. "20 cells out of 100. That's 2 full rows. So 20/100 = 2/10."
"What happened? 20 ÷ 10 = 2; 100 ÷ 10 = 10. We divided both by 10."

"But can we do this for every fraction with denominator 100? Try 37/100."
"37 ÷ 10 = 3.7 — not a whole number! So 37/100 cannot be written as a whole-number tenths fraction. It stays as 37/100."

**Key insight:** Only hundredths fractions whose numerator is a multiple of 10 (10, 20, 30, 40, …) can be converted to tenths.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Convert 6/10 to hundredths.
- Multiply numerator and denominator by 10:
  6 × 10 = 60; 10 × 10 = 100
- Answer: 6/10 = 60/100
- Grid check: 6 rows = 60 cells. Confirmed.

**Example 2:** Convert 70/100 to tenths.
- Divide numerator and denominator by 10:
  70 ÷ 10 = 7; 100 ÷ 10 = 10
- Answer: 70/100 = 7/10
- Grid check: 70 cells = 7 full rows. Confirmed.

**Example 3:** Can 45/100 be written as a fraction with denominator 10?
- 45 ÷ 10 = 4.5 — not a whole number.
- No, 45/100 cannot be expressed as a whole-number fraction with denominator 10.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together:

1. Convert to hundredths: a) 2/10  b) 9/10  c) 5/10
2. Convert to tenths (if possible): a) 30/100  b) 60/100  c) 55/100
3. On a 10×10 grid, shade 4/10. Then write the same amount as a fraction with denominator 100.
4. Circle the fractions that are equivalent to 8/10: a) 80/100  b) 8/100  c) 800/1000  d) 88/100
5. Fill in the missing number: ___ /100 = 2/10

**Facilitation questions:**
- "What do we multiply the denominator by to get from 10 to 100? What must we do to the numerator?"
- "Why can we multiply by 10/10 without changing the value?" (Because 10/10 = 1, and multiplying by 1 doesn't change a number)
- "How can you check your answer using the grid?"

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Convert each fraction from tenths to hundredths:
   a) 1/10     b) 4/10     c) 7/10     d) 10/10
2. Convert each fraction from hundredths to tenths (if possible; write "cannot" if not):
   a) 50/100     b) 20/100     c) 43/100     d) 90/100
3. Fill in the blanks:
   a) 8/10 = ___/100
   b) ___/100 = 5/10
   c) 3/10 = ___/100
   d) 10/100 = ___/10
4. Are 6/10 and 60/100 equivalent? Show using a grid or by multiplying.

**Stretch:**
5. A class survey: 3/10 of students chose cats as their favorite pet. Write this as a fraction with denominator 100. If there are 100 students total, how many chose cats?
6. Find the missing value: 4/10 = ___/100 = ___/1000 (hint: what do you multiply by to get to 1000?)
7. Which of these are equivalent to 9/10? Circle all that apply: 90/100, 900/1000, 9/100, 81/90

**Challenge:**
8. Explain in words why you cannot write 37/100 as a fraction with denominator 10, but you CAN write it as a fraction with denominator 1000. What would it be?
9. I have a fraction with denominator 100. When I divide the numerator by 10, I get 6. What is the original fraction? What is it in tenths?
10. Create your own example: write a fraction with denominator 10, convert it to hundredths, and draw the grid to verify.

### Interleaved Review
R1. *(Unit 4 — Comparing Fractions)* Compare using >, <, or =: 2/3 ___ 3/4
R2. *(Unit 1 — Multiplication)* Use the area model to find: 24 × 13
R3. *(Unit 3 — Prime/Composite)* Is 17 prime or composite? Explain.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Convert 5/10 to hundredths. Show your work.
2. Convert 40/100 to tenths. Show your work.
3. Can 33/100 be written as a fraction with denominator 10? Write "yes" or "no" and explain in one sentence.

**Mastery = all 3 correct**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Walk through the multiplication step explicitly: "The denominator needs to go from 10 to 100. What do I multiply 10 by to get 100? [10] If I do that to the denominator, what must I also do to the numerator? [multiply by 10 also]"
- For the reverse: "What do I divide 100 by to get 10? [10] If I do that to the denominator, what must I do to the numerator? [divide by 10]"
- Use the grid: "Count the shaded rows. That is the tenths fraction. Count all shaded cells. That is the hundredths fraction."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Pose this question: "We can convert between /10 and /100 by multiplying or dividing by 10. What if we needed a fraction with denominator 1000? How might that work?"
- Preview Lesson 4: "Now that we can convert between tenths and hundredths, we're ready to learn a shorthand way of writing these fractions — called decimals."

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Fraction chain:** Start with 1/10. Convert it to hundredths (10/100). Now convert to thousandths: 10/100 = 100/1000. Continue the pattern. What do you notice about the numerators and denominators?

**True or false — and explain:** "All fractions with denominator 100 are equivalent to a fraction with denominator 10." (False — only those with numerators that are multiples of 10.)

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- This lesson is a hinge point. The skill of converting between /10 and /100 underpins everything in Lessons 4–9. If a student struggles here, spend extra time before proceeding.
- The multiplication connection (multiply both by 10) ties directly to the equivalent fraction work from Unit 4. Explicitly name this connection: "We did this same move in Unit 4 — we just didn't know we'd need it for decimals yet."
- Students may ask why we can only divide when the numerator is a multiple of 10. This is a good conceptual question: because fractions require whole numbers in numerator and denominator (for now). Affirm the question and explain.
- For the conversion 10/100 → 1/10: some students will be surprised that 10/100 and 1/10 are the same. Reinforce with the grid: 10 cells = 1 row.
