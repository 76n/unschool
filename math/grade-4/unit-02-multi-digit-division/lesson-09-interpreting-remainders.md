# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic

Interpreting Remainders in Word Problems

## Learning Objectives

- Students will determine what to do with a remainder based on the context of a word problem.
- Students will identify three ways to handle a remainder: ignore it, round the quotient up, or report the remainder as the answer.
- Students will justify their choice using the problem's context.

## Prerequisites

- Long division with remainders (Lessons 5, 7, 8)
- Ability to solve division word problems

## Materials Needed

- Whiteboard and markers (teacher)
- Student scratch paper
- Word problem sorting cards (optional — see Guided Practice)

**Standards:** 4.OA.3 — Solve multistep word problems with whole numbers, including interpreting remainders.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Highlight that the same division problem can have different real-world answers depending on context.

Present this situation:
"23 students need to cross a river. Each canoe holds 4 students."
- Calculate: 23 ÷ 4 = 5 R3

Ask three questions about this same calculation:
1. "How many canoes are completely full?" (5 canoes — ignore the remainder)
2. "How many canoes are needed in total?" (6 canoes — round up, because the 3 remaining students still need a canoe)
3. "How many students are in the last canoe?" (3 students — the remainder IS the answer)

Say: "Same math, three different answers. The context tells you what to do with the remainder."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary

- **Ignore the remainder:** The answer is just the quotient (whole groups only matter).
- **Round up:** Add 1 to the quotient (you need an extra group for the leftovers).
- **Remainder is the answer:** The question specifically asks about what's left over.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Type 1: Ignore the remainder (drop it).**
Problem: "A ribbon is 25 inches long. How many 4-inch pieces can you cut?"
- 25 ÷ 4 = 6 R1
- You can cut 6 complete pieces. The remaining 1 inch isn't enough for another piece.
- Answer: **6 pieces.**

Ask: "Why don't we say 7?" (Because you can't make a 7th full piece from just 1 inch.)

**Step 2 — Type 2: Round up the quotient.**
Problem: "There are 25 students going on a field trip. Each van holds 4 students. How many vans are needed?"
- 25 ÷ 4 = 6 R1
- 6 vans hold 24 students, but there's 1 more student. That student still needs a ride!
- Answer: **7 vans.**

Ask: "Would it be okay to leave 1 student behind?" (No! We need one more van even though it won't be full.)

**Step 3 — Type 3: The remainder is the answer.**
Problem: "25 stickers are shared equally among 4 children. How many stickers are left over?"
- 25 ÷ 4 = 6 R1
- The question asks specifically about leftovers.
- Answer: **1 sticker left over.**

**Step 4 — Strategy for deciding.**
Write on the board:

| Ask yourself... | Then... |
|----------------|---------|
| "Can I only use complete groups?" | Ignore the remainder (quotient only) |
| "Does everyone/everything need to be included?" | Round up (quotient + 1) |
| "Is the question asking about the leftovers?" | The remainder is the answer |

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** "A baker has 130 cookies. He puts 8 cookies in each bag. How many full bags can he make?"
- 130 ÷ 8 = 16 R2
- The question asks for "full bags" — only complete groups count.
- Answer: **16 full bags.** (Ignore the remainder.)

**Example B:** "130 people need to take an elevator that holds 8 people. How many trips does the elevator need to make?"
- 130 ÷ 8 = 16 R2
- All 130 people need to ride. The 2 remaining people need one more trip.
- Answer: **17 trips.** (Round up.)

**Example C:** "130 marbles are divided equally among 8 children. How many marbles are left undistributed?"
- 130 ÷ 8 = 16 R2
- The question asks about what's left over.
- Answer: **2 marbles.** (Remainder is the answer.)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

For each problem, solve the division and decide how to handle the remainder. Explain your reasoning.

1. "A teacher has 47 books to place equally on 5 shelves. How many books go on each shelf?"
   - 47 ÷ 5 = 9 R2 → **9 books per shelf** (ignore the remainder — you're asking about complete equal groups; the 2 extra books don't go on a specific shelf equally)

2. "47 students need chairs. Chairs come in packs of 5. How many packs must the school buy?"
   - 47 ÷ 5 = 9 R2 → **10 packs** (round up — every student needs a chair)

3. "A rope is 47 feet long. It is cut into 5-foot pieces. How many feet of rope are left over?"
   - 47 ÷ 5 = 9 R2 → **2 feet** (remainder is the answer)

4. "A parking lot has 83 cars. Each row holds 9 cars. How many rows are completely full?"
   - 83 ÷ 9 = 9 R2 → **9 full rows** (ignore the remainder)

5. "83 plants need to be placed in rows of 9. How many rows are needed to fit all the plants?"
   - 83 ÷ 9 = 9 R2 → **10 rows** (round up)

**Facilitator tip:** For each problem, ask: "What happens to the leftover if we ignore it? Does that make sense in this situation?"

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A: Solve and Interpret

For each problem, perform the division and interpret the remainder. Write your answer as a complete sentence.

1. A factory packs 6 toys per box. There are 157 toys. How many boxes are completely full?

2. 157 students need to ride buses that hold 6 students each. How many buses are needed?

3. 157 beads are shared equally among 6 friends. How many beads does each friend get, and how many are left over?

4. A roll of fabric is 200 inches long. A craft project needs 7-inch strips. How many complete strips can be cut?

5. 200 children need to be divided into teams of 7 for a relay race. How many full teams can be formed?

6. After forming teams of 7 from 200 children, how many children are waiting without a team?

### Part B: Sort the Problems

Read each problem and write whether you would "ignore," "round up," or "remainder is answer."

1. "Pies come in boxes of 3. We need 20 pies. How many boxes do we buy?" → ___
2. "20 pencils shared among 3 students. How many does each get?" → ___
3. "20 tiles laid in rows of 3. How many complete rows?" → ___
4. "20 tiles laid in rows of 3. How many tiles in the incomplete row?" → ___

### Answer Key

**Part A:**
1. 157 ÷ 6 = 26 R1. **26 boxes** are completely full. (Ignore remainder.)
2. 157 ÷ 6 = 26 R1. **27 buses** are needed. (Round up.)
3. 157 ÷ 6 = 26 R1. Each friend gets **26 beads**, and **1 bead** is left over.
4. 200 ÷ 7 = 28 R4. **28 complete strips** can be cut. (Ignore remainder.)
5. 200 ÷ 7 = 28 R4. **28 full teams** can be formed. (Ignore remainder — the question asks for "full teams.")
6. 200 ÷ 7 = 28 R4. **4 children** are waiting without a team. (Remainder is the answer.)

**Part B:**
1. Round up (you need enough boxes for all 20 pies → 7 boxes, since 20 ÷ 3 = 6 R2)
2. Ignore (each student gets 6, with 2 left over — the question asks per student)
3. Ignore (6 complete rows)
4. Remainder is answer (2 tiles)

## Assessment Check

Present: "A school has 250 students. Buses hold 8 students each. How many buses are needed for everyone?"

**Target response:** 250 ÷ 8 = 31 R2. We need 32 buses because the 2 remaining students still need a bus. (Student identifies the "round up" interpretation and justifies it.)

## Extension

- Challenge: Write your own set of three word problems that all use the division 100 ÷ 7 = 14 R2, where each problem requires a different interpretation of the remainder.
- Discuss: "Can you think of a real-life situation where you'd express the remainder as a fraction?" (e.g., sharing 7 pizzas among 4 people — each gets 1 3/4 pizzas. Preview of fractions unit.)
