# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6, Lesson 4

## Topic
Adding and Subtracting Angle Measures (Angle Addition Postulate)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Understand that angle measure is additive: when adjacent angles share a side, their measures add together to form the larger angle
- Use addition to find the total measure of combined adjacent angles
- Use subtraction to find the measure of an unknown part of an angle
- Write and solve equations to find missing angle measures in diagrams

## Prerequisites
- Measuring angles with a protractor (Lesson 3)
- Classifying angles (Lesson 2)
- Writing and solving simple equations with unknowns (Grade 3/4 operations)

## Materials Needed
- Protractors
- Rulers
- Pencils
- Graph paper or plain paper
- Prepared diagrams showing angles split into two parts
- Colored pencils (to shade the two parts of an angle)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review angle measurement and activate understanding of part-whole relationships.

Draw an angle on the board. Then draw a ray inside the angle that splits it into two smaller angles. Label the two smaller angles.

Ask:
1. "What is the measure of the left part?" (Measure it together with a protractor.)
2. "What is the measure of the right part?" (Measure it together.)
3. "If we added those two measurements together, what would we expect?" (The total angle.)
4. Verify: measure the total angle with the protractor. Is the sum correct?

Tell students: "This is the idea for today — when two angles share a side and sit next to each other, their measures add together like pieces of a puzzle."

---

## Direct Instruction (13 minutes)
**Purpose:** Formalize the angle addition concept and connect it to equation writing and solving.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Adjacent angles:** Two angles that share a common vertex and a common side, and do not overlap.
- **Angle addition:** The principle that the measure of a whole angle equals the sum of the measures of its parts.
- **Unknown angle:** An angle whose measure we do not know; we solve for it using what we do know.
- **Equation:** A mathematical statement that two expressions are equal (e.g., 50° + n = 120°).

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete:** Use physical materials.
- Draw a large angle (about 120°) on paper. Cut it out.
- Draw a line from the vertex to the edge, splitting it into two smaller angles.
- Color one part blue and one part orange.
- Show: the blue angle + the orange angle = the whole angle.
- Rearrange the pieces: cover one part and ask "What's missing?" → subtraction.

**Representational:** Draw diagrams with labeled angles:

Diagram 1 — Both parts known, find the total:
```
Two adjacent angles: one measures 45°, the other measures 35°
Total angle = 45° + 35° = 80°
```

Diagram 2 — Total known, one part known, find the other:
```
Total angle = 110°
One part = 70°
Other part = ?
70° + ? = 110°
? = 110° - 70° = 40°
```

Diagram 3 — Angles on a straight line:
```
Two angles together form a straight line (180°)
One angle = 65°
Other angle = 180° - 65° = 115°
```

**Abstract:** Introduce the equation format:
- If angle A and angle B are adjacent and together form angle C:
  - m∠A + m∠B = m∠C
  - m∠C - m∠A = m∠B

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** A large angle is split into two parts. One part measures 48°. The total angle measures 97°. What is the measure of the other part?
- Write: 48° + n = 97°
- Solve: n = 97° - 48° = 49°
- Check: Is 97° obtuse? Yes. Does 48° + 49° = 97°? Yes.

**Example 2:** Two adjacent angles share a ray. One measures 32°, the other measures 58°. What is the combined angle?
- Write: 32° + 58° = n
- n = 90°
- This is a right angle — the two angles are complementary (they add to 90°).

**Example 3:** Three adjacent angles share a vertex and together form a straight angle (180°). Two of the angles measure 60° and 75°. What is the third?
- Write: 60° + 75° + n = 180°
- 135° + n = 180°
- n = 180° - 135° = 45°
- Check: 60° + 75° + 45° = 180°. Correct.

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through together. Students set up and solve the equation, then verify.

1. Two adjacent angles form a right angle (90°). One measures 34°. What does the other measure?
   34° + n = 90°; n = 56°

2. An angle measures 130°. A ray divides it into two parts. One part is 80°. What is the other?
   80° + n = 130°; n = 50°

3. Two adjacent angles lie along a straight line (180°). One angle is 115°. What is the other?
   115° + n = 180°; n = 65°

4. Three angles together form a right angle. Two of them are 25° and 40°. What is the third?
   25° + 40° + n = 90°; 65° + n = 90°; n = 25°

5. James draws a large angle. He says it measures 135°. He splits it into two parts. One part measures 72°. What should the other part measure? Is the whole angle obtuse?
   n = 135° - 72° = 63°; Yes, 135° is obtuse.

6. An angle along a straight line is divided into 3 equal parts. What does each part measure?
   180° ÷ 3 = 60°; each part is 60°.

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Find the unknown angle measure. Write an equation first.
   a. Two adjacent angles form a right angle. One is 51°. The other is _____.
   b. A straight angle is split into two parts. One part is 112°. The other is _____.
   c. Two adjacent angles form a 145° angle. One is 90°. The other is _____.
   d. Three adjacent angles sum to 180°. Two are 70° and 40°. The third is _____.

2. A park has a triangular section. The architect draws two of the angles: 65° and 75°. She needs to find the third angle. The three angles of a triangle always add up to 180°. What is the third angle?
   65° + 75° + n = 180°; n = 40°

3. A carpenter cuts a piece of wood at an angle. The full angle at the cut is a straight angle. One side of the cut measures 130°. What does the other side measure?
   n = 50°

4. Look at the diagram. A large angle of 100° is divided by a ray into two parts. The left part is labeled 37°. What is the right part?
   100° - 37° = 63°

**Stretch:**
5. Two angles are adjacent and together form a straight angle. One angle is twice the other. What are the two angle measures?
   Let the smaller = n. Then 2n + n = 180°; 3n = 180°; n = 60°. Angles: 60° and 120°.

6. A full rotation is 360°. A sprinkler rotates and covers three adjacent sections: 90°, 115°, and an unknown section. After the three sections, the sprinkler has completed a full 360° rotation. Wait — the problem says there are only three sections. The total is 90° + 115° + n = 360°. Find n.
   n = 360° - 205° = 155°

**Challenge:**
7. The angles in a triangle always sum to 180°. One angle in a triangle is a right angle (90°). The other two angles are equal. What is each of the other two angles?
   90° + n + n = 180°; 2n = 90°; n = 45° each.

8. A pizza is cut into 6 equal slices. Each slice is a sector of a full circle (360°). What is the angle of each slice? If one slice is removed, what angle is left?
   Each slice: 360° ÷ 6 = 60°. After removing one: 5 × 60° = 300°, or 360° − 60° = 300°.

### Interleaved Review
R1. A rectangle is 8 cm long and 5 cm wide. What is the area? (G3/Unit 1: area)
   Answer: 40 cm²

R2. Write 0.7 as a fraction. (Unit 5: decimals and fractions)
   Answer: 7/10

R3. List all multiples of 8 up to 80. (Unit 3: multiples)
   Answer: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, 48, 56, 64, 72, 80

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Two adjacent angles form a straight line. One angle measures 73°. Write an equation and solve for the other angle.

2. A large angle of 95° is split into two parts. One part is 42°. What is the other part? Show your work.

**Mastery = student writes correct equations (73° + n = 180° and 42° + n = 95°) and solves correctly (n = 107° and n = 53°).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the concrete cut-out model: "We know the whole and one piece. We cover the unknown piece. The equation is: known piece + unknown = whole."
- Have student practice just setting up equations (not solving) for 2–3 additional diagrams
- Confirm arithmetic accuracy — errors may be in the subtraction, not the concept
- Use a number line or counting back to support subtraction

### If Mastery Is Met
- Pose a two-step problem: "Three angles together form a straight angle. The middle angle is 3 times the size of the left angle. The right angle measures 50°. Find the left and middle angles." (Left = n, middle = 3n; n + 3n + 50° = 180°; 4n = 130°; n = 32.5° — note this is not a whole number, which makes it challenging)
- Preview Lesson 5: "We will look at triangles, which always have angles that sum to 180°."

## Extension (Optional)
**Design a Star:** Students draw a 5-pointed star. They measure all the angles at the points and all the interior angles. They investigate whether any of the angle relationships follow addition rules.

**Angle Equations Gallery Walk:** Create 6–8 different angle diagrams on large paper posted around the room. Students rotate to each diagram, write an equation, and solve for the unknown angle. Compare answers with a partner.

**Make Your Own Problem:** Students draw a large angle, split it into two or three parts with rays, label all but one angle measure, and challenge a partner to find the unknown.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- This lesson connects geometry to algebra in a concrete way: students see that a visual diagram leads directly to an equation. Emphasize the translation process (diagram → equation → solve → check).
- Always encourage students to verify by adding all parts — this checking habit catches arithmetic errors and reinforces the concept.
- The straight angle (180°) context is very powerful because students can visually verify: the two angles together should look like a flat line.
- Some students will be tempted to measure the unknown angle directly with a protractor rather than solving algebraically. Allow this as a check, but insist that they also write and solve the equation — the algebraic method is the standard.
- The extension to three angles summing to 180° previews triangle angle sums in Lesson 5. Make this connection explicit.
- Watch for arithmetic errors: subtraction is the primary operation, and multi-digit subtraction (e.g., 180° − 73°) sometimes requires regrouping. Address these as calculation errors distinct from conceptual errors.
- Students who struggled with Lesson 3 protractor use may benefit from having reference diagrams with angles pre-labeled rather than needing to measure.
