# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6, Lesson 1

## Topic
Points, Lines, Rays, and Segments

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define and identify points, lines, line segments, and rays
- Use correct notation to name each geometric figure (e.g., line AB, ray CD, segment EF)
- Identify examples of lines, segments, and rays in real-world contexts
- Draw each type of figure and label it correctly

## Prerequisites
- Recognizing basic shapes from Grade 3
- Understanding the concept of straight vs. curved
- Familiarity with using a ruler to draw straight lines

## Materials Needed
- Rulers (one per student)
- Plain white paper (2 sheets per student)
- Pencils
- Colored pencils (optional, for distinguishing figures)
- Index cards for vocabulary reference
- Anchor chart paper or whiteboard for class diagrams

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge about shapes and lines; set the stage for geometric vocabulary.

Ask students: "Look around the classroom. Can you find anything that looks perfectly straight? Something that has a definite start and end point? Something that seems to go on forever in both directions?"

Give students 2 minutes to look and note observations. Take 3–4 responses and draw rough sketches on the board as students describe what they see. This surfaces informal understanding that will be formalized during instruction.

**Prompt to record:** Have students write one example each of: something with a definite start AND end, something that starts at one point and goes on forever in one direction, something that goes on forever in both directions.

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce and formalize the four foundational geometric figures with precise vocabulary and notation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Point:** An exact location in space; it has no size. Named with a capital letter (e.g., point A).
- **Line:** A straight path that extends infinitely in both directions; no endpoints. Named with two points on the line and a double-headed arrow: ↔AB (line AB).
- **Line segment:** A part of a line with two endpoints. Named with its two endpoints and a bar: AB̄ (segment AB).
- **Ray:** A part of a line that has one endpoint and extends infinitely in one direction. Named starting with the endpoint first: →AB (ray AB, starting at A going through B).
- **Endpoint:** A point at the end of a segment or the starting point of a ray.

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete:** Begin with physical demonstrations.
- Stretch a piece of string between two hands — that is a segment (it has two endpoints: your hands).
- Extend one hand out and ask students to imagine the string going forever past your other hand — that is a ray.
- Imagine the string continuing forever past BOTH hands — that is a line.
- A single dot on the board — that is a point.

**Representational:** Draw and label each figure on the board as you discuss it.
```
Point A:     •A

Line AB:     •←————————————→•
             A               B

Segment AB:  •————————————•
             A               B

Ray AB:      •————————————→
             A               (starts at A, goes through B forever)
```

Emphasize notation:
- Lines and rays get arrows to show direction
- Line: arrows on BOTH ends
- Ray: arrow on ONE end (the non-endpoint end)
- Segment: no arrows (both ends are closed)

**Abstract:** Introduce formal notation symbols. Explain that mathematicians use shorthand:
- Line through A and B: written as AB with a double-headed arrow above it
- Segment from A to B: written as AB with a bar above it
- Ray starting at A through B: written as AB with a single arrow above it pointing right
- Point: just the capital letter

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Draw and label a ray that starts at point C and goes through point D.
- Draw a point, label it C
- Draw a point further right, label it D
- Draw an arrow starting at C, going through D and continuing
- This is ray CD (C is the endpoint, written first)

**Example 2:** Draw segment EF where E and F are 6 cm apart.
- Use a ruler to draw a 6 cm line
- Mark and label both endpoints
- Note: segment EF and segment FE name the same segment

**Example 3:** Look at a diagram with multiple figures. Identify each:
```
   P•————————————→Q         [This is ray PQ]
   R•————————•S              [This is segment RS]
   ←—————•T•—————→          [This is a line through T]
```

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Students attempt each, then share and discuss.

1. Draw point M on your paper. Now draw line segment MN where N is about 5 cm to the right.

2. Draw ray XY where X is on the left and Y is to the right. How is your drawing different from segment XY?

3. A road on a map goes from City A to City B. Does the road best represent a point, line, line segment, or ray? Why?

4. The edge of a ruler — what geometric figure does it best represent? (Accept "line segment" — it has two ends — or "line" with justification that it could extend.)

5. Look at the letter "T." Identify the line segments that make up this letter. Name the endpoints for each.

6. Name these figures from the notation:
   - AB with a bar above: _______
   - CD with a double arrow above: _______
   - EF with a single right arrow above: _______

**Answers:**
3. Line segment — it has two definite endpoints (the cities)
4. Line segment — has two endpoints (the ends of the ruler)
6. Segment AB; Line CD; Ray EF

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Draw each of the following and label with correct notation:
   a. Point J
   b. Ray JK (J is the starting point)
   c. Segment LM
   d. Line NO

2. For each description, write the correct geometric term (point, line, line segment, or ray):
   a. A laser beam shooting from a flashlight into the night sky
   b. The tip of a pencil touching paper
   c. A clothesline stretched between two poles
   d. The path of a train track shown on a map between two stations
   e. The edge of a table top

3. True or False:
   a. A line segment has two endpoints. (T)
   b. A ray has two endpoints. (F — it has one)
   c. A line has no endpoints. (T)
   d. Ray AB and Ray BA are the same ray. (F — they go in opposite directions)
   e. You can measure the length of a line. (F — it is infinite)

**Stretch:**
4. Gemma draws ray PQ. Marcus draws ray QP. They use the same two points. Explain why these are different rays.

5. A sunbeam comes from the sun, passes through a window, and hits a spot on the floor. Which geometric figure best models the sunbeam? Justify your answer.

6. Can a point be on more than one line? Draw an example to support your answer.

**Challenge:**
7. How many different line segments can you draw using exactly 3 points (A, B, C) if no three points are on the same line? Name them all.

8. Two rays share the same endpoint. What figure do they form? Draw two examples.

### Interleaved Review
R1. A store sells pencils in boxes of 24. How many pencils are in 15 boxes? (Unit 1: multi-digit multiplication)

R2. Write 3/4 as an equivalent fraction with a denominator of 12. (Unit 4: equivalent fractions)

R3. Round 4,758 to the nearest hundred. (prior rounding knowledge)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Draw a ray that starts at point A and goes through point B. Label it correctly.

2. What is the difference between a line and a line segment? Write one sentence.

3. Which figure has exactly one endpoint: a line, a line segment, or a ray?

**Mastery = student correctly draws and labels the ray, writes a correct distinction (line goes on forever/segment has two ends), and identifies ray as the figure with exactly one endpoint.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the "string" concrete model — stretch a string to show segment, then let one end go to show ray, then let both ends go to show line
- Use the vocabulary index card with diagrams as a reference
- Have student draw each figure next to the definition before trying problems again
- Pair with a student who has demonstrated mastery for a brief peer explanation

### If Mastery Is Met
- Extend: "Can a ray and a line segment share an endpoint? Draw an example."
- Begin thinking about: where do two rays meet? (preview of angles in Lesson 2)

## Extension (Optional)
**Geometry in the Real World:** Give students an old magazine or newspaper. Ask them to find and circle examples of:
- Points (isolated dots, period marks, stars in night sky photos)
- Line segments (edges of buildings, stripes, letter shapes)
- Rays (beams of light, arrows in diagrams)
- Lines (horizon, road lanes extending into distance)

Create a collage labeling each example.

**Letter Geometry:** Analyze capital letters of the alphabet. Which letters are made entirely of line segments? (E, F, H, I, L, T, etc.) Which contain rays? Which approximate lines?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- This lesson establishes vocabulary for the entire unit — do not rush. Spending time on precise language now prevents confusion throughout.
- The most common confusion is between ray and line segment. Repeatedly emphasize: ray has ONE endpoint (the starting point) and ONE end with an arrow; segment has TWO endpoints and NO arrows.
- Watch for students who draw ray AB with the arrow on the wrong end (pointing back toward A rather than away from A). Reinforce: the endpoint is where the ray STARTS; the arrow shows where it GOES (forever).
- When naming a ray, the endpoint ALWAYS comes first in the name. This is different from segments (where order doesn't matter) and lines (where order doesn't matter).
- The notation with bars and arrows above letters can be introduced informally — it is more important that students can correctly draw, identify, and describe the figures than that they master the notation symbols in this lesson.
- Connect to architecture: buildings have edges (segments), laser levels shoot rays, a plumb line hangs from a point — geometry is everywhere.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:**

2. Ray has one arrow extending from X through Y; segment has endpoints at both X and Y
3. Line segment — has two definite endpoints (the cities)
4. Line segment — has two endpoints
5. "T" has a vertical segment (bottom to top) and a horizontal segment (left to right), meeting at the center-top
6. Segment AB; Line CD; Ray EF

**Independent Practice:**
1. Student drawings — check for correct arrows/no arrows and labels
2a. Ray (starts at the flashlight, goes forever into the sky)
2b. Point
2c. Line segment (two endpoints: the poles)
2d. Line segment (two endpoints: the stations)
2e. Line segment (two endpoints: the corners of the table edge)
3a. True
3b. False
3c. True
3d. False
3e. False

4. Ray PQ starts at P and goes through Q, continuing forever past Q. Ray QP starts at Q and goes through P, continuing forever past P — they go in opposite directions from different starting points.

5. Ray — it begins at the sun (one endpoint / source) and travels in one direction forever (or at least past the window and floor); it doesn't end at the floor, it just hits it there.

6. Yes — any point can be on infinitely many lines. Student drawings should show multiple lines all passing through the same point.

7. Three segments: AB, BC, AC (or equivalently BA, CB, CA — same segments, different naming order). Total: 3 segments.

8. Two rays sharing an endpoint form an angle. Students should draw two examples (e.g., an acute angle and an obtuse angle) — preview for Lesson 2.
