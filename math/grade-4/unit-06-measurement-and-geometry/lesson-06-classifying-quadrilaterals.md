# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6, Lesson 6

## Topic
Classifying Quadrilaterals (Parallelogram, Rectangle, Rhombus, Square, Trapezoid)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define quadrilateral and identify its properties (4 sides, 4 angles, sum of angles = 360°)
- Identify and define: parallelogram, rectangle, rhombus, square, and trapezoid
- Classify quadrilaterals by properties: parallel sides, perpendicular sides, equal sides, right angles
- Understand hierarchical classification: a square IS a rectangle IS a parallelogram
- Identify parallel and perpendicular lines within 2D figures (4.G.1)

## Prerequisites
- Classifying angles (Lesson 2)
- Understanding parallel and perpendicular from classroom experience (or introduce here)
- Triangle classification as a model for property-based classification (Lesson 5)

## Materials Needed
- Rulers (one per student)
- Protractors (one per student)
- Pattern blocks (squares, rhombuses, rectangles if available, trapezoids, hexagons)
- Colored pencils
- Index cards or sticky notes for sorting activity
- Shape classification hierarchy poster (to build during lesson)
- Plain paper for drawing

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce parallel and perpendicular lines within shapes; preview lesson vocabulary.

Draw several four-sided shapes on the board:
- A rectangle
- A tilted parallelogram
- A trapezoid
- An irregular quadrilateral

Ask: "What do all of these have in common?" (4 sides, 4 corners/angles)

Then ask: "Are any of the sides parallel?" Review parallel lines: lines that never meet, always the same distance apart. Model with two rulers held side by side.

"Are any of the sides perpendicular?" Perpendicular lines: lines that meet at a right angle. Model with two rulers making an L.

Tell students: "Today we are going to classify these four-sided shapes — quadrilaterals — using their properties, just like we classified triangles."

---

## Direct Instruction (15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce each quadrilateral type with precise properties and build the hierarchy.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Quadrilateral:** A polygon with exactly 4 sides and 4 angles. The sum of the interior angles is always 360°.
- **Parallel lines:** Lines in the same plane that never intersect; always the same distance apart. Marked with matching arrows on sides.
- **Perpendicular lines:** Lines that intersect at exactly 90°. Marked with a small square.
- **Parallelogram:** A quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel sides. Opposite sides are equal. Opposite angles are equal.
- **Rectangle:** A parallelogram with four right angles. Opposite sides are equal.
- **Rhombus:** A parallelogram with four equal sides. Opposite angles are equal.
- **Square:** A rectangle with four equal sides (also a rhombus with four right angles). All four sides equal AND all four angles = 90°.
- **Trapezoid:** A quadrilateral with exactly one pair of parallel sides. (Note: some definitions use "at least one pair" — we use "exactly one pair" at this level.)
- **Kite:** A quadrilateral with two pairs of consecutive equal sides (optional — introduce for enrichment only).

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete:** Use pattern blocks for hands-on exploration.
- Have students handle a rhombus pattern block and a square pattern block side by side
- Measure sides with a ruler: "Both have all equal sides."
- Use index card corner to test angles: "The square has all right angles; the rhombus has two acute and two obtuse angles."
- This makes the distinction between rhombus and square concrete.

Use the trapezoid pattern block: "Count the pairs of parallel sides. Only one pair — top and bottom are parallel, the two sides are not."

**Representational:** Build the hierarchy diagram on the board as you introduce each shape:

```
           QUADRILATERAL
           (4 sides, 4 angles)
                 |
         ________|_________
        |                   |
   TRAPEZOID           PARALLELOGRAM
   (1 pair              (2 pairs of
   parallel sides)      parallel sides)
                              |
                    __________|___________
                   |                      |
               RECTANGLE              RHOMBUS
             (all right angles)    (all equal sides)
                   |                      |
                   |______________________|
                              |
                           SQUARE
                    (all right angles
                     AND all equal sides)
```

**Key hierarchy points to emphasize:**
- "A square IS a rectangle — it has all the properties of a rectangle, PLUS equal sides."
- "A square IS a rhombus — it has all the properties of a rhombus, PLUS right angles."
- "Every rectangle IS a parallelogram."
- "Every rhombus IS a parallelogram."
- "A square IS all of: square, rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, AND quadrilateral."

**Abstract:** Properties table:

| Shape | Parallel sides | Right angles | Equal sides |
|-------|---------------|--------------|-------------|
| Parallelogram | 2 pairs | Not necessarily | Opposite pairs equal |
| Rectangle | 2 pairs | All 4 | Opposite pairs equal |
| Rhombus | 2 pairs | Not necessarily | All 4 equal |
| Square | 2 pairs | All 4 | All 4 equal |
| Trapezoid | 1 pair | Not necessarily | Varies |

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** A shape has 4 equal sides and 4 right angles. Classify it as specifically as possible.
- 4 right angles → rectangle
- 4 equal sides → rhombus
- Both → square (also rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram, quadrilateral)

**Example 2:** A shape has 2 pairs of parallel sides, opposite sides equal, but angles are NOT right angles (they are about 60° and 120°).
- 2 pairs parallel sides → parallelogram
- Not all right angles → not rectangle
- Sides not all equal → not rhombus
- Classification: parallelogram (non-special)

**Example 3:** Can a trapezoid also be a parallelogram?
- Trapezoid has EXACTLY one pair of parallel sides
- Parallelogram has TWO pairs of parallel sides
- They are mutually exclusive under this definition: no.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Sort and classify together.

1. Pattern block rhombus: "How many pairs of parallel sides? (2) Are all sides equal? (Yes) Are any angles 90°? (No) What is it?" → Rhombus (not a square, not a rectangle)

2. Pattern block square: "Same tests." → Square (and therefore also rectangle, rhombus, parallelogram)

3. The capital letter "D" has a curved side — is it a quadrilateral? (No — one side is curved, not a straight line segment.)

4. A baseball diamond: "What shape is it?" (Square — all sides 90 feet, all corners right angles.) "Is it also a parallelogram? A rectangle?" (Yes to both.)

5. Name all the quadrilateral types that a rectangle belongs to. (Rectangle, parallelogram, quadrilateral — and if it also has equal sides, then also rhombus and square.)

6. True or False: "All squares are rectangles." (True — every square has 4 right angles, which is the key requirement of a rectangle.)
   "All rectangles are squares." (False — a rectangle doesn't need all equal sides.)

7. Draw a quadrilateral that is NOT a parallelogram and NOT a trapezoid. (An irregular quadrilateral with no parallel sides.)

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Classify each shape as specifically as possible (use the most specific name):
   a. 4 sides, 4 right angles, all sides 5 cm
   b. 4 sides, 4 right angles, sides 3 cm and 7 cm (alternating)
   c. 4 sides, opposite sides parallel, all sides 4 cm, angles 70° and 110°
   d. 4 sides, only one pair of parallel sides
   e. 4 sides, no parallel sides, no right angles

2. List all the categories each shape belongs to:
   a. Square → ___
   b. Rectangle (non-square) → ___
   c. Rhombus (non-square) → ___
   d. Trapezoid → ___

3. Circle the properties that a parallelogram ALWAYS has:
   a. 4 right angles
   b. 2 pairs of parallel sides
   c. 4 equal sides
   d. Opposite sides equal
   e. Opposite angles equal

4. Draw an example of each (label with shape name):
   a. Parallelogram (that is NOT a rectangle or rhombus)
   b. Trapezoid

**Stretch:**
5. A rectangle has a perimeter of 30 cm. Its length is 9 cm. What is its width? What kind of rectangle is it if the width equals the length? (Width = 30 - 18 = 12 cm... wait: 2l + 2w = 30; 2(9) + 2w = 30; 2w = 12; w = 6 cm. It would be a square if length = width = 7.5 cm, but here length ≠ width, so it is just a rectangle.)

6. A shape has 4 equal sides and opposite angles equal, but the angles are 80° and 100°. What is it? (Rhombus — not a square, since angles are not 90°.)

7. Explain why a square is the most "specific" quadrilateral — it belongs to more categories than any other quadrilateral type.

**Challenge:**
8. A quadrilateral has angles of 90°, 90°, 70°, and 110°. Can it be a parallelogram? Why or why not?
   (No — in a parallelogram, opposite angles are equal. Here: 90° and 70° are opposite; 90° and 110° are opposite. These are not equal pairs. And the angles 90+90+70+110 = 360°, which is correct for a quadrilateral, but the unequal opposite angles rule out parallelogram. It could be a trapezoid.)

9. Draw the shape classification hierarchy from memory, starting with quadrilateral at the top.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Name the most specific shape: "4 sides, 4 right angles, all sides equal." (Square)

2. True or False: "A square is a rectangle." Explain your answer in one sentence.

3. What is the difference between a rhombus and a square?

**Mastery = student correctly names square, correctly says "True — a square has four right angles, which is what makes it a rectangle," and correctly states the difference (a square has right angles; a rhombus may not).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the hierarchy diagram with hands-on pattern blocks: "If I pick up the square, it also fits the description of a rectangle. Let me show you why."
- Focus first on the simpler distinction: parallelogram vs. trapezoid (number of parallel sides)
- Use a sorting activity where students physically place shape cards in the hierarchy

### If Mastery Is Met
- Pose: "Is there a quadrilateral that is both a rectangle and a rhombus but is NOT a square? Explain." (No — if all four angles are 90° AND all four sides equal, it IS a square by definition.)
- Research: "Where do you see quadrilaterals in architecture? Which types appear most often?" (Rectangles and squares dominate building design; parallelograms and trapezoids appear in rooflines and decorative elements.)

## Extension (Optional)
**Architecture Connection:** Show photographs of famous buildings. Students identify all the quadrilateral types they can find. Bonus: identify any triangles from Lesson 5 as well.

**Venn Diagram Sort:** Create a large Venn diagram with overlapping regions for "4 right angles" and "4 equal sides." Students place quadrilateral type cards in the correct region:
- Only right angles: rectangle (non-square)
- Only equal sides: rhombus (non-square)
- Both: square
- Neither: parallelogram (non-special), trapezoid, irregular

**Quadrilateral Construction:** Using a ruler and protractor, students construct each quadrilateral type with exact measurements. For example: "Construct a rectangle with length 8 cm and width 5 cm" and verify with the protractor that all angles are 90°.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The hierarchy is the most important and often most difficult idea. Students are often resistant to the claim that "a square is a rectangle." The key is to say: "Check the definition of rectangle. Does a square meet it? Yes — so it IS a rectangle." The issue is students often think of "rectangle" as meaning "not square."
- The hierarchy poster built during this lesson should remain displayed through the rest of the unit (and ideally for the year). Reference it during area/perimeter lessons.
- Parallel sides can be tested with a ruler: measure the perpendicular distance between the two sides at two different points; if the distances are equal, the sides are parallel. This is a great concrete check.
- Right angles can be tested with the index card corner from Lesson 2.
- The trapezoid definition varies by source. Some define it as "at least one pair of parallel sides" (which would make parallelograms special trapezoids); others define it as "exactly one pair." This lesson uses "exactly one pair" to keep the hierarchy clean at Grade 4.
- Pattern blocks are very effective in this lesson because students can handle, compare, and sort them physically. If pattern blocks are not available, cardboard cut-out shapes work equally well.
