# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6, Lesson 9

## Topic
Area of Composite Shapes (L-Shapes and Combining Rectangles)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define a composite shape as a shape made by combining two or more rectangles
- Find the area of an L-shaped figure using the addition method (split into two rectangles)
- Find the area of an L-shaped figure using the subtraction method (complete rectangle minus cutout)
- Determine missing dimensions from given information and labeled diagrams
- Apply composite area to real-world contexts (floor plans, plots of land)

## Prerequisites
- Area and perimeter of rectangles (Lesson 8)
- Multi-digit multiplication (Unit 1)
- Addition and subtraction of multi-digit numbers

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper (1 cm grid)
- Colored pencils (2 colors — for the two-rectangle split method)
- Rulers
- Prepared diagrams of composite shapes with some dimensions labeled
- Scissors (optional — cut shapes apart to visualize splitting)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review area of rectangles and ensure readiness for combining multiple rectangle areas.

Quick-fire review: students write answers on paper or mini-whiteboards.
1. Area of a rectangle: l = 8 m, w = 5 m → 40 m²
2. Area of a rectangle: l = 12 cm, w = 7 cm → 84 cm²
3. Area of a rectangle: l = 15 m, w = 9 m → 135 m²

Then show an L-shaped figure on graph paper. "Count the squares." Students count and get a specific number.

"Today we learn to find the area of shapes like this without counting every single square — by breaking them into rectangles we already know how to solve."

---

## Direct Instruction (13 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach two strategies for composite area (addition and subtraction methods) using CRA progression.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Composite shape:** A shape made by combining two or more simpler shapes (in this lesson, always rectangles).
- **Addition method:** Split the composite shape into two (or more) separate rectangles; find each area; add the areas together.
- **Subtraction method:** Imagine completing the composite shape into a full rectangle; find the area of the full rectangle; subtract the area of the "missing" piece.
- **Missing dimension:** A side length that is not labeled but can be calculated from the other given measurements.

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete:** Give students a paper L-shape (pre-cut or drawn on graph paper). Have them:
- Cut it into two rectangles with scissors (addition method)
- Count the squares in each piece
- Verify that piece 1 area + piece 2 area = total area (by counting all squares first)

Then give them the same L-shape but NOT on graph paper. "How will you find the area now? We need to use the dimensions."

**Representational:** Draw a standard L-shape on the board with labeled dimensions:

```
      ___
     |   |  4 m
     |   |___________
     |               | 3 m
     |_______________|
        10 m
    (left segment: 4 m wide)
```

Finding missing dimensions:
- Total height: 4 + 3 = 7 m? No — the shape needs to be read carefully.
- Always label ALL dimensions before calculating. Identify what is given and what can be inferred.

**Method 1: Split into two rectangles (Addition)**
```
         4 m
        ____
       |    | 4 m     ←  Rectangle 1
       |    |
       | 4m |_______________
       |                    | 3 m   ← Rectangle 2
       |____________________|
              10 m
```
- Rectangle 1: Determine its dimensions from the overall figure
- Rectangle 2: Determine its dimensions
- Area = Area 1 + Area 2

**Method 2: Complete rectangle minus cutout (Subtraction)**
```
       __________________
      |         |        |
      |  FULL   | CUT    |
      | RECT.   | OUT    |
      |_________|        |
      |                  |
      |__________________|
```
- Full rectangle: overall width × overall height
- Cutout rectangle: the "missing" piece
- Area = Full area - Cutout area

**Note:** Both methods give the same answer. Students may prefer either method; teach both and let students choose.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 (L-shape, Addition Method):**
```
         ___
        |   | 5 m (height of top piece)
    4 m |   |
        |   |________
        |            | 3 m (height of bottom piece)
        |____________|
              8 m (total width)
        2 m          (width of left portion — given or calculated)
```

Given: total width = 8 m, top portion width = 4 m, top height = 5 m, bottom height = 3 m
Missing: bottom piece width = 8 − 4 = 4 m? Wait — need to read the specific dimensions.

Use a clearly labeled diagram. Suppose:
- Top rectangle: 4 m wide × 5 m tall → area = 20 m²
- Bottom rectangle: 8 m wide × 3 m tall → area = 24 m²
- Total area = 20 + 24 = 44 m²

**Example 2 (L-shape, Subtraction Method):**
Same shape as Example 1.
- Full rectangle: 8 m wide × (5+3) m tall = 8 × 8 = 64 m²
- Cutout rectangle (top right): width = 8 − 4 = 4 m, height = 5 m → area = 4 × 5 = 20 m²
- Total area = 64 − 20 = 44 m² ✓ Same answer!

**Example 3:** Finding a missing dimension.
An L-shape has total width 12 cm and total height 10 cm. The notch cut out is 5 cm wide and 6 cm tall. Find the area.
- Full rectangle: 12 × 10 = 120 cm²
- Cutout: 5 × 6 = 30 cm²
- Area = 120 − 30 = 90 cm²

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through each problem together. Students draw the shape, identify the method, and solve.

1. **L-shape:** Total dimensions 10 cm × 8 cm. A 4 cm × 3 cm rectangle is removed from one corner.
   - Subtraction: 10 × 8 − 4 × 3 = 80 − 12 = 68 cm²
   - Addition: two rectangles (student determines dimensions after splitting); answer should be 68 cm²

2. **Plus (+) shape:** (Optional extension) A plus sign made by two overlapping rectangles.
   - 9 m tall × 3 m wide vertical bar; 3 m tall × 9 m wide horizontal bar.
   - Add: vertical (9×3=27) + horizontal (9×3=27) = 54; BUT the center (3×3=9) is counted twice → 54 − 9 = 45 m²
   - This is more complex — introduce only if students are ready.

3. **Real world:** A school cafeteria floor plan looks like an L. The full rectangle would be 20 m × 15 m. A storage room (8 m × 7 m) cuts into one corner.
   - Area = 20 × 15 − 8 × 7 = 300 − 56 = 244 m²

4. **Missing dimensions:** An L-shape. One dimension is not labeled. Given: total height = 9 m, top height = 4 m. What is the missing bottom height?
   - 9 − 4 = 5 m

5. A family plans to tile their bathroom. The floor is L-shaped with dimensions (from diagram). Calculate the area to determine how many tiles are needed.

## Independent Practice (13 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Find the area of each L-shape. Show your work and label the method you used (addition or subtraction).

   **Shape A:** Total width = 12 m, total height = 10 m. A rectangular notch of 5 m × 4 m is removed from the top-right corner.
   Area = 12 × 10 − 5 × 4 = 120 − 20 = 100 m²

   **Shape B:** (Described as two rectangles joined):
   - Top rectangle: 6 cm wide × 3 cm tall
   - Bottom rectangle: 10 cm wide × 5 cm tall
   Area = (6×3) + (10×5) = 18 + 50 = 68 cm²

   **Shape C:** Total width = 15 m, total height = 8 m. Notch: 6 m × 3 m from top-left corner.
   Area = 15 × 8 − 6 × 3 = 120 − 18 = 102 m²

2. Find the missing dimension in this L-shape:
   - Total width = 14 cm
   - Left section width = 6 cm
   - Right section width = ? (Answer: 14 − 6 = 8 cm)

3. A backyard is L-shaped. The overall space would be a 20 m × 16 m rectangle, but the garage (8 m × 6 m) cuts into one corner. What is the area of the backyard?
   Area = 20 × 16 − 8 × 6 = 320 − 48 = 272 m²

**Stretch:**
4. A room is L-shaped. Using the addition method, split it into two rectangles in a DIFFERENT way than the subtraction method would suggest. Verify you get the same answer both ways.

5. A decorative rug is L-shaped with total dimensions 8 ft × 9 ft, with a 3 ft × 4 ft corner notch. If the rug costs $15 per square foot, what is the total cost?
   Area = 8×9 − 3×4 = 72 − 12 = 60 ft². Cost = 60 × $15 = $900

6. Two L-shaped garden beds both have the same perimeter. Do they necessarily have the same area? Investigate with two different L-shapes.
   (They do not necessarily have the same area — the "same perimeter, different area" concept from Lesson 8 extends here.)

**Challenge:**
7. Design your own L-shaped room with an area of exactly 80 square meters. Your room must have all whole-number dimensions. Show your work.

8. A staircase seen from above creates a stair-step shape made of 3 rectangles: top step (2 m × 1 m), middle step (4 m × 1 m), bottom step (6 m × 1 m). What is the total area of the staircase?
   Area = (2×1) + (4×1) + (6×1) = 2 + 4 + 6 = 12 m²

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Describe in words how you would find the area of an L-shaped figure using the subtraction method.

2. An L-shape has overall dimensions 9 m × 7 m. A 3 m × 2 m notch is cut from one corner. What is the area of the L-shape?

**Mastery = student correctly describes the subtraction method (complete the rectangle, find the missing piece area, subtract) and correctly computes 9 × 7 − 3 × 2 = 63 − 6 = 57 m².**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to graph paper: draw the actual L-shape, shade it in, count the squares
- Then connect each square to the formulas: "You just counted — now let's see how the formula gets you to the same number"
- Focus on one method (addition) and practice 2–3 simpler problems (smaller numbers) before attempting the subtraction method
- Common error: forgetting to find missing dimensions before calculating. Review "finding missing dimensions" explicitly.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Pose: "What if the notch is in the MIDDLE of a side rather than at a corner? Can you still use these methods?" (Yes — this creates a U-shape, which requires more careful splitting.)
- Preview: "In Lesson 10, we will review all the geometry from this unit. Start thinking about which topics felt most comfortable and which you want to practice more."

## Extension (Optional)
**Floor Plan Project:** Students design a floor plan for a small apartment (living room, kitchen, one bedroom). At least one room must be L-shaped. They calculate the area of each room and the total area.

**Tiling a Pool Deck:** A rectangular pool (6 m × 4 m) sits in the center of a rectangular yard (12 m × 10 m). Students calculate the deck area (yard minus pool) and determine how many tiles are needed.

**Architecture Connection:** Show photographs of buildings with non-rectangular floor plans (L-shaped buildings, T-shaped buildings). Students identify the component rectangles and estimate the total floor area.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The key skill in this lesson is finding missing dimensions. Students often forget that not all sides are labeled and that they must calculate unlabeled dimensions from the given information. Always have students label ALL dimensions before calculating.
- Both the addition and subtraction methods produce the same result. Students who struggle with one method should be guided toward the other. Neither method is preferred, but the subtraction method often requires fewer calculations for complex shapes.
- When drawing composite shapes for problems, always provide enough dimensions to determine the shape completely. A good rule: provide at least n−1 dimensions for an n-sided shape where some are derivable.
- Graph paper is essential as a check: after computing algebraically, students can draw the shape on graph paper and count squares to verify. This prevents and catches errors.
- For the "finding missing dimensions" step: the total side = sum of the parts. For L-shapes: if the total width is 12 m and one portion is 7 m, the other portion is 5 m. Practice this explicitly — it is a recurring source of error.
- The problem in Guided Practice item 2 (plus sign / cross shape) requires subtracting an overlap. This is more sophisticated and should only be introduced with confident students. The standard lesson focuses on simple L-shapes.
- Real-world problems (rooms, gardens, floors) make this lesson very engaging. If possible, measure the classroom or a hallway together to create a genuine composite area problem.
