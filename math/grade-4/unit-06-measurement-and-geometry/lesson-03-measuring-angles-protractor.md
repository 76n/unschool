# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6, Lesson 3

## Topic
Measuring Angles with a Protractor

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the parts of a protractor (baseline, center point, inner and outer scales)
- Measure an angle to the nearest degree using a protractor
- Draw an angle of a given degree measure using a protractor and ruler
- Estimate angle measures before measuring to check reasonableness

## Prerequisites
- Classifying angles as acute, right, obtuse, or straight (Lesson 2)
- Identifying rays and their endpoints (Lesson 1)
- Basic ruler use

## Materials Needed
- Protractors (one per student — standard 180° semicircular protractor)
- Rulers (one per student)
- Plain white paper (multiple sheets)
- Pencils
- Index card right angle reference from Lesson 2
- Prepared practice worksheet with pre-drawn angles of various sizes

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Reinforce angle classification as an estimation skill before measuring; build the habit of estimating before measuring.

Present 4 angles drawn on the board or a handout. Ask students to:
1. Classify each as acute, right, obtuse, or straight (using their index card reference)
2. Make a degree estimate: "About how many degrees do you think this angle is?"

Discuss estimates briefly — reinforce:
- Acute: between 0° and 89°
- Right: exactly 90°
- Obtuse: between 91° and 179°
- Straight: exactly 180°

"Today we learn to measure exactly. But estimating first is an important habit — it will help you catch mistakes when using the protractor."

---

## Direct Instruction (15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach accurate protractor use through step-by-step demonstration, addressing the dual-scale confusion explicitly.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Protractor:** A tool used to measure angles in degrees.
- **Baseline (base line):** The flat, straight edge of the protractor, used to align with one ray of the angle.
- **Center point:** The small hole or mark at the midpoint of the baseline; placed on the vertex of the angle.
- **Inner scale:** The row of numbers that reads from right to left (0° on the right, 180° on the left).
- **Outer scale:** The row of numbers that reads from left to right (0° on the left, 180° on the right).
- **Degree (°):** The unit for measuring angles; a full circle = 360°.

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete:** Hold up the protractor and identify each part while students follow along with their own protractors.
- Show: baseline (the flat bottom edge)
- Show: center point (the small mark in the middle of the baseline)
- Show: the two scales (inner and outer) — both go from 0 to 180, but in opposite directions

**Steps for measuring an angle:**

**Step 1: Place the protractor.**
- Put the center point exactly on the vertex (corner) of the angle.
- Line up the baseline with one ray of the angle.

**Step 2: Read the correct scale.**
- Find where the ray you are NOT aligned with crosses the scale.
- IMPORTANT: Use the scale that starts at 0° for the ray you aligned.
- If you aligned the baseline along the bottom ray and used the LEFT end as 0°, use the OUTER scale.
- If you aligned the baseline along the bottom ray and used the RIGHT end as 0°, use the INNER scale.
- Tip: First classify the angle. If it is acute, your reading should be less than 90°. If obtuse, more than 90°. This catches scale errors.

**Step 3: Record the measurement.**
- Read the number where the second ray crosses the scale.
- Write the measure followed by the degree symbol (°).

**Representational:** Draw a large angle on the board. Walk through all three steps aloud and deliberately, reading the measurement. Then show what happens if you accidentally read the WRONG scale — demonstrate how classification helps catch the error.

**Abstract:** The final recorded angle measure is a number with a degree symbol: e.g., m∠A = 47° (read as "the measure of angle A is 47 degrees").

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Measure an acute angle that is approximately 40°.
- Place center on vertex, align baseline with bottom ray
- The second ray crosses the scale at... if using the correct scale: 40°
- Check: "Is 40° acute? Yes — it is less than 90°. This seems right."

**Example 2:** Measure an obtuse angle that is approximately 130°.
- Place center on vertex, align baseline
- The second ray crosses: if wrong scale, might read 50°. But 50° is acute, not obtuse!
- Correct scale reads 130°. "130° is obtuse — this matches our classification."

**Example 3:** Draw an angle that measures 65°.
- Draw a ray on your paper
- Place the protractor with center on the endpoint of the ray, baseline along the ray
- Find 65° on the correct scale; make a mark at that point
- Remove the protractor; draw a second ray from the endpoint through the mark
- Label the angle 65°

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Each student has a worksheet with 4 pre-drawn angles. Work through them together:

1. **Angle A** (pre-drawn, approximately 35°):
   - Step 1: What type of angle is this? (acute)
   - Step 2: Place protractor and measure
   - Step 3: Record measurement. Should be between 1° and 89°.

2. **Angle B** (pre-drawn, approximately 90°):
   - Type: right
   - Measure: should be very close to 90°

3. **Angle C** (pre-drawn, approximately 115°):
   - Type: obtuse
   - Measure: should be between 91° and 179°

4. **Angle D** (pre-drawn, approximately 160°):
   - Type: obtuse
   - Measure: should be between 91° and 179°

After measuring: "Check your answer — does your measurement match the type you predicted? If not, you may have used the wrong scale. Try again with the other scale."

Then draw together:
5. Draw an angle measuring 45° (acute — narrow)
6. Draw an angle measuring 120° (obtuse — wide)

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Measure each of the following pre-drawn angles (on worksheet):
   - Angle P (approximately 55°)
   - Angle Q (approximately 90°)
   - Angle R (approximately 145°)
   - Angle S (approximately 25°)
   - Angle T (approximately 170°)
   Before measuring each: write your estimate and classification.

2. Draw angles with these measures:
   a. 30°
   b. 75°
   c. 100°
   d. 155°
   Label each with its measure and type.

3. Circle the measurement that is most likely correct given the angle type:
   a. Acute angle: 47° OR 133°
   b. Obtuse angle: 68° OR 148°
   c. Right angle: 90° OR 180°

**Stretch:**
4. Lena measures an angle and gets 72°. She says it is obtuse. Is she correct? What type is it?

5. An architect draws a corner where two walls meet. She measures the angle as 95°. Is this a right angle, or is it slightly off? Is it acute or obtuse?

6. Using a protractor, draw a triangle (3 connected line segments) where one angle measures exactly 90°. Label all three angles with their measures. What do you notice about the other two angles? (They should sum to 90°; both should be acute.)

**Challenge:**
7. Draw a quadrilateral (4-sided shape) where all four angles measure exactly 90°. What is the sum of all four angles? What shape have you drawn?

8. The minute hand of a clock is at 12. What angle does it make with the hour hand at:
   a. 1:00 (30°)
   b. 3:00 (90°)
   c. 5:00 (150°)
   Measure each with a protractor after drawing the clock hands.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

A student measures an angle and reads 125°. The angle looks narrower than a right angle.

1. What type of angle does 125° represent? (obtuse)
2. What type of angle does the student's description suggest it looks like? (acute)
3. What mistake did the student likely make, and how should they fix it?

**Mastery = student identifies the inconsistency (125° is obtuse but student said it looks acute), explains that the student likely read the wrong scale, and describes the fix (use the scale that starts at 0° on the aligned ray; check that the answer matches the visual classification).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to concrete protractor work: have the student measure a right angle first (using the index card to verify) — this calibrates their protractor use
- Practice with 3–4 more guided measurements where the student states the classification before measuring each time
- Create a step-by-step cue card: (1) Classify. (2) Place center on vertex. (3) Align baseline. (4) Read scale where 0° is on the aligned ray. (5) Check: does my answer match my classification?

### If Mastery Is Met
- Practice drawing angles: "Draw three angles of your choice; label each with its measure and type"
- Begin thinking about Lesson 4: "If I know two angles together make a straight line (180°), and one is 70°, how would you find the other?"

## Extension (Optional)
**Protractor Art:** Students draw a design using only lines and angles. Each angle in the design must be measured and labeled. Finished pieces make a good bulletin board display showing precision measurement skills.

**Angle Hunt:** With facilitator supervision and a clipboard, students walk through the school building (or look at photographs of architecture) and use a protractor to measure real angles — corners of windows, roof peaks, handicap ramp slopes, etc.

**Degree Estimation Game:** Two students take turns drawing angles. Before measuring, both estimate. The player whose estimate is closer to the actual measurement earns a point.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The dual scale is THE primary source of protractor error. The key strategy is: always estimate type first, then check that the reading is consistent with the type.
- Allow ample time for students to handle the protractor. Physical manipulation is essential — reading about how to use a protractor is far less effective than trying it with actual angles.
- When students place the protractor, the most common physical errors are: (1) center point not on the vertex (slightly off), and (2) baseline not aligned with the ray. Both produce inaccurate readings. Circulate during guided practice to check placement.
- It can help to draw angles with rays that are 5–6 cm long so students can see clearly where the ray crosses the scale.
- Students may find it easier to always rotate the paper so that the bottom ray of the angle is aligned with the baseline direction — this is fine. The angle measure does not change with rotation.
- Precision: measuring to the nearest degree is the standard. Accepting answers within ±2° of the key is reasonable.
- This skill takes repeated practice. Consider beginning Lessons 4, 5, and 6 with a 2-minute angle measurement warm-up to maintain the skill.
