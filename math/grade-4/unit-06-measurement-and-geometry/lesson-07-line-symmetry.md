# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6, Lesson 7

## Topic
Line Symmetry (Identifying Lines of Symmetry in 2D Shapes)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define a line of symmetry as a line that divides a shape into two mirror-image halves
- Identify whether a shape has a line of symmetry by folding or visual inspection
- Identify the number of lines of symmetry in common shapes
- Draw lines of symmetry on given shapes
- Recognize that some shapes have no lines of symmetry

## Prerequisites
- Identifying 2D shapes and their properties (Lessons 5–6)
- Understanding reflections informally (from everyday experience with mirrors)
- Basic paper folding

## Materials Needed
- Plain white paper (multiple sheets for folding)
- Scissors
- Rulers
- Pencils and colored pencils
- Mirror (small, handheld) — optional but very effective
- Pre-printed or hand-drawn shapes on paper for folding
- Pattern blocks

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Surface informal understanding of symmetry from everyday experience; connect to geometric context.

Ask students: "Can you think of something that looks the same on both sides? Something where if you drew a line down the middle, both halves would match exactly?"

Accept responses: butterflies, faces, letters, buildings, leaves.

Then show two shapes:
- A shape that IS symmetric (e.g., a square)
- A shape that is NOT symmetric (e.g., a scalene triangle or irregular blob)

Ask: "Which of these could be folded so that both halves match perfectly? Let's test it!"

Fold the paper. Students observe: one shape's halves align; the other's do not.

"The fold line is called the line of symmetry. Today we learn how to find and draw these lines."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define line of symmetry formally and explore multiple lines of symmetry in regular shapes using CRA.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Line of symmetry:** A line that divides a figure into two congruent (mirror-image) halves. When folded along this line, the two halves match exactly.
- **Symmetric figure:** A figure that has at least one line of symmetry.
- **Asymmetric figure:** A figure that has no line of symmetry.
- **Mirror image:** The reflection of a figure; it looks exactly like the original, but reversed.
- **Congruent:** Having exactly the same shape and size.

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete:** Paper folding activity.
- Give students square paper. Have them fold it in half vertically — open it. The fold line is a vertical line of symmetry.
- Fold in half horizontally — that is another line of symmetry.
- Fold diagonally — two more lines of symmetry.
- A square has 4 lines of symmetry.

Repeat with:
- Rectangle (not square): vertical and horizontal fold lines match — 2 lines of symmetry. Diagonal fold does NOT match — not a line of symmetry.
- Circle: any fold through the center matches — infinite lines of symmetry.
- Scalene triangle: no fold makes both halves match — 0 lines of symmetry.

**Representational:** Draw shapes and their lines of symmetry on the board. Use dashed lines to show each line of symmetry:

```
Square: 4 lines         Rectangle: 2 lines      Equilateral triangle: 3 lines
   |      \     /           |                           /|\
   |       \   /            |                          / | \
   |        \ /          ---|---                      /  |  \
   |---------|                                       /___|___\
   |        / \
   |       /   \         Isosceles triangle: 1 line     Letter A: 1 line
   |      /     \             /|\                          /|\
                              / | \                        / | \
                             /  |  \                      /__|__\
                            /___|___\
                              (base not symmetric)
```

**Abstract:** Students can count lines of symmetry without folding, by visual inspection. Regular polygons:
- Equilateral triangle: 3 lines
- Square: 4 lines
- Regular pentagon: 5 lines
- Regular hexagon: 6 lines
- A regular polygon with n sides has n lines of symmetry.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Does the letter "A" have a line of symmetry?
- The vertical center line divides A into two mirror halves. Yes — 1 line of symmetry.

**Example 2:** Does the letter "S" have a line of symmetry?
- Try vertical: left half ≠ right half. Try horizontal: top ≠ bottom (it is close but reversed).
- S has no line of symmetry (but it does have rotational symmetry, not covered here).

**Example 3:** How many lines of symmetry does a regular hexagon have?
- A regular hexagon has 6 equal sides.
- 3 lines through opposite vertices + 3 lines through midpoints of opposite sides = 6 lines total.
- Or use the rule: n sides → n lines of symmetry.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. Fold and check each shape (or trace and check visually):
   - Isosceles triangle: 1 line of symmetry (the line from vertex to midpoint of base)
   - Scalene triangle: 0 lines
   - Rectangle (non-square): 2 lines
   - Regular pentagon: 5 lines

2. For each letter, determine how many lines of symmetry it has:
   A: 1, B: 1 (horizontal... actually B is debatable; accept 1 if student identifies horizontal), C: 1, D: 1, E: 1 (horizontal), H: 2, I: 2, M: 1, O: many/infinite, T: 1, U: 1, V: 1, W: 1, X: 2, Y: 1

3. Draw all lines of symmetry on a given square (4 lines) and an equilateral triangle (3 lines).

4. True or False:
   - A rectangle (not square) has a diagonal line of symmetry. (False — fold along diagonal and check: corners don't match.)
   - Every circle has a line of symmetry. (True — infinitely many.)

5. Does a parallelogram (non-rectangle) have a line of symmetry? Test by folding.
   (No — no line divides it into two matching halves.)

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. For each shape, write the number of lines of symmetry:
   a. Equilateral triangle: ___
   b. Square: ___
   c. Isosceles triangle (not equilateral): ___
   d. Scalene triangle: ___
   e. Rectangle (not square): ___
   f. Regular hexagon: ___
   g. Circle: ___

2. Draw all lines of symmetry on each shape:
   a. A square (draw 4 lines)
   b. An isosceles triangle (draw 1 line)
   c. A rectangle (draw 2 lines)

3. Which of the following shapes has NO line of symmetry?
   A) Square   B) Scalene triangle   C) Equilateral triangle   D) Regular pentagon

4. The letter "H" has a vertical line of symmetry and a horizontal line of symmetry. Draw the letter and show both.

**Stretch:**
5. Design a shape of your own that has exactly 2 lines of symmetry. It does not have to be a regular shape.

6. A butterfly wing is cut out from paper. The student folds it along a vertical line and the two sides match. What does this tell us about the design on the wing?
   (The design has vertical line symmetry — the markings on the left half are a mirror image of the markings on the right half.)

7. How many lines of symmetry does a regular octagon (8 equal sides) have? How do you know?
   (8 lines — 4 through opposite vertices + 4 through midpoints of opposite sides; also follows the rule n sides = n lines for regular polygons.)

**Challenge:**
8. Can a shape have exactly 3 lines of symmetry without being a regular polygon? Design one and explain.
   (Possible — e.g., an equilateral triangle, which is the simplest regular polygon with 3 lines, or a star with 3 points. Students may also draw abstract shapes.)

9. A quilt is made of 4 identical squares arranged in a 2×2 grid. Each small square has a diagonal design. Under what conditions would the entire quilt have a line of symmetry?
   (If the design within each square is arranged so that the mirror image relationship is maintained across the fold line — e.g., each pair of squares on either side of the fold line are mirror images of each other.)

### Interleaved Review
R1. A garden is 14 meters long and 9 meters wide. What is its area? Its perimeter? (G3/Unit: area and perimeter review)
   Answer: Area = 14 × 9 = 126 m²; Perimeter = 2(14) + 2(9) = 28 + 18 = 46 m

R2. Write 0.35 as a fraction in simplest form. (Unit 5: decimals to fractions)
   Answer: 35/100 = 7/20

R3. 3/5 + 1/5 = _____ (Unit 4: adding fractions with like denominators)
   Answer: 4/5

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. What is a line of symmetry? Write a definition in your own words.

2. How many lines of symmetry does an equilateral triangle have? Draw them.

3. Does a scalene triangle have any lines of symmetry? How do you know?

**Mastery = student writes a definition that captures the idea of folding/mirror image and two matching halves; correctly identifies 3 lines for equilateral triangle and draws them; correctly says scalene triangle has 0 lines with a valid explanation (no fold creates two matching halves, all sides are different).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Have student physically fold paper cut-outs of the shapes in question
- Use a small mirror: place the mirror along a proposed line of symmetry; does the reflection complete the shape?
- Focus on just one shape — the equilateral triangle — and explore it thoroughly before expanding
- Key question: "When I fold along this line, do both halves match EXACTLY?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Explore rotational symmetry (informal introduction): "Does the shape look the same if you rotate it before it completes a full turn?" Connect to regular polygons.
- Research: "Find examples of symmetry in Islamic geometric art, quilt patterns, or Aboriginal art. How many lines of symmetry do these patterns have?"

## Extension (Optional)
**Symmetry Art:** Students create a symmetry artwork by folding paper, making a cut-out design on one half, and unfolding to reveal a symmetric shape. Connect to paper snowflake making.

**Nature Symmetry:** Students examine photographs of butterflies, leaves, flowers, and insects. They identify the lines of symmetry and note whether natural objects are perfectly or approximately symmetric.

**Logo Design:** Students design a logo for a made-up company that must have at least 2 lines of symmetry. They identify and label the lines of symmetry on their finished design.

**Alphabet Symmetry Project:** Complete analysis of all 26 capital letters for lines of symmetry. Which letter(s) have the most? (O has infinite; H and I have 2 each.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- Paper folding is essential in this lesson — physical verification is more convincing than any verbal argument for whether a shape has a line of symmetry.
- The rectangle vs. square diagonal symmetry distinction is a common source of surprise: students expect the diagonal fold of a rectangle to work, but it does not (the corners don't match). This is a concrete illustration of why a rectangle is NOT a square — a square has diagonal lines of symmetry, a rectangle does not.
- A parallelogram (non-rectangle) has NO lines of symmetry. This surprises many students who expect it to have two. Physical folding confirms this.
- Some students may ask about the letter "B" — it depends on how precisely drawn it is. If perfectly drawn with a horizontal axis of symmetry, B has 1 line. In practice, accept student reasoning about specific letter designs.
- The "regular polygon: n sides = n lines of symmetry" rule is a powerful generalization. Encourage students to test it with several cases and then state it as a rule.
- Symmetry is deeply connected to art, design, nature, and architecture — make these connections explicit and enthusiastic.
- Some students may have already encountered symmetry informally through art or craft. Build on this experiential knowledge.
