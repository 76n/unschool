# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6, Lesson 5

## Topic
Classifying Triangles (by Sides and by Angles)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Classify triangles by their side lengths: equilateral, isosceles, scalene
- Classify triangles by their angle types: acute, right, obtuse
- Understand that a triangle can be classified by BOTH its sides AND its angles
- Use 4.G.2 to explain classification based on properties (parallel/perpendicular lines, angle types)

## Prerequisites
- Classifying individual angles (Lesson 2)
- Measuring angles with a protractor (Lesson 3)
- Angle addition and triangle angle sum = 180° (Lesson 4)
- Understanding that classification is based on properties, not appearance

## Materials Needed
- Rulers (one per student)
- Protractors (one per student)
- Colored pencils (3 colors for marking equal sides)
- Paper for drawing
- Pre-cut triangle shapes of various types (or triangle cards)
- Sorting mat or T-chart for classification

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate angle knowledge and preview triangle angle sum.

Show a triangle on the board. Ask students to measure all three angles with their protractors. Add the measurements. Ask: "What do you get? Try with a different triangle."

Students should find that all triangles have angles that sum to 180° (with small measurement errors accepted). Confirm: "This is always true — the three angles of any triangle always add up to 180°."

Use this to connect to Lesson 4: "Just like we found unknown angles using addition and subtraction, we can find a missing triangle angle if we know the other two."

---

## Direct Instruction (13 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce both classification systems (sides and angles) and show how they combine.

### Key Vocabulary

**By Sides:**
- **Equilateral triangle:** All three sides are equal in length. (Also: all three angles are equal — each is 60°.)
- **Isosceles triangle:** Exactly two sides are equal in length. The two angles opposite the equal sides are also equal.
- **Scalene triangle:** All three sides have different lengths. All three angles are also different.

**By Angles:**
- **Acute triangle:** All three angles are acute (less than 90°).
- **Right triangle:** Has exactly one right angle (90°). The other two angles are acute and add up to 90°.
- **Obtuse triangle:** Has exactly one obtuse angle (greater than 90°). The other two angles are acute.

**Notation:**
- **Tick marks:** Short marks drawn on sides to show equal lengths. One tick = one equal side, two ticks = another pair, etc.
- **Small square:** Drawn in the corner of a right angle.

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete:** Distribute triangle shape cards (or pre-cut triangles). Students handle each and:
1. Use a ruler to measure all three sides
2. Use a protractor to measure all three angles
3. Record measurements; classify by sides AND angles

**Representational:** Draw examples of each triangle type on the board with tick marks and angle labels:

```
Equilateral:       Isosceles:          Scalene:
    /\                 /\                  /\
   /  \               /  \                /  \
  /    \             /    \              /    \
 /______\           /______\            /______\
All 3 sides equal  2 sides equal        All different

Right triangle:    Acute triangle:     Obtuse triangle:
     |\              /\                  /\
     | \            /  \               /   \
     |__\          /____\             /____/
90° corner       all angles <90°    one angle >90°
```

**Combined classification example:** Draw a right isosceles triangle:
- Has a right angle (90°) → right triangle (by angles)
- Two equal legs → isosceles (by sides)
- So it is a "right isosceles triangle"

**Abstract:** Students use a two-way sorting table:

| | Acute | Right | Obtuse |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equilateral | Yes | No | No |
| Isosceles | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Scalene | Yes | Yes | Yes |

Note: An equilateral triangle cannot be right or obtuse because all angles are 60°.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** A triangle has sides of 5 cm, 5 cm, and 7 cm, and angles of 68°, 68°, and 44°.
- By sides: two sides equal → isosceles
- By angles: all angles less than 90° → acute
- Classification: acute isosceles triangle

**Example 2:** A triangle has sides of 3 cm, 4 cm, and 5 cm, and angles of 37°, 53°, and 90°.
- By sides: all different → scalene
- By angles: one 90° angle → right
- Classification: right scalene triangle

**Example 3:** Find the missing angle. A triangle has angles of 120° and 35°. What is the third angle?
- 120° + 35° + n = 180°
- n = 180° - 155° = 25°
- Classification: one angle is 120° (obtuse) → obtuse triangle

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through together. Students classify, then verify.

1. A triangle has angles of 60°, 60°, and 60°. All sides are equal.
   - By sides: equilateral
   - By angles: acute (all 60°)
   - Combined: equilateral acute triangle (or simply equilateral triangle)

2. A triangle has angles of 90°, 45°, and 45°. Two sides are equal (the legs).
   - By sides: isosceles
   - By angles: right
   - Combined: right isosceles triangle

3. A right triangle has one angle of 38°. Find the third angle.
   - 90° + 38° + n = 180°; n = 52°
   - What type of triangle? Right scalene (angles are all different: 90°, 38°, 52°)

4. Can a triangle be obtuse AND equilateral? Why or why not?
   - No — equilateral means all angles are 60°, which is acute.

5. A triangle has one side of 8 cm and two sides of 6 cm each. Two angles are 70° each. Find the third angle.
   - 70° + 70° + n = 180°; n = 40°
   - By sides: isosceles; By angles: acute (all < 90°)

6. Look at three triangles (drawn on board or worksheet). Classify each by sides and angles using measurement.

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Classify each triangle by BOTH sides and angles.
   a. Sides: 4 cm, 4 cm, 4 cm; Angles: 60°, 60°, 60°
   b. Sides: 5 cm, 5 cm, 8 cm; Angles: 72°, 72°, 36°
   c. Sides: 3 cm, 4 cm, 5 cm; Angles: 37°, 53°, 90°
   d. Sides: 6 cm, 9 cm, 11 cm; Angles: 31°, 50°, 99°

2. Find the missing angle in each triangle. Then classify the triangle by its angles.
   a. Angles: 45°, 45°, ___°
   b. Angles: 100°, 40°, ___°
   c. Angles: 60°, 70°, ___°
   d. Angles: 90°, 55°, ___°

3. True or False:
   a. An equilateral triangle is also an isosceles triangle. (T — it has at least two equal sides)
   b. A scalene triangle can be a right triangle. (T)
   c. A triangle can have two obtuse angles. (F)
   d. A right triangle always has two acute angles and one right angle. (T)

**Stretch:**
4. A right triangle has two equal legs. What are the measures of the three angles?
   90°, 45°, 45° (since the two acute angles must sum to 90° and are equal)

5. Draw a triangle and measure all three sides and all three angles. Classify it by both sides and angles. Verify the angles sum to 180°.

6. An architect designs a triangular roof support. The angles she needs are 50° and 70°. What is the third angle? What type of triangle is the support?
   n = 60°; all angles acute → acute scalene triangle (50°, 60°, 70° are all different)

**Challenge:**
7. Is it possible to have an isosceles right triangle? If yes, what must the two equal angles be?
   Yes — the right angle is 90°, and the remaining 90° is split equally: 45°, 45°. So the equal angles are 45°.

8. The sides of a triangle are in the ratio 3:4:5. If the shortest side is 9 cm, what are the other two sides? (This is a famous right triangle.)
   3 units = 9 cm, so 1 unit = 3 cm. Other sides: 4 × 3 = 12 cm and 5 × 3 = 15 cm.

   Answer: 3,888

R2. What fraction is equivalent to 2/5 with denominator 20? (Unit 4: equivalent fractions)
   Answer: 8/20

R3. A bag has 48 marbles sorted equally into 6 groups. How many in each group? (Unit 2: division)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Name two ways to classify a triangle. (By sides: equilateral, isosceles, scalene; by angles: acute, right, obtuse)

2. A triangle has angles 35°, 55°, and 90°. Classify it by angles. What type is it by angles?

3. Can a triangle have two right angles? Explain.

**Mastery = student names both classification systems, identifies the triangle as right (one 90° angle), and correctly explains that two right angles would total 180°, leaving 0° for the third angle, which is impossible.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Work with physical triangle manipulatives: sort a set of triangles by sides, then re-sort by angles
- Use a T-chart or Venn diagram to organize the classification
- Practice finding the third angle from two given angles using the 180° rule before re-attempting classification

### If Mastery Is Met
- Explore: "What would happen if a triangle had an angle of 0°? Can you draw it?" (No — it would just be a line segment, not a triangle.)
- Preview: "Tomorrow we look at four-sided shapes — quadrilaterals. How do you think they might be classified?"

## Extension (Optional)
**Triangle Scavenger Hunt:** Students find triangles in the classroom, school building, or photographs and classify each by sides and angles. A score sheet tracks findings: "1 point for acute scalene, 2 points for right isosceles," etc.

**Triangle Design Challenge:** Using a ruler and protractor, students design a logo or artwork made entirely of triangles. Each triangle must be labeled with its classification.

**Pythagorean Preview (Challenge):** Show students a 3-4-5 right triangle. Demonstrate: 3² + 4² = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5². While the formula is a Grade 8 topic, curious students can explore whether this works for other right triangles.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The two-system classification (by sides AND by angles) is the core of this lesson. Make sure students understand these are independent systems: a triangle can be, for example, acute AND isosceles, or right AND scalene.
- The claim "equilateral triangle is also isosceles" is a good discussion point: technically, equilateral triangles satisfy the isosceles condition (at least two equal sides). Some older definitions require EXACTLY two equal sides for isosceles, but the current standard considers equilateral a special case of isosceles. If this comes up, acknowledge both views and explain that in this class, we use the "at least two" definition.
- The triangle angle sum (180°) should be used actively in this lesson — finding the third angle requires it.
- Using tick marks to show equal sides is an important notation habit. Encourage students to mark equal sides whenever they identify them.
- Draw triangles in non-standard orientations (vertex pointing down, tilted) so students learn that classification depends on properties, not orientation.
- The 3-4-5 triangle (Example 2 and Challenge 8) is historically important and worth calling attention to — it is a famous right triangle used by builders since ancient Egypt to make right angles.
