# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6, Lesson 8

## Topic
Area and Perimeter of Rectangles (Review and Extension with Larger Numbers)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply the area formula A = l × w to find the area of rectangles with multi-digit dimensions
- Apply the perimeter formula P = 2l + 2w (or P = 2(l + w)) to find the perimeter of rectangles
- Distinguish between area (square units) and perimeter (linear units)
- Find a missing dimension when given the area or perimeter and one dimension
- Solve real-world problems involving area and perimeter

## Prerequisites
- Basic area and perimeter concepts from Grade 3 (counting square units, adding sides)
- Multi-digit multiplication (Unit 1: needed for larger area calculations)
- Multi-digit division (Unit 2: finding missing dimensions)
- Factor pairs (Unit 3: rectangle dimensions as factor pairs of area)

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper (1 cm grid)
- Rulers
- Colored pencils
- String (for perimeter demonstration)
- Calculators (optional, for checking larger computations)
- Real-world contexts: printed floor plans, garden layouts, or room diagrams

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review the concept of area and perimeter from Grade 3 before extending to larger numbers.

On the board, draw a rectangle on a grid (3 × 5 squares).

Ask:
1. "How many square units are inside the rectangle?" (15 — students count or recognize)
2. "How many units do you travel if you walk around the edge?" (3+5+3+5 = 16 units)
3. "What do we call these two measurements?" (Area = 15 square units; Perimeter = 16 units)
4. "What is the formula for area of a rectangle?" (A = length × width)
5. "What is the formula for perimeter?" (P = sum of all sides, or 2l + 2w)

Tell students: "Today we use these formulas with bigger numbers — the same ideas, just with multi-digit multiplication and division."

---

## Direct Instruction (13 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review and formalize area and perimeter formulas; connect to factor pairs for missing dimension problems.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Area:** The number of square units needed to cover a flat region. Measured in square units (cm², m², ft², etc.).
- **Perimeter:** The total distance around the outside edge of a shape. Measured in linear units (cm, m, ft, etc.).
- **Length (l):** The longer dimension of a rectangle.
- **Width (w):** The shorter dimension of a rectangle. (Note: "length" and "width" are informal terms — some problems use "base" and "height.")
- **Formula:** A mathematical rule written with variables. A = l × w; P = 2l + 2w.
- **Square units:** The unit of measurement for area — cm², m², in², ft², etc.

### Instruction Notes

**Concrete:** Use graph paper.
- Draw a 6 cm × 4 cm rectangle on graph paper.
- Count interior squares: 24 → 24 cm² area.
- Lay string around the perimeter, then straighten it along a ruler: 20 cm → 20 cm perimeter.
- Connect to formula: 6 × 4 = 24 and 2(6) + 2(4) = 20.

**Representational:** Show formulas applied to drawn diagrams with labeled dimensions.

Finding area (forward):
```
   l = 12 m
  ___________
 |           |  w = 7 m
 |___________|
A = l × w = 12 × 7 = 84 m²
```

Finding perimeter (forward):
```
P = 2l + 2w = 2(12) + 2(7) = 24 + 14 = 38 m
```

Finding missing dimension from area (backward):
```
Area = 63 cm²; width = 7 cm; find length.
A = l × w
63 = l × 7
l = 63 ÷ 7 = 9 cm
```
Connection to factor pairs: 63 = 7 × 9 — what are the factor pairs of 63?

Finding missing dimension from perimeter (backward):
```
P = 34 cm; length = 11 cm; find width.
P = 2l + 2w
34 = 2(11) + 2w
34 = 22 + 2w
2w = 12
w = 6 cm
```

**Abstract:** Formulas and equations. Key note:
- Area is ALWAYS in square units (cm², m²)
- Perimeter is ALWAYS in regular (linear) units (cm, m)
- If area and perimeter have the same number (coincidence), they still have different units.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** A classroom floor is 9 m long and 8 m wide. Find the area and perimeter.
- A = 9 × 8 = 72 m²
- P = 2(9) + 2(8) = 18 + 16 = 34 m

**Example 2:** A garden has an area of 96 m². Its length is 12 m. Find the width.
- 96 = 12 × w
- w = 96 ÷ 12 = 8 m

**Example 3:** A picture frame has a perimeter of 56 cm. Its width is 11 cm. Find the length.
- 56 = 2l + 2(11)
- 56 = 2l + 22
- 2l = 34
- l = 17 cm

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through together. Students set up the formula and solve.

1. A rectangular soccer field is 75 m long and 50 m wide.
   a. What is its area?
   b. What is its perimeter?
   a. A = 75 × 50 = 3,750 m²
   b. P = 2(75) + 2(50) = 150 + 100 = 250 m

2. A rectangular rug has an area of 45 ft². Its width is 5 ft. What is its length?
   l = 45 ÷ 5 = 9 ft

3. A park has a perimeter of 72 m. Its length is 24 m. What is its width?
   72 = 2(24) + 2w → 72 = 48 + 2w → 2w = 24 → w = 12 m

4. Two rectangles:
   - Rectangle A: length 8 cm, width 6 cm
   - Rectangle B: length 12 cm, width 4 cm
   Which has more area? Which has more perimeter?
   A: area = 48 cm², perimeter = 28 cm
   B: area = 48 cm², perimeter = 32 cm
   Same area, different perimeter!

5. A rectangle has length 15 m and width 6 m. How many meters of fencing are needed to enclose it?
   P = 2(15) + 2(6) = 30 + 12 = 42 m of fencing

6. A tile measures 4 cm × 4 cm. A floor area measures 40 cm × 60 cm. How many tiles are needed? (This is extension of area concept — find the floor area and divide by tile area.)
   Floor area = 40 × 60 = 2,400 cm². Tile area = 4 × 4 = 16 cm². Tiles = 2,400 ÷ 16 = 150 tiles.

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Find the area and perimeter of each rectangle:
   a. l = 7 cm, w = 4 cm
   b. l = 15 m, w = 9 m
   c. l = 25 ft, w = 12 ft
   d. l = 100 m, w = 40 m

2. Find the missing dimension:
   a. Area = 56 cm², width = 7 cm → length = ___
   b. Area = 120 m², length = 15 m → width = ___
   c. Perimeter = 40 cm, length = 13 cm → width = ___
   d. Perimeter = 86 m, width = 18 m → length = ___

3. A school hallway is 3 m wide and 45 m long. If carpet costs $8 per square meter, what is the total cost to carpet the hallway?

**Stretch:**
4. A farmer wants to enclose a rectangular field with 120 m of fencing. The field must be 35 m long. What is the width? What is the area of the field?

5. Can a rectangle have a perimeter of 24 cm and an area of 32 cm²? Find the dimensions or explain why not.

6. Two rooms have the same area (48 m²). Room A is 8 m × 6 m. Room B has length 12 m. Find Room B's width and compare the perimeters of both rooms.

**Challenge:**
7. A rectangular picture (not including frame) is 20 cm long and 15 cm wide. It is placed in a frame that adds 3 cm on each side. What is the area of the frame? (Find area with frame minus area without frame.)

8. A gardener has 60 m of fence. She wants to make the largest possible rectangular garden. What dimensions will give the greatest area? Explore several options and find the maximum.
   (Hint: Try 1×29, 5×25, 10×20, 15×15 — perimeter 2l+2w = 60, so l+w = 30. The square 15×15 gives maximum area = 225 m².)

   Answer: Right isosceles triangle

   Answer: 6.8

R3. What are the factor pairs of 36? (Unit 3: factors)
   Answer: (1,36), (2,18), (3,12), (4,9), (6,6)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. A rectangle has length 13 m and width 5 m. Find its area AND perimeter. Include units.

2. A rectangle has a perimeter of 44 cm and a length of 14 cm. Write an equation and solve for the width.

**Mastery = student correctly computes A = 65 m² (correct value and square units label), P = 36 m (correct value and linear units label), and sets up and solves 44 = 2(14) + 2w → w = 8 cm.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to graph paper: draw the actual rectangle, count the squares (area), trace the perimeter with string (perimeter)
- The formulas should emerge from the concrete model, not be applied as memorized rules
- Work with simpler numbers (l = 4, w = 3) to build confidence before returning to larger values
- For the missing dimension problem, connect explicitly to factor pairs: "63 = 7 × ?" is the same as "what is 63 ÷ 7?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Pose the "same perimeter, different area" investigation: find all rectangles with whole-number dimensions that have a perimeter of 20 cm. Which has the greatest area?
- Preview Lesson 9: "What if a shape is not a single rectangle but is made of two rectangles stuck together?"

## Extension (Optional)
**Floor Plan Design:** Students design a floor plan for their "dream bedroom." They must include at least one rectangular room, one rectangular rug, and one rectangular window wall. They calculate area (for carpet) and perimeter (for baseboard trim) for each section.

**Farmer's Fence Problem:** Given a fixed amount of fencing (e.g., 48 m), students explore all possible rectangle dimensions (with whole number lengths) and record area for each. They discover that the square maximizes area.

**Tile Estimation:** Students estimate how many floor tiles would be needed to tile the classroom floor. They measure the floor and one tile, calculate, and check their estimate.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- This lesson builds directly on Grade 3 area and perimeter, but extends to multi-digit multiplication — the skill from Unit 1. Make this connection explicit.
- The most persistent confusion is area vs. perimeter: area = inside (square units), perimeter = outside (linear units). Return to the concrete model (graph paper for area, string for perimeter) whenever confusion arises.
- Units are critical: area must be in square units. Watch for students who write "m" instead of "m²" for area. The square is not optional.
- The "same area, different perimeter" demonstration in Guided Practice item 4 is powerful. It prevents the misconception that area and perimeter always increase together.
- Finding missing dimensions from area connects directly to Unit 1 division (A = l × w → l = A ÷ w) and to Unit 3 factor pairs (dimensions of a rectangle are factor pairs of the area).
- For the missing dimension from perimeter problem: some students will benefit from breaking P = 2l + 2w into two steps — first find 2l, then subtract from P to get 2w, then divide by 2.
- Real-world contexts make this lesson engaging: room measurements, garden plots, sports fields. If possible, measure an actual room in the school building.
