# Math — Grade 4 — Unit 6: Measurement and Geometry
## Facilitator Guide

---

## Unit Overview

This unit develops students' geometric reasoning and measurement skills through exploration of angles, shape classification, symmetry, and area/perimeter. Students move from concrete manipulation of physical shapes and protractors to drawn diagrams and finally to symbolic classification and formula application. The unit integrates prior knowledge of multiplication, division, and fractions to solve real-world measurement problems.

**Duration:** 10 lessons (~50 minutes each)
**Grade Level:** Grade 4 (ages 9–10)
**Subject:** Mathematics

---

## Standards Addressed

### Measurement and Data (4.MD)
- **4.MD.1** — Know relative sizes of measurement units within one system of units. Convert larger units to smaller units.
- **4.MD.2** — Use the four operations to solve word problems involving distances, intervals of time, liquid volumes, masses of objects, and money.
- **4.MD.3** — Apply the area and perimeter formulas for rectangles in real-world and mathematical problems.
- **4.MD.5** — Recognize angles as geometric shapes formed wherever two rays share a common endpoint, and understand concepts of angle measurement.
- **4.MD.6** — Measure angles in whole-number degrees using a protractor.
- **4.MD.7** — Recognize angle measure as additive. Solve addition and subtraction problems to find unknown angles on a diagram.

### Geometry (4.G)
- **4.G.1** — Draw points, lines, line segments, rays, angles (right, acute, obtuse), and perpendicular and parallel lines. Identify these in two-dimensional figures.
- **4.G.2** — Classify two-dimensional figures based on the presence or absence of parallel or perpendicular lines, or the presence or absence of angles of a specified size.
- **4.G.3** — Recognize a line of symmetry for a two-dimensional figure as a line across the figure such that the figure can be folded along the line into matching parts.

## Prerequisites

Students should arrive at this unit with the following skills:

- **From G3:** Basic area and perimeter concepts, measuring length with a ruler, identifying basic shapes (triangles, rectangles, squares)
- **From Unit 1 (G4):** Multi-digit multiplication (needed for area calculations with larger numbers)
- **From Unit 2 (G4):** Multi-digit division (needed for finding missing dimensions from area)
- **From Unit 3 (G4):** Factor pairs (rectangle dimensions as factor pairs of area)
- **From Unit 4 (G4):** Fractions and mixed numbers (fractional measurements)
- **From Unit 5 (G4):** Decimals (decimal measurements in centimeters, meters)

## Facilitation Approach

### CRA Progression Throughout the Unit

**Concrete (C):** Students handle physical materials — protractors measuring real angles, pattern blocks sorted by attributes, paper folding for symmetry, string laid around shapes for perimeter. Concrete work is essential at the start of each new concept.

**Representational (R):** Students draw diagrams, sketch shapes with labeled measurements, create angle drawings, and draw composite shapes on grid paper. Drawings serve as a bridge between physical manipulation and abstract formulas.

**Abstract (A):** Students use formulas (A = l × w, P = 2l + 2w), angle equations (m∠A + m∠B = 90°), and written geometric classifications. Abstract work builds on and refers back to earlier concrete and representational experiences.

### Discussion Norms
- Encourage students to use precise geometric vocabulary once introduced
- When classifying shapes, always ask "How do you know?" to elicit reasoning
- Accept initial informal language ("pointy," "flat side") and redirect toward formal terms
- Normalize the use of protractors — they take practice to use accurately

### Lesson Structure
Each lesson follows a predictable 50-minute structure:
- Warm-Up: 5 minutes (reviews prior knowledge or previews new concept)
- Direct Instruction: 10–15 minutes (CRA introduction with worked examples)
- Guided Practice: 10 minutes (facilitator-led, student-attempted problems)
- Independent Practice: 10–15 minutes (tiered problems + interleaved review)
- Check for Understanding: 3 minutes (exit ticket)

## Common Misconceptions and How to Address Them

### Angles and Measurement
**Misconception:** "A bigger angle has longer rays."
- Address: Use a protractor to show that rotating the same rays further makes a larger angle. Emphasize that angle size is about the amount of rotation, not ray length.

**Misconception:** Students read the wrong scale on the protractor (protractors have two scales: 0–180° reading left-to-right and right-to-left).
- Address: Teach students to always start by identifying which direction the angle opens, and use the scale where 0° aligns with one ray. Have students estimate first ("Is this more or less than 90°?") to check their reading.

**Misconception:** "A right angle must look like a corner of a page" (only recognized in standard orientation).
- Address: Show right angles in many orientations. Use a corner of an index card to check any angle in any orientation.

### Shape Classification
**Misconception:** "A square is not a rectangle" (or "a rectangle is not a parallelogram").
- Address: Emphasize hierarchical classification. Post and refer to the hierarchy: parallelogram → rectangle → square. A shape that meets all the requirements of the parent category IS that shape, even if it also belongs to a more specific category.

**Misconception:** "A shape must look like the textbook picture" (e.g., trapezoids must have the longer base on the bottom).
- Address: Show shapes in non-standard orientations. Emphasize that classification depends on properties, not orientation.

**Misconception:** Confusing perimeter and area.
- Address: Use physical demonstrations — string around the border of a shape = perimeter; counting squares inside = area. Return to concrete models whenever confusion arises.

### Composite Shapes
**Misconception:** Adding all visible numbers on a composite shape diagram (including internal segments) to find perimeter.
- Address: Trace the perimeter physically with a finger or string. Internal lines are not part of the perimeter.

**Misconception:** For area, forgetting to account for the "missing" piece (subtracting vs. adding method confusion).
- Address: Have students draw the full bounding rectangle first, then decide: "Are we adding two pieces or subtracting a cutout?"

## Differentiation Guidance

### Students Who Need Additional Support
- Provide geometric vocabulary reference cards with labeled diagrams
- Use large-print protractors or digital protractors
- Reduce the number of independent practice problems; prioritize on-level problems
- Use grid paper for all area/perimeter work so squares can be counted as a check
- Allow partners for classification activities; one student sorts, one explains
- Provide partially completed diagrams (angles already drawn, dimensions already labeled)

### Students Who Are Ready for Enrichment
- Explore sum of interior angles of polygons (triangles = 180°, quadrilaterals = 360°)
- Investigate which shapes tile a plane and connect to pattern block explorations
- Research architectural examples of geometric shapes in famous buildings
- Explore perimeter and area relationships: "Can two rectangles have the same perimeter but different areas?"
- Connect angle addition to clock angles (what is the angle between clock hands at 3:00? At 2:00?)

### English Language Learners
- Geometry vocabulary is highly visual — use diagrams with labels prominently displayed
- Pair vocabulary instruction with physical models (point to the angle while saying "angle")
- Provide bilingual vocabulary cards if available
- Allow demonstration of understanding through drawing and gesture as well as verbal explanation

## Assessment Guidance

### Formative Assessment (During Unit)
- **Exit Tickets (daily):** Each lesson includes a 3-minute exit ticket. Review before the next lesson to adjust pacing or address errors.
- **Observations during Guided Practice:** Note which students struggle with protractor use, which confuse perimeter and area, which apply the hierarchy correctly.
- **Error analysis:** When reviewing independent practice, focus on the type of error (conceptual vs. procedural) to plan targeted re-teaching.

### Summative Assessment
The unit assessment (separate document) covers all 10 lessons. It includes:
- Part 1: Vocabulary and identification (matching, labeling)
- Part 2: Angle measurement and calculation
- Part 3: Shape classification
- Part 4: Symmetry
- Part 5: Area and perimeter (including composite shapes)
- Part 6: Word problems

**Recommended mastery threshold:** 75% overall, with at least partial credit on each section. Students scoring below 60% on a specific section should receive targeted re-teaching before moving to Unit 7.

## Connections to Other Subjects

- **Art:** Line symmetry in visual art; geometric patterns in Islamic art, quilts, and tessellations
- **Architecture:** Angles and shapes in building design; parallel and perpendicular lines in floor plans
- **Maps/Social Studies:** Grid maps use perpendicular lines; compass directions involve angle concepts
- **Science:** Crystals have geometric shapes; understanding angles supports protractor use in science labs

## Family Communication Notes

At the start of this unit, you may wish to send home a brief note informing families that:
- Students will be using protractors (a measurement tool for angles) — if families wish to purchase one for home use, a standard 180° plastic protractor is inexpensive and widely available
- Students may bring home folded paper symmetry activities — encourage families to find symmetry in everyday objects (butterflies, windows, floor tiles)
- Real-world connections to reinforce at home: looking for parallel and perpendicular lines in architecture, estimating and measuring angles on a clock face
