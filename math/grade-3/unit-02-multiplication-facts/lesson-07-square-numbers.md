# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 2, Lesson 7

## Topic
Square Numbers (Perfect Squares 1 × 1 through 10 × 10)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define "square number" as the product of a number multiplied by itself
- List all perfect squares from 1 × 1 through 10 × 10
- Build square arrays on grid paper and explain why they form squares
- Identify visual patterns in the sequence of square numbers

## Prerequisites
- All fact families ×0 through ×10 (Unit 1 and Unit 2, Lessons 1-6)
- Understanding of arrays (Unit 1, Lesson 4)
- Commutative property (Unit 1, Lesson 5)

## Materials Needed
- Grid paper
- Colored pencils or markers
- Counters (optional, 100+)
- Paper and pencil

**Standards:** 3.OA.7

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Square number (perfect square):** A number you get when you multiply a whole number by itself (e.g., 9 is a square number because 3 × 3 = 9)
- **Square array:** An array with the same number of rows and columns, forming a square shape

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — What Is a Square Number? (4 minutes)**

"When both numbers in a multiplication fact are the SAME, the product is called a 'square number.' Watch why."

Draw on grid paper:
- 1 × 1 = 1 (a single dot — it's a tiny square!)
- 2 × 2 = 4 (a 2-by-2 square)
- 3 × 3 = 9 (a 3-by-3 square)
- 4 × 4 = 16 (a 4-by-4 square)

"Each array makes a perfect square shape. That's why we call these 'square numbers.'"

**Step 2 — Build All the Square Numbers (5 minutes)**

Have the student draw each square array on grid paper and fill in the table:

| Multiplication | Array Size | Product (Square Number) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 × 1 | 1 by 1 | 1 |
| 2 × 2 | 2 by 2 | 4 |
| 3 × 3 | 3 by 3 | 9 |
| 4 × 4 | 4 by 4 | 16 |
| 5 × 5 | 5 by 5 | 25 |
| 6 × 6 | 6 by 6 | 36 |
| 7 × 7 | 7 by 7 | 49 |
| 8 × 8 | 8 by 8 | 64 |
| 9 × 9 | 9 by 9 | 81 |
| 10 × 10 | 10 by 10 | 100 |

"The square numbers are: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100."

**Step 3 — Patterns in Square Numbers (3 minutes)**

"Let's look for patterns in this sequence."

Write the differences between consecutive squares:
- 4 - 1 = 3
- 9 - 4 = 5
- 16 - 9 = 7
- 25 - 16 = 9
- 36 - 25 = 11
- 49 - 36 = 13
- 64 - 49 = 15
- 81 - 64 = 17
- 100 - 81 = 19

"The differences are: 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19 — they're all odd numbers, and they go up by 2 each time! That's a beautiful pattern."

**Visual explanation:** When you go from a 3 × 3 square to a 4 × 4 square, you add one new row of 4 and one new column of 3 (plus one corner), which is 4 + 3 = 7 new dots.

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** "Is 25 a square number?"
- Yes! 5 × 5 = 25. You can make a 5-by-5 square array.

**Example B:** "What is 8 × 8?"
- 8 × 8 = 64. Strategy check: 4 × 8 = 32, double 32 = 64.
- This is a square number — the 8th perfect square.

**Example C:** "Is 15 a square number?"
- Let's check: 3 × 3 = 9 (too small). 4 × 4 = 16 (too big). No whole number times itself equals 15, so 15 is NOT a square number.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Problem 1:** "What is 6 × 6?"
*"Use any strategy you like — or you might just know it!"* (36)
Answer: 36

**Problem 2:** "Is 49 a square number? How do you know?"
*"What number times itself gives 49?"* (7 × 7 = 49, so yes.)
Answer: Yes — 7 × 7 = 49

**Problem 3:** "Draw a 5 × 5 square array on grid paper. How many squares (dots) are there?"
*Student draws and counts.* (25)
Answer: 25

**Problem 4:** "What is the next square number after 64?"
*"64 = 8 × 8. The next would be 9 × 9 = ?"* (81)
Answer: 81

**Problem 5:** "A square garden has 7 rows of plants with 7 plants in each row. How many plants are in the garden?"
*"What fact?" (7 × 7 = 49)*
Answer: 49 plants

### Challenge
9. "What is the only square number that is also a single digit?" (Think: 1 × 1 = 1, 2 × 2 = 4, 3 × 3 = 9. So 1, 4, and 9 are single-digit square numbers.)
10. "If you know 7 × 7 = 49, can you use that to quickly figure out 7 × 8?" (Yes: 49 + 7 = 56, because 7 × 8 is one more group of 7.)
11. "The differences between consecutive square numbers are 3, 5, 7, 9, 11... What would the difference between 10 × 10 and 11 × 11 be?" (The next odd number: 21. Check: 121 - 100 = 21.)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket
1. 4 × 4 = ___ (16)
2. 8 × 8 = ___ (64)
3. "Is 40 a square number? Why or why not?" (No — 6 × 6 = 36 and 7 × 7 = 49, and there's no whole number between 6 and 7, so no whole number times itself equals 40.)
4. "A square-shaped tile is 9 small squares long and 9 small squares wide. How many small squares make up the tile?" (81)

**Scoring:**
- 4/4 correct = Mastery
- 2-3/4 correct = Approaching mastery
- 0-1/4 correct = Needs reteaching

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to building square arrays with grid paper or counters. The visual connection between "same number times itself" and "square shape" is the key concept.
- Focus on memorizing just a few key squares: 3 × 3 = 9, 4 × 4 = 16, 7 × 7 = 49, 8 × 8 = 64. These are the ones most often forgotten.
- Remind: square number facts can always be derived from their strategies (e.g., 8 × 8 via double ×4: 4 × 8 = 32, double = 64).

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 8 (Mixed Fact Practice). Preview: "Next time we'll mix ALL the facts together and practice choosing the right strategy."

## Extension (Optional)
**Square Number Art:** "On grid paper, draw and color all squares from 1 × 1 up to 10 × 10, nesting them inside each other (start with the biggest square and work inward, each a different color). How does this show the pattern of square numbers growing?"

**Challenge pattern:** "Square numbers alternate odd-even-odd-even: 1(odd), 4(even), 9(odd), 16(even), 25(odd)... Why?" (An odd number times itself is odd; an even number times itself is even. Odd and even numbers alternate: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5...)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
1. **Confuses "square number" with "double":** Student thinks 6 × 6 = 12 (doubles instead of squares). Reinforce: "6 × 6 means 6 groups of 6, not 6 + 6."
2. **Cannot recall 7 × 7 or 8 × 8:** These are the hardest perfect squares. Use mnemonics: "7 × 7 = 49" and "8 × 8 = 64" (I ate and I ate until I was sick on the floor: 8 × 8 = 64).
3. **Thinks any even number is a square number:** Student says 8 is a square number. Test it: "What times itself gives 8? 2 × 2 = 4, 3 × 3 = 9. Nothing gives 8."
4. **Doesn't see the square shape in the array:** Make sure the grid paper arrays are drawn accurately with equal rows and columns.

### Pacing Advice
- This lesson is more conceptual and visual than the previous strategy-heavy lessons. Let the student spend time drawing and coloring arrays — the visual understanding is valuable.
- The pattern of differences (3, 5, 7, 9...) is an enrichment observation, not a required skill. If the student is fascinated, explore it. If not, move on.
- Total lesson time: approximately 40-45 minutes.
