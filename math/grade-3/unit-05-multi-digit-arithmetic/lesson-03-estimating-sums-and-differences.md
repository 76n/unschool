# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 5, Lesson 3

## Topic
Estimating Sums and Differences Using Rounding

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Round addends or numbers being subtracted to the nearest 10 or 100 before calculating.
- Compute an estimated sum or difference using rounded numbers.
- Explain whether an exact answer is reasonable by comparing it to the estimate.
- Choose an appropriate place to round (nearest 10 or nearest 100) based on the context.

## Prerequisites
- Round to the nearest 10 (Lesson 1)
- Round to the nearest 100 (Lesson 2)
- Add and subtract two-digit numbers (G2)

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers
- Math journal or lined paper
- Number lines (for rounding reference, if needed)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Fluency review of rounding and mental connection to addition/subtraction.

**Rapid rounding:** Call out these numbers one at a time. Students write their rounded answer (to the nearest 10) on their whiteboard and hold it up.
- 47 (50), 83 (80), 255 (260), 619 (620), 945 (950)

Then say: "Now I'm going to add two numbers, and I want a fast estimate. I'll round both numbers first."
Write on board: 52 + 37
Ask: "Round each to the nearest 10 first. What do we get?" (50 + 40 = 90.) "So our estimate is 90. The real answer will be close to 90."
Quickly verify: 52 + 37 = 89. "Yes — 89 is very close to 90. Our estimate was useful!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach estimation as a deliberate strategy — round first, then compute with friendly numbers, then check reasonableness.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Estimate:** A thoughtful approximation, not an exact calculation. An estimate tells you about how much.
- **Reasonable:** An answer that makes sense — close to the estimate and appropriate for the problem situation.
- **Front-end estimation:** Rounding each number to its largest (leading) place value before adding or subtracting.

### Instruction Notes

**Why estimate?**
Help students understand that estimation has practical value:
1. It gives a quick sense of the answer before doing the exact calculation.
2. It allows you to check whether an exact answer makes sense (catch errors).
3. In real life, exact numbers are often not needed — "about 200 people" or "roughly $30."

**When to round to the nearest 10 vs. nearest 100:**
- Round to the nearest 10 when the numbers are small (two-digit or low three-digit) or when you need more precision.
- Round to the nearest 100 when dealing with larger numbers or when a ballpark estimate is sufficient.

**Procedure:**
1. Round each number to the same place value (nearest 10 or nearest 100).
2. Add or subtract the rounded numbers.
3. Label the answer as an estimate using the "≈" symbol (approximately equal to).
4. When exact answer is given, compare: Is the exact answer close to the estimate? Is it reasonable?

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Estimate 324 + 152.**
- Round to nearest 100: 324 → 300; 152 → 200
- Estimate: 300 + 200 = **500** (so 324 + 152 ≈ 500)
- Exact: 324 + 152 = 476. Is 476 close to 500? Yes — reasonable.

**Example 2: Estimate 487 − 213.**
- Round to nearest 100: 487 → 500; 213 → 200
- Estimate: 500 − 200 = **300** (so 487 − 213 ≈ 300)
- Exact: 487 − 213 = 274. Is 274 close to 300? Yes — reasonable.

**Example 3: Estimate 362 + 149 using nearest 10.**
- Round to nearest 10: 362 → 360; 149 → 150
- Estimate: 360 + 150 = **510** (so 362 + 149 ≈ 510)
- Exact: 362 + 149 = 511. Very close — the nearest-10 estimate is more precise.

**Example 4: Reasonableness check.**
A student calculated 567 − 284 = 823. Is this reasonable?
- Round to nearest 100: 567 → 600; 284 → 300
- Estimate: 600 − 300 = 300
- Is 823 close to 300? No! 823 is much larger. The answer is NOT reasonable. (The student likely added instead of subtracted.)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
For each problem:
(a) Round each number to the nearest 100.
(b) Write the estimate using ≈.
(c) After computing the exact answer, confirm it is reasonable.

1. Estimate: 318 + 245 ≈ ?
   Round: 300 + 200 = **500**
   Exact (compute together): 318 + 245 = 563. Reasonable? (Yes, close to 500.)

2. Estimate: 724 − 389 ≈ ?
   Round: 700 − 400 = **300**
   Exact (compute together): 724 − 389 = 335. Reasonable? (Yes, close to 300.)

3. Estimate: 451 + 376 ≈ ?
   Round: 500 + 400 = **900**
   Exact: 451 + 376 = 827. Reasonable? (Yes, close to 900.)

4. A student says 193 + 412 = 923. Is this reasonable? Estimate to check.
   Round: 200 + 400 = 600. Is 923 close to 600? No — NOT reasonable.

**Facilitator prompts:**
- "Before you add the exact numbers, tell me your estimate. Hold onto that."
- "After you add, is your answer in the ballpark of your estimate?"
- For #4: "The estimate gives us a way to catch mistakes. What mistake do you think the student might have made?" (Possible: added 1 + 4 in the hundreds and got an extra hundred, or made an error in tens/ones.)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Estimate each sum or difference by rounding to the nearest 100. Write the estimate using ≈.
1. 432 + 251 ≈ ____
2. 678 − 312 ≈ ____
3. 555 + 223 ≈ ____
4. 741 − 490 ≈ ____
5. 186 + 537 ≈ ____
6. 900 − 348 ≈ ____

**Stretch:**
Estimate by rounding to the nearest 10. Then estimate by rounding to the nearest 100. Which estimate is closer to the exact answer?
1. 362 + 145
   - Nearest 10 estimate: ____; Nearest 100 estimate: ____; Exact: 507
2. 818 − 263
   - Nearest 10 estimate: ____; Nearest 100 estimate: ____; Exact: 555

**Challenge:**
1. A student's exact answer to 543 + 289 is 732. Is this answer reasonable? Show your estimation work and explain.
2. Keisha wants to buy a book for $4.75 and a game for $12.25. She estimates the total by rounding each price to the nearest dollar: $5 + $12 = $17. She has $18. Should she have enough? Explain.
3. Create your own estimation problem: write two three-digit numbers, estimate their sum, then find the exact sum. Were you close?

### Interleaved Review
*(Review from Units 1–4)*
1. What is 7 × 8? (56)
2. 24 ÷ 6 = ____? (4)
3. Name a fraction equivalent to 1/2. (Possible answers: 2/4, 3/6, etc.; accept any correct response.)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
Estimate each by rounding to the nearest 100. Write the estimate and explain if the exact answer is reasonable.

a) 627 + 241 ≈ ____. A student got 868. Reasonable? ____
b) 753 − 428 ≈ ____. A student got 1,181. Reasonable? ____

**Mastery = both estimates correct AND correct reasonableness judgment for both.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Re-teach rounding review (Lessons 1–2 warm-up) before estimation.
- Break the process into two steps on separate days: Day A — practice rounding both numbers in a sum. Day B — connect to estimation with the ≈ symbol.
- Use simple, real-world contexts: "You have 312 stickers and a friend has 248. About how many do you have together?"

### If Mastery Is Met
- Challenge: "Can you think of a situation where a nearest-100 estimate would be 'too rough' and you'd need to round to the nearest 10 instead?"
- Preview Lesson 4: "Now that we can estimate, we're going to calculate exact answers. What strategies do you already know for adding three-digit numbers?"

## Extension (Optional)
**Estimation in real life:** Look at a grocery receipt (real or teacher-made). Practice rounding each item to the nearest dollar (or nearest 10 cents) and estimating the total. Compare to the actual total.

**Estimation challenge:** "Without using exact computation, put these sums in order from least to greatest:
- 423 + 319
- 198 + 502
- 350 + 350
- 645 + 147"

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students sometimes think estimation means "guess randomly." Emphasize that estimation is a mathematical process — it uses rounding, which is a precise skill.
- The "≈" symbol should be explicitly taught and required whenever writing an estimate. This helps students maintain the distinction between an estimate and an exact answer.
- Some students will insist on calculating the exact answer first, then "going back to estimate." Encourage the habit of estimating FIRST — it trains number sense and genuine checking behavior.
- Real-world estimation problems (grocery totals, distances on a map, time estimates) can make the skill feel relevant beyond math class.
