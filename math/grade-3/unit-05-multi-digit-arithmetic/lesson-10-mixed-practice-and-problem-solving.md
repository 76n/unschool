# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 5, Lesson 10

## Topic
Mixed Practice and Problem Solving

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Round numbers to the nearest 10 and nearest 100 fluently.
- Estimate sums and differences and judge reasonableness.
- Add and subtract three-digit numbers with regrouping (including across zeros) using the standard algorithm.
- Solve one- and two-step word problems involving addition and subtraction within 1,000.
- Demonstrate fluency and flexibility across all Unit 5 skills.

## Prerequisites
- All Unit 5 skills: Lessons 1–9

## Materials Needed
- Grid paper or lined paper
- Whiteboard and markers
- Math journal
- Optional: base-ten blocks for any student who needs them

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Rapid mixed review to activate all unit skills.

**5-in-5 challenge:** Students complete 5 problems in 5 minutes (individually, on paper or whiteboard). Work quickly — this is fluency practice, not a test.

1. Round 674 to the nearest 100. (700)
2. Round 537 to the nearest 10. (540)
3. Estimate: 482 + 339 ≈ ____. (800)
4. 456 + 278 = ____. (734)
5. 803 − 465 = ____. (338)

After 5 minutes, review answers together. Ask: "Which problem felt hardest? Which felt easiest?" Use this to guide differentiation during independent practice.

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Synthesize the unit skills, model flexible problem-solving, and introduce mixed-operation choice.

### Key Vocabulary
(Review all unit vocabulary — no new terms.)
- Round, estimate, regroup, sum, difference, reasonable, multi-step.

### Instruction Notes

**Mixed-operation decision-making:**
One of the key skills in this lesson is not just "can you add or subtract" but "can you figure out which one to do?" Present a think-aloud model that makes the decision process visible.

**Flexible strategy use:**
Some problems are easier with mental math than with the standard algorithm. Encourage students to consider both:
- Mental math works well for: multiples of 10/100, near-round numbers (e.g., 299 + 147 = 300 + 147 − 1 = 446).
- Standard algorithm works well for: numbers requiring regrouping across non-round values.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Rounding, then estimating, then exact.**
Problem: A school ordered 465 pencils. They used 237 of them. Later, they ordered 185 more. How many pencils do they have now?

Step 1 (Estimate): 500 − 200 + 200 = 500. We expect an answer around 500.
Step 2 (Compute):
- 465 − 237 = 228 (ones 5<7, regroup; tens: 5 left → becomes 4→ 15−7=8; tens 5→4: 4<3? No wait: 465: hundreds=4, tens=6, ones=5. Ones: 5<7, regroup: tens 6→5, ones 15; 15−7=8; tens 5>3: 5−3=2; hundreds 4−2=2 → 228.)
- 228 + 185 = 413. (ones 8+5=13 regroup; tens 2+8+1=11 regroup; hundreds 2+1+1=4 → 413.)
Step 3 (Check): 413 is somewhat less than 500 — the subtraction took away more than the estimate because 237 rounded down to 200 (was actually 37 more than 200) while 185 rounded to 200 (actually 15 less). Reasonable.

Answer: **413 pencils**.

**Example 2: Multi-step with both operations.**
Problem: A collection drive started with 0 books. In Week 1, 348 books were donated. In Week 2, 219 books were donated, but 175 were given away. How many books remain?

Step 1: 348 + 219 = 567.
Step 2: 567 − 175 = 392.

Answer: **392 books** remain.

Work through these mixed problems together, letting the student take the lead while you prompt.

**Set A — Computation review:**

2. 548 + 376 = ____
3. 904 − 467 = ____

**Set B — Word problem review:**
4. "An art supply store had 518 paint brushes. They sold 234 on Saturday and 87 on Sunday. How many paint brushes are left?"
5. "A farmer grew 425 tomatoes and 368 peppers. He used 150 tomatoes for salsa. How many vegetables does he have in total now?"

For each:
- Estimate first.
- Show algorithm clearly.
- Write answer sentence for word problems.

**Facilitator prompts:**
- "Before you start, estimate. What are you expecting?"
- "Is this one step or two steps?"
- "After you compute, compare to your estimate. Does it make sense?"

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
**This exit ticket reviews the entire unit.**

1. Round 563 to the nearest 100: ____
2. Estimate: 427 + 348 ≈ ____
3. 764 − 486 = ____
4. Write one sentence: "When I subtract a three-digit number and I see a 0 in the tens place, I need to ____."

**Mastery = #1, #2, #3 all correct; #4 shows understanding of regrouping across zero.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Use the unit assessment results to identify specific skill gaps.
- Create a targeted mini-review session focused on 1–2 weak areas before re-assessing.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Preview Unit 6: "We've been working with place value to add and subtract. In Unit 6, we'll use measurement. Do you think we might need to add or subtract to solve measurement problems?"
- Explore mental math strategies: compensation, making hundreds, breaking apart numbers.

**Math olympiad warm-up:** "How many different three-digit numbers can you make with the digits 2, 5, and 8 (each used once)? What is the sum of all of them?"
(Numbers: 258, 285, 528, 582, 825, 852. Sum: 258+285=543; 528+582=1,110; 825+852=1,677. Grand total: 543+1,110+1,677=3,330.)

**Newspaper math:** Find three-digit numbers in a newspaper, magazine, or book. Create an addition and a subtraction problem using real numbers. Estimate first, then solve exactly.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Lesson 10 is a review and fluency consolidation — do not rush to introduce new concepts. The focus is confidence and accuracy across all unit skills.
- Use this lesson to identify any students who are not yet fluent and need additional support before the unit assessment.
- The "5-in-5" warm-up gives you a quick informal assessment at the start. If many students struggle with one type (e.g., subtraction across zeros), spend additional guided practice time there.
- The unit assessment should be administered the following session. Remind students to use ECC (Estimate–Compute–Check) as a habit during the assessment.
- Celebrate growth! Many students entered this unit uncertain about regrouping in subtraction. Acknowledge the progress explicitly.
