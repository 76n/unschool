# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 6, Lesson 3

## Topic
Area of Rectangles — Length × Width Formula

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify the length and width of a rectangle
- Calculate the area of a rectangle using A = l × w (or A = b × h)
- Apply the area formula to rectangles given side lengths (without grid paper)
- Find the area of a rectilinear (L-shaped or step-shaped) figure by decomposing it into non-overlapping rectangles
- Solve one-step word problems involving area of rectangles

## Prerequisites
- Area as rows × columns of unit squares (Lesson 2)
- Multiplication facts through 10 × 10 (Units 1–2)
- Measuring length with a ruler (G2)

## Materials Needed
- Rulers (centimeter side)
- Grid paper and plain paper
- Color tiles (available for students who need concrete support)
- Colored pencils (for decomposing figures)
- Index cards for exit tickets

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Transition from counting rows/columns to measuring side lengths.

Draw a 4 × 6 rectangle on the board without grid lines. Ask: "If I told you this rectangle is 4 cm wide and 6 cm long, how would you find the area without counting tiles?"

Take a few guesses. Then reveal: "We measured the side lengths and multiplied. Today you will learn the formula."

Quick mental math check: "What is 4 × 6?" (24) "What is 5 × 9?" (45) "What is 7 × 8?" (56) (30 seconds of call-and-response)

---

## Direct Instruction (12–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Formalize the formula A = l × w; extend to rectilinear figures via decomposition; remove dependency on grid paper.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Length (l):** The longer dimension of a rectangle (sometimes called "base")
- **Width (w):** The shorter dimension of a rectangle (sometimes called "height")
- **Formula:** A rule written with variables that works for every case; A = l × w
- **Rectilinear figure:** A shape made up of rectangles joined together (no diagonal sides)
- **Decompose:** To break a shape into smaller, non-overlapping parts

### Instruction Notes

**Bridging from Lesson 2 to the Formula:**

Draw a 5 × 3 rectangle on the board with grid lines. "From Lesson 2, area = 3 rows × 5 in each row = 15. Now look at the side lengths: the length is 5 and the width is 3. So Area = length × width = 5 × 3 = 15."

Erase the grid lines. Draw the same outline with only the side labels "5 cm" and "3 cm." "Can we still find the area?" (Yes — A = 5 × 3 = 15 sq cm.) "We don't need grid paper anymore — measuring the sides tells us everything."

Write: **A = l × w**

Point out: "It doesn't matter which side you call length and which you call width, because multiplication is commutative. 5 × 3 = 3 × 5 = 15."

**Applying with a Ruler:**
Give the student a small piece of paper. Have them measure the length and width in centimeters with a ruler, then calculate the area. (This is a tactile confirmation that the formula works on real objects.)

**Rectilinear Figures (3.MD.7d):**
Draw an L-shape on the board. "Can I use A = l × w here?" (No — it's not a rectangle.) "But I can cut it into rectangles that don't overlap, find each area, and add them up."

Show two ways to cut the same L-shape (horizontal cut vs. vertical cut) and show both give the same total area. Emphasize: the cut can go either way — choose whichever makes the numbers friendlier.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Rectangle: length = 8 cm, width = 4 cm
- A = l × w = 8 × 4 = 32
- Area = 32 square centimeters (sq cm)

**Example 2:** Rectangle: length = 7 in, width = 7 in
- A = 7 × 7 = 49
- Area = 49 square inches (sq in)
- Note: "This is a square. A square is a special rectangle."

**Example 3 — Rectilinear figure:**
L-shape:
- Top rectangle: 2 cm × 5 cm = 10 sq cm
- Bottom rectangle: 4 cm × 3 cm = 12 sq cm
- Total area = 10 + 12 = 22 sq cm

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. A rectangle is 6 cm long and 4 cm wide. Find the area. (A = 6 × 4 = 24 sq cm)

2. A square has a side length of 9 inches. Find the area. (A = 9 × 9 = 81 sq in)

3. A rug is 8 feet long and 5 feet wide. What is the area of the rug? (A = 8 × 5 = 40 sq ft)

4. Decompose this L-shape into two rectangles and find the total area:
   - The overall shape is 7 cm wide at the bottom and 3 cm tall; the top part is 4 cm wide and 2 cm tall.
   - Possible decomposition: Bottom section: 7 × 1 cm = 7 sq cm; Top section: 4 × 2 = 8 sq cm; Total = 15 sq cm
   - (Accept any correct decomposition yielding 15 sq cm)

5. A classroom floor is 9 meters long and 6 meters wide. How many square meters of tile are needed to cover it? (A = 9 × 6 = 54 sq m)

---

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Find the area of each rectangle. Show the formula.
   - 3 cm × 7 cm: A = ___ sq cm
   - 5 in × 5 in: A = ___ sq in
   - 10 m × 4 m: A = ___ sq m

2. A garden is 6 feet wide and 8 feet long. What is its area? (48 sq ft)

3. A picture frame has length 9 cm and width 6 cm. What area does the picture fill? (54 sq cm)

**Stretch:**
4. James builds a bookshelf. Each shelf is 4 feet long and 1 foot wide. The bookshelf has 5 shelves. What is the total area of all the shelves combined? (4×1=4 sq ft per shelf; 5×4=20 sq ft total)

5. Decompose this rectilinear figure and find its area:
   - A shape that is 10 cm × 4 cm with a 3 cm × 2 cm piece cut out of one corner.
   - (Whole: 10×4=40; missing: 3×2=6; area = 40−6 = 34 sq cm OR decompose into two rectangles)

**Challenge:**
6. A rectangle has an area of 36 square inches. One side is 4 inches. What is the other side? (36 ÷ 4 = 9 inches)

7. Two rectangles each have an area of 18 sq cm. Rectangle A is 2 cm × 9 cm. Rectangle B is 3 cm × 6 cm. Which rectangle has a longer perimeter? (A: 2+9+2+9=22; B: 3+6+3+6=18; A has a longer perimeter — preview of L6)

### Interleaved Review
R1. (Multiplication — Unit 2) What is 8 × 9? (72)

R2. (Division — Unit 3) If 56 ÷ 7 = 8, write the two multiplication facts in the same fact family. (7×8=56; 8×7=56)

R3. (Fractions — Unit 4) Draw a number line from 0 to 1. Mark and label 1/4, 2/4, and 3/4 on it.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. A rectangle is 7 cm long and 3 cm wide. What is its area? Show the formula.
2. True or False: To find the area of a rectangle, you can either use the formula or count the unit squares on a grid. Both give the same answer. Explain your thinking.

**Mastery = correct area (21 sq cm) with formula shown AND True with a reasonable explanation.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Draw the 7 × 3 rectangle on grid paper; count the squares (21); then show the formula gives the same result. Emphasize they are the same operation.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask the student to find the area of the L-shape formed by a 5×4 block with a 2×2 block removed from one corner. (20 − 4 = 16 sq units)

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Tile the Bathroom:** Give the student a drawing of a bathroom floor (rectilinear shape) with labeled dimensions. Ask them to find the total area and figure out how many 1-sq-ft tiles are needed. (The area in square feet = number of tiles.)

**Mystery Dimensions:** "A rectangle has an area of 42 square inches. What could the length and width be? Find at least three options." (1×42, 2×21, 3×14, 6×7)

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Students sometimes forget to write the square unit label (e.g., write "24" instead of "24 sq cm"). Make this a non-negotiable habit from today forward — always write the unit.
- The rectilinear (L-shape) decomposition is important for state assessments but should not overshadow the primary objective (A = l × w). Keep the decomposition examples simple (two clean rectangles).
- If a student struggles to identify which dimension is "length" and which is "width," reassure them that it does not matter — the formula works either way because multiplication is commutative.
- Some students will be ready to try: "If I know the area and one side, I can find the other side using division." Do not push this in whole-group instruction, but affirm it when students discover it (as in Challenge #6).
