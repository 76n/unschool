# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 6: Measurement, Time, and Data
## Facilitator Guide

---

## Unit Overview

Unit 6 brings together four connected measurement domains: area, perimeter, time, and data. Students extend their multiplication understanding (Units 1–2) to discover that area of a rectangle equals length × width — a conceptual bridge that deepens both skills simultaneously. Perimeter is developed as repeated addition of side lengths, clearly contrasted with area so students do not conflate the two. Elapsed time is solved with number-line jumps in friendly chunks, avoiding the procedural complexity of regrouping hours and minutes. Mass and volume are introduced using metric units with hands-on weighing and measuring activities. The unit closes with scaled bar graphs and picture graphs, which require multiplication to interpret scales, and a mixed review lesson integrating all four domains.

**Grade Level:** 3
**Unit Number:** 6
**Title:** Measurement, Time, and Data
**Duration:** 10 lessons (~50 minutes each)
**Standards:** 3.MD.1, 3.MD.2, 3.MD.3, 3.MD.4, 3.MD.5, 3.MD.6, 3.MD.7, 3.MD.8, 3.G.1 (partial)

## Prerequisites

Students should have mastered or be fluent with:

- **Multiplication facts** (Units 1–2): Arrays, skip-counting, fact fluency through 10 × 10 — essential for computing area as rows × columns
- **Division** (Unit 3): Relating division to unknown factor — used when finding unknown side lengths from area or perimeter
- **Fractions on a number line** (Unit 4): Number-line reasoning — supports elapsed-time number-line strategy
- **Multi-digit addition and subtraction within 1,000** (Unit 5): Computing perimeters, finding elapsed time
- **G2 foundations**: Measuring length in inches and centimeters with a ruler; telling time to the nearest 5 minutes; reading and creating bar graphs with scale = 1

## Facilitation Tips

### Building Conceptual Understanding Before Formulas
Resist the urge to introduce "length × width" in Lesson 1. Students need the concrete experience of covering a surface with tiles and counting all the squares before the formula emerges naturally in Lesson 3. The CRA sequence is deliberate:
- **Concrete (L1–L2):** Tiles on real surfaces; count every square
- **Representational (L2–L3):** Draw arrays on grid paper; skip-count rows
- **Abstract (L3 onward):** A = l × w formula with numeric computation

### Area–Perimeter Confusion (the Most Critical Issue)
More third graders confuse area and perimeter than any other measurement pair. Strategies to combat this:
- Display an anchor chart from L1 onward: "Area = how much SURFACE (square units). Perimeter = how far AROUND (units)."
- Color-code: shade the inside of shapes blue (area) and trace the outside in red (perimeter).
- Use the word "peRIMeter" — the RIM goes around the edge.
- Lesson 6 is entirely devoted to comparing the two. Do not skip it.

### Elapsed Time — Number Line Strategy
Do NOT teach borrowing (e.g., changing 3:15 to 2:75). Instead, teach students to make jumps to friendly numbers:
  - Jump to the next hour
  - Jump by hours
  - Jump remaining minutes
This mirrors the fraction/number-line work in Unit 4 and is accessible to all learners.

### Mass and Volume — Safety and Setup
- Use dry sand instead of water if spills are a concern.
- Pre-fill containers at stations so students focus on reading measurements.
- Emphasize that a gram is very light (paper clip) and a kilogram is quite heavy (liter of water = about 1 kg — a natural bridge between mass and volume).

### Graph Scale — Multiplication Connection
Explicitly remind students: "If the scale is 'each picture = 5,' we multiply to find the total." This reinforces Unit 1–2 multiplication in an applied context.

## Common Misconceptions and Corrections

| Misconception | Correction Strategy |
|---|---|
| "Perimeter and area are the same thing" | Use the shading (area) vs. tracing (perimeter) strategy every time a shape appears |
| "Area is always bigger than perimeter" (or vice versa) | Show a 1 × 12 rectangle: perimeter = 26, area = 12. Show a 4 × 4 square: perimeter = 16, area = 16. Numbers can go either way. |
| "You need a special formula to find perimeter" | Perimeter is just adding all side lengths — no formula needed, just careful adding |
| Counting only the squares on the border for area | Explicitly cover the interior and count every square; use the array model to show all rows |
| "Elapsed time = just subtract the numbers on the clock" | The number-line strategy makes it visual; subtracting clock numbers fails when crossing the hour |
| Confusing grams and kilograms (thinking 1 kg = 100 g) | Use the mnemonic "kilo = 1,000"; hold a 1 kg weight alongside 1,000 paper clips if possible |
| Reading bar graphs with scale > 1 by reading the bar value literally | Explicitly ask "What does each unit on the scale stand for?" before every reading |

## Assessment Guidance

### Formative Assessment
- **Exit tickets** at the end of every lesson (3–4 minutes)
- **Mastery criteria** listed in each lesson (typically 2 out of 3 correct, or 3 out of 4)
- **Whiteboard checks:** Students write answers on whiteboards and hold up simultaneously; allows the facilitator to see all responses at once

### Summative Assessment
- **Unit Assessment** (separate file): 25 questions covering all standards
  - Part A: Multiple choice (10 items)
  - Part B: Short answer (10 items)
  - Part C: Extended response / word problems (5 items)
- **Suggested time:** 45–60 minutes
- **Mastery threshold:** 80% (20/25 points)

### Diagnostic — Prior to Lesson 1
Before starting the unit, ask students:
1. "Draw an array that shows 4 × 6. What is the total?"
2. "What does it mean to measure something?"
3. "Show me 3:45 on this clock."
This surfaces who needs extra support with arrays (will struggle with area) and clock-reading (will struggle with elapsed time).

## Differentiation

### Students Who Need Additional Support
- Provide grid paper with squares pre-drawn for area tasks
- Allow use of multiplication tables during area and graph work
- For elapsed time, pre-draw a number line labeled with every 5-minute mark
- Pair with a more confident peer during guided practice
- Reduce independent practice to on-level tier only

### Students Who Are Ready for Extension
- Challenge them to find all rectangles with the same area (e.g., area = 24 sq units): 1×24, 2×12, 3×8, 4×6
- Ask: "Can two rectangles have the same perimeter but different areas?" (Yes — see L6)
- Introduce composite/rectilinear shapes (L-shapes) for area via decomposition (3.MD.7d)
- For elapsed time, include problems that span two time periods (e.g., start 10:45 AM, end 1:20 PM)
- Have advanced students create their own scaled graph problems for classmates to solve

### English Language Learners
- Post a visual vocabulary wall with labeled diagrams: square unit, area, perimeter, side length, scale, elapsed time, mass, volume
- Use manipulatives extensively — understanding does not require language
- Allow students to show work with diagrams rather than written explanations

## Cross-Unit Connections

- **Units 1–2 (Multiplication):** Area formula A = l × w directly applies multiplication; graphs use multiplication to interpret scales
- **Unit 3 (Division):** Finding an unknown side length when area or perimeter is known (e.g., if A = 20 and l = 4, then w = 5)
- **Unit 4 (Fractions / Number Line):** The elapsed-time number-line strategy mirrors fraction number-line jumps
- **Unit 5 (Multi-digit Arithmetic):** Adding side lengths for perimeter; computing elapsed time; adding data values in graphs
- **Looking Ahead to G4:** Area of irregular shapes; converting measurement units; multi-step time and data problems

## Notes for the Facilitator

This is a rich, hands-on unit. The variety of domains (area, perimeter, time, mass/volume, data) can feel scattered — frame it for students under the unifying theme: "We are learning to measure and describe the world around us." Every lesson should feel grounded in something physical and real. Avoid rushing to the abstract; the concrete phase is where the deepest understanding is built.

Lessons 1–6 (area and perimeter) form the densest conceptual block. If students are struggling with Lesson 3 (the formula), loop back to the array model in Lesson 2 before moving forward. The formula only makes sense when students can see it as "number of rows × number in each row."

Plan ahead for Lesson 8 (mass and volume): gather materials at least one day in advance. The tactile experience of holding a gram weight vs. a kilogram is irreplaceable.
