# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 6, Lesson 2

## Topic
Finding Area by Counting and Multiplication — Arrays of Unit Squares

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Organize unit squares into rows and columns to form an array
- Skip-count by rows to find the total area of a rectangle
- Explain that the number of rows × the number in each row = total area
- Connect the array model for multiplication directly to the array model for area

## Prerequisites
- Area as covering with unit squares (Lesson 1)
- Multiplication as repeated addition and arrays (Units 1–2)
- Skip-counting by 2s, 3s, 4s, 5s, 6s

## Materials Needed
- Color tiles or square inch tiles (30 per student)
- 1-inch grid paper
- Colored pencils (two contrasting colors)
- Multiplication fact chart (available as reference, not for copying answers)
- Index cards for exit tickets

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Bridge multiplication arrays to area arrays.

Show a 3 × 4 array of dots (like a multiplication fact visual). Ask: "What multiplication fact does this show?" (3 × 4 = 12 or 4 × 3 = 12)

Now show a 3 × 4 grid of shaded squares (same arrangement, but filled squares instead of dots). Ask: "What do you notice? How is this the same as the dots? How is it different?"

Guide students to see: the dots became squares, and counting each square is just like counting each dot. "Today we're going to use what you know about multiplication arrays to find area faster."

---

## Direct Instruction (12–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Show that area of a rectangle = rows × columns (connecting to multiplication); introduce skip-counting rows as an efficient counting strategy.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Array:** An arrangement of objects (or squares) in equal rows and columns
- **Row:** A horizontal line of squares going across
- **Column:** A vertical line of squares going up and down
- **Skip-count:** Counting by a number other than 1 (e.g., 3, 6, 9, 12…)

### Instruction Notes

**CRA — Concrete to Representational**

**Step 1 — Build with tiles:**
Have the student build a 4-row, 5-column rectangle with tiles on grid paper. Ask: "How many rows? (4) How many tiles in each row? (5) Let's skip-count by 5s: 5, 10, 15, 20. Area = 20 square units."

**Step 2 — Connect to multiplication:**
Write: 4 rows × 5 in each row = 20 total squares. Ask: "What multiplication fact is this?" (4 × 5 = 20)

**Step 3 — Representational:**
Draw the same rectangle on grid paper. Color one row at a time with alternating colors. Write the skip-count running total under each row: 5, 10, 15, 20.

Say: "The area is the SAME as the multiplication fact. Area and multiplication are connected — they're both about equal groups."

**Key insight to verbalize:** "Every rectangle we can draw on grid paper matches a multiplication fact. Area = number of rows × number of squares in each row."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Build and draw a 3 × 6 rectangle.
- Rows: 3, squares in each row: 6
- Skip-count by 6: 6, 12, 18
- Area = 18 square units
- Multiplication fact: 3 × 6 = 18

**Example 2:** Build and draw a 5 × 4 rectangle.
- Rows: 5, squares in each row: 4
- Skip-count by 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20
- Area = 20 square units
- Compare to Example above: same area as 4 × 5 — why? (commutative property)

**Example 3 — Partial rows for non-rectangular shapes:**
Show an L-shape: a 3 × 3 block plus a 2 × 1 strip on top. "Can I use one multiplication fact for this whole shape?" (No — not a rectangle.) "What can I do?" (Split into two rectangles.) "3 × 3 = 9 and 2 × 1 = 2. Total area = 11 sq units." (Decomposing shapes — a preview of 3.MD.7d, return to fully in L3.)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

For each, the student builds with tiles, then draws and labels on grid paper.

1. Build a rectangle with 2 rows and 8 squares in each row. Skip-count to find the area.
   What multiplication fact matches? (2 × 8 = 16 sq units)

2. Build a rectangle with 6 rows and 3 squares in each row. Skip-count.
   What multiplication fact matches? (6 × 3 = 18 sq units)

3. Draw (without tiles) a rectangle that matches 7 × 4 on grid paper. Color alternating rows. What is the area? (28 sq units)

4. "If I have a rectangle with 5 rows and I counted 30 total squares, how many squares are in each row?" Think: 5 × ? = 30. (6 squares per row — preview of finding side lengths)

---

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. For each rectangle drawn on grid paper, write the multiplication fact and the area.
   - 4 rows × 7 per row → fact: ___ × ___ = ___; Area = ___ sq units
   - 3 rows × 9 per row → fact: ___ × ___ = ___; Area = ___ sq units
   - 6 rows × 6 per row → fact: ___ × ___ = ___; Area = ___ sq units

2. Draw a rectangle on grid paper with an area of 24 square units. Label your rows and columns.

**Stretch:**
3. Maria draws a 5 × 7 rectangle. Her brother draws a 7 × 5 rectangle. Do they have the same area? Show how you know. (Yes — commutative property; both = 35 sq units)

4. An array has 8 rows. The total area is 40 square units. How many squares are in each row? (5 — because 8 × 5 = 40)

**Challenge:**
5. This L-shape is made of two rectangles. Find the area of each part, then the total.
   - Top part: 2 rows × 3 columns
   - Bottom part: 4 rows × 5 columns
   (Top: 6 sq units; Bottom: 20 sq units; Total: 26 sq units)

6. Find all the different rectangles you can make with exactly 16 tiles. Record each as rows × columns. Which one looks most like a square? (1×16, 2×8, 4×4 — the 4×4 is a square)

### Interleaved Review
R1. (Multiplication — Unit 1) Write the fact family for 3, 7, 21. (3×7=21, 7×3=21, 21÷3=7, 21÷7=3)

R2. (Fractions — Unit 4) Which fraction is larger: 3/4 or 3/8? How do you know? (3/4 is larger — same numerator but fourths are bigger pieces than eighths)

R3. (Subtraction — Unit 5) 500 − 247 = ___? (253)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. Draw a 3 × 8 rectangle on grid paper. Color the rows alternating colors. Write the multiplication fact and the area.
2. Finish the sentence: "Area and multiplication are connected because ___."

**Mastery = correct rectangle drawn AND area = 24 sq units AND a reasonable connection statement (e.g., "rows times columns equals area").**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to tiles; explicitly point to each row: "This row has 8. This row has 8. This row has 8. That's 3 eights. 3 × 8 = 24."
- Practice skip-counting before returning to area

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask: "If a rectangle has an area of 36 square units, what are three possible row/column combinations?" (1×36, 2×18, 3×12, 4×9, 6×6)

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Multiplication Area Chart:** Create a 10 × 10 grid. Label rows 1–10 and columns 1–10. The student finds the area of each rectangle and fills in the grid — it becomes their own multiplication chart built from area!

**Same Area, Different Shape:** Find all rectangles with area = 12 sq units. Record in a table: Length | Width | Area. Notice the pattern.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The key conceptual move in this lesson is explicit: say "area IS a multiplication fact" and point back and forth between the array of tiles and the multiplication expression. Students who connect these two representations will find area computation effortless in Lesson 3.
- Students who struggled with multiplication fluency (Units 1–2) should keep a fact chart accessible. The goal here is the conceptual connection, not timed recall. Return to fact fluency as a separate practice.
- When students build arrays with tiles, insist on straight rows and columns — a sloppy array makes it hard to see the rectangular structure.
- Notice which students already want to say "rows times columns." Encourage them to explain to others.
