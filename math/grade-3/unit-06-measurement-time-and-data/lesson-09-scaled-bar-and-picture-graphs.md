# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 6, Lesson 9

## Topic
Scaled Bar Graphs and Picture Graphs — Reading and Creating

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read a scaled picture graph where each symbol represents more than 1 (e.g., each picture = 5)
- Read a scaled bar graph where each grid line or interval represents more than 1 (e.g., scale = 2, 5, or 10)
- Multiply by the scale to find the total for a category in a picture graph
- Create a scaled bar graph or picture graph from a data table
- Solve one- and two-step problems using information in a graph

## Prerequisites
- Reading bar graphs with scale = 1 (G2)
- Multiplication by 2, 5, and 10 (Units 1–2) — essential for interpreting scales
- Multi-digit addition and subtraction for comparing categories (Unit 5)
- Basic data concepts: categories, totals, more than / less than / equal to

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper (large squares)
- Ruler and pencils
- Colored pencils or markers
- Pre-made graph examples (facilitator prepares or projects two graphs — see Worked Examples)
- Index cards for exit tickets

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate G2 bar graph knowledge; expose the need for scaled graphs.

Show a table: "Favorite Fruit Survey — 25 students voted: Apple = 20, Banana = 15, Grapes = 10, Strawberry = 5."

Ask: "If we made a bar graph where each square = 1 vote, the apple bar would be 20 squares tall. Is that a problem?" (Yes — the graph would be huge.)

"What if each square = 5 votes? Then apple = 4 squares tall." Show: 4 squares × 5 = 20. "This is a scaled graph. The scale tells us the value of each unit or symbol."

---

## Direct Instruction (12–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach how to read a scale (multiply to find actual value); model creating scaled graphs; develop problem-solving from graph data.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Scale:** The value assigned to each unit on the bar axis or each picture symbol in a picture graph (e.g., "each unit = 5 votes" or "each picture = 2 students")
- **Key / Legend:** A box on a picture graph that tells you what each symbol represents
- **Data:** Information collected by counting, measuring, or surveying
- **Category:** A group label in a graph (e.g., "Apple," "Banana")

### Instruction Notes

**Part 1 — Reading Picture Graphs with a Scale:**

Draw a picture graph:

**Favorite Pet Survey (each paw print = 2 votes)**
```
Dog:      [paw][paw][paw][paw][paw]
Cat:      [paw][paw][paw]
Fish:     [paw][paw]
Hamster:  [paw]
Key: [paw] = 2 votes
```

Ask: "How many students chose Dog?" Strategy: count the symbols (5), then multiply by the scale (5 × 2 = 10).

Ask: "How many more students chose Dog than Cat?" (Dog: 10, Cat: 6; 10 − 6 = 4 more)
Ask: "How many students voted in total?" (10 + 6 + 4 + 2 = 22 students)

**Key teaching point:** "The scale is the MULTIPLIER. Always multiply the number of symbols by the scale."

**Half-symbols:** What if a category shows 2.5 symbols? "Sometimes picture graphs show half a symbol, which means half the scale value. 2.5 symbols × 2 = 5. (You do not need to include half-symbols in the graphs you create, but you should be able to read them.)"

**Part 2 — Reading Scaled Bar Graphs:**

Draw a bar graph:

**Books Read This Month (scale: each gridline = 5 books)**

Y-axis: 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25
Bars (with bar tops at these levels): Monday: 10, Tuesday: 15, Wednesday: 5, Thursday: 20, Friday: 25

Ask: "On which day were the most books read?" (Friday — 25)
Ask: "How many more books were read on Thursday than Wednesday?" (20 − 5 = 15)
Ask: "What is the total number of books read Monday through Wednesday?" (10 + 15 + 5 = 30)

**Tip for students:** "When the bar lands BETWEEN grid lines, estimate. A bar halfway between 10 and 15 = about 12 or 13. If the scale goes by 2s, a bar between 4 and 6 = about 5."

**Part 3 — Creating a Scaled Bar Graph:**

Model the process with the Favorite Fruit data from the Warm-Up:
1. Choose a scale (here, scale = 5 because all values are multiples of 5).
2. Draw and label the axes (categories on x-axis; numbers on y-axis).
3. Determine the height of each bar: Apple → 20÷5 = 4 squares; Banana → 15÷5 = 3 squares; etc.
4. Draw bars with a ruler. Fill with color.
5. Give the graph a title. Label both axes.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 — Picture graph reading:** (Paw print graph above)
- Hamster voters: 1 × 2 = 2
- Fish voters: 2 × 2 = 4
- Cat voters: 3 × 2 = 6
- Dog voters: 5 × 2 = 10
- Total: 2+4+6+10 = 22

**Example 2 — Two-step problem:**
"How many more students voted for Dog or Cat combined than for Fish or Hamster combined?"
- Dog + Cat = 10 + 6 = 16
- Fish + Hamster = 4 + 2 = 6
- 16 − 6 = 10 more students

**Example 3 — Bar graph creation:**
Data: Hours of sunshine: Mon = 6, Tue = 4, Wed = 8, Thu = 2, Fri = 6.
Scale = 2. Bar heights (in grid squares): Mon=3, Tue=2, Wed=4, Thu=1, Fri=3.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Use this picture graph to answer questions:

**Sports Played by Third Graders (each star = 3 students)**
```
Soccer:     [star][star][star][star]
Basketball: [star][star][star][star][star]
Swimming:   [star][star]
Tennis:     [star]
```

1. How many students play soccer? (4 × 3 = 12)
2. How many students play basketball? (5 × 3 = 15)
3. How many students play swimming? (2 × 3 = 6)
4. How many more students play basketball than tennis? (15 − 3 = 12)
5. How many students were surveyed in total? (12+15+6+3 = 36)

Create activity: Give this data table. The student creates a scaled bar graph.
**Snack Preference Survey:**
| Snack | Number of students |
|---|---|
| Crackers | 10 |
| Fruit | 20 |
| Chips | 15 |
| Yogurt | 5 |

"Choose a scale of 5. Draw and label your bar graph. Give it a title."
(Bars: Crackers=2 squares, Fruit=4, Chips=3, Yogurt=1)

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

Use this bar graph for questions 1–3 (facilitator provides or draws it):

**Favorite Season Survey (scale: each unit = 4 students)**
Bar heights (grid squares): Spring=3, Summer=5, Fall=2, Winter=4

**On-level:**
1. How many students chose Summer? (5 × 4 = 20)
2. How many students chose Fall? (2 × 4 = 8)
3. How many students were surveyed in total? (3+5+2+4) × 4 = 14 × 4 = 56

4. The following data shows birds spotted at a feeder. Create a picture graph. Choose a scale that works well (scale = 2 or 5).
   | Bird | Count |
   |---|---|
   | Robin | 10 |
   | Sparrow | 6 |
   | Blue Jay | 4 |
   | Cardinal | 8 |

**Stretch:**
5. Using the bird data above (scale = 2): How many more robins were spotted than blue jays? (10 − 4 = 6)

6. A picture graph shows 3 and a half symbols for one category. The scale is "each symbol = 4." What is the value for that category? (3.5 × 4 = 14)

7. Rewrite the bird data as a bar graph using a scale of 2. What is the height of each bar?
   (Robin=5, Sparrow=3, Blue Jay=2, Cardinal=4)

**Challenge:**
8. The bar graph of favorite seasons shows Summer is the most popular. If 8 more students were added to the survey and they all picked Winter, would Winter now be the most popular? Explain.
   (Summer: 20; Winter now: 16+8=24; Yes — Winter would be most popular at 24)

9. If the scale of the bird picture graph was changed from 2 to 5, could you show the bird data accurately? Why or why not?
   (No — the counts 6 and 10 are multiples of 2 but not all are multiples of 5; 6 is not a multiple of 5, so you could not show it with whole symbols)

### Interleaved Review
R1. (Mass — L8) A bag of apples has a mass of 2 kg. A bag of oranges has a mass of 1,500 g. Which is heavier? By how much? (2,000g vs 1,500g; apples are heavier by 500 g)

R2. (Area — L3) What is the area of a rectangle with length 8 cm and width 6 cm? (48 sq cm)

R3. (Multiplication — Unit 1) 9 × 7 = ___? (63)

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

A picture graph shows:
- Red apples: 4 symbols; Green apples: 6 symbols; Yellow apples: 2 symbols
- Scale: each symbol = 5 apples

1. How many red apples are there? ___
2. How many more green apples are there than yellow apples? ___
3. What is the total number of apples in the survey? ___

**Mastery = 20 red, 20 more green than yellow, 60 total — OR at least 2 of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice one-step "multiply by scale" with simpler scales (scale = 2): "2 symbols × 2 = ___"; "5 symbols × 2 = ___"
- Then return to the graph and apply the same multiplication

### If Mastery Is Met
- "If the total had to be at most 50 apples, what is the maximum scale this graph could use?" (50 ÷ 12 symbols ≈ 4.17; so the scale would need to be 4 or fewer)

## Extension (Optional)
**Class Survey and Graph:** Conduct a survey of 4 questions (favorite color, animal, season, snack). Collect data for at least 10 people (family members, classmates). Create one picture graph and one bar graph. Write 5 questions about each graph for a partner to answer.

**Graph Analysis:** Find a real bar graph from a newspaper, magazine, or website. Identify: the scale, the categories, the largest and smallest values, and two facts you can learn from the graph.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The core skill here is reading the scale and multiplying — not drawing perfectly. If a student draws neat bars but cannot answer questions about the graph, redirect to the "read and multiply" skill.
- When students create graphs, the choice of scale matters. Guide them: "If the data values are 10, 20, 30, a scale of 5 or 10 makes sense. If values are 3, 6, 9, 12, a scale of 3 makes sense." The scale should make every bar end at a whole grid line.
- Common error: students add the number of symbols rather than multiply. Correct this directly: "The scale says each symbol represents 3. So 4 symbols = 4 GROUPS of 3 = 4 × 3 = 12, not 4 + 3 = 7."
- This lesson reinforces Unit 1–2 multiplication in a visual, applied context. Naming this connection explicitly ("You are using your multiplication skills to read graphs!") builds metacognitive awareness.
- Allow students to use the multiplication chart if fact retrieval is still effortful — the focus here is on the graphing concept.
