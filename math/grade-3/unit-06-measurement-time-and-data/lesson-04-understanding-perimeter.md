# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 6, Lesson 4

## Topic
Understanding Perimeter — Distance Around a Shape

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define perimeter as the total distance around the outside of a shape
- Measure the side lengths of a polygon using a ruler
- Find the perimeter of a polygon by adding all side lengths
- Use the unit "centimeters" or "inches" (not square units) when reporting perimeter

## Prerequisites
- Area of rectangles and counting unit squares (Lessons 1–3)
- Measuring length with a ruler in inches and centimeters (G2)
- Multi-digit addition (Unit 5)
- Understanding of polygons (closed shapes with straight sides)

## Materials Needed
- Rulers (inches and centimeters)
- Plain paper and pencils
- Color tiles (optional — for building shapes to trace)
- String or yarn (one 30 cm piece per student, for the Warm-Up)
- Colored pencils (red specifically — for tracing the perimeter)
- Index cards for exit tickets

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce perimeter physically before defining it; connect to real-world "distance around."

Give each student a 30 cm piece of string. Say: "Place this string around the outside edge of your math book. Start at one corner and go all the way around until you meet where you started."

After students lay the string, ask: "What do you notice? Does the string cover the whole surface of the book, or just the edge?" (Just the edge — the outside.) "The distance all the way around the outside of a shape is called the perimeter. The string shows us the perimeter."

Ask: "Did the string go on top of the book? Did it fill the inside?" (No — it only traced the outside.) "This is different from area, which fills the inside."

---

## Direct Instruction (12–15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Define perimeter; establish the method of adding all side lengths; introduce the "tracing the outside" visualization.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Perimeter:** The total distance around the outside of a closed flat shape
- **Side length:** The length of one side of a polygon
- **Polygon:** A flat closed shape with straight sides (triangles, rectangles, pentagons, etc.)

### Instruction Notes

**Memory anchor:** Write on the board: "peRIMeter — the RIM goes all the way around."

**CRA — Concrete:**
Build a rectangle with 4 tiles × 3 tiles. Say: "To walk around the outside of this shape, I would go along the top (4 tiles = 4 units), down the right side (3 tiles = 3 units), across the bottom (4 tiles = 4 units), up the left side (3 tiles = 3 units). Perimeter = 4 + 3 + 4 + 3 = 14 units."

Emphasize: "Each side gets counted once. I must walk all the way around and end where I started."

**CRA — Representational:**
Draw a rectangle. Use a red pencil to trace the outside edge. Label each side as you go: "This side is 5 cm. This side is 2 cm. This side is 5 cm. This side is 2 cm." Add: 5 + 2 + 5 + 2 = 14 cm.

**Units matter:** "Perimeter is measured in centimeters, inches, feet, etc. — regular length units. NOT square units. Square units are only for area."

**Introduce other polygons:** Draw a triangle with sides 3 cm, 4 cm, and 5 cm. "Polygons can have any number of sides. To find perimeter, I always add all the side lengths." 3 + 4 + 5 = 12 cm.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Rectangle: 6 cm × 4 cm
- Perimeter: 6 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 20 cm
- Or: (2 × 6) + (2 × 4) = 12 + 8 = 20 cm (show the shortcut but do not require it)

**Example 2:** Square: each side = 5 in
- Perimeter: 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20 in
- Or: 4 × 5 = 20 in (because all sides are equal)

**Example 3:** Irregular pentagon with sides 3 cm, 4 cm, 5 cm, 4 cm, 2 cm
- Perimeter: 3 + 4 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 18 cm

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. Trace the perimeter of a shape drawn on paper in red. Label each side. Add to find the perimeter.
   - Rectangle: length = 8 cm, width = 3 cm. Perimeter = ___. (8+3+8+3 = 22 cm)

2. A triangle has sides measuring 5 in, 7 in, and 6 in. What is its perimeter? (18 in)

3. Measure this shape with your ruler (hand student a small card with a drawn polygon — or draw one with labeled sides 4 cm, 4 cm, 4 cm, 4 cm). What is the perimeter? What shape is it? (16 cm; square)

4. A hexagon has all six sides equal to 3 cm. What is the perimeter? (3 × 6 = 18 cm)

5. Discussion: "If you were going to fence in a garden, would you need to know the area or the perimeter? Why?" (Perimeter — fencing goes around the outside; you need the total distance around the edge.)

---

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Find the perimeter of each shape. Show your addition.
   - Rectangle: 9 cm long, 5 cm wide → P = ___
   - Square: each side = 6 in → P = ___
   - Triangle: sides 7 cm, 8 cm, 9 cm → P = ___

2. Marcus wants to put a border around his bulletin board. The board is 4 feet tall and 6 feet wide. How much border does he need? (4+6+4+6 = 20 feet)

3. Label the missing side lengths of this rectangle: length = 10 m, width = 4 m (the student writes in the two missing sides on a diagram). Find the perimeter. (10+4+10+4 = 28 m)

**Stretch:**
4. A rectangle has a perimeter of 24 cm. One side is 4 cm. What is the length of the other side? (4 + ? + 4 + ? = 24; 8 + 2×? = 24; 2×? = 16; ? = 8 cm)

5. An equilateral triangle (all sides equal) has a perimeter of 18 inches. How long is each side? (18 ÷ 3 = 6 in)

6. The perimeter of a square is 36 cm. What is the length of one side? (36 ÷ 4 = 9 cm)

**Challenge:**
7. A fence is built around a rectangular yard. The perimeter is 40 meters. The yard is 6 meters wide. What is the length? Draw and label the rectangle. (6+?+6+? = 40; 12+2×length=40; length=14 m)

8. Can two different rectangles have the same perimeter? Try to find two rectangles with perimeter = 20 cm. Are their shapes the same? (E.g., 8×2 and 6×4 both have P=20 but look different — preview of L6)

### Interleaved Review
R1. (Multiplication — Unit 1) 9 × 6 = ___? (54)

R2. (Area — L3) A rectangle has length 7 cm and width 4 cm. What is its area? (28 sq cm)

R3. (Multi-digit addition — Unit 5) 476 + 358 = ___? (834)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

1. What is the difference between area and perimeter? (1 sentence each)
2. Find the perimeter of a rectangle that is 5 in long and 3 in wide.

**Mastery = both concepts correctly distinguished AND perimeter = 16 in.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Use the string activity again: wrap string around the shape for perimeter, then shade the inside for area. "The string shows perimeter. The shading shows area."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask: "A square has a perimeter of 20 cm. What is the area?" (each side = 5; A = 5×5 = 25 sq cm — combines perimeter and area knowledge)

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Perimeter Walk:** Go outside or to a hallway. Measure the length and width of a real rectangle (a door, a window, a table) using a measuring tape or ruler. Calculate the perimeter and discuss what it represents in that context.

**Polygon Perimeter Challenge:** Using a geoboard (or dot paper), create five different polygons. Measure each side and find the perimeter of each.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The string activity in the warm-up is crucial for building a physical sense of perimeter. Do not skip it. Students who "feel" perimeter as a distance around will not confuse it with area as easily.
- Consistently color-code throughout this unit: BLUE = area (filled in), RED = perimeter (traced edge). This visual cue helps students quickly distinguish the two concepts.
- Emphasize that the unit for perimeter is a regular length unit, not "square." Write an example of the wrong unit and cross it out dramatically: "NOT 20 square cm — just 20 cm."
- The shortcut formulas (P = 2l + 2w for rectangles; P = 4s for squares) can be introduced as patterns students notice, but do not require them to memorize formulas. The conceptual method of "add all sides" always works for any polygon.
