# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 6, Lesson 6

## Topic
Area vs. Perimeter — Comparing and Distinguishing the Two Concepts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Correctly identify whether a problem is asking for area or perimeter
- Calculate both the area AND the perimeter of the same shape and explain the difference
- Demonstrate that two shapes can have the same area but different perimeters (and vice versa)
- Choose the appropriate measurement (area or perimeter) for a given real-world situation

## Prerequisites
- Area: counting unit squares, A = l × w (Lessons 1–3)
- Perimeter: adding all side lengths, unknown sides (Lessons 4–5)
- Multi-digit addition and multiplication facts (Units 1–2, 5)

## Materials Needed
- Color tiles (at least 24 per student)
- Grid paper
- Rulers
- Two colored pencils: blue (area) and red (perimeter)
- "Area vs. Perimeter" sorting cards (facilitator prepares 10 cards with real-world scenarios — see notes)
- Index cards for exit tickets

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Rapid sorting — develop automatic recognition of which measurement a situation calls for.

Read each scenario aloud. Student responds "Area" or "Perimeter" on a whiteboard:
1. "How much carpet to cover a bedroom floor?" (Area)
2. "How much fencing to go around a yard?" (Perimeter)
3. "How many tiles to cover a tabletop?" (Area)
4. "How much ribbon to go around a picture frame?" (Perimeter)
5. "How much paint to cover a wall?" (Area)
6. "How far does an ant walk when it walks around a cracker?" (Perimeter)

Discuss: "Area fills the INSIDE. Perimeter goes around the OUTSIDE."

---

## Direct Instruction (10–12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Place area and perimeter side by side on the same shapes; show they can vary independently; build vocabulary for recognizing which is needed.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Area:** Number of square units to cover the interior surface — unit: square units
- **Perimeter:** Total distance around the exterior — unit: linear units (cm, in, m, etc.)
- **Fixed area:** When a shape's area stays the same
- **Fixed perimeter:** When a shape's perimeter stays the same

### Instruction Notes

**Anchor — both at once:**
Draw a 4 × 3 rectangle. Blue pencil: shade the inside. "Area = 4 × 3 = 12 square units." Red pencil: trace the outside edge. "Perimeter = 4 + 3 + 4 + 3 = 14 units."

Say: "Same shape — two different measurements. Area tells me about the INSIDE. Perimeter tells me about the OUTSIDE."

**Key demonstration — same area, different perimeters:**
Use 12 tiles. Build the following rectangles one at a time:
- 1 × 12: Area = 12; Perimeter = 1+12+1+12 = 26
- 2 × 6: Area = 12; Perimeter = 2+6+2+6 = 16
- 3 × 4: Area = 12; Perimeter = 3+4+3+4 = 14

Record in a table:
| Shape | Area | Perimeter |
|-------|------|-----------|
| 1 × 12 | 12 sq units | 26 units |
| 2 × 6 | 12 sq units | 16 units |
| 3 × 4 | 12 sq units | 14 units |

"All three have the SAME area (12 sq units) but DIFFERENT perimeters! Area and perimeter are NOT the same thing."

**Key demonstration — same perimeter, different areas:**
Build rectangles with perimeter = 16:
- 1 × 7: Area = 7; Perimeter = 16
- 2 × 6: Area = 12; Perimeter = 16
- 3 × 5: Area = 15; Perimeter = 16
- 4 × 4: Area = 16; Perimeter = 16

"Same perimeter, different areas. You cannot tell one from the other — they are completely independent measurements."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Rectangle 6 cm × 5 cm
- Area (blue): 6 × 5 = 30 sq cm
- Perimeter (red): 6 + 5 + 6 + 5 = 22 cm

**Example 2:** Square 7 in × 7 in
- Area (blue): 7 × 7 = 49 sq in
- Perimeter (red): 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 = 28 in

**Example 3 — Word problem decision:**
"Maria wants to buy sod (grass) for her yard. The yard is 10 m × 8 m. How much sod does she need?"
- Key: sod covers the surface → Area = 10 × 8 = 80 sq m

"She also wants to put edging around the yard. How much edging does she need?"
- Key: edging goes around the outside → Perimeter = 10+8+10+8 = 36 m

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

For each rectangle, find BOTH the area and the perimeter using blue and red.

1. Rectangle: 8 cm × 4 cm
   - Area = ___ sq cm; Perimeter = ___ cm

2. Rectangle: 5 in × 5 in (a square)
   - Area = ___ sq in; Perimeter = ___ in

3. Rectangle: 9 m × 2 m
   - Area = ___ sq m; Perimeter = ___ m

4. Sorting: "Mark is putting tile on his kitchen floor (12 sq ft) and framing his kitchen window (perimeter = 12 ft). What does each number represent — area or perimeter?" (Tile = area; framing = perimeter)

5. Build with 16 tiles: make two different rectangles that each have an area of 16 sq units. Find the perimeter of each. Are they the same? (1×16: P=34; 2×8: P=20; 4×4: P=16 — all different perimeters)

---

## Independent Practice (10–15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. For each shape, find both measurements. Label them clearly.
   - 7 cm × 3 cm rectangle: Area = ___, Perimeter = ___
   - 4 in × 9 in rectangle: Area = ___, Perimeter = ___
   - 6 m × 6 m square: Area = ___, Perimeter = ___

2. Circle which measurement is needed:
   - Buying flooring for a room: **Area / Perimeter**
   - Putting a fence around a pool: **Area / Perimeter**
   - Painting a wall: **Area / Perimeter**
   - Putting a border on a bulletin board: **Area / Perimeter**

3. Two rectangles: A is 2 cm × 8 cm; B is 4 cm × 4 cm.
   - Do they have the same area? (A: 16, B: 16 — yes)
   - Do they have the same perimeter? (A: 20, B: 16 — no)
   - Which rectangle has a larger perimeter? (A)

**Stretch:**
4. Using 20 tiles, build a rectangle with the SMALLEST possible perimeter. What is it? (4 × 5: P = 18 — the closest to a square shape minimizes perimeter)

5. Find a rectangle where the area and perimeter have the same number (with different units). Hint: try small squares. (4×4: Area=16 sq units, P=16 units — the numbers are the same, but the units are different!)

**Challenge:**
6. A farmer has exactly 24 meters of fence for a rectangular pen. What dimensions give the pen the LARGEST area? (Use the table: 1×11=11, 2×10=20, 3×9=27, 4×8=32, 5×7=35, 6×6=36 — the square shape 6×6 gives the largest area)

7. Can a shape have an area of 5 square units and a perimeter of 12 units? Draw it if possible. (Yes: a 1×5 rectangle has A=5, P=12. Others also possible.)

### Interleaved Review
R1. (Multiplication — Unit 1) What is 8 × 8? (64)

R2. (Fractions — Unit 4) Is 2/3 greater than or less than 1/2? Explain. (Greater — 2/3 > 1/2 because 2×2=4 > 1×3=3 with common denominators, or draw number line)

R3. (Division — Unit 3) 45 ÷ 9 = ___? (5)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**

A rectangle is 10 cm long and 3 cm wide.
1. What is its area? (Show work.) ___ sq cm
2. What is its perimeter? (Show work.) ___ cm
3. "I want to put a wallpaper border around this wall (the rectangle above). Should I use the area or the perimeter? Why?"

**Mastery = area = 30 sq cm AND perimeter = 26 cm AND perimeter selected for the border with correct reasoning.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Re-do the shading (blue = area, inside) and tracing (red = perimeter, outside) together on the same rectangle
- Say: "Area is how many squares fill the inside. Perimeter is how far around the outside."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask: "If this rectangle's area stayed 30 sq cm but the shape changed to 5 × 6, what would the new perimeter be?" (P = 22 cm — smaller perimeter for the same area)

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Farmer's Fence Problem:** "You have 20 meters of fence. Design the rectangular garden with the greatest area. Record your results in a table. What shape gives the largest area?" (The 5×5 square gives area = 25 sq m — students discover the square maximizes area for a given perimeter.)

**Design Challenge:** "Design a bedroom floor plan on grid paper. The room must have an area of exactly 48 sq units. Choose dimensions, then calculate the perimeter. How does the shape of the room change the perimeter?"

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This is the most important lesson in the area/perimeter sequence. More time on the table demonstrations (same area / different perimeters, same perimeter / different areas) is worth it even if it shortens independent practice.
- Preparing the sorting cards (facilitator note from the Materials section): Write 10 real-world scenarios on index cards. On the back, write "Area" or "Perimeter." Examples: covering a pool with a cover, framing a photo, painting a floor, edging a garden, installing carpet, building a fence, wrapping ribbon around a box face, adding trim to a wall, buying glass to fill a window, running around a track.
- The color-coding (blue = area, red = perimeter) should now be second nature for students. Enforce it consistently.
- Do NOT let students say "area is bigger" or "perimeter is bigger" as a general rule — the demonstrations in this lesson explicitly show that either can be larger depending on the shape. Correct this misconception directly and forcefully.
