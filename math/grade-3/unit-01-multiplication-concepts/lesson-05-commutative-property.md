# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 1, Lesson 5

## Topic
The Commutative Property of Multiplication

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Demonstrate that changing the order of factors does not change the product (3 × 4 = 4 × 3)
- Use arrays to prove the commutative property visually (rotating an array)
- Apply the commutative property to simplify solving: if you know 2 × 7, you also know 7 × 2
- State the commutative property in their own words

## Prerequisites
- Arrays (Lesson 4)
- Multiplication notation (Lesson 3)

## Materials Needed
- Counters (at least 30)
- Grid paper
- Index cards (optional, for fact pairs)
- Paper and pencil

**Standards:** 3.OA.5

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

1. "Draw a 3 × 4 array. How many total?" (12) *(Lesson 4)*
2. "Draw a 4 × 3 array. How many total?" (12) *(Lesson 4)*
3. "Hmm... both were 12. Is that a coincidence?"

---

## Direct Instruction (15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Commutative property:** Changing the order of factors does not change the product
- **Turn/rotate:** Spinning the array so rows become columns

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Build two arrays (5 minutes)**

Build a 3 × 4 array:
```
● ● ● ●
● ● ● ●
● ● ● ●
```
"3 rows of 4 = 3 × 4 = 12."

Now build a 4 × 3 array:
```
● ● ●
● ● ●
● ● ●
● ● ●
```
"4 rows of 3 = 4 × 3 = 12."

"Both have 12 counters. Same total!"

**Step 2 — The rotation trick (3 minutes)**

Take the 3 × 4 array and physically rotate it 90 degrees.

"Look — when I turn the 3 × 4 array on its side, it becomes a 4 × 3 array! The same counters, just viewed from a different direction."

"This is why the products are the same — it's the same array!"

**Step 3 — Test with more examples (4 minutes)**

Have the student verify:
- 2 × 5 and 5 × 2: both = 10
- 6 × 3 and 3 × 6: both = 18
- 1 × 7 and 7 × 1: both = 7

"Does this ALWAYS work?" (Yes!) "This is called the COMMUTATIVE PROPERTY of multiplication."

**Step 4 — Why this is useful (3 minutes)**

"If you know 2 × 8 = 16, do you also know 8 × 2?" (Yes — 16.)

"This means you don't have to memorize twice as many facts! If you know 3 × 7 = 21, you automatically know 7 × 3 = 21."

"When you see a hard fact like 8 × 3, think: 'Is 3 × 8 easier for me?' If so, use it!"

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Prove 5 × 3 = 3 × 5
- Array 1: 5 rows of 3 = 15
- Array 2: 3 rows of 5 = 15
- Same product. ✓

**Example 2:** Use the commutative property to solve 7 × 2
- "I know 2 × 7 = 14. So 7 × 2 = 14."

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Problem 1:** "Build a 2 × 6 array. Now rotate it. What do you get?"
2 × 6 = 12. Rotated: 6 × 2 = 12. Same. ✓

**Problem 2:** "If 4 × 9 = 36, what is 9 × 4?" (36) "How do you know?" (Commutative property.)

**Problem 3:** "Fill in the blank: 5 × 3 = 3 × ___" (5)

**Problem 4:** "Which is easier to solve: 7 × 2 or 2 × 7? Solve it."
Both equal 14. Most students find 2 × 7 easier (skip-count by 7 twice, or think "double 7"). ✓

**Problem 5:** "Write a pair of facts that the commutative property connects."
Example: 6 × 4 = 24 and 4 × 6 = 24. ✓

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

**★ On-level:**
1. 3 × 5 = ___ and 5 × 3 = ___ (Both 15)
2. 4 × 2 = ___ and 2 × 4 = ___ (Both 8)
3. If 6 × 7 = 42, then 7 × 6 = ___ (42)
4. Fill in: 8 × 3 = 3 × ___ (8)

**★★ Stretch:**
5. "Draw two arrays that show 2 × 6 = 6 × 2." (A 2×6 and a 6×2 array, both totaling 12.)
6. "Does the commutative property work for addition too? Give an example." (Yes: 3 + 4 = 4 + 3 = 7.)

**★★★ Challenge:**
7. "I know that 4 × 5 = 20. Without calculating, tell me three other facts I now know." (5 × 4 = 20. Also connected via addition: no new multiplication facts, but the student might suggest division facts — that's fine as a preview.)
8. "If I need to memorize all facts from 1×1 to 10×10, that's 100 facts. With the commutative property, how many do I actually need to learn?" (Only about 55 unique facts — because each pair like 3×7/7×3 is just one fact.)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

1. 6 × 3 = ___ and 3 × 6 = ___ (Both 18)
2. "In your own words, what is the commutative property?" (You can multiply numbers in any order and get the same answer.)
3. "I know 5 × 8 = 40. What is 8 × 5?" (40)

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Build both arrays side by side and count both. Repeat for 3-4 pairs.
- Physically rotate the array — this concrete experience is often the "click" moment.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6 (Multiplying by 0 and 1). Preview: "What happens when one of the factors is 0? Or 1? Something special!"

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Commutative Property Proof:** "Can you think of any operation where the order DOES matter?" (Yes — subtraction: 7 − 3 ≠ 3 − 7. Division too: 12 ÷ 3 ≠ 3 ÷ 12.)

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**The array rotation is the conceptual proof.** When the student sees that turning a 3×4 array sideways produces a 4×3 array with the same counters, the commutative property becomes obvious. Make sure this happens physically, not just in discussion.

**This halves the memorization load.** When the student eventually memorizes multiplication facts, knowing 3×7 means knowing 7×3. Explicitly make this connection: "Every fact you learn is actually two facts."

**Don't use the term "commutative" as a requirement.** What matters is that the student understands and can use the property. If they say "you can flip the numbers," that's fine.
