# Math -- Grade 3 -- Unit 4, Lesson 5

## Topic
Fractions Greater Than 1

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Understand that when the numerator is greater than the denominator, the fraction is greater than 1
- Represent fractions greater than 1 with area models using multiple wholes
- Place fractions greater than 1 on a number line from 0 to 2 (or beyond)
- Informally connect fractions greater than 1 to mixed numbers (e.g., 5/4 is "one whole and one-fourth more")

## Prerequisites
- Read, write, and understand fractions with numerator and denominator (Lessons 2-3)
- Place fractions on a number line between 0 and 1 (Lesson 4)
- Understand that a fraction equal to 1 has the same numerator and denominator (e.g., 4/4 = 1)

## Materials Needed
- Multiple copies of identical area models (e.g., 4 circles each divided into 4 equal parts, 3 rectangles each divided into 3 equal parts)
- Colored pencils or crayons
- Pre-drawn number lines from 0 to 2 and from 0 to 3
- Paper and pencil
- Paper strips (0 to 1) from Lesson 4 and additional strips to extend the line

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review what happens when all parts of a whole are shaded, and prompt curiosity about what comes next.

1. Show a circle divided into 4 equal parts. Shade 1 part. "What fraction is this?" (1/4.) Shade another part. "Now?" (2/4.) Shade another. "Now?" (3/4.) Shade the last part. "Now?" (4/4.)
2. "4/4 means all 4 parts are shaded -- that is the whole thing! 4/4 = 1."
3. "But what if I need to shade MORE than 4 parts out of fourths? What if I have 5 fourths or 7 fourths? Where do those numbers go? Let's find out!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Extend fraction understanding beyond 1 using multiple wholes in area models and an extended number line. CRA progression: concrete (multiple paper shapes), representational (area models and number lines), abstract (fraction notation and informal mixed numbers).

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fraction greater than 1:** A fraction where the numerator is greater than the denominator
- **Improper fraction:** Another name for a fraction greater than 1 (the numerator is larger than the denominator)
- **Mixed number:** A whole number and a fraction together (e.g., 1 and 1/4) -- introduced informally
- **Multiple wholes:** Using more than one whole shape to represent a fraction

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Area model -- one whole is not enough.**
Place two identical circles on the table, each divided into 4 equal parts. "Each circle is one whole. Each whole has 4 equal parts -- fourths."

Shade all 4 parts of the first circle. "I shaded 4/4. That is 1 whole." Now shade 1 part of the second circle. "I shaded 1 more fourth. In total I have shaded 5 fourths."

Write 5/4. "The denominator is still 4 because we are still working with fourths. The numerator is 5 because we shaded 5 parts total. 5/4 is more than 1 whole."

"We can also say this as 'one whole and one-fourth more,' which we write as 1 and 1/4. But the fraction 5/4 and the mixed number 1 and 1/4 are the same amount."

**Step 2: Another example with thirds.**
Place three circles, each divided into 3 equal parts. Shade all 3 parts of the first circle (3/3 = 1 whole). Shade all 3 parts of the second circle (another whole -- now 6/3 = 2 wholes). Shade 2 parts of the third circle.

"How many thirds did we shade in total?" (8.) "So the fraction is 8/3." Write it. "Is this more than 1?" (Yes.) "More than 2?" (Yes -- we filled 2 whole circles and part of a third.) "8/3 is 2 wholes and 2/3 more. We can write it as 2 and 2/3."

**Step 3: Number line -- going past 1.**
Draw a number line from 0 to 2. "Let's divide each whole into 4 equal parts." Make tick marks: between 0 and 1, place 3 tick marks (1/4, 2/4, 3/4). Between 1 and 2, place 3 more tick marks (5/4, 6/4, 7/4). Label 0, 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, 4/4 (which is 1), 5/4, 6/4, 7/4, 8/4 (which is 2).

"Look: 4/4 = 1 and 8/4 = 2. Fractions keep going past 1 the same way whole numbers do. 5/4 is just one-fourth past 1."

Place a dot at 5/4. "5/4 is here -- past 1, but before 2."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Represent 7/4 with an area model. Use 2 rectangles, each divided into 4 equal parts. Shade all 4 parts of the first rectangle (= 4/4 = 1 whole). Shade 3 parts of the second rectangle. Total shaded: 7 fourths = 7/4. "That is 1 whole and 3/4 more, which is 1 and 3/4."

**Example B:** Place 5/3 on a number line from 0 to 2. Divide each whole into 3 equal parts. Label: 0, 1/3, 2/3, 3/3 (= 1), 4/3, 5/3, 6/3 (= 2). Place a dot at 5/3. "5/3 is between 1 and 2. It is 1 whole and 2/3 more."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices representing and locating fractions greater than 1 with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Build It with Area Models**
Give the student multiple copies of the same shape (e.g., circles divided into 4 parts each).

- "Show me 6/4." (Shade all 4 parts of one circle + 2 parts of a second circle.) "How many wholes and how much more?" (1 whole and 2/4 more = 1 and 2/4.)
- "Show me 5/3 using circles divided into thirds." (Shade all 3 parts of one circle + 2 parts of a second circle.) "How many wholes and how much more?" (1 whole and 2/3 more.)
- "Show me 9/4." (Shade all 4 parts of the first circle + all 4 of the second + 1 of the third.) "How many wholes?" (2 wholes and 1/4 more = 2 and 1/4.)

**Activity 2: Plot on the Number Line**
Give the student a number line from 0 to 3, divided into fourths (tick marks every 1/4).

- "Place a dot at 3/4." (Between 0 and 1, at the third tick mark.)
- "Place a dot at 5/4." (Between 1 and 2, at the first tick mark past 1.)
- "Place a dot at 10/4." (Between 2 and 3, at the second tick mark past 2.) "Is 10/4 greater than 2?" (Yes.) "How much more?" (2/4 more, so 10/4 = 2 and 2/4.)

"Look at all three dots. Which fraction is the largest?" (10/4.) "Which is the smallest?" (3/4.)

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
1. Is 5/4 greater than 1, equal to 1, or less than 1? (Greater than 1, because 5 is more than 4.)
2. Shade area models to show 5/4. Use two rectangles divided into fourths. How many parts do you shade in total? (5)
3. On a number line from 0 to 2 divided into fourths, where is 4/4? (At 1.)
4. On the same number line, where is 6/4? (At the second tick mark past 1.)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. Draw an area model for 7/3 using rectangles divided into thirds. How many whole rectangles do you fill completely? (2) How many extra thirds? (1) Write it as a mixed number. (2 and 1/3)
2. Place 7/4 on a number line from 0 to 2. Is it closer to 1 or closer to 2? (Closer to 2 -- it is 1 and 3/4.)
3. Is 6/6 greater than 1, equal to 1, or less than 1? (Equal to 1, because 6/6 = 1.)
4. Write a fraction greater than 1 where the denominator is 3. (Answers vary, e.g., 4/3, 5/3, 7/3 -- any fraction where the numerator is greater than 3.)
5. 8/4 = ___ (2, because 8/4 means 8 fourths, and 4 fourths make 1 whole, so 8 fourths = 2 wholes.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. Place 11/4 on a number line from 0 to 3. Write it as a mixed number. (2 and 3/4, because 8/4 = 2 and there are 3 fourths remaining.)
2. Lina ate 5/3 of a granola bar (she had more than one bar). How many whole bars did she eat, and how much of another bar? (1 whole bar and 2/3 of another bar.)
3. On a number line, which is farther from 0: 9/4 or 7/3? Explain. (9/4 = 2 and 1/4. 7/3 = 2 and 1/3. 2 and 1/3 is farther from 0 because 1/3 is greater than 1/4.)
4. Write three different fractions that are all equal to 2. (Answers vary, e.g., 2/1, 4/2, 6/3, 8/4, 10/5 -- any fraction where the numerator is exactly twice the denominator.)
5. Is there a largest fraction with a denominator of 4? Explain. (No -- you can always add more fourths: 100/4, 1000/4, and so on. Fractions can keep getting bigger.)

---

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. Use area models to show 7/4. How many wholes and how much more? (1 whole and 3/4 more.)
2. On a number line from 0 to 2, place 5/3. Is 5/3 between 1 and 2, or past 2? (Between 1 and 2.)
3. How can you tell a fraction is greater than 1 just by looking at the numerator and denominator? (The numerator is greater than the denominator.)

## Teacher Notes

**CRA progression in this lesson:** Multiple concrete shapes (C) are used to physically build fractions greater than 1. The representational layer (R) includes both area model drawings with multiple wholes and extended number lines. The abstract layer (A) includes fraction notation and the informal introduction of mixed numbers.

**Keep mixed numbers informal.** The standard (3.NF.2) expects students to understand fractions on the number line, including those greater than 1. Formal conversion between improper fractions and mixed numbers typically comes in Grade 4. In this lesson, we introduce the mixed number language naturally -- "one whole and three-fourths more" -- but do NOT require the student to do formal conversion procedures.

**Multiple wholes are the key visual.** The most common confusion with fractions greater than 1 is that students think the numerator cannot be larger than the denominator. Using multiple identical shapes makes this visible: you simply need more than one whole to show all the parts. Always use identical shapes so the "whole" stays consistent.

**Common errors:**
- Student thinks fractions cannot be greater than 1. Prompt: "When we shaded 4/4, that was 1 whole. What if we keep shading more fourths? We need another whole shape."
- Student confuses the whole number of shapes with the fraction. For 5/4, they may say "2" because there are 2 shapes on the table. Prompt: "Count ALL the shaded parts. The denominator stays 4 because we are working in fourths."
- On the number line, student restarts counting at 1 (calls the first tick mark past 1 as 1/4 instead of 5/4). Prompt: "Keep counting from where you left off. After 4/4 comes 5/4, then 6/4."

**Facilitator language tip:** "5/4 means five fourths. You already know that four-fourths is one whole. So five-fourths is one whole and one more fourth." Use this pattern -- relate back to the "full whole" -- every time.

**Standard alignment:** This lesson addresses 3.NF.2 -- representing fractions on a number line, extended to values greater than 1. Understanding that fractions are not limited to the interval between 0 and 1 is a critical conceptual milestone that prepares students for operations with fractions in later grades.

**Extension if time allows:** Ask the student to find a fraction greater than 2 but less than 3. They should write it and place it on the number line. This reinforces that the number line extends indefinitely and fractions exist between every pair of whole numbers, not just between 0 and 1.
