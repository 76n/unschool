# Math — Grade 3 — Unit 4, Lesson 7

## Topic
Comparing Fractions with the Same Numerator

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Compare two fractions that have the same numerator but different denominators
- Explain that when you have the same number of pieces, bigger pieces make a larger fraction
- Recognize that a larger denominator means smaller pieces
- Use fraction strips and number lines to verify same-numerator comparisons

## Prerequisites
- Comparing fractions with the same denominator (Lesson 6)
- Understanding that the denominator tells the size of each piece (Lessons 2-3)
- Placing fractions on a number line (Lesson 4)

## Materials Needed
- Fraction strips (halves, thirds, fourths, sixths, eighths)
- Number lines (0 to 1) marked in thirds, fourths, sixths, and eighths
- Colored pencils or crayons
- Paper and pencil
- Two sheets of paper the same size (for folding demonstration)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review same-denominator comparisons and activate thinking about piece size.

1. "Compare 2/8 and 5/8. Which is greater?" (5/8 > 2/8) "How did you decide?" (Same denominator, so compare numerators)
2. "If you cut a pizza into 2 big slices and another same-size pizza into 8 small slices, which slices are bigger?" (The slices from the pizza cut into 2)
3. "Today we are going to compare fractions where the numerators are the same but the denominators are different. This is trickier — watch carefully!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build understanding that with the same numerator, the fraction with the smaller denominator is greater.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Same numerator:** Both fractions have the same top number — you have the same number of pieces
- **Piece size:** Determined by the denominator — more cuts means smaller pieces
- **Denominator:** The bottom number tells how many equal pieces the whole was cut into

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Concrete exploration with paper folding.**

Take two identical sheets of paper. Fold one into 3 equal parts (thirds). Fold the other into 6 equal parts (sixths). Shade 1 part on each sheet.

"Both sheets show 1 piece. But look — the piece from the paper cut into thirds is much bigger than the piece from the paper cut into sixths. So 1/3 is greater than 1/6."

"This might seem surprising. 6 is a bigger number than 3, but sixths are SMALLER than thirds. Why? Because when you cut the same whole into more pieces, each piece gets smaller."

**Step 2: Fraction strip comparison.**

Lay out fraction strips. Line up 1/3 and 1/8 side by side, starting at the left edge.

"Both strips show 1 piece. But the third is much longer than the eighth. 1/3 > 1/8."

Now try 2/4 and 2/8. "Both show 2 pieces. But fourths are bigger pieces than eighths. Two big pieces is more than two small pieces. 2/4 > 2/8."

**Step 3: State the rule.**

"When the numerators are the same, look at the denominators. The smaller denominator means bigger pieces. Bigger pieces means a bigger fraction."

"Think of it this way: would you rather have 1 slice of a pizza cut into 4 pieces, or 1 slice of the same pizza cut into 8 pieces? The fourths are bigger slices!"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Compare 1/3 and 1/6.

"Same numerator: both are 1. So I have 1 piece of each. Now I compare piece sizes. Thirds means the whole is cut into 3 pieces — big pieces. Sixths means the whole is cut into 6 pieces — smaller pieces. 1 big piece > 1 small piece. So 1/3 > 1/6."

Verify with fraction strips: lay the 1/3 strip above the 1/6 strip. The 1/3 strip extends farther.

**Example B:** Compare 3/4 and 3/8.

"Same numerator: both are 3. I have 3 pieces of each. Fourths are bigger pieces than eighths because 4 < 8. Three big pieces is more than three small pieces. 3/4 > 3/8."

Verify on a number line: 3/4 is at the three-quarter mark (close to 1). 3/8 is just past the one-third mark. 3/4 is clearly farther right.

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice same-numerator comparisons with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Fraction strip check.**

For each pair, first predict which is greater, then verify with fraction strips.

- Compare 1/4 and 1/8. "Both show 1 piece. Which pieces are bigger?" (Fourths. 1/4 > 1/8)
- Compare 2/3 and 2/6. "Both show 2 pieces. Thirds or sixths — which are bigger pieces?" (Thirds. 2/3 > 2/6)
- Compare 1/2 and 1/4. (1/2 > 1/4)
- Compare 3/6 and 3/8. "Both show 3 pieces." (Sixths are bigger than eighths. 3/6 > 3/8)

"If the student says the fraction with the bigger denominator is greater, go back to the paper strips. Have them physically hold up the two pieces side by side."

**Activity 2: The tricky question.**

"Marcus says 1/8 > 1/3 because 8 is bigger than 3. What would you tell Marcus?"

Guide the student to explain: "8 is a bigger number, but eighths are smaller pieces. When you cut something into 8 parts, each part is tiny. When you cut it into 3 parts, each part is big. So 1/3 is actually greater than 1/8."

"If the student agrees with Marcus, pull out fraction strips immediately. Show them physically that the 1/3 strip is longer than the 1/8 strip."

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
Compare. Write >, <, or =. Use fraction strips to check your answers.

1. 1/2 ___ 1/4 (1/2 > 1/4)
2. 1/3 ___ 1/8 (1/3 > 1/8)
3. 1/6 ___ 1/3 (1/6 < 1/3)
4. 1/4 ___ 1/6 (1/4 > 1/6)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
Compare. Write >, <, or =.

1. 2/4 ___ 2/8 (2/4 > 2/8)
2. 1/6 ___ 1/2 (1/6 < 1/2)
3. 3/4 ___ 3/6 (3/4 > 3/6)
4. 2/3 ___ 2/8 (2/3 > 2/8)
5. "Is 1/4 greater or less than 1/8? Explain how you know." (1/4 > 1/8. Fourths are bigger pieces than eighths because the whole is cut into fewer parts.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
Compare. Write >, <, or =. Then explain your reasoning.

1. 4/6 ___ 4/8 (4/6 > 4/8)
2. 2/3 ___ 2/4 (2/3 > 2/4)
3. "Mia says 1/2 is less than 1/6 because 2 < 6. Is she right? Explain." (No. Halves are much bigger pieces than sixths. 1/2 > 1/6.)
4. "Order from least to greatest: 1/8, 1/3, 1/4." (1/8, 1/4, 1/3 — the bigger the denominator, the smaller the piece)
5. "Name a fraction with a numerator of 2 that is greater than 2/6 but less than 2/3." (2/4 — fourths are between thirds and sixths in piece size)

---

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. Compare: 1/3 ___ 1/8. Write >, <, or =. (1/3 > 1/8)
2. Compare: 2/6 ___ 2/4. Write >, <, or =. (2/6 < 2/4)
3. "Why is 1/3 greater than 1/6 even though 6 is bigger than 3?" (When you cut a whole into more pieces, each piece is smaller. Thirds are bigger pieces than sixths, so 1/3 > 1/6.)

**Mastery = All 3 correct.**

## Teacher Notes

**This is one of the hardest fraction ideas for 8-year-olds.** The relationship between the denominator and piece size is counterintuitive: a bigger number on the bottom means a SMALLER fraction. Expect this lesson to need extra time and repeated physical demonstrations.

**Do not rush to the abstract rule.** The biggest mistake is skipping straight to "smaller denominator means bigger fraction" without enough hands-on work. Let the student fold, cut, and compare strips until the pattern clicks. The abstract rule should feel obvious, not memorized.

**Connect to real life:** "Would you rather have 1/2 of a candy bar or 1/8 of the same candy bar?" This question makes the concept click for many students because they instinctively know they want the bigger piece.

**Common error:** Students who learned "bigger number is greater" from whole numbers will apply that rule to denominators. Be patient. This is a conceptual shift, not a careless mistake.

**Standard:** 3.NF.3d — Compare two fractions with the same numerator or the same denominator by reasoning about their size.
