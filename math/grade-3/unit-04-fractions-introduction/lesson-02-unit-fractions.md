# Math -- Grade 3 -- Unit 4, Lesson 2

## Topic
Unit Fractions -- Naming Parts

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Understand that a unit fraction has 1 as its numerator
- Name unit fractions: 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8
- Identify the numerator (1 part shaded) and denominator (total equal parts)
- Connect the fraction symbol to a shaded area model

## Prerequisites
- Identify equal parts of a whole (Lesson 1)
- Divide shapes into 2, 3, and 4 equal parts (Lesson 1)
- Understand that "the whole" is the entire shape before dividing

## Materials Needed
- Pre-drawn area models (circles and rectangles divided into 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8 equal parts)
- Colored pencils or crayons
- Fraction cards (shapes with one part shaded, fraction written on back)
- Paper and pencil
- Paper shapes from Lesson 1 (for reference)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review equal parts and set the stage for naming those parts with fractions.

1. Show a rectangle divided into 4 equal parts. "How many equal parts?" (4.) "Good -- we said these are called fourths."
2. Show a circle divided into 3 equal parts. "How many equal parts?" (3.) "These are called thirds."
3. "Today we are going to learn how to write a special number that tells us exactly how much of the shape one part is. These special numbers are called fractions!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce fraction notation for unit fractions, connecting the concrete area model (R) to the abstract symbol (A). CRA progression: representational models with shading, then the abstract fraction symbol.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fraction:** A number that names part of a whole
- **Numerator:** The top number -- tells how many parts are shaded (or selected)
- **Denominator:** The bottom number -- tells how many equal parts the whole is divided into
- **Unit fraction:** A fraction where the numerator is 1 (one part out of the total)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: From shape to symbol.**
Show a circle divided into 4 equal parts with 1 part shaded. "How many equal parts does this circle have?" (4.) "How many parts are shaded?" (1.) "We write this as a fraction: 1/4. The bottom number, 4, tells us the whole is cut into 4 equal parts. The top number, 1, tells us 1 part is shaded."

Write 1/4 clearly. Point to the numerator: "This is the numerator -- it counts the shaded parts." Point to the denominator: "This is the denominator -- it tells the total number of equal parts."

**Step 2: Try more unit fractions.**
Show a rectangle divided into 2 equal parts with 1 shaded. "How many equal parts?" (2.) "How many shaded?" (1.) "The fraction is 1/2." Write it down.

Show a rectangle divided into 3 equal parts with 1 shaded. "The fraction is?" (1/3.) "How do you know?" (3 equal parts total, 1 is shaded.)

Show a circle divided into 6 equal parts with 1 shaded. "Write the fraction." (1/6.) "And one divided into 8 equal parts with 1 shaded?" (1/8.)

**Step 3: What makes these unit fractions?**
"Look at all the fractions we wrote: 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8. What do they all have in common?" (They all have 1 on top.) "Exactly! When the numerator is 1, we call it a unit fraction. It means we are talking about just one part of the whole."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** A rectangle is divided into 3 equal parts. The left part is shaded. "What fraction of the rectangle is shaded?" The whole has 3 equal parts (denominator = 3). One part is shaded (numerator = 1). The fraction is 1/3.

**Example B:** A circle is divided into 8 equal parts (like a pizza cut into 8 slices). One slice is shaded. "What fraction is shaded?" The whole has 8 equal parts (denominator = 8). One part is shaded (numerator = 1). The fraction is 1/8. "Is this a unit fraction?" (Yes, because the numerator is 1.)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices reading area models and writing unit fractions with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Name That Fraction**
Show 5 area models one at a time. Each has one part shaded out of equal parts. The student writes the fraction.

- Circle divided into 2, one part shaded. (1/2)
- Rectangle divided into 4, one part shaded. (1/4)
- Rectangle divided into 6, one part shaded. (1/6)
- Circle divided into 3, one part shaded. (1/3)
- Rectangle divided into 8, one part shaded. (1/8)

After each: "What is the denominator? What is the numerator? Is this a unit fraction?"

**Activity 2: Draw and Shade**
The student draws the area model to match a given fraction.

- "Draw a rectangle. Divide it into 4 equal parts. Shade 1 part. Write 1/4 below it."
- "Draw a circle. Divide it into 3 equal parts. Shade 1 part. Write 1/3 below it."
- "Draw a rectangle. Divide it into 2 equal parts. Shade 1 part. Write 1/2 below it."

"For each one, point to the numerator and tell me what it means. Then point to the denominator and tell me what it means."

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
1. A circle is divided into 2 equal parts. One part is shaded. Write the fraction. (1/2)
2. A rectangle is divided into 4 equal parts. One part is shaded. Write the fraction. (1/4)
3. In the fraction 1/3, what does the 3 mean? (The whole is divided into 3 equal parts.)
4. In the fraction 1/6, what does the 1 mean? (One part is shaded or selected.)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. A rectangle is divided into 8 equal parts with 1 shaded. Write the fraction. (1/8)
2. Draw an area model for 1/6. (A shape divided into 6 equal parts with 1 shaded.)
3. Is 1/4 a unit fraction? Explain. (Yes, because the numerator is 1.)
4. A circle has 3 equal parts and none are shaded. What fraction is shaded? (0/3 or zero -- no parts are shaded.)
5. Name three unit fractions. (Answers vary, e.g., 1/2, 1/5, 1/10 -- any fraction with 1 on top.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. Draw area models for 1/2, 1/4, and 1/8. Which shaded piece is the biggest? Which is the smallest? (1/2 is biggest, 1/8 is smallest.)
2. Jayden says 1/8 is bigger than 1/2 because 8 is bigger than 2. Is Jayden correct? Explain. (No. More equal parts means each part is smaller. 1/8 is smaller than 1/2.)
3. Write a unit fraction where the denominator is 10. Draw an area model for it. (1/10 -- a shape divided into 10 equal parts with 1 shaded.)
4. If a pizza is cut into 6 equal slices and you eat 1 slice, what unit fraction of the pizza did you eat? (1/6)
5. Without drawing, which is bigger: 1/3 or 1/6? Explain your thinking. (1/3 is bigger because dividing into fewer parts makes each part larger.)

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. A rectangle is divided into 4 equal parts. One part is shaded. Write the fraction and label the numerator and denominator. (1/4; numerator = 1, denominator = 4)
2. What makes a fraction a "unit fraction"? (The numerator is 1 -- it represents one part of the whole.)

## Teacher Notes

**CRA progression in this lesson:** Students work primarily at the representational level (area models with shading) and the abstract level (fraction symbols). If a student struggles, return to the concrete level from Lesson 1 -- fold a paper shape, shade one section, and then write the fraction.

**The denominator is the hardest idea.** Many students initially think the denominator counts "the other parts" or "the unshaded parts." Consistently reinforce: "The denominator is the TOTAL number of equal parts the whole is divided into -- shaded AND unshaded."

**Unit fractions are the building blocks.** Understanding that 1/4 means "one out of four equal parts" is the foundation for all fraction work. Non-unit fractions (Lesson 3) are simply multiples of unit fractions: 3/4 is three copies of 1/4.

**Common errors:**
- Student swaps numerator and denominator (writes 4/1 instead of 1/4). Prompt: "Which number goes on top -- the parts you shaded or the total parts?"
- Student writes the fraction based on unequal parts. Remind: "Check first -- are the parts equal? If not, we cannot write a fraction."
- Student thinks 1/8 is bigger than 1/4 because 8 is bigger. Use area models side by side to show that more parts means smaller pieces.
- Student reads 1/3 as "one and three" rather than "one-third." Model the correct pronunciation repeatedly and have the student practice saying it aloud.

**Facilitator language tip:** Use the phrase "one out of [number] equal parts" every time you read a unit fraction aloud: "1/4 means one out of four equal parts."

**Standard alignment:** This lesson addresses 3.NF.1 -- understanding a fraction 1/b as the quantity formed by 1 part when a whole is partitioned into b equal parts. Unit fractions are the building blocks of all fraction reasoning, so mastery here is essential before moving to non-unit fractions in Lesson 3.

**Extension if time allows:** Challenge the student to order unit fractions from largest to smallest: 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/6, 1/8. Use area models side by side to verify that more parts means smaller pieces. Ask: "Which would you rather have -- 1/3 of a cake or 1/8 of the same cake?" This informal comparison sets the stage for formal fraction comparison later in the unit.
