# Math -- Grade 3 -- Unit 4, Lesson 1

## Topic
Equal Parts of a Whole

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Identify whether shapes are divided into equal or unequal parts
- Divide shapes (circles, rectangles, squares) into 2, 3, and 4 equal parts
- Understand that "the whole" is the entire shape before it is divided
- Use paper folding to create and verify equal parts physically

## Prerequisites
- Recognize basic geometric shapes (circle, rectangle, square)
- Understand the concept of "same size" or "the same amount"
- Experience with equal sharing from Unit 3 (division)

## Materials Needed
- Paper shapes pre-cut: circles, rectangles, and squares (at least 6 of each)
- Blank paper for folding
- Colored pencils or crayons
- Scissors
- Ruler or straight edge
- Pencil

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of equal sharing and connect it to shapes.

1. "If you have a sandwich and want to share it with one friend so you each get the same amount, what would you do?" (Cut it in half / into 2 equal pieces.)
2. "What if three people want to share a pizza equally? How many pieces do we need?" (3 equal pieces.)
3. "Today we are going to explore what it means to divide shapes into equal parts. This is the very first step toward understanding fractions!"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build understanding of equal vs. unequal parts using concrete and representational models (CRA progression: concrete first with paper folding, then representational with drawn shapes).

### Key Vocabulary
- **Equal parts:** Pieces that are exactly the same size
- **Unequal parts:** Pieces that are NOT the same size
- **Whole:** The entire shape before it is divided into parts
- **Halves:** 2 equal parts; **Thirds:** 3 equal parts; **Fourths (quarters):** 4 equal parts

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Concrete -- Paper folding.**
Hand the student a paper rectangle. "Fold this paper so you get 2 parts that are exactly the same size." Let them fold. Open it up. "Are both parts the same size?" (Yes.) "These are called equal parts. We split the whole rectangle into 2 equal parts."

Now hand them another rectangle. "Can you fold it into 2 parts that are NOT the same size?" Let them fold unevenly. Open it up. "Are these parts the same size?" (No.) "These are unequal parts. When we talk about fractions, we always need equal parts."

**Step 2: Concrete -- Folding into 3 and 4 parts.**
Give the student a fresh rectangle. "Try to fold this into 4 equal parts." (Fold in half, then in half again.) Open and check. "Count the sections -- how many?" (4.) "Are they equal?" (Yes.)

Give another rectangle. "Now try 3 equal parts. This is trickier!" Help as needed -- fold into thirds by bringing one edge a third of the way, then folding the other side over. Open and check. "We have 3 equal parts. These are called thirds."

**Step 3: Representational -- Looking at pictures.**
Draw or show two circles side by side. One is split evenly down the middle. The other is split with a line off-center. "Which circle is divided into equal parts?" (The first one.) "How do you know?" (Both halves look the same size.)

Repeat with a rectangle split into 4 equal pieces versus 4 unequal pieces. "Always check: are the parts the same size?"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** A square is drawn and divided by one vertical line through the center and one horizontal line through the center, making 4 equal parts. "How many equal parts?" (4.) "Is this divided equally?" (Yes, all 4 parts are the same size.) "The whole square is divided into fourths."

**Example B:** A circle is drawn with two lines, but one line is near the edge, creating one large section and three small sections. "Is this divided into equal parts?" (No.) "Even though there are 4 pieces, they are not the same size, so these are NOT equal parts. For fractions, we need equal parts."

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices identifying and creating equal parts with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Sort It Out**
Present 6 shape drawings (mix of circles, rectangles, and squares). Some are divided into equal parts, some are not. The student sorts each into "Equal Parts" or "Unequal Parts."

- Rectangle split into 2 equal halves (Equal)
- Circle split into 3 unequal pieces (Unequal)
- Square split into 4 equal quarters (Equal)
- Rectangle split into 3 equal thirds (Equal)
- Circle split in half but the line is off-center (Unequal)
- Square with one diagonal line, then another line making 3 unequal triangles and a quadrilateral (Unequal)

"For each one, tell me: how many parts are there, and are they equal?"

**Activity 2: Fold and Divide**
Give the student 3 blank paper shapes (circle, rectangle, square). Ask them to:
- Fold the rectangle into 2 equal parts. Trace the fold line with a pencil. Label: "2 equal parts."
- Fold the square into 4 equal parts. Trace the fold lines. Label: "4 equal parts."
- Try to fold the circle into 3 equal parts (this is hard -- guide as needed). Trace and label: "3 equal parts."

"Each time, the whole shape is what we started with, and the pieces are the equal parts."

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
1. A rectangle is divided into 2 parts by a line through the center. Are these equal parts? (Yes)
2. A circle is divided into 2 parts by a line that is NOT through the center. Are these equal parts? (No)
3. Draw a line to divide this rectangle into 2 equal parts. (Student draws a vertical or horizontal line through the center.)
4. A square is divided into 4 parts by two centered lines. How many equal parts? (4)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. A rectangle is divided into 3 parts. Two parts are large and one is small. Are these equal parts? (No)
2. Draw lines to divide a rectangle into 4 equal parts. (Student draws 3 equally spaced vertical or horizontal lines, or a cross through the center.)
3. A circle is divided into 2 equal parts. What do we call each part? (A half)
4. Fold a square piece of paper into 3 equal parts. How many fold lines did you make? (2)
5. True or false: If a shape is split into 4 pieces, it is always divided into equal parts. (False -- the pieces might not be the same size.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. Draw a rectangle and divide it into 3 equal parts. Then divide it a different way into 3 equal parts. (Vertical thirds vs. horizontal thirds.)
2. A pizza is cut into 6 pieces, but 2 pieces are much bigger than the others. Is this divided into equal parts? Explain. (No -- equal parts must all be the same size, even if there are 6 pieces.)
3. Can you divide a square into 2 equal parts in more than one way? Show two different ways. (Vertical line through center; horizontal line through center; diagonal line -- all valid.)
4. Marcus says this shape is divided into 3 equal parts, but one part is clearly bigger. What would you tell Marcus? (The parts are not equal because they are not the same size. He needs to make all three parts the same size.)
5. Draw a shape of your choice and divide it into 4 equal parts. Explain how you know they are equal. (Answers vary -- student should articulate that all 4 parts are the same size.)

---

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. Look at this rectangle divided into 3 parts. Are the parts equal or unequal? How do you know? (Depends on drawing -- student should check whether all parts are the same size.)
2. Fold a piece of paper into 4 equal parts. How many fold lines do you need? (2 -- fold in half, then in half again.)
3. What does "the whole" mean? (The whole is the entire shape before it is divided into parts.)

---

## Answer Key

**Independent Practice:**

*Tier 1:* 1. Yes  2. No  3. Line through center  4. 4

*Tier 2:* 1. No  2. Cross through center or 3 equally spaced lines  3. A half  4. 2  5. False

*Tier 3:* 1. Two different orientations of thirds  2. No -- equal parts must be the same size  3. Yes -- vertical, horizontal, or diagonal splits  4. The parts are not the same size, so they are not equal  5. Answers vary

**Exit Ticket:** 1. Check if parts are the same size  2. 2 fold lines  3. The entire shape before dividing

## Teacher Notes

**CRA progression in this lesson:** We begin with concrete paper folding (C), move to representational drawings of divided shapes (R), and the abstract vocabulary -- halves, thirds, fourths -- is introduced alongside the concrete and representational models. Full abstract fraction notation (1/2, 1/3, etc.) comes in Lesson 2.

**Why equal parts matter so much:** The entire concept of fractions depends on equal parts. If students do not firmly grasp that fractions require equal-sized pieces, they will miscount fractional amounts later. Spend extra time here if needed -- it is worth it.

**Common errors:**
- Student counts pieces without checking size. Prompt: "Are all the pieces the same size? Count is not enough -- they have to be equal."
- Student struggles to fold into thirds. This is normal. Guide them physically and reassure them that thirds are harder to fold than halves or fourths.
- Student confuses "parts" with "cuts." Clarify: "One cut makes 2 parts. Two cuts make 3 parts."
- Student thinks differently shaped parts cannot be equal. In rectangles, a vertical half and a horizontal half are both valid halves even though they look different in orientation. If the student questions this, fold and compare by overlapping.

**Facilitator language tip:** Consistently use the phrase "equal parts" rather than just "parts" so the idea of equality becomes automatic.

**Standard alignment:** This lesson addresses 3.NF.1 -- understanding a fraction 1/b as the quantity formed by 1 part when a whole is partitioned into b equal parts. The focus here is on the foundational idea of partitioning into equal parts, which directly supports the fraction notation introduced in Lesson 2.

**Extension if time allows:** Have the student find real-world examples of equal parts -- a window pane divided into equal sections, a chocolate bar with equal squares, or a book shelf with equal-width compartments. Ask: "Are the parts equal? How do you know?" This builds the habit of seeing fractions in everyday life.
