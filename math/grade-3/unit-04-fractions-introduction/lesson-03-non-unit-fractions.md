# Math -- Grade 3 -- Unit 4, Lesson 3

## Topic
Non-Unit Fractions

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Understand fractions with numerators greater than 1 (e.g., 2/3, 3/4, 5/8)
- Read and write fractions using numerator/denominator notation
- Shade area models to represent non-unit fractions
- Use set models to connect fractions to groups of objects (e.g., 3/4 of 8 objects = shade 6)

## Prerequisites
- Identify and write unit fractions from area models (Lesson 2)
- Know the meaning of numerator and denominator (Lesson 2)
- Divide shapes into equal parts (Lesson 1)

## Materials Needed
- Pre-drawn area models (circles and rectangles divided into 2, 3, 4, 6, and 8 equal parts)
- Colored pencils or crayons
- Counters or small objects (at least 24)
- Paper and pencil
- Small cups or plates (for set models)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review unit fractions and activate readiness for fractions with larger numerators.

1. Show a circle divided into 4 equal parts with 1 shaded. "What fraction is shaded?" (1/4.) "What does the 1 mean?" (1 part shaded.) "What does the 4 mean?" (4 equal parts total.)
2. "What if I shade one more part -- now 2 parts out of 4 are shaded. Can we still write a fraction for that?" (Yes.) "What would it be?" (2/4.)
3. "Today we are going to learn about fractions where more than one part is shaded. These are called non-unit fractions."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Extend fraction understanding from unit fractions to non-unit fractions using area models and set models. CRA progression: concrete set models with counters, representational area models with shading, abstract fraction notation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Non-unit fraction:** A fraction where the numerator is greater than 1 (more than one part is selected)
- **Numerator:** The top number -- how many equal parts are shaded or selected
- **Denominator:** The bottom number -- the total number of equal parts in the whole
- **Set model:** A group of objects used to show a fraction (e.g., 3 out of 4 objects)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Area models with multiple parts shaded.**
Draw a rectangle divided into 4 equal parts. Shade 3 of them. "How many equal parts total?" (4.) "How many are shaded?" (3.) "We write this fraction as 3/4. The numerator is 3 because 3 parts are shaded. The denominator is 4 because the whole is divided into 4 equal parts."

Draw a circle divided into 3 equal parts. Shade 2. "Write the fraction." (2/3.) "How did you know?" (2 parts shaded out of 3 equal parts.)

"Notice that in Lesson 2, all our fractions had 1 on top. Now the top number can be 2, 3, 5, or any number. As long as we count shaded parts on top and total equal parts on the bottom, we have a fraction."

**Step 2: Reading fractions aloud.**
"Let's practice reading fractions: 2/3 is 'two-thirds.' 3/4 is 'three-fourths.' 5/8 is 'five-eighths.' The denominator tells us the name of the parts, and the numerator tells us how many of those parts."

Write several fractions and have the student read them: 3/6 ("three-sixths"), 5/8 ("five-eighths"), 2/4 ("two-fourths"), 4/6 ("four-sixths").

**Step 3: Set models -- fractions of a group.**
Place 8 counters on the table. "These 8 counters are the whole set. Let's find 3/4 of these 8 counters."

First, divide the 8 counters into 4 equal groups (2 in each group). "The denominator is 4, so we make 4 equal groups. How many in each group?" (2.)

"The numerator is 3, so we take 3 of those groups." Push 3 groups forward (6 counters). "3/4 of 8 is 6."

Try another: "Find 2/3 of 6 counters." Divide 6 into 3 groups (2 each). Take 2 groups. "2/3 of 6 is 4."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** A rectangle is divided into 6 equal parts. 4 parts are shaded. "What fraction is shaded?" Numerator = 4 (shaded parts). Denominator = 6 (total equal parts). The fraction is 4/6. "Read it aloud: four-sixths."

**Example B:** There are 12 counters. Find 3/4 of 12. Step 1: Divide 12 into 4 equal groups = 3 in each group. Step 2: Take 3 groups = 3 x 3 = 9. "3/4 of 12 is 9."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices writing non-unit fractions from area models and using set models with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Shade and Write**
Give the student pre-drawn shapes and ask them to shade the given fraction, then write it.

- "Shade 2/4 of this rectangle." (Rectangle divided into 4 parts -- shade 2.) "Write the fraction." (2/4)
- "Shade 5/6 of this circle." (Circle divided into 6 parts -- shade 5.) "Write the fraction." (5/6)
- "Shade 3/8 of this rectangle." (Rectangle divided into 8 parts -- shade 3.) "Write the fraction." (3/8)

After each: "Read the fraction aloud. What is the numerator? What is the denominator?"

**Activity 2: Set Model Practice**
Use counters on the table.

- "Find 1/2 of 10 counters." (Divide 10 into 2 groups of 5. Take 1 group = 5.)
- "Find 2/3 of 9 counters." (Divide 9 into 3 groups of 3. Take 2 groups = 6.)
- "Find 3/4 of 8 counters." (Divide 8 into 4 groups of 2. Take 3 groups = 6.)

"Each time, the denominator tells you how many groups to make, and the numerator tells you how many groups to take."

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
1. A rectangle is divided into 4 equal parts. 2 parts are shaded. Write the fraction. (2/4)
2. A circle is divided into 3 equal parts. 2 parts are shaded. Write the fraction. (2/3)
3. Shade 3/4 of this rectangle (divided into 4 equal parts). (Shade 3 out of 4 parts.)
4. Read this fraction aloud: 5/8. (Five-eighths.)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
1. A circle is divided into 8 equal parts. 5 parts are shaded. Write the fraction. (5/8)
2. Shade 4/6 of this rectangle (divided into 6 equal parts). Write the fraction below it. (4/6)
3. Find 3/4 of 12 counters. (9)
4. Find 2/3 of 6 counters. (4)
5. What is the difference between a unit fraction and a non-unit fraction? (A unit fraction has a numerator of 1. A non-unit fraction has a numerator greater than 1.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. Find 5/6 of 18 counters. Show your work. (Divide 18 into 6 groups of 3. Take 5 groups = 15.)
2. A rectangle is divided into 8 equal parts. You shade some parts and write the fraction 6/8. How many parts did you shade? How many are unshaded? (6 shaded, 2 unshaded.)
3. Mia says that 3/4 and 3/8 are the same because they both have 3 on top. Is she correct? Explain. (No. 3/4 means 3 out of 4 equal parts, and 3/8 means 3 out of 8 equal parts. The denominators are different, so the fractions represent different amounts.)
4. Find 3/4 of 20 counters. (Divide 20 into 4 groups of 5. Take 3 groups = 15.)
5. Draw an area model for 7/8. How many parts are NOT shaded? (1 part is not shaded.)

---

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. A rectangle is divided into 6 equal parts. 5 parts are shaded. Write the fraction. (5/6)
2. Find 2/4 of 8 counters. (4)
3. Read this fraction aloud and tell me what the numerator and denominator mean: 3/8. (Three-eighths. The numerator 3 means 3 parts are shaded. The denominator 8 means the whole is divided into 8 equal parts.)

## Teacher Notes

**CRA progression in this lesson:** The set model activity (counters on the table) is the concrete layer. Area models with shading are the representational layer. Writing and reading fraction symbols is the abstract layer. Move back to concrete if the student struggles with the representational or abstract.

**Set models are powerful but tricky.** Students sometimes confuse the total number of objects with the denominator. Reinforce: "The denominator tells you how many GROUPS to make, not how many objects there are. Then the numerator tells you how many groups to select."

**Non-unit fractions build on unit fractions.** Help the student see that 3/4 is just three copies of 1/4. If they can find 1/4 of a shape, they can find 3/4 by taking three of those parts. This "unit fraction as building block" idea is central to fraction reasoning.

**Common errors:**
- Student shades the wrong number of parts (shading the denominator number instead of the numerator). Prompt: "The top number tells you how many to shade. Which number is on top?"
- In set models, student does not divide into equal groups first. Prompt: "Before you take any, first split all the counters into [denominator] equal groups."
- Student writes fraction as denominator/numerator. Prompt: "The shaded parts always go on top."

**Facilitator language tip:** When reading fractions aloud, emphasize the denominator name: "Three-FOURTHS -- fourths tells us what kind of parts we have, and three tells us how many of them."
