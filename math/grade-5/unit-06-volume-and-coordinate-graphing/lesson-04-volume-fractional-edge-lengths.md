# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 6, Lesson 4

## Topic
Volume with Fractional Edge Lengths — Extending the Formula

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply V = l × w × h to find the volume of rectangular prisms with fractional or mixed-number edge lengths
- Connect fraction multiplication to volume calculation
- Explain why the formula still works when edge lengths are fractions
- Solve real-world volume problems involving fractional dimensions

## Prerequisites
- V = l × w × h and V = B × h (Lesson 3)
- Multiplying fractions: a/b × c/d = (a×c)/(b×d) (Unit 2)
- Multiplying mixed numbers (Unit 2)
- Area of rectangles with fractional side lengths (5.NF.4b)

## Materials Needed
- Grid paper (for drawing fractional prisms)
- Fraction strips or fraction tiles (optional, for concrete support)
- Colored pencils
- Student notebooks
- Whiteboard

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Revisit fraction multiplication as a direct prerequisite; connect area with fractional sides to volume.

Write on the board: "Find the area of a rectangle with length 3/4 cm and width 2/3 cm."

Give students 1 minute. Expected: 3/4 × 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2 cm².

Then ask: "If we stack this rectangle 4 layers tall, what would the volume be?"
Expected: 1/2 × 4 = 2 cm³.

Say: "Today we extend this — what if all three dimensions are fractions?"

---

## Direct Instruction (13 minutes)
**Purpose:** Show how V = l × w × h extends naturally to fractional dimensions; build conceptual grounding with diagrams.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fractional edge length:** a side length that is a fraction or mixed number rather than a whole number
- All vocabulary from Lesson 3 applies

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Connect to area with fractions**
On grid paper, draw a 2 × 3 rectangle. Say: "Area = 6 square units." Now subdivide each unit into halves in both directions (create a 4 × 6 grid of half-unit squares). Ask: "What is the area of each small square?" (1/4 square unit) "How many small squares?" (24) "Total area?" (24 × 1/4 = 6 — same answer.)

This shows that fraction dimensions don't break our formula; they just create smaller pieces.

**Step 2 — Apply to 3D**
Present a box with dimensions 1/2 ft × 1/2 ft × 1/2 ft (half-foot cube). Ask: "How many of these half-cubes fit in a 1-foot cube?"
- Along each edge: 2 half-units fit
- Total: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 half-cubes fill one cubic foot
- Each half-cube has volume = 1/8 cubic foot

Verify with formula: V = 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8 ft³ ✓

**Step 3 — Mixed number dimensions**
For mixed numbers, convert to improper fractions first, then apply the formula.
- Example: 2 1/2 ft = 5/2 ft

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Find the volume of a prism: l = 2/3 cm, w = 3/4 cm, h = 1/2 cm.
- V = 2/3 × 3/4 × 1/2
- First: 2/3 × 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2
- Then: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 cm³

**Example 2:** A small box measures 1 1/2 in × 2 in × 3/4 in. Find its volume.
- Convert: 1 1/2 = 3/2
- V = 3/2 × 2 × 3/4
- 3/2 × 2 = 3; 3 × 3/4 = 9/4 = 2 1/4 in³

**Example 3:** A tile is 3/5 m long, 1/2 m wide, and 1/4 m thick. What is its volume?
- V = 3/5 × 1/2 × 1/4
- 3/5 × 1/2 = 3/10; 3/10 × 1/4 = 3/40 m³

**Example 4 (real-world):** A cabinet shelf is 2 1/4 ft long, 1 ft wide, and 3/4 ft tall. What volume of space does it take up?
- 2 1/4 = 9/4
- V = 9/4 × 1 × 3/4 = 27/16 = 1 11/16 ft³

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work in pairs; discuss approach before calculating.

**Problem 1:** V = l × w × h; l = 1/2, w = 1/2, h = 1/2 (all same units). Find V.
- 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8 cubic unit

**Problem 2:** A box is 3/4 ft long, 2 ft wide, and 1/2 ft tall. Find the volume.
- 3/4 × 2 × 1/2 = 3/4 cubic foot

**Problem 3:** A prism has l = 1 1/2 m, w = 2 m, h = 1/3 m.
- 1 1/2 = 3/2; V = 3/2 × 2 × 1/3 = 6/6 × (1/3 × 2) — let's compute step by step: 3/2 × 2 = 3; 3 × 1/3 = 1 m³

**Problem 4:** A block of cheese is 3/4 cm thick, 2 1/2 cm wide, and 4 cm long.
- 2 1/2 = 5/2; V = 3/4 × 5/2 × 4
- 3/4 × 5/2 = 15/8; 15/8 × 4 = 60/8 = 7 1/2 cm³

---

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Find the volume: l = 1/2 cm, w = 3 cm, h = 4 cm.
   - 1/2 × 3 × 4 = 6 cm³

2. A book is 1/4 ft thick, 1 ft wide, and 1 ft tall. What is its volume?
   - 1/4 ft³

3. Find the volume: l = 2/3 m, w = 3/4 m, h = 2 m.
   - 2/3 × 3/4 = 1/2; 1/2 × 2 = 1 m³

4. A prism has dimensions 1 1/2 cm × 2 cm × 3 cm. What is its volume?
   - 3/2 × 2 × 3 = 9 cm³

**Stretch:**
5. A tray of chocolate is 1/2 in thick, 3 1/2 in wide, and 6 in long. Find the volume.
   - 1/2 × 7/2 × 6 = 1/2 × 7/2 = 7/4; 7/4 × 6 = 42/4 = 10 1/2 in³

6. A small storage unit is 2 1/4 ft × 1 1/2 ft × 3/4 ft. Find the volume.
   - 9/4 × 3/2 × 3/4 = 27/8 × 3/4 = 81/32 = 2 17/32 ft³

7. Two prisms: Prism A has dimensions 1/2 × 1/2 × 8. Prism B has dimensions 1/4 × 1/4 × 8. Which has greater volume? How many times greater?
   - A: 1/2 × 1/2 × 8 = 2; B: 1/4 × 1/4 × 8 = 1/2; A is 4 times greater (2 ÷ 1/2 = 4)

**Challenge:**
8. A swimming pool is being filled with water. The pool is 15 1/2 m long, 8 m wide, and 1 3/4 m deep. What is the volume of water needed to fill it completely?
   - 15 1/2 = 31/2; 1 3/4 = 7/4
   - V = 31/2 × 8 × 7/4
   - 31/2 × 8 = 248/2 = 124; 124 × 7/4 = 868/4 = 217 m³

### Interleaved Review
R1. Multiply: 2 1/3 × 1 1/2. *(Unit 2 — mixed number multiplication)*
   - 7/3 × 3/2 = 21/6 = 3 1/2

R2. What is 5.25 + 3.8? *(Unit 4 — decimal addition)*
   - 9.05

R3. A box has 4 layers of cubes; each layer has 15 cubes. What is the total volume? *(Lesson 2 review)*
   - 60 cubic units

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Find the volume: l = 3/4 cm, w = 2 cm, h = 1/2 cm.
2. A prism has dimensions 1 1/2 ft × 2 ft × 1/3 ft. Find its volume.
3. Why can we still use V = l × w × h when the dimensions are fractions?

**Mastery = Correctly computes both volumes and provides a reasonable explanation (e.g., "because fraction multiplication still works the same way").**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Go back to the warm-up: confirm student can find the area of a rectangle with fractional sides
- Have student write the multiplication as a fraction multiplication problem: V = (3/4) × (2/1) × (1/2) = ?
- Use fraction tiles to model the dimensions if available

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask: "What if one of the dimensions was a decimal, like 1.5 cm? Could you still find the volume? How?"

---

## Extension (Optional)
**Fractional Prism Puzzle:** A rectangular box with fractional dimensions has a volume of 3/8 cubic feet. One dimension is 1/2 ft and another is 3/4 ft. What is the third dimension? Show your work.
- 3/8 = 1/2 × 3/4 × h → 3/8 = 3/8 × h → h = 1 ft

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The key prerequisite here is fraction multiplication from Units 1-2. If students are shaky on multiplying fractions, spend extra time on a brief review before proceeding.
- The biggest conceptual hurdle is convincing students the formula still works — the diagram approach (subdividing grid units) in Step 1 of Direct Instruction is worth doing visually.
- For mixed numbers, always convert to improper fractions first. Students who try to multiply mixed numbers directly (e.g., 2 × 1 = 2, 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, so 1 1/2 × 1 1/2 = 2 1/4) will get wrong answers.
- The Challenge problem (swimming pool) is computationally intensive. Allow students to use scratch paper freely.
- Students should recognize that the units work the same way: cm × cm × cm = cm³.
