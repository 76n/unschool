# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 6, Lesson 1

## Topic
Understanding Volume — Filling Three-Dimensional Space with Unit Cubes

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define volume as the number of unit cubes needed to fill a solid figure without gaps or overlaps
- Identify a unit cube and explain that it has a volume of 1 cubic unit
- Distinguish between area (2D) and volume (3D)
- Estimate and verify volume by filling rectangular boxes with unit cubes

## Prerequisites
- Area of rectangles (l × w) from Grade 4
- Multiplication fluency through 100
- Understanding of 2D vs. 3D shapes

## Materials Needed
- Unit cubes (at least 40 per student or pair)
- Small rectangular boxes (e.g., small tissue boxes, jewelry boxes, crayon boxes — one per pair)
- Rulers
- Student notebooks or recording sheets
- Whiteboard and markers

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of area to build the bridge to volume.

Display two shapes on the board:
- A 3 × 4 rectangle labeled in squares
- A rectangular box (drawn in 3D)

Ask: "What do you know about each shape? What could you measure about each one?"

Take 2–3 responses. Listen for the word "area" for the rectangle. If students mention "how big the inside is" or "how much it holds" for the box, affirm and say: "Today we're going to give that idea a name — volume."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Establish the meaning of volume and the unit cube as the standard measurement unit.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Volume:** the amount of space a solid figure takes up; measured by counting unit cubes
- **Unit cube:** a cube with side length 1 unit on every edge; has a volume of exactly 1 cubic unit
- **Cubic unit:** the unit for measuring volume (written as cm³, in³, ft³, or "cubic units")
- **Rectangular prism:** a 3D shape with 6 rectangular faces — the main shape we measure today

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — What is volume?**
Hold up a single unit cube. Say: "This is a unit cube. Every edge is 1 unit long. It takes up exactly 1 cubic unit of space."

Place two cubes side by side. Ask: "How much space do these take up together?" (2 cubic units)
Add a third. (3 cubic units.) Emphasize: counting cubes = measuring volume.

**Step 2 — Area vs. Volume**
Draw a 2 × 3 rectangle on the board. Ask: "What is the area?" (6 square units.) Say: "Area measures flat, 2D space in square units."

Now build a 2 × 3 × 1 layer of unit cubes on the desk. Say: "This takes up 6 cubic units of space — the same count, but now we're in 3D."

Add a second layer (2 × 3 × 2). Ask: "How many cubic units now?" (12.) Emphasize: volume measures 3D space in cubic units.

**Step 3 — No gaps, no overlaps**
Build a prism that has a "gap" in the middle (remove one cube). Ask: "Is this a fair measurement?" Discuss — for volume to be accurate, we must fill the entire space.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** A box is filled with unit cubes. The arrangement shows 2 rows of 3 cubes. What is the volume?
- Count all cubes: 2 × 3 = 6 cubes → Volume = 6 cubic units

**Example 2:** A student fills a box with unit cubes and counts 10 cubes. What is the volume?
- Volume = 10 cubic units (each cube is 1 cubic unit)

**Example 3:** Look at two boxes:
- Box A: 8 unit cubes packed inside
- Box B: 12 unit cubes packed inside
Which box has more volume? By how much?
- Box B; 12 − 8 = 4 more cubic units

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these problems together, allowing student responses at each step.

**Problem 1:** Build a row of 4 unit cubes. Then place another identical row next to it. How many cubic units total?
- Answer: 8 cubic units

**Problem 2:** A small box holds exactly 3 unit cubes in a row, 2 rows across. How many cubic units of space does the box hold?
- Students build: 3 × 2 = 6 cubic units

**Problem 3:** Two unit cubes are glued together on a table. Three more unit cubes are stacked on top of them (in an L-shape). How many cubic units in all?
- Count: 2 + 3 = 5 cubic units

Ask: "Could we always just count? What if we had 1,000 cubes?" Let this question linger — it sets up Lessons 2 and 3.

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. A rectangular box is filled with unit cubes as shown (draw a 2 × 2 × 2 arrangement). How many cubic units is the volume?
   - 8 cubic units

2. A student fills a crayon box with unit cubes and counts 15. What is the volume of the crayon box?
   - 15 cubic units

3. Box A holds 9 unit cubes. Box B holds 14 unit cubes. Which has more volume? How much more?
   - Box B; 5 more cubic units

4. Circle the correct unit: Area is measured in ___. Volume is measured in ___.
   - Area: square units. Volume: cubic units.

**Stretch:**
5. A rectangular box is 3 units long, 2 units wide, and 1 unit tall. How many unit cubes fill it? Draw your arrangement.
   - 6 cubic units

6. If you add one more layer (1 unit tall) to the box in problem 5, what is the new volume?
   - 12 cubic units

7. Jalen says: "A box with a volume of 12 cubic units must be 3 × 4 × 1." Is Jalen correct? Can you think of another arrangement with a volume of 12 cubic units?
   - Jalen is not necessarily wrong, but other arrangements work too: 2 × 2 × 3, 2 × 6 × 1, 1 × 12 × 1, etc.

**Challenge:**
8. You have 24 unit cubes. How many different rectangular prisms can you build with all 24 cubes? List the dimensions of each.
   - Possible: 1×1×24, 1×2×12, 1×3×8, 1×4×6, 2×2×6, 2×3×4 (and their rotations)

### Interleaved Review
R1. Find the area of a rectangle with length 8 cm and width 5 cm. *(Unit 4 — multiplication of whole numbers)*
   - 40 cm²

R2. Multiply: 3/4 × 2/5. *(Unit 2 — fraction multiplication)*
   - 6/20 = 3/10

R3. Write the value of the 4 in 847,293. *(Unit 5 — place value)*
   - 40,000

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
1. What does "volume" mean in your own words?
2. A box is completely filled with 18 unit cubes. What is the volume of the box?
3. Name the unit for measuring volume (choose: square units or cubic units).

**Mastery = Student correctly answers all 3 items.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Re-examine whether the student understands "cubic unit" — return to physically handling unit cubes
- Use three physical cubes and count slowly together
- Check whether student is confusing area and volume; draw side-by-side comparison diagrams

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask: "Do you think there's a faster way to find volume without counting every single cube?"
- Encourage students to predict how counting layers might work (previewing Lesson 2)

## Extension (Optional)
**Volume Explorer:** Find three rectangular objects in your home (a book, a cereal box, a shoebox). Estimate how many unit cubes you would need to fill each one. Record your estimates and explain your reasoning. You do not need to actually fill them — use what you know about size to estimate.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson is primarily concrete. Do NOT introduce the formula V = l × w × h today. Students need time to build the concept.
- If unit cubes are scarce, pairs work well; students can take turns building.
- When distinguishing area from volume, hold up a piece of paper (area — flat, 2D) and a box (volume — 3D, has depth). Physical objects are powerful here.
- The question at the end of Guided Practice ("What if we had 1,000 cubes?") is intentionally unanswered — let it motivate Lesson 2's layer-counting strategy.
- Expect some students to initially confuse "cubic units" with "square units." Correct gently and often.
