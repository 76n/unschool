# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 6, Lesson 3

## Topic
Volume Formula for Rectangular Prisms — V = l × w × h and V = B × h

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply the formula V = l × w × h to find the volume of rectangular prisms with whole-number dimensions
- Apply the formula V = B × h (Volume = Area of Base × height) to find volume
- Explain why the formula works using the layer strategy from Lesson 2
- Solve real-world volume problems involving packing boxes and filling containers

## Prerequisites
- Volume as number of unit cubes (Lesson 1)
- Layer-counting strategy: cubes per layer × number of layers (Lesson 2)
- Area of rectangles: A = l × w
- Multi-digit multiplication

## Materials Needed
- Unit cubes (for verification)
- Student notebooks
- Whiteboard
- Rulers (optional, for measuring objects)
- Small rectangular boxes to measure (optional, for application problems)

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Bridge layer counting directly to multiplication, making the formula feel like a natural shortcut.

Write on the board:

"A prism is 6 units long, 4 units wide, and 3 units tall."

Ask: "Using the layer strategy, how would you find the volume?"
Expected response: "One layer = 6 × 4 = 24. Three layers = 24 × 3 = 72 cubic units."

Write on the board: 6 × 4 × 3 = 72.

Ask: "What did we really do?" (Multiply all three dimensions together.)

Say: "Today we're going to formalize that into a formula."

---

## Direct Instruction (13 minutes)
**Purpose:** Formalize V = l × w × h and V = B × h from the layer strategy; apply to real-world contexts.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Formula:** a rule written with symbols showing how to calculate a quantity
- **l (length):** one dimension of the base of a prism
- **w (width):** the second dimension of the base
- **h (height):** how tall the prism is; the number of layers
- **B (area of base):** the area of the bottom face of the prism; B = l × w

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — From layers to formula**
Write the layer strategy visibly:
> Volume = (cubes in one layer) × (number of layers)
> Volume = (l × w) × h
> Volume = l × w × h

Say: "The formula V = l × w × h is just the layer strategy written with letters. Length times width gives us the area of one layer (the base). Multiplying by height tells us how many layers."

**Step 2 — Introduce B × h**
Write: V = B × h, where B = area of the base.

Say: "We can also write volume as B times h. B stands for the area of the base — which for a rectangular prism is l × w. So B × h = (l × w) × h = l × w × h. Both formulas are the same."

Show: For the prism above: B = 6 × 4 = 24; V = 24 × 3 = 72. Same answer.

**Step 3 — Units reminder**
Emphasize: if dimensions are in cm, volume is in cm³. If dimensions are in in, volume is in in³.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Find the volume of a box that is 5 cm long, 3 cm wide, and 4 cm tall.
- V = l × w × h = 5 × 3 × 4 = 60 cm³
- Check with B × h: B = 5 × 3 = 15; V = 15 × 4 = 60 cm³ ✓

**Example 2:** A rectangular fish tank is 12 in long, 6 in wide, and 8 in tall. What is the volume?
- V = 12 × 6 × 8
- 12 × 6 = 72; 72 × 8 = 576 in³

**Example 3:** A moving box has a volume of 120 cubic feet. Its base is 4 ft × 5 ft. What is the height?
- B = 4 × 5 = 20 ft²
- V = B × h → 120 = 20 × h → h = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 ft

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Students work with a partner; call on pairs to share.

**Problem 1:** A sandbox is 8 ft long, 5 ft wide, and 2 ft deep. Find the volume.
- V = 8 × 5 × 2 = 80 ft³

**Problem 2:** A gift box measures 9 in × 4 in × 3 in. Find the volume.
- V = 9 × 4 × 3 = 108 in³

**Problem 3:** A prism has a base area of 35 cm² and a height of 6 cm. Find the volume.
- V = B × h = 35 × 6 = 210 cm³

**Problem 4:** A box has a volume of 90 cm³ and a height of 5 cm. What is the area of its base?
- B = 90 ÷ 5 = 18 cm²

**Discussion:** "When we used the layer strategy in Lesson 2, we were finding B (area of base) first, then multiplying by h. Was anyone already using the formula without knowing it?"

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Find the volume of a rectangular prism with l = 7 cm, w = 3 cm, h = 4 cm.
   - 84 cm³

2. A wooden block is 10 in long, 2 in wide, and 2 in tall. What is its volume?
   - 40 in³

3. A prism has a base area of 48 ft² and a height of 3 ft. Find the volume.
   - 144 ft³

4. A box has a volume of 60 in³. Its base is 5 in × 4 in. What is its height?
   - 3 in

**Stretch:**
5. A moving company makes boxes in three sizes:
   - Small: 2 ft × 2 ft × 2 ft
   - Medium: 3 ft × 2 ft × 2 ft
   - Large: 4 ft × 3 ft × 2 ft
   Find the volume of each box. Which box has the greatest volume?
   - Small: 8 ft³; Medium: 12 ft³; Large: 24 ft³; Large has the greatest.

6. A swimming pool is 25 m long, 10 m wide, and 2 m deep. What is the volume of water it can hold?
   - 500 m³

7. Two prisms both have a volume of 48 cm³. Prism A is 4 cm tall and has a square base. Prism B is 6 cm tall and has a square base. What are the side lengths of each base?
   - Prism A: B = 48 ÷ 4 = 12 cm²; √12 is not a whole number — no whole-number square base. This is a stretch; accept students who find non-square rectangles: e.g., 3×4 base.
   - Prism B: B = 48 ÷ 6 = 8 cm²; base could be 2 × 4

**Challenge:**
8. A company needs to ship 180 cubic inches of product in a box. The height of the box must be 5 in. The base must be a rectangle where the length is twice the width. Find the length and width of the base.
   - V = B × h → 180 = B × 5 → B = 36 in²
   - l = 2w; l × w = 36; 2w × w = 36; 2w² = 36; w² = 18; w = √18 ≈ 4.24 — not a whole number.
   - Accept students who set up the equation correctly; note this leads to an irrational answer; adjust: if the base area must be 36 in² with l = 2w, closest whole number is w = 4, l = 9 (gives B = 36 but l ≠ 2w exactly). Teacher note: consider changing the constraint to l + w = 9 or using 200 in³ with h = 4, B = 50, l = 2w, w² × 2 = 50 — also non-integer. For the challenge, the expected outcome is the student setting up and solving the equation, not necessarily getting a clean answer.
   - Alternative clean version: V = 144 in³, h = 4, l = 2w → B = 36, 2w² = 36, w = √18 — still irrational. Use: V = 128, h = 8, B = 16, l = 2w: 2w² = 16, w² = 8 — still irrational. Best clean version: V = 150, h = 6, B = 25, l = 2w: 2w² = 25 — not integer. Use V = 54, h = 6, B = 9: l = 2w, 2w² = 9 — not integer. Teacher note: use V = 72, h = 2, B = 36, l = 2w → w = √18 ≈ 4.2. The challenge value of this problem is the algebraic reasoning, not the clean answer; assess setup and process.

### Interleaved Review
R1. Find the product: 3/5 × 10/9. *(Unit 2 — fraction multiplication)*
   - 30/45 = 2/3

R2. A store sells oranges for $0.75 each. How much do 8 oranges cost? *(Unit 4 — decimal multiplication)*
   - $6.00

R3. What is 10⁴? How many zeros? *(Unit 5 — powers of 10)*
   - 10,000; 4 zeros

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
1. Write both volume formulas for a rectangular prism.
2. Find the volume: l = 6 cm, w = 5 cm, h = 3 cm.
3. A box has a volume of 80 ft³ and a height of 4 ft. What is the area of the base?

**Mastery = Correctly answers all 3 items.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to layer strategy: build or draw the prism, count one layer's cubes, then multiply by height
- Confirm student can calculate l × w first as area of base before multiplying by h
- Check whether difficulty is formula recall vs. computation errors

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask: "What do you think would happen if one of the dimensions was a fraction, like 1/2 a foot? Could we still use the formula?" (Previewing Lesson 4)

## Extension (Optional)
**Box Designer Challenge:** A company wants a box with a volume of exactly 120 cm³. Design 4 different rectangular boxes with this volume. For each box, give the dimensions (l, w, h) and verify V = 120 cm³. Are any of your boxes the same shape? Explain.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The formula derivation is the key outcome of this lesson. Students should be able to say WHY the formula works, not just use it.
- Write the three equivalent forms side by side: "l × w × h" and "B × h" and "(layer area) × h." Students should see these as the same thing.
- Problem 4 in Guided Practice introduces working backwards — a critical skill for real-world applications and the unit assessment.
- The Challenge problem in Independent Practice is intentionally open-ended and algebraically complex. Use it to stretch strong students; do not require it of all students.
- Real-world context is important: boxes, tanks, sandboxes. Make explicit connections to things students can visualize.
