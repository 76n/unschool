# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 6, Lesson 9

## Topic
Real-World Coordinate Graphing — Maps, Data Display, and Movement Patterns

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Interpret coordinate values in real-world contexts (maps, data tables, movement)
- Graph and read data points in a real-world scenario
- Answer questions about real-world situations using information from a coordinate graph
- Create a coordinate-based map and locate places using ordered pairs

## Prerequisites
- Plotting and reading ordered pairs (Lessons 6–7)
- Graphing patterns from tables (Lesson 8)

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper (2 sheets per student)
- Rulers
- Colored pencils
- "Town map" activity template (teacher draws a labeled 10 × 10 grid on the board or on a handout)
- Whiteboard

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect coordinate reading to everyday navigation experience.

Show (or describe) a simple city map with a grid overlay. Label the axes: x-axis = blocks east, y-axis = blocks north. Ask:

"If the library is at (3, 7) and the school is at (3, 2), how far apart are they?"
- Same x-coordinate (3), so they're on the same vertical line. Distance = 7 − 2 = 5 blocks.

"If the park is at (1, 4) and the gym is at (6, 4), how far apart are they?"
- Same y-coordinate (4), so they're on the same horizontal line. Distance = 6 − 1 = 5 blocks.

Say: "Maps have been using coordinate systems for centuries. Today we use coordinate math to read maps, understand data, and track movement."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model three real-world uses of coordinate graphing: maps, data plots, and tracking movement.

### Key Vocabulary
(All vocabulary from Lessons 6–7; apply to real contexts)
- **Scale:** the relationship between units on a graph and real-world measurements (e.g., 1 unit = 1 mile)
- **Data plot / scatter plot (informal):** a graph where each real-world measurement is shown as a point

### Instruction Notes

**Context 1 — Maps**
Draw a town map on the board (10 × 10 grid). Label:
- Post Office: (2, 8)
- Fire Station: (5, 6)
- School: (8, 4)
- Library: (4, 4)
- Park: (6, 9)
- Home: (1, 1)

Say: "Imagine each unit is 1 block. If I'm at the Fire Station (5, 6) and I want to go to the Library (4, 4), how many blocks east or west do I go? How many north or south?"
- East/West: 5 → 4 = 1 block west
- North/South: 6 → 4 = 2 blocks south

"How many total blocks do I walk if I only move along the grid (no diagonals)?" (1 + 2 = 3 blocks)

**Context 2 — Data display**
Say: "Coordinate graphs can show data from the real world — not just math rules."

Present: "Mrs. Chen measured 5 students' heights and ages:"

| Age (years) | Height (inches) |
|-------------|-----------------|
| 8 | 50 |
| 9 | 52 |
| 10 | 54 |
| 11 | 57 |
| 12 | 60 |

Plot with x = age, y = height. Say: "Each person is one dot. The dots don't have to form a perfect line — real data is messy. But we can see the trend: as age increases, height tends to increase."

**Context 3 — Movement / paths**
Describe a robot on a grid: it starts at (0, 0). Give movement instructions:
- Move right 4 → now at (4, 0)
- Move up 3 → now at (4, 3)
- Move right 2 → now at (6, 3)
- Move up 5 → now at (6, 8)

Plot each location and connect the path with arrows. Students can "follow" a robot's path by tracking coordinates.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 (Map):** On the town map described above, which location is closest to (6, 6)?
- Park is at (6, 9): distance = 3 units north
- Fire Station is at (5, 6): distance = 1 unit west
- Closest is Fire Station — only 1 block away.

**Example 2 (Data):** According to the height/age data graph, approximately how tall would you expect a 10.5-year-old to be?
- Between the points for age 10 (54 in) and age 11 (57 in). Estimate: ~55–56 inches.

**Example 3 (Path):** A treasure map says: "Start at (0, 0). Move 5 steps east and 3 steps north. Then move 2 steps east and 4 steps north." Where is the treasure?
- (0,0) → (5,3) → (7,7). Treasure at (7, 7).

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Use the town map on the board for parts of this.

**Problem 1 — Map navigation:**
Using the town map from Direct Instruction, answer:
- a. What building is at (8, 4)? (School)
- b. What are the coordinates of the Park? (6, 9)
- c. Starting from Home (1, 1), list the path (as coordinates) to go to the Library (4, 4) by moving right first, then up.
  - (1,1) → (4,1) → (4,4)
- d. How many total blocks is that path?
  - 3 blocks right + 3 blocks up = 6 blocks

**Problem 2 — Data reading:**
A student recorded how many books she read each month for 5 months:

| Month | Books read |
|-------|-----------|
| 1 | 3 |
| 2 | 5 |
| 3 | 4 |
| 4 | 7 |
| 5 | 6 |

Plot these points (x = month, y = books). Which month had the most books? (Month 4: 7 books) Which had the fewest? (Month 1: 3 books)

**Problem 3 — Path tracking:**
A delivery robot starts at (2, 1). It moves:
- 3 units up
- 5 units right
- 2 units up
Where does it end up? What is its final location?
- (2,1) → (2,4) → (7,4) → (7,6). Final: (7, 6).

## Independent Practice (13 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
Use graph paper for all problems.

1. On a coordinate grid (each unit = 1 mile), plot these city landmarks:
   - Hospital: (2, 7)
   - Mall: (6, 5)
   - Airport: (9, 2)
   - Library: (1, 4)
   - Stadium: (7, 8)

   a. Which landmark is furthest north (highest y-value)? (Stadium at y=8)
   b. Which is furthest east (highest x-value)? (Airport at x=9)
   c. How many miles directly north is the Hospital from the Library? (7 − 4 = 3 miles; same x=1? No — library is (1,4) and hospital is (2,7), so they're not in line. Adjust: ask "Which two landmarks share the same x-coordinate?" — none share exact x. Change: ask "If you drive from the Mall to the Stadium and only travel north (same x), how far do you go?" Mall (6,5), Stadium (7,8) — different x. Adjust problem: "The Hospital is at (2, 7) and the Library is at (2, 4). How far apart are they?" Library moved to (2,4) for this problem.)
   - Answer to adjusted c: 7 − 4 = 3 miles north

2. A student tracked how many hours she practiced piano each week for 6 weeks:

   | Week | Hours |
   |------|-------|
   | 1 | 2 |
   | 2 | 3 |
   | 3 | 3 |
   | 4 | 5 |
   | 5 | 4 |
   | 6 | 6 |

   Plot these points on a coordinate grid (x = week, y = hours). Is the trend going up or down overall?
   - Generally going up (she practices more over time)

3. A mouse starts at (1, 1) on a grid. It moves right 4 spaces, then up 6 spaces. Where does the mouse end up?
   - (1,1) → (5,1) → (5,7). Final: (5, 7)

**Stretch:**
4. On a treasure hunt, the clues are:
   - Start at the big oak tree at (0, 0)
   - Go east 7 steps and north 2 steps to find a red ribbon
   - From the ribbon, go west 3 steps and north 5 steps to find a rock
   - From the rock, go east 4 steps and south 1 step to find the treasure

   Track each position. Where is the treasure?
   - Start: (0,0) → Red ribbon: (7, 2) → Rock: (4, 7) → Treasure: (8, 6)

5. Using the data from problem 2 (piano practice), between which two weeks did hours increase the most?
   - Week 3 to Week 4: went from 3 to 5 hours (increase of 2)

6. On a coordinate map, two friends agree to meet at the midpoint between their homes. Rosa lives at (2, 6); Marcus lives at (8, 2). What is the midpoint?
   - Midpoint: ((2+8)/2, (6+2)/2) = (5, 4). Note: formal midpoint formula is Grade 6 — guide students to find this by averaging or "meet halfway" reasoning.

**Challenge:**
7. A hiking trail is plotted on a coordinate map. Checkpoints are at: (0, 0), (2, 4), (5, 6), (8, 6), (10, 3).
   - a. How many units right does the trail go from start to finish? (10 units)
   - b. At which checkpoint is the hiker furthest north? ((5,6) or (8,6) — both at y=6)
   - c. Between which two consecutive checkpoints does the trail go south (y decreases)?
     - From (8,6) to (10,3): y decreases from 6 to 3

### Interleaved Review
R1. What is the volume of a box with a base area of 36 ft² and a height of 5 ft? *(Lesson 3 — V = B × h)*
   - 180 ft³

R2. Kai has 2 3/4 cups of flour. He uses 1 1/2 cups. How much is left? *(Unit 1 — mixed number subtraction)*
   - 2 3/4 − 1 1/2 = 2 3/4 − 1 2/4 = 1 1/4 cups

R3. Write 3.07 × 10² in standard form. *(Unit 5 — powers of 10 with decimals)*
   - 307

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
Use this grid (first quadrant, 0–10):
- The school is at (4, 8).
- The park is at (4, 3).

1. Plot and label both locations.
2. How many blocks apart are the school and the park? (Only travel north or south — same x-coordinate.)
3. A new library will be built halfway between the school and the park. What are its coordinates?

**Mastery = Both points correctly plotted; distance = 5 blocks; library at (4, 5.5) or (4, 5 and a half) or equivalent.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Practice reading and plotting on the map; ask student to verbally describe what x and y represent in the context
- Connect "distance on the same vertical line" back to the number line (just subtraction of y-values)

### If Mastery Is Met
- Ask: "How would you find the distance between two points that are NOT on the same row or column — like (2, 3) and (5, 7)?"

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Real-world contexts make coordinate graphing accessible and motivating. Treasure maps and town navigation are especially engaging for 10–11 year olds.
- When interpreting distance, this lesson uses "grid distance" (horizontal + vertical travel), not the Pythagorean distance formula — that is a Grade 8 standard.
- The data plotting activity introduces the idea that coordinates don't always follow a neat rule — real data is messier than y = 2x. This is an important conceptual point.
- The "movement path" problems require students to track changing coordinates step by step — this builds procedural fluency with ordered pairs.
- Stretch problem 6 (midpoint) previews Grade 6 work. The informal "meet halfway" reasoning is appropriate here; the formal formula is not expected.
- Make sure students understand that axes can represent different quantities (blocks, miles, hours, weeks) depending on the context. The context determines what each coordinate means.
