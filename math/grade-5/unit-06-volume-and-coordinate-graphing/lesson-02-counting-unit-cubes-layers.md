# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 6, Lesson 2

## Topic
Counting Unit Cubes to Find Volume — Building and Counting Layers

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Count unit cubes organized in layers to find the volume of a rectangular prism
- Use skip-counting and repeated addition to efficiently count cubes by layers
- Express volume in cubic units (cubic cm, cubic in, cubic ft)
- Measure volume by counting unit cubes from a drawing or physical arrangement

## Prerequisites
- Volume as the number of unit cubes that fill a solid figure (Lesson 1)
- Skip-counting and repeated addition
- Multiplication facts through 10 × 10

## Materials Needed
- Unit cubes (40–60 per student or pair)
- Isometric dot paper or plain paper for drawing prisms (optional)
- Student notebooks
- Whiteboard

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Reinforce the concept of volume as cubic unit count and activate skip-counting skills.

Place 3 unit cubes in a row on a surface. Ask a student to count them: "3 cubic units."
Make 4 identical rows of 3. Ask: "How many cubic units in this layer? How did you count them?"
Expect: "I skip-counted by 3s: 3, 6, 9, 12." or "3 × 4 = 12."

Say: "Today we're going to use that same idea — but for 3D shapes with multiple layers stacked on top."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)
**Purpose:** Develop the layer-counting strategy as an organized, efficient way to count unit cubes in a rectangular prism.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Layer:** one flat level of unit cubes in a rectangular prism; like one floor of a building
- **Cubic centimeter (cm³):** volume of a cube with 1 cm edges
- **Cubic inch (in³):** volume of a cube with 1 in edges
- **Cubic foot (ft³):** volume of a cube with 1 ft edges

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Build a 3-layer prism**
Build a 3 × 4 × 1 layer of unit cubes. Ask: "How many cubes in this one layer?" (12)
Add a second layer on top. Ask: "How many cubes total now?" (24: 12 + 12 or 2 × 12)
Add a third layer. Ask: "How many now?" (36: 12 + 12 + 12 or 3 × 12)

Say: "I counted the first layer, then I knew every layer above had the same number. So I just added the same number of cubes for each layer."

**Step 2 — Connect to multiplication**
Write on the board:
- Layer 1: 12 cubes
- Layer 2: 12 cubes
- Layer 3: 12 cubes
- Total: 12 + 12 + 12 = 3 × 12 = 36 cubic units

Say: "Number of cubes in ONE layer × number of layers = total volume."

**Step 3 — Measuring with standard units**
Hold up a centimeter cube. Say: "This is 1 cubic centimeter — written cm³. If every cube were 1 cm on each edge, we'd say this prism is 36 cm³."

Briefly show the notation cm³, in³, ft³. Students will encounter these in the problems today.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** A rectangular prism has a 2 × 5 base and is 3 layers tall.
- Cubes in one layer: 2 × 5 = 10
- Number of layers: 3
- Total volume: 10 × 3 = 30 cubic units

**Example 2:** A box is drawn with 4 rows, 3 columns, and 2 layers. What is the volume?
- One layer: 4 × 3 = 12 cubes
- Two layers: 12 × 2 = 24 cubic units

**Example 3:** A fish tank is 6 in long, 3 in wide, and 4 in tall. If unit cubes each measure 1 cubic inch, how many unit cubes fill the tank?
- One layer: 6 × 3 = 18 cubic inches
- 4 layers: 18 × 4 = 72 cubic inches = 72 in³

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through together, having students predict before confirming.

**Problem 1:** A prism has a 3 × 3 base and is 4 layers tall. Find the volume.
- One layer: 3 × 3 = 9
- 4 layers: 9 × 4 = 36 cubic units
- Confirm by asking: "Could we skip-count by 9 four times? 9, 18, 27, 36 — yes!"

**Problem 2:** Draw (or show) a prism that is 5 cubes long, 2 cubes wide, and 3 cubes tall.
- One layer: 5 × 2 = 10
- 3 layers: 10 × 3 = 30 cubic units

**Problem 3:** A layer has 8 cubes. If there are 6 identical layers, what is the volume?
- 8 × 6 = 48 cubic units

**Problem 4 (backwards):** A prism has a volume of 40 cubic units. Each layer has 8 cubes. How many layers are there?
- 40 ÷ 8 = 5 layers

---

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. A prism is 4 cubes long, 3 cubes wide, and 2 cubes tall.
   a. How many cubes are in one layer?
   b. What is the total volume?
   - a. 12; b. 24 cubic units

2. A brick measures 8 cm long, 4 cm wide, and 3 cm tall. What is the volume in cm³?
   - One layer: 8 × 4 = 32; 3 layers: 32 × 3 = 96 cm³

3. A prism has 5 layers. Each layer contains 6 unit cubes. What is the total volume?
   - 30 cubic units

4. Circle the correct unit for measuring volume: square inches / cubic inches / plain inches
   - cubic inches

**Stretch:**
5. A storage crate is 6 ft long, 4 ft wide, and 5 ft tall. How many cubic-foot-sized boxes fit inside?
   - One layer: 6 × 4 = 24; 5 layers: 24 × 5 = 120 ft³

6. Two prisms both have a volume of 24 cubic units. One is 4 layers tall; the other is 2 layers tall. How many cubes are in one layer of each prism?
   - 4-layer prism: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 cubes/layer; 2-layer prism: 24 ÷ 2 = 12 cubes/layer

7. A prism has a 3 × 7 base. Its volume is 63 cubic units. How many layers does it have?
   - One layer: 3 × 7 = 21; 63 ÷ 21 = 3 layers

**Challenge:**
8. A layer of cubes is 4 × 5. You want the prism to have a volume between 80 and 100 cubic units. What values could the height be? List all possibilities.
   - One layer = 20. 80 ÷ 20 = 4 (volume = 80); 100 ÷ 20 = 5 (volume = 100). Heights 4 and 5 give volumes of exactly 80 and 100. Any height strictly between 4 and 5 is not a whole number of layers, so only h = 4 (volume 80) and h = 5 (volume 100) work for whole-number layers.

### Interleaved Review
R1. A rectangle has a perimeter of 24 cm. Its length is 8 cm. What is its width? *(Grade 4 — perimeter)*
   - 2(8 + w) = 24; 8 + w = 12; w = 4 cm

R2. Calculate: 4.6 × 3. *(Unit 4 — decimal multiplication)*
   - 13.8

R3. What is 10³? *(Unit 5 — powers of 10)*
   - 1,000

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)

**Exit Ticket:**
1. A prism is 5 units long, 3 units wide, and 4 units tall. What is the volume? Show your work using the layer strategy.
2. A prism has 7 layers, each with 9 cubic units. What is the total volume?
3. What unit would you use to measure the volume of a shoebox if the dimensions are given in inches?

**Mastery = Correctly answers all 3 (full layer strategy shown in problem 1).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to physical building: have the student build a simple prism and literally count each layer
- Write out layer-by-layer addition before multiplying
- Check whether difficulty is conceptual (what a layer is) or computational (multiplication errors)

### If Mastery Is Met
- Pose: "I know that one layer of my prism has an area of l × w. What if I called that the 'area of the base'? How does that connect to volume?" (Previewing V = B × h in Lesson 3)

## Extension (Optional)
**Layer Investigation:** Using exactly 24 unit cubes, build three different rectangular prisms. For each:
- Record the dimensions (length × width × height)
- Identify how many cubes per layer and how many layers
- Confirm all three have a volume of 24 cubic units

Challenge question: Is it possible to build a rectangular prism with 24 cubes that has only 1 layer? What would it look like?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The layer strategy is the conceptual bridge between counting individual cubes (Lesson 1) and using the formula (Lesson 3). Do not skip it.
- Students who already know V = l × w × h may try to jump ahead. Encourage them to verify their formula answer with the layer method today.
- The standard unit notation (cm³, in³, ft³) is introduced here because students will encounter it on the unit assessment. Spend 2 minutes on notation; do not dwell.
- Problem 4 in Guided Practice introduces division as the inverse — an important move for later problem solving. If it feels premature, it can be skipped and revisited in Lesson 3.
