# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 6, Lesson 10

## Topic
Mixed Volume and Coordinate Practice — Integration and Problem Solving

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply volume formulas (V = l × w × h, V = B × h, additive volume) to solve multi-step real-world problems
- Plot, read, and interpret ordered pairs on a coordinate plane
- Connect volume and coordinate graphing concepts within integrated problem scenarios
- Demonstrate readiness for the unit assessment through review and self-assessment

## Prerequisites
- All prior lessons in this unit (Lessons 1–9)

## Materials Needed
- Graph paper
- Unit cubes (available for students who want to revisit concrete support)
- Rulers and colored pencils
- Whiteboard
- Index cards for self-assessment

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Rapid review of key formulas and procedures before the integration lesson.

Conduct a "Formula Sprint" — display each item for 30 seconds; students answer on mini-whiteboards or paper:

1. V = l × w × h — what do l, w, h stand for? (length, width, height)
2. V = B × h — what does B stand for? (area of the base)
3. What is the volume of a prism with l = 4, w = 3, h = 6? (72 cubic units)
4. In the ordered pair (5, 3), which coordinate is the x-value? (5)
5. Plot (2, 7) — go how far right? How far up? (right 2, up 7)

Quickly review any items where more than half the class hesitates.

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model multi-step, integrated problem solving that combines unit concepts.

### Instruction Notes

**No new vocabulary today.** Review any terms that surface during problem modeling.

**Problem Modeling — Step by step:**

Present this scenario on the board:

> "The Okafor family is designing a backyard with two garden beds and a shed. On a coordinate map (1 unit = 1 meter):
> - Garden Bed A is at coordinates with corners: (1, 1), (4, 1), (4, 5), (1, 5)
> - Garden Bed B is at coordinates with corners: (5, 2), (8, 2), (8, 6), (5, 6)
> - Each garden bed is 0.5 meters deep
> - The shed occupies 3 m × 2 m × 2.5 m
>
> Questions:
> a. Plot all four corners of each garden bed and label them.
> b. What is the perimeter of Garden Bed A?
> c. What is the volume of soil needed to fill Garden Bed A?
> d. What is the volume of soil needed to fill Garden Bed B?
> e. What is the total volume of soil for both beds?
> f. What is the volume of the shed?"

Model each step:
a. Plotting: (1,1),(4,1),(4,5),(1,5) — rectangle; (5,2),(8,2),(8,6),(5,6) — rectangle
b. Garden Bed A dimensions: length = 4-1 = 3 m; width = 5-1 = 4 m; perimeter = 2(3+4) = 14 m
c. V_A = 3 × 4 × 0.5 = 6 m³
d. Garden Bed B: length = 8-5 = 3 m; width = 6-2 = 4 m; V_B = 3 × 4 × 0.5 = 6 m³
e. Total = 6 + 6 = 12 m³
f. Shed: V = 3 × 2 × 2.5 = 15 m³

Say: "Notice how we used the coordinate graph to find dimensions (from the corner coordinates), then used volume formulas. Both skills work together."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these together, asking students to direct each step.

**Problem 1:** A swimming pool is shown on a coordinate map. Its corners are at (0, 0), (8, 0), (8, 5), (0, 5). Each unit represents 1 yard. The pool is 3 yards deep.
- a. What are the length and width of the pool? (8 yards × 5 yards)
- b. What is the volume of the pool in cubic yards? (8 × 5 × 3 = 120 yd³)

**Problem 2:** A garden has two sections shaped like rectangular prisms. Section 1: l = 6 ft, w = 4 ft, depth = 1/2 ft. Section 2: l = 3 ft, w = 4 ft, depth = 1/2 ft.
- Total volume: (6 × 4 × 1/2) + (3 × 4 × 1/2) = 12 + 6 = 18 ft³

**Problem 3:** A student plots these points and claims they form a straight-line pattern: (0, 0), (2, 4), (4, 8), (6, 12). Is the student correct?
- y = 2x for each point: ✓. Yes, they form a straight line; the rule is y = 2x.

**Problem 4 (discussion):** "Why is it helpful to use a coordinate graph when describing the location of a garden bed or building? What information does the graph give us?"
- The coordinates tell us the exact size and position without needing to carry a tape measure to every description. We can calculate distances and areas from the coordinates alone.

## Independent Practice (15 minutes)
This is a mixed review across the full unit, organized by difficulty.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Find the volume: l = 9 cm, w = 4 cm, h = 3 cm.
   - 108 cm³

2. A prism has two layers of 15 unit cubes each. What is the volume?
   - 30 cubic units

3. A composite shape: Prism A is 5 × 3 × 4; Prism B is 2 × 3 × 4. Find the total volume.
   - 60 + 24 = 84 cubic units

4. Plot and label: P = (3, 6), Q = (7, 2), R = (5, 9).

5. What is the ordered pair of a point that is 6 units right and 0 units up from the origin?
   - (6, 0)

6. Write the rule for this table: x: 0, 1, 2, 3 → y: 0, 3, 6, 9.
   - y = 3x

**Stretch:**
7. A fish tank is 1 1/2 ft long, 3/4 ft wide, and 1 ft tall. What volume of water can it hold?
   - 3/2 × 3/4 × 1 = 9/8 = 1 1/8 ft³

8. Two rectangular buildings are joined together (additive volume):
   - Building 1: 20 m × 12 m × 15 m
   - Building 2: 10 m × 12 m × 25 m
   Total volume?
   - 3,600 + 3,000 = 6,600 m³

9. On a coordinate map, a fence goes from (1, 2) to (7, 2) to (7, 8) to (1, 8) to (1, 2) (a rectangle). Each unit = 1 yard. What is the area enclosed by the fence?
   - Length = 7 − 1 = 6 yd; Width = 8 − 2 = 6 yd; Area = 36 yd²

10. The rule y = x + 3 is plotted. A point on this line has y = 10. What is x?
    - 10 = x + 3 → x = 7; point is (7, 10)

**Challenge:**
11. A warehouse has an unusual floor plan. The full building would be a 20 m × 15 m × 8 m rectangular prism, but a loading dock notch (4 m × 5 m × 8 m) has been cut from one corner. What is the volume of usable interior space?
    - Full: 20 × 15 × 8 = 2,400 m³
    - Notch: 4 × 5 × 8 = 160 m³
    - Usable: 2,400 − 160 = 2,240 m³

12. A coordinate mystery: "I am a rectangle. My four corners are at (a, 2), (a, 7), (6, 2), and (6, 7). My perimeter is 20 units. Find a."
    - Width = 7 − 2 = 5; let length = 6 − a; perimeter = 2(5 + (6-a)) = 20 → 2(5 + 6 − a) = 20 → 5 + 6 − a = 10 → 11 − a = 10 → a = 1. So a = 1.

### Interleaved Review
R1. Multiply: 2/3 × 3/4 × 1/2. *(Unit 2 — fraction multiplication chain)*
   - 2/3 × 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2; 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4

R2. What is 6.8 − 2.35? *(Unit 4 — decimal subtraction)*
   - 4.45

R3. Write 10⁵ as a whole number. *(Unit 5 — powers of 10)*
   - 100,000

Students rate themselves on each skill using 1–3:
1 = Not yet confident | 2 = Getting there | 3 = Got it!

- I can find the volume of a rectangular prism using V = l × w × h. ___
- I can find volume using layers (V = B × h). ___
- I can find volume with fractional edge lengths. ___
- I can find volume of composite shapes by adding or subtracting. ___
- I can plot an ordered pair in the first quadrant. ___
- I can read the ordered pair of a plotted point. ___
- I can graph a pattern from a table. ___
- I can use coordinates to solve a real-world problem. ___

**One more thing to write:** "The skill I feel LEAST confident about in this unit is ___."

Teacher collects cards. Review responses before the assessment to identify any students who need targeted support.

### If Mastery Is Not Met (any area rated 1)
- Hold a small-group review session on the skill(s) rated 1 before the unit assessment
- Offer optional review problems from earlier lessons
- Allow students to revisit their exit tickets from those lessons

### If Mastery Is Met (all areas rated 3)
- Assign the Extension activity or challenge problems from today's independent practice
- Encourage students to create their own volume or coordinate word problem for extra challenge

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- This lesson serves as a consolidation and review before the unit assessment. Prioritize seeing student misconceptions rather than rushing through new ideas.
- The self-assessment exit ticket is diagnostic data for you, not a grade. Review cards quickly after class to identify any students who need last-minute support.
- If class time is limited, the self-assessment is the non-negotiable element — even 3 minutes of honest self-reflection significantly improves student readiness for the assessment.
- Students who rate themselves 1 on any skill should be given a targeted mini-review problem to work through before or during the assessment day.
- The multi-step problem from Direct Instruction (Okafor backyard) models precisely the level of complexity on the unit assessment. If students can follow that problem, they are ready.
- Composite problems that combine both coordinate reading and volume calculation represent the highest rigor of this unit. Use these selectively for students who are ready.
