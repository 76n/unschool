# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 4, Lesson 1

## Topic
Decimal Place Value Review

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read, write, and represent decimals through thousandths in standard form, expanded form, and word form.
- Explain the relationship between adjacent place values (each place is 10 times the place to its right and 1/10 of the place to its left).
- Compare and order decimals through thousandths using place-value reasoning.
- Identify the value of a specific digit in a decimal number.

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of decimal notation for tenths and hundredths (Grade 4).
- Ability to compare decimals to hundredths (Grade 4).
- Understanding of fractions with denominators of 10 and 100.

## Materials Needed
- Base-ten blocks (flat = 1 whole, rod = 1 tenth, unit cube = 1 hundredth).
- Decimal grid paper (10x10 grids).
- Place-value chart (ones through thousandths).
- Whiteboard and markers.
- Index cards for exit ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.1, 5.NBT.3, 5.NBT.7

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of decimals from Grade 4.

1. Write on the board: 0.7, 0.70, 0.700
   Ask: "Are these three numbers equal? How do you know?"
2. Write on the board: 0.3 ___ 0.25. Ask: "Which symbol goes in the blank: <, >, or =? Explain your thinking."
3. Ask: "What fraction is the same as 0.4? What about 0.04?"

Say: "Today we are going to go deeper into decimal place value. You already know tenths and hundredths -- now we are adding thousandths and exploring why each place is connected to the ones around it."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Tenths:** The first place to the right of the decimal point (1/10 of a whole).
- **Hundredths:** The second place to the right of the decimal point (1/100 of a whole).
- **Thousandths:** The third place to the right of the decimal point (1/1,000 of a whole).
- **Expanded form:** Writing a number as the sum of the values of each digit (e.g., 3.254 = 3 + 0.2 + 0.05 + 0.004).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Review the place-value chart.**

Draw a place-value chart on the board:

| Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths |
|----------|------|------|---|--------|------------|-------------|
| 100 | 10 | 1 | . | 0.1 | 0.01 | 0.001 |

Say: "Look at the pattern. Moving left, each place is worth 10 times more. Moving right, each place is worth 10 times less -- or 1/10 as much."

Point to the ones place: "1 whole." Move right: "One-tenth. That is 1/10 of a whole. Ten of these make 1 whole." Move right again: "One hundredth. That is 1/10 of a tenth. Ten hundredths make one tenth." Move right again: "One thousandth. That is 1/10 of a hundredth. Ten thousandths make one hundredth."

**Step 2 — Build decimals with base-ten blocks.**

Hold up a flat (10x10 grid): "Let's say this flat represents 1 whole."
Hold up a rod: "This rod is 1 column of the flat -- it is 1/10 of the whole, or 0.1."
Hold up a unit cube: "This tiny cube is 1 square of the flat -- it is 1/100 of the whole, or 0.01."

Say: "For thousandths, imagine cutting one of these tiny cubes into 10 even tinier slices. Each slice would be 0.001. We cannot easily show that with blocks, but we can mark it on a grid."

Build the number 2.357 with blocks: 2 flats, 3 rods, 5 unit cubes, and write +7 thousandths beside it.

Say: "2.357 means 2 ones, 3 tenths, 5 hundredths, and 7 thousandths."

Write the expanded form: 2 + 0.3 + 0.05 + 0.007

**Step 3 — The multiply-by-10 and divide-by-10 pattern.**

Write on the board:
- 0.003 x 10 = 0.03
- 0.03 x 10 = 0.3
- 0.3 x 10 = 3
- 3 x 10 = 30

Say: "Every time we multiply by 10, each digit shifts one place to the left. Its value becomes 10 times greater."

Write:
- 30 / 10 = 3
- 3 / 10 = 0.3
- 0.3 / 10 = 0.03
- 0.03 / 10 = 0.003

Say: "Every time we divide by 10, each digit shifts one place to the right. Its value becomes 1/10 as much."

**Step 4 — Comparing decimals.**

Write on the board: 0.45 ___ 0.409

Say: "To compare, we look place by place from the left. Both have 0 ones. Both have 4 tenths. Now look at hundredths: the first number has 5 hundredths, the second has 0 hundredths. Since 5 hundredths is more than 0 hundredths, 0.45 > 0.409."

Say: "A helpful trick is to annex zeros so both decimals have the same number of places: 0.450 and 0.409. Now you can compare like whole numbers: 450 thousandths versus 409 thousandths."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Write 4.062 in expanded form and word form.
- Expanded: 4 + 0.0 + 0.06 + 0.002 = 4 + 0.06 + 0.002
- Word form: four and sixty-two thousandths
- Check: 4 ones, 0 tenths, 6 hundredths, 2 thousandths.

**Example B:** What is the value of the digit 8 in 12.083?
- The 8 is in the hundredths place.
- Its value is 0.08 (8 hundredths).

**Example C:** Order from least to greatest: 0.6, 0.59, 0.605
- Annex zeros: 0.600, 0.590, 0.605
- Compare: 590 < 600 and 590 < 605 and 600 < 605
- Wait -- is 600 < 605? Yes.
- But 590 < 600 and 590 < 605.
- Order: 0.59, 0.6, 0.605

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Use base-ten blocks or the place-value chart as needed.

1. Write 7.304 in expanded form.
   - **Answer:** 7 + 0.3 + 0.00 + 0.004 = 7 + 0.3 + 0.004

2. What is the value of the 5 in 0.256?
   - **Answer:** 0.05 (5 hundredths)

3. Compare: 3.72 ___ 3.702
   - Annex zeros: 3.720 vs. 3.702
   - **Answer:** 3.72 > 3.702 (because 720 thousandths > 702 thousandths)

4. If you start with 0.04 and multiply by 10, what do you get? Multiply by 10 again?
   - **Answer:** 0.04 x 10 = 0.4; 0.4 x 10 = 4

5. Order from greatest to least: 2.1, 2.09, 2.105
   - Annex zeros: 2.100, 2.090, 2.105
   - **Answer:** 2.105, 2.1, 2.09

**Facilitator tip:** If the student struggles with comparing, always have them annex zeros first. This removes the confusion about different-length decimals.

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (On Level)
1. Write 9.415 in word form.
2. Write "twelve and seven hundredths" in standard form.
3. What is the value of the 6 in 3.061?
4. Compare using <, >, or =: 0.8 ___ 0.80
5. Order from least to greatest: 5.3, 5.03, 5.33

### Tier 2 (Scaffolded)
1. Fill in the place-value chart for 6.208, then write it in expanded form.
2. Circle the digit in the hundredths place in 4.379. What is its value?
3. Annex zeros so both numbers have 3 decimal places, then compare: 0.5 ___ 0.48
4. Complete: 0.007 x 10 = ___; 0.07 x 10 = ___; 0.7 x 10 = ___

### Tier 3 (Challenge)
1. The digit 4 appears in both 0.403 and 0.043. In which number does the 4 have a greater value? How many times greater?
2. A number has a 6 in the tenths place, a 0 in the hundredths place, and a 9 in the thousandths place. The whole-number part is 15. Write the number in standard form, expanded form, and word form.
3. True or false: 0.299 is less than 0.3. Explain your reasoning.
4. Find a decimal between 0.45 and 0.46. Can you find another one? How many are there?
5. If you multiply 0.006 by 10 three times in a row, what do you get? What single number could you multiply by instead?

## Exit Ticket

1. Write 5.038 in expanded form.
2. Compare: 0.62 ___ 0.602
3. What happens to the value of a digit when it moves one place to the left? One place to the right?

**Mastery: 3 out of 3 correct.**

## Teacher Notes

- This lesson is a review and extension. Most students will be familiar with tenths and hundredths from Grade 4. The new content is thousandths and the explicit x10 / divide-by-10 pattern between places.
- If students are already solid on place value, this lesson can be completed in one session. If they show confusion (especially comparing decimals of different lengths), take an extra session with additional block work.
- The "annex zeros" strategy for comparing is a powerful tool that removes common errors. Encourage its use throughout the unit.
- Connect to money frequently: one dollar = 1.00, one dime = 0.10, one penny = 0.01. Ask: "How many pennies make a dime? How many dimes make a dollar?" This reinforces the x10 pattern.
- Preview for the student: "Now that we understand decimal place value deeply, we are ready to add, subtract, multiply, and divide decimals in the upcoming lessons."
