# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 4, Lesson 8

## Topic
Dividing Whole Numbers by Decimals

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Divide a whole number by a decimal by multiplying both the divisor and dividend by a power of 10 to create an equivalent whole-number division problem.
- Explain why multiplying both numbers by the same power of 10 does not change the quotient.
- Connect dividing by a decimal to the measurement interpretation of division ("how many groups of 0.5 fit into 3?").
- Use estimation to verify the reasonableness of quotients when dividing by a decimal.

## Prerequisites
- Dividing a decimal by a whole number (Lesson 7).
- Understanding equivalent fractions and the concept that multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number preserves value.
- Division of unit fractions and whole numbers (Unit 3).

## Materials Needed
- Number line handouts (0 to 5, marked in tenths).
- Base-ten blocks.
- Whiteboard and markers.
- Index cards for exit ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.7

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect to fraction division from Unit 3 and review equivalent fractions.

1. How many halves are in 3? (3 / 1/2 = ?)
   - **Answer: 6**
2. Simplify: 12/4 = ? and 24/8 = ? Are these equal?
   - **Answer: 3 and 3. Yes, they are equal.** (Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number gives an equivalent fraction.)
3. Estimate: 6 / 0.5. Think: "How many groups of 0.5 fit in 6?"
   - **Answer: 12** (each whole contains 2 halves, so 6 wholes contain 12 halves)

Say: "In Unit 3, you divided whole numbers by fractions like 1/2 and 1/4. Now we will divide by decimals like 0.5 and 0.25. The ideas are the same -- we just write the fractions as decimals."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Equivalent division:** A division problem that has the same quotient as the original because both dividend and divisor were multiplied by the same number.
- **Power of 10:** 10, 100, 1000, etc. -- used to shift the decimal point.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Number line model.**

Write: 3 / 0.5

Say: "This asks: how many groups of 0.5 fit into 3?"

Draw a number line from 0 to 3, marked in halves (0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0).

Say: "Let's count the jumps of 0.5: 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0. That is 6 jumps."

**Answer: 3 / 0.5 = 6**

Say: "Notice that dividing by 0.5 gave a bigger answer than we started with. Just like in Unit 3, dividing by a number less than 1 makes the quotient larger than the dividend."

**Step 2 — The "multiply both by 10" strategy.**

Say: "Counting on a number line works, but it gets hard with numbers like 4 / 0.25. There is a faster strategy."

Write: 3 / 0.5

Say: "Think of this as a fraction: 3/0.5. If I multiply both the top and bottom by 10, I get 30/5. That is an easier division."

30 / 5 = 6

Say: "The quotient did not change! Multiplying both numbers by 10 is like creating an equivalent fraction. The value stays the same."

**Step 3 — Why does this work?**

Say: "Remember: 3/0.5 = 30/5 because we multiplied both parts by 10. This is the same idea as 1/2 = 2/4 = 5/10. Multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number keeps the value."

Write the general strategy:
1. Look at the divisor. Count its decimal places.
2. Multiply both the dividend and the divisor by 10 (for 1 decimal place) or 100 (for 2 decimal places) to make the divisor a whole number.
3. Divide the new whole numbers.

**Step 4 — A two-decimal-place divisor.**

Write: 6 / 0.25

Say: "0.25 has 2 decimal places. Multiply both by 100."
- 6 x 100 = 600
- 0.25 x 100 = 25
- 600 / 25 = 24

**Answer: 6 / 0.25 = 24**

Say: "Check: How many quarters are in 6 dollars? 24 quarters. Makes sense!"

Estimate: 0.25 is one-quarter. 6 / (1/4) = 6 x 4 = 24. Matches.

**Step 5 — Another example.**

Write: 8 / 0.4

- 0.4 has 1 decimal place. Multiply both by 10.
- 80 / 4 = 20

**Answer: 8 / 0.4 = 20**

Estimate: 0.4 is a bit less than 0.5. 8 / 0.5 = 16. So 8 / 0.4 should be a bit more than 16. 20 is reasonable.

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** 5 / 0.5
- Multiply both by 10: 50 / 5 = 10
- **Answer: 10**
- Check: 10 x 0.5 = 5. Correct.

**Example B:** 9 / 0.3
- Multiply both by 10: 90 / 3 = 30
- **Answer: 30**
- Check: 30 x 0.3 = 9. Correct.

**Example C:** 4 / 0.08
- Multiply both by 100: 400 / 8 = 50
- **Answer: 50**
- Check: 50 x 0.08 = 4. Correct.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Solve together. For each problem, state what you multiply by, then divide.

1. 7 / 0.5 = ?
   - Multiply both by 10: 70 / 5 = **14**

2. 12 / 0.4 = ?
   - Multiply both by 10: 120 / 4 = **30**

3. 3 / 0.25 = ?
   - Multiply both by 100: 300 / 25 = **12**

4. 10 / 0.2 = ?
   - Multiply both by 10: 100 / 2 = **50**

5. 2 / 0.05 = ?
   - Multiply both by 100: 200 / 5 = **40**

**Facilitator tip:** The most common error is multiplying only one of the two numbers by 10. Always ask: "Did you multiply both the dividend and the divisor?" Use the equivalent fraction analogy: "You have to multiply the top and bottom to keep the fraction the same."

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (On Level)
1. 6 / 0.3 = ?
2. 15 / 0.5 = ?
3. 8 / 0.2 = ?
4. 4 / 0.25 = ?
5. A piece of wire is 9 meters long. How many pieces of 0.3 meters can be cut from it?

### Tier 2 (Scaffolded)
1. Write as an equivalent whole-number division: 5 / 0.5 = ___ / ___ = ___
2. Fill in: To divide by 0.4, multiply both numbers by ___. So 8 / 0.4 = ___ / ___ = ___
3. Use a number line (0 to 4, marked in 0.5s) to find 4 / 0.5.
4. Check by multiplying: If 6 / 0.2 = 30, does 30 x 0.2 = 6?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)
1. 20 / 0.04 = ? (Think carefully about the power of 10!)
2. A roll of tape is 12 meters long. Each piece used is 0.15 meters. How many pieces can be cut? (This requires dividing by a number with 2 decimal places.)
3. Explain why 3 / 0.5 and 3 / (1/2) give the same answer.
4. Without computing, predict: is 10 / 0.1 greater than, less than, or equal to 100? Explain. Then verify.
5. A student says "6 / 0.3 = 2 because 6 / 3 = 2." What mistake did the student make?

2. Compute: 9 / 0.3 = ?
3. Explain the strategy you used in Problem 2. Why does multiplying both numbers by 10 give the correct answer?

**Mastery: 3 out of 3 correct (Problem 3 requires a clear explanation).**

## Teacher Notes

- This lesson connects directly to Unit 3 (dividing by unit fractions). The measurement interpretation is the same: "How many 0.5s fit in 3?" is identical to "How many 1/2s fit in 3?" Remind students of this connection.
- The equivalent fraction/equivalent division idea is the conceptual anchor. Students who understand why 6/3 = 12/6 = 24/12 (all equal 2) will understand why 3/0.5 = 30/5.
- Common error: students multiply only one number (usually just the divisor). Always write the problem as a fraction and show both numerator and denominator being multiplied.
- Students may be surprised that dividing by 0.1 is the same as multiplying by 10. This is a powerful insight worth exploring.
- For struggling students, the number line model makes the measurement interpretation visible. Keep using it alongside the algebraic method.
- For the wire/tape problems in independent practice, remind students that this is measurement division: "How many pieces of a given size fit into the whole?"
- This is often the most difficult lesson in the unit. Be prepared to spend an extra session if needed.
