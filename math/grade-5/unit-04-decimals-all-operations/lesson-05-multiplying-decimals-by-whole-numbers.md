# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 4, Lesson 5

## Topic
Multiplying Decimals by Whole Numbers

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Multiply a decimal by a whole number using repeated addition, base-ten models, and the standard algorithm.
- Explain why the number of decimal places in the product matches the number of decimal places in the decimal factor.
- Place the decimal point correctly in the product.
- Use estimation to verify the reasonableness of the product.

## Prerequisites
- Decimal place value through thousandths (Lesson 1).
- Fluent multi-digit whole-number multiplication (Grade 5 prerequisite).
- Estimating with decimals (Lesson 4).

## Materials Needed
- Base-ten blocks (flat = 1 whole, rod = 0.1, unit cube = 0.01).
- Decimal grid paper (10x10 grids).
- Play money (dollars, dimes, pennies).
- Whiteboard and markers.
- Index cards for exit ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.7

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review whole-number multiplication and connect to money.

1. Compute: 245 x 3 = ?
   - **Answer: 735**
2. If one notebook costs $2.45, what is the total cost for 3 notebooks? (You can use any method.)
   - **Answer: $7.35**
3. Estimate: 4 x 6.9 is approximately ___
   - **Answer: about 28** (4 x 7 = 28)

Say: "You already know how to multiply whole numbers. And you just estimated a decimal product. Today we will learn the exact method for multiplying a decimal by a whole number. The key question is: where does the decimal point go?"

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Factor:** A number being multiplied.
- **Product:** The result of multiplication.
- **Decimal places:** The number of digits to the right of the decimal point.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete model with money.**

Say: "Let's find 3 x $2.45. Think of it as buying 3 items that each cost $2.45."

Use play money or draw:
- 3 groups of: 2 dollar bills, 4 dimes, 5 pennies.
- Combine the dollars: 3 x 2 = 6 dollars.
- Combine the dimes: 3 x 4 = 12 dimes = 1 dollar and 2 dimes.
- Combine the pennies: 3 x 5 = 15 pennies = 1 dime and 5 pennies.
- Total: 6 + 1 = 7 dollars, 2 + 1 = 3 dimes, 5 pennies = $7.35.

Say: "So 3 x 2.45 = 7.35. Let's see why the algorithm gives us the same answer."

**Step 2 — The algorithm: multiply as whole numbers, then place the decimal.**

Write: 3 x 2.45

Say: "Ignore the decimal point for now. Multiply as if it were 3 x 245."
- 3 x 245 = 735

Say: "Now think about what happened. 2.45 has 2 decimal places. It means 245 hundredths. When we multiply 245 hundredths by 3, we get 735 hundredths. 735 hundredths = 7.35."

Write the rule: **The product has the same number of decimal places as the decimal factor.**
- 2.45 has 2 decimal places, so the product has 2 decimal places: 7.35.

**Step 3 — Another example with regrouping understanding.**

Write: 7 x 0.6

Say: "Think of it as 7 groups of 6 tenths."
- 7 x 6 = 42 (if we ignore the decimal)
- 0.6 has 1 decimal place, so the product has 1 decimal place: 4.2

Model: 7 rods (each is 0.1, but we need 6 of them per group). 7 x 6 rods = 42 rods. 10 rods = 1 whole. 42 rods = 4 wholes and 2 rods = 4.2.

Estimate check: 7 x 1 = 7. 7 x 0.6 should be less than 7. 4.2 < 7. Makes sense.

**Step 4 — Example with zeros in the product.**

Write: 4 x 0.25

- Multiply as whole numbers: 4 x 25 = 100
- 0.25 has 2 decimal places, so place decimal 2 places from the right: 1.00 = 1

Say: "Check: 4 quarters make 1 dollar. 4 x $0.25 = $1.00. It works!"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** 5 x 3.4
- Multiply: 5 x 34 = 170
- Decimal places: 1 (from 3.4)
- Product: 17.0 = 17
- Estimate: 5 x 3 = 15. 17 is close. Reasonable.

**Example B:** 6 x 1.08
- Multiply: 6 x 108 = 648
- Decimal places: 2 (from 1.08)
- Product: 6.48
- Estimate: 6 x 1 = 6. 6.48 is close to 6. Reasonable.

**Example C:** 8 x 0.75
- Multiply: 8 x 75 = 600
- Decimal places: 2 (from 0.75)
- Product: 6.00 = 6
- Estimate: 8 x 1 = 8. 6 is a bit less. Since 0.75 < 1, the product should be less than 8. Reasonable.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Solve together. For each problem, estimate first, then compute exactly.

1. 4 x 2.3 = ?
   - Estimate: 4 x 2 = 8
   - Multiply: 4 x 23 = 92. One decimal place. **Answer: 9.2**

2. 3 x 1.56 = ?
   - Estimate: 3 x 2 = 6
   - Multiply: 3 x 156 = 468. Two decimal places. **Answer: 4.68**

3. 9 x 0.4 = ?
   - Estimate: 9 x 0 = 0... better: 9 x 0.5 = 4.5
   - Multiply: 9 x 4 = 36. One decimal place. **Answer: 3.6**

4. 5 x $4.60 = ?
   - Estimate: 5 x 5 = 25
   - Multiply: 5 x 460 = 2300. Two decimal places. **Answer: $23.00**

5. 12 x 0.35 = ?
   - Estimate: 12 x 0.3 is about 4 (actually closer to 12 x 0.4 = 4.8)
   - Multiply: 12 x 35 = 420. Two decimal places. **Answer: 4.20 = 4.2**

**Facilitator tip:** After each problem, ask: "How many decimal places does the decimal factor have? How many decimal places does the product have? Are they the same?" This reinforces the rule through repetition.

## Teacher Notes

- The conceptual foundation for this lesson is: a decimal is a fraction with a power-of-10 denominator. Multiplying 3 x 2.45 is the same as 3 x 245/100 = 735/100 = 7.35. The "count the decimal places" rule is a shortcut for this reasoning.
- Students sometimes place the decimal point by "eyeballing" rather than counting places. This works for simple problems but fails for products like 15 x 0.08 = 1.20 (not 12.0 or 0.120). Insist on the counting method.
- Estimation is the safety net. If a student computes 4 x 2.3 = 92 (forgot the decimal), estimating 4 x 2 = 8 immediately reveals the error.
- Connect to repeated addition for students who need a concrete bridge: 3 x 1.5 = 1.5 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 4.5.
- Money contexts work especially well here because students have intuition about prices.
- Preview: "Next lesson, we will multiply a decimal by another decimal. The same counting rule will apply -- but with decimal places in both factors."
