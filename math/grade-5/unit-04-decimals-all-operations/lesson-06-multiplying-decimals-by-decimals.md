# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 4, Lesson 6

## Topic
Multiplying Decimals by Decimals

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Multiply a decimal by a decimal using the area model and the standard algorithm.
- Explain why the total number of decimal places in the product equals the sum of decimal places in both factors.
- Recognize that multiplying two numbers each less than 1 produces a product less than either factor.
- Use estimation to verify the placement of the decimal point in a product.

## Prerequisites
- Multiplying a decimal by a whole number (Lesson 5).
- Decimal place value and the meaning of tenths and hundredths (Lesson 1).
- Area model for whole-number multiplication (Grade 4).

## Materials Needed
- Decimal grid paper (10x10 grids -- each small square = 0.01).
- Colored pencils (two colors for the area model).
- Whiteboard and markers.
- Index cards for exit ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.7

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review decimal-by-whole-number multiplication and activate area model knowledge.

1. Compute: 4 x 0.3 = ?
   - **Answer: 1.2**
2. Compute: 7 x 0.12 = ?
   - **Answer: 0.84**
3. Use an area model to show 3 x 4. (Draw a rectangle with length 4 and width 3. The area is 12.)

Say: "You know how to multiply a decimal by a whole number. Today we are going to multiply a decimal by another decimal. The area model will help us understand what is happening."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Area model:** A rectangle whose length and width represent the two factors; the area represents the product.
- **Partial products:** The results of multiplying parts of each factor, which are then added together.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Area model for 0.3 x 0.4.**

Draw a 10x10 grid on the board. Say: "This entire grid represents 1 whole. Each column is 1 tenth. Each row is 1 tenth. Each small square is 1 hundredth."

Say: "Let's find 0.3 x 0.4. Shade 3 columns in blue to represent 0.3. Shade 4 rows in yellow to represent 0.4."

Say: "The overlap -- where blue and yellow mix -- represents the product. Count the overlapping squares."

Count: 3 columns x 4 rows = 12 small squares. Each square is 0.01.

**Answer: 0.3 x 0.4 = 0.12**

Say: "Notice something important. 0.3 is less than 1, and 0.4 is less than 1. The product, 0.12, is less than both 0.3 and 0.4. When you multiply two numbers that are each less than 1, the product is smaller than either factor."

**Step 2 — Connect to the counting rule.**

Say: "Let's see why counting decimal places works."
- 0.3 has 1 decimal place.
- 0.4 has 1 decimal place.
- Total: 1 + 1 = 2 decimal places in the product.
- 3 x 4 = 12. Place the decimal to get 2 decimal places: 0.12.

Say: "This works because 0.3 = 3/10 and 0.4 = 4/10. When we multiply: (3/10) x (4/10) = 12/100 = 0.12. Tenths times tenths gives hundredths."

**Step 3 — A larger example: 2.4 x 1.3.**

Say: "Let's use the area model with partial products."

Draw a rectangle divided into four sections:

|       | 2     | 0.4   |
|-------|-------|-------|
| **1** | 2 x 1 = 2 | 0.4 x 1 = 0.4 |
| **0.3** | 2 x 0.3 = 0.6 | 0.4 x 0.3 = 0.12 |

Add the partial products: 2 + 0.4 + 0.6 + 0.12 = 3.12

Now check with the algorithm:
- Multiply as whole numbers: 24 x 13 = 312
- Decimal places: 1 (from 2.4) + 1 (from 1.3) = 2
- Place decimal: 3.12

**Answer: 2.4 x 1.3 = 3.12**

Estimate: 2 x 1 = 2. The answer should be a bit more than 2. 3.12 makes sense.

**Step 4 — When both factors have two decimal places.**

Write: 0.25 x 0.14

- Multiply: 25 x 14 = 350
- Decimal places: 2 + 2 = 4
- Place decimal 4 places from the right: 0.0350 = 0.035

Say: "Notice we needed to add a leading zero to have 4 decimal places. 350 becomes 0.0350."

Estimate: 0.25 is about 1/4 and 0.14 is about 1/7. A quarter of one-seventh is a very small number. 0.035 is indeed very small. Reasonable.

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** 0.6 x 0.8
- Area model: 6 rows x 8 columns = 48 small squares on a hundredths grid.
- Multiply: 6 x 8 = 48
- Decimal places: 1 + 1 = 2
- **Answer: 0.48**
- Note: 0.48 < 0.6 and 0.48 < 0.8. Both factors < 1, so product < both.

**Example B:** 3.5 x 0.4
- Multiply: 35 x 4 = 140
- Decimal places: 1 + 1 = 2
- **Answer: 1.40 = 1.4**
- Estimate: 4 x 0.4 = 1.6. Close to 1.4. Reasonable.

**Example C:** 1.2 x 4.5
- Multiply: 12 x 45 = 540
- Decimal places: 1 + 1 = 2
- **Answer: 5.40 = 5.4**
- Estimate: 1 x 5 = 5. 5.4 is close. Reasonable.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Solve together. Use the grid for Problems 1-2 and the algorithm for Problems 3-5.

1. Use a 10x10 grid to model 0.5 x 0.7.
   - Shade 5 columns and 7 rows. Count overlap: 35 squares = 0.35
   - **Answer: 0.35**

2. Use a 10x10 grid to model 0.2 x 0.9.
   - Shade 2 columns and 9 rows. Count overlap: 18 squares = 0.18
   - **Answer: 0.18**

3. 1.6 x 0.3 = ?
   - Multiply: 16 x 3 = 48. Decimal places: 1 + 1 = 2.
   - **Answer: 0.48**

4. 4.2 x 2.1 = ?
   - Multiply: 42 x 21 = 882. Decimal places: 1 + 1 = 2.
   - **Answer: 8.82**

5. 0.15 x 0.6 = ?
   - Multiply: 15 x 6 = 90. Decimal places: 2 + 1 = 3.
   - **Answer: 0.090 = 0.09**

**Facilitator tip:** For Problem 5, students may write 0.90 (only 2 decimal places) instead of 0.090 (3 decimal places). Remind them to count the total decimal places from both factors.

## Exit Ticket

1. Use the counting rule to compute: 0.6 x 0.3 = ?
2. Compute: 2.5 x 1.4 = ?
3. Bella says 0.4 x 0.5 = 2.0 because "4 x 5 = 20 and you move the decimal one place." What is wrong with Bella's reasoning?

**Mastery: 3 out of 3 correct.**

4. 8.82

### Independent Practice — Tier 1
1. 0.36
2. 0.49
3. 1.60 = 1.6
4. 3.64

### Independent Practice — Tier 2
1. 18 overlapping squares = 0.18
2. 24 x 3 = 72; 2.4 x 0.3 = 0.72
3. 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.25. The product is less than 0.5. (Both factors are less than 1.)
4. Estimate: 2 x 3 = 6. Exact: 1.9 x 3.1 = 5.89.

### Independent Practice — Tier 3
1. 12 x 5 = 60. Decimal places: 2 + 2 = 4. Answer: 0.0060 = 0.006.
2. 0.8 + 0.6 = 1.4 is larger. 0.8 x 0.6 = 0.48. When both numbers are less than 1, their product is smaller than either number, while their sum is larger. 1.4 > 0.48.
3. Examples: 0.6 x 0.6 = 0.36. Also 0.4 x 0.9 = 0.36. Also 1.2 x 0.3 = 0.36. Many possible pairs.
4. Area = 0.9 x 0.9 = 0.81 square meters. The area (0.81) is less than the side length (0.9) because 0.9 < 1, so multiplying by 0.9 makes the number smaller.
5. 2.3 x 4.7: decimal places = 1 + 1 = 2, so 10.81. 0.23 x 0.47: decimal places = 2 + 2 = 4, so 0.1081.

### Exit Ticket
1. 6 x 3 = 18. Decimal places: 1 + 1 = 2. Answer: 0.18.
2. 25 x 14 = 350. Decimal places: 1 + 1 = 2. Answer: 3.50 = 3.5.
3. Bella counted only 1 decimal place (moving it "one place"), but there are 2 total: 0.4 has 1 decimal place and 0.5 has 1 decimal place. 1 + 1 = 2. The correct answer is 0.20 = 0.2, not 2.0.

## Teacher Notes

- The area model on the 10x10 grid is the single most important tool in this lesson. It makes visible why tenths times tenths equals hundredths. Do not skip it, even for students who seem to grasp the algorithm quickly.
- The misconception that "multiplication makes things bigger" is directly challenged here. When both factors are less than 1, the product is smaller than either factor. Use the grid to show this concretely: the overlap region is smaller than either the shaded rows or shaded columns.
- Students often struggle with problems like 0.15 x 0.6 where the counting rule requires adding a leading zero (3 decimal places: 0.090). Practice several of these with explicit counting.
- For students who struggle, have them write both factors as fractions first: 0.3 x 0.4 = 3/10 x 4/10 = 12/100 = 0.12. This connects to their fraction work in Units 1-3.
- The area model with partial products (used in the 2.4 x 1.3 example) bridges to multi-digit decimal multiplication. It is the same structure they used for whole-number multiplication in Grade 4.
- Preview: "Next we will divide decimals. First, we will divide a decimal by a whole number, then divide a whole number by a decimal."
