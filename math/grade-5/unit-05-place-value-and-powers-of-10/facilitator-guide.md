# Facilitator Guide — Grade 5 Math, Unit 5: Place Value and Powers of 10

**Grade:** 5
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 5 — Place Value and Powers of 10
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4-5 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Partially facilitated (facilitator introduces lessons and supports guided practice; student works more independently during warm-up and independent practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit deepens and extends students' understanding of the base-ten number system far beyond what they have encountered before. Students move from familiar millions-level numbers up through billions, encounter exponents as a powerful shorthand for repeated multiplication, explore the elegant patterns that arise when multiplying and dividing by powers of 10, and take their first steps into scientific notation — the language scientists use to write numbers that are too large or too small for everyday notation.

Students enter this unit with a strong foundation: they can read and write multi-digit whole numbers to the millions (Grade 4), understand the x10/÷10 relationship between adjacent place values (Grade 4 and Unit 4 of this grade), and have fluency with decimal operations through thousandths (Unit 4). The current unit shows them that the patterns they already know extend in both directions — infinitely far left toward billions and beyond, and infinitely far right toward thousandths and beyond.

The unit follows a Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) progression. Students begin with place-value charts as concrete organizers, move through visual patterns with powers-of-10 tables, and finish with the abstract notation of exponents and scientific notation. Real-world contexts — astronomical distances, world populations, cell sizes, national debts — provide ongoing motivation for why enormous numbers matter and why compact notation is necessary.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Read, write, and compare whole numbers through the billions, using standard form, expanded form, and word form.
- Understand and apply the relationship between adjacent place values: each place is 10 times the place to its right and one-tenth of the place to its left.
- Define and evaluate exponential expressions with whole-number bases and exponents (e.g., 2³ = 8, 10⁴ = 10,000).
- Read and write powers of 10 using exponent notation (10¹ through 10⁹ and beyond) and explain the pattern connecting the exponent to the number of zeros.
- Multiply whole numbers and decimals by powers of 10 by recognizing the shift of digits to higher place values (the decimal point appears to move right).
- Divide whole numbers and decimals by powers of 10 by recognizing the shift of digits to lower place values (the decimal point appears to move left).
- Describe and apply the pattern that ×10 shifts every digit one place to the left and ÷10 shifts every digit one place to the right on a place-value chart.
- Read and write numbers in scientific notation (a × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ a < 10 and n is a whole number) at an introductory level: translating between standard form and scientific notation for very large numbers.
- Apply place value and powers-of-10 reasoning to real-world contexts in astronomy, population, and microscopy.

**Why this matters:** The base-ten number system is the most fundamental structure in all of school mathematics. Deep fluency with how it extends — in both directions, and using exponent shorthand — is essential for algebraic reasoning, scientific work, financial literacy, and data interpretation. This unit prepares students for Grade 6 work with exponents (including negative exponents), for scientific notation operations in Grade 8, and for using proportional reasoning with very large and very small quantities.

**Standards:** 5.NBT.1, 5.NBT.2, 5.NBT.3

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, confirm:

- [ ] Ability to read and write whole numbers to the millions in standard, expanded, and word form (Grade 4).
- [ ] Understanding that each place in a number is 10 times the value of the place to its right (Grade 4 place value).
- [ ] Fluency with multi-digit whole-number multiplication and division (Grade 4 and earlier Grade 5 units).
- [ ] Understanding of the x10/÷10 relationship between decimal place values, including tenths, hundredths, and thousandths (Unit 4, this grade).
- [ ] Familiarity with the "digit shifts one place" model when multiplying or dividing decimals by 10 (Unit 4, Lesson 1).
- [ ] Successful completion of Unit 4 (Decimals: All Operations) or equivalent mastery.

- **Extended place-value chart** (from billions to thousandths; reproducible template in each lesson or drawn on whiteboard) — the single most important tool for this unit
- **Colored markers or pencils** (one color per period/era: billions green, millions blue, thousands orange, ones red; helps students see groupings of three)
- **Whiteboard and markers** (or chalkboard/projector)
- **Paper and pencil**
- **Index cards or half-sheets** (for exit tickets)

- **Printed or drawn number-line strips** (for the billions lesson — showing relative scale)
- **Optional: science reference sheet** with real-world examples (distances in space, sizes of cells, population data) — included as context in lessons

**Total: 10-17 sessions** depending on student pace.

**Pacing tips:**
- Lesson 1 is a review lesson. Students who are already solid on place value to millions can move through it quickly; treat it as a confidence-builder and review rather than new instruction.
- Lessons 3-4 (exponents and powers of 10) are conceptually new for most students. Do not rush through exponents; the "why" matters far more than the "what." Take an extra session if needed.
- Lessons 5-7 (multiplying and dividing by powers of 10) go together naturally and students often find them satisfying once the pattern clicks. If the student grasps Lesson 5 quickly, the move to decimals in Lesson 6 can happen in the same session.
- Lesson 9 (scientific notation) is introductory only. Students should be able to read and write numbers in scientific notation — they are not expected to perform arithmetic in scientific notation at this grade level. Maintain that boundary.
- Lesson 10 is a consolidation lesson. Use it diagnostically: if the student struggles with specific problems, loop back to the corresponding lesson before the assessment.

## Lesson Summaries and Facilitation Notes

### Lesson 1: Place Value to Millions (Review and Extension)
**Goal:** Reactivate and solidify understanding of the place-value system from ones through millions, setting the stage for the extension to billions.
**Key moves:** Build the place-value chart from ones upward, naming each period (ones period: ones/tens/hundreds; thousands period: thousands/ten-thousands/hundred-thousands; millions period: millions/ten-millions/hundred-millions). Emphasize that the three-place groupings (periods) repeat. Use commas as markers for each period boundary.
**Watch for:** Students who can name place values by rote but cannot state the value of a specific digit. Press them to say "the 4 in 4,200,000 is in the millions place; its value is 4,000,000" — not just "the 4 is in the millions place."

### Lesson 2: Place Value to Billions
**Goal:** Extend the place-value chart to include the billions period (billions, ten-billions, hundred-billions) and read/write/compare very large numbers.
**Key moves:** Show that the billions period is simply the next three-place group after millions, following the same hundreds/tens/ones pattern within the period. Read large numbers by reading each period's value and appending the period name. Practice with real data: the approximate distance from Earth to the Sun (93,000,000 miles ≈ 93 million), the approximate distance to a nearby star (25,000,000,000,000 miles), U.S. population (~330,000,000), world population (~8,000,000,000).
**Watch for:** Students who read a number like 5,000,000,000 as "five million million" or "fifty hundred million." Use the period-by-period reading strategy and the place-value chart consistently.

### Lesson 3: Understanding Exponents
**Goal:** Define exponents as shorthand for repeated multiplication. Build the concept from scratch so students understand why the notation exists and what it means.
**Key moves:** Begin with repeated multiplication: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16. Introduce the term "base" and "exponent": 2⁴ means "multiply four 2s together." Contrast exponent notation with multiplication: 2⁴ ≠ 2 × 4 = 8 (this is the most common error). Practice with various bases (3², 5³, 2⁵, etc.) before narrowing focus to base-10 powers.
**Watch for:** The critical misconception that 5³ = 5 × 3 = 15 rather than 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. This is extremely common. Return to the definition every time: the exponent tells you how many times to use the base as a factor.

### Lesson 4: Powers of 10
**Goal:** Apply the exponent definition specifically to base 10, discover the zeros-counting pattern, and connect powers of 10 to the place-value chart.
**Key moves:** Build a table of powers of 10 from 10⁰ through 10⁹. Show that 10¹ = 10, 10² = 100, 10³ = 1,000, and that the exponent tells you exactly how many zeros follow the 1. Connect each power of 10 to a place on the place-value chart: the ones place is 10⁰ = 1, the tens place is 10¹ = 10, the hundreds place is 10² = 100, and so on.
**Watch for:** Students who think 10⁰ = 0. Establish 10⁰ = 1 firmly — the pattern (10³ = 1000, 10² = 100, 10¹ = 10, so 10⁰ = 1) is the most accessible argument. Do not leave this misconception unaddressed.

### Lesson 5: Multiplying Whole Numbers by Powers of 10
**Goal:** Develop fluency multiplying whole numbers by 10, 100, 1,000, etc., grounded in the understanding that each factor of 10 shifts all digits one place to the left.
**Key moves:** Use the place-value chart to show what happens step by step when a number is multiplied by 10. Then connect to repeated multiplication: multiplying by 1,000 = multiplying by 10 three times = shifting three places left = appending three zeros. Practice with a range of whole numbers.
**Watch for:** Students who append zeros mechanically without understanding why. Ask: "Why does multiplying by 100 add two zeros?" The answer must reference place value, not just the rule.

### Lesson 6: Multiplying Decimals by Powers of 10
**Goal:** Extend the multiplication-by-powers-of-10 pattern to decimals, using the decimal point as a reference that appears to shift right when multiplying.
**Key moves:** Use the place-value chart with decimal places included (through thousandths). Show that 3.456 × 10 shifts all digits one place to the left relative to the decimal point — the same rule as for whole numbers. Draw attention to the apparent movement of the decimal point as an alternative way to think about the shift. Practice problems spanning from tenths-level starting values to whole-number results (e.g., 0.045 × 1,000 = 45).
**Watch for:** Students who believe the decimal point itself moves, rather than the digits. Both framings yield correct answers, but the "digits shift left" framing preserves the underlying place-value understanding. Discuss both, preferring the digit-shift language.

### Lesson 7: Dividing by Powers of 10
**Goal:** Apply the inverse of multiplication-by-powers-of-10: dividing by a power of 10 shifts all digits to the right (toward smaller place values) and produces smaller numbers.
**Key moves:** Show division as the inverse of multiplication using the place-value chart. If 45 × 100 = 4,500, then 4,500 ÷ 100 = 45. Extend to decimals: 4,500 ÷ 10,000 = 0.45. Practice the pattern both ways.
**Watch for:** Students who think dividing by 10 means "subtract a zero" (so that 50 ÷ 10 = 5 is explained as "drop a zero"). This works only for multiples of 10 and fails completely for 45 ÷ 10 = 4.5. Require the place-value chart explanation.

### Lesson 8: Patterns in Powers of 10
**Goal:** Synthesize the multiplication and division patterns into a single unified framework: × 10 shifts every digit one place left; ÷ 10 shifts every digit one place right. Apply this to a wide range of problems and contexts.
**Key moves:** Create a "powers of 10" reference strip showing × 10 arrows going left and ÷ 10 arrows going right across the full place-value chart (billions through thousandths). Show how the same number can be produced by different combinations of multiplication and division (e.g., 0.3 × 1,000 = 300; 300 ÷ 10 = 30). Practice multi-step patterns.
**Watch for:** Confusion when both multiplication and division by powers of 10 appear in the same problem. Draw the place-value chart every time until the pattern is automatic.

### Lesson 9: Introduction to Scientific Notation
**Goal:** Introduce scientific notation as a compact, standardized way to write very large numbers. Students learn to read and write numbers in scientific notation; they do not perform arithmetic in scientific notation at this grade level.
**Key moves:** Establish the form: a × 10ⁿ where the lead factor a is at least 1 and less than 10. Show the two-step process: (1) identify the significant digits and write them with a decimal point after the first digit, (2) count how many places the decimal point moved to determine the exponent. Practice converting both ways.
**Watch for:** Students who write 46 × 10⁵ and think it is in scientific notation (it is not; the lead factor must be ≥ 1 and < 10). Correct this immediately: 46 × 10⁵ = 4.6 × 10⁶.

### Lesson 10: Mixed Practice and Real-World Applications
**Goal:** Apply all unit skills together in rich real-world contexts — astronomy, population, microbiology — and consolidate readiness for the unit assessment.
**Key moves:** Work through multi-step problems that require reading large numbers, applying powers of 10, and sometimes converting to/from scientific notation. Use the context to motivate the mathematics. Include error analysis: show a worked problem with a mistake and ask the student to find and fix it.
**Watch for:** Students who can do isolated skills but struggle when they are combined. The most common point of confusion is correctly deciding whether to multiply or divide by a power of 10 in a word problem. Encourage rereading and estimation before computing.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Why It Happens | How to Address |
|---------------|---------------|----------------|
| Reading 5,000,000,000 as "five million million" | The word "billion" is not reinforced; students know millions and extrapolate incorrectly | Explicitly teach the period names and practice with real-world billion-scale numbers (world population, distances) |
| 2⁴ = 2 × 4 = 8 | Confusing exponential notation with multiplication notation | Return to the definition every time: the exponent counts how many times the base is used as a factor. Write out 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 fully before condensing. |
| 10⁰ = 0 | The exponent "0" looks like it should make the answer 0 | Use the division-by-10 pattern: 10³ = 1000, 10² = 100, 10¹ = 10, 10⁰ = 10 ÷ 10 = 1 |
| "Multiplying by 10 means adding a zero" | This shortcut works for whole numbers ending in a non-zero digit but breaks down for decimals | Use place-value charts exclusively, not shortcut rules; show a decimal example like 3.4 × 10 = 34 to reveal the flaw |
| Moving the decimal point vs. shifting digits | Students think the decimal point travels; this creates confusion with direction | Describe consistently as "the digits shift left (when multiplying) or right (when dividing) relative to the fixed decimal point" |
| 46 × 10⁵ is already in scientific notation | Students focus only on the power-of-10 part | Explicitly define: scientific notation requires the lead factor to be ≥ 1 and < 10. Check every conversion. |
| Confusing scientific notation exponent with number of zeros | 4.5 × 10³ = 4,500 (not 4.5000 or 45,000) | Count place-shifts carefully on the chart; draw arrow diagrams showing how many places the decimal moves |

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Place value** | The value of a digit based on its position in a number |
| **Period** | A group of three consecutive places in a number (ones period, thousands period, millions period, billions period) |
| **Standard form** | The usual way of writing a number using digits (e.g., 4,200,000) |
| **Expanded form** | Writing a number as the sum of the values of each digit (e.g., 4,000,000 + 200,000) |
| **Word form** | Writing a number using words (e.g., four million, two hundred thousand) |
| **Exponent** | A number that tells how many times a base is used as a factor (in 2⁴, the exponent is 4) |
| **Base** | The number being repeatedly multiplied in an exponential expression (in 2⁴, the base is 2) |
| **Power** | The result of raising a base to an exponent; also used as a synonym for the exponential expression itself |
| **Power of 10** | Any number that can be written as 10ⁿ (e.g., 10, 100, 1,000) |
| **Scientific notation** | A way of writing numbers as a product of a factor (1 ≤ a < 10) and a power of 10 (e.g., 3.2 × 10⁶) |
| **Coefficient** | In scientific notation, the factor in front of the power of 10 (must be at least 1 and less than 10) |

## Differentiation Strategies

### Struggling Learners
- Keep the extended place-value chart (billions through thousandths) always visible; require its use for every problem until the pattern is internalized.
- When introducing exponents, spend additional sessions on bases other than 10 (2², 3², 2³, etc.) before connecting to powers of 10. Isolate the concept before the application.
- For multiplying/dividing by powers of 10, start exclusively with whole numbers before introducing decimals. Ensure Lesson 5 is solid before moving to Lesson 6.
- Provide a step-by-step checklist for scientific notation conversion: (1) underline the first non-zero digit, (2) place the decimal after it, (3) count the places, (4) write as a × 10^n.
- Limit the range of scientific notation practice to exponents 3 through 9 (familiar numbers) and defer very small exponents for later grades.

### Advanced Learners
- Investigate: What happens with exponents on bases other than 10? What patterns appear in 2ⁿ, 3ⁿ, 5ⁿ?
- Introduce the concept of negative exponents as a preview (10⁻¹ = 0.1, 10⁻² = 0.01) and ask the student to extend the powers-of-10 table downward.
- Challenge: Write numbers in scientific notation and order them without converting back to standard form.
- Research and report on one real-world application where scientific notation is essential (e.g., measuring distances in light-years, the mass of an electron, the number of stars in the Milky Way).
- Explore: Can you write 0.000045 in a compact form similar to scientific notation? (Preview of scientific notation with negative exponents for Grade 8.)

## Assessment Guidance

Each lesson ends with a 3-minute exit ticket that provides immediate formative data. Use exit ticket results to determine whether to move forward or revisit content:

- **Student answers all exit ticket items correctly:** Proceed to the next lesson.
- **Student misses one item:** Briefly address the misconception at the start of the next session before proceeding.
- **Student misses two or more items:** Reteach the lesson concept using a different model (e.g., switch from numerical examples to a place-value chart, or vice versa) before assessing again.

The unit assessment at the end covers all 10 lessons and requires 80% (40/50 points) for mastery. If a student does not reach mastery, use the section-by-section score breakdown to identify which lessons need reteaching. The assessment includes two items from prior units (fractions and decimals) as spaced review.

## Connection to Later Learning

- **Grade 5, Unit 6:** Volume and coordinate graphing builds on the measurement sense developed here when working with real-world data.
- **Grade 6, The Number System:** Students extend integer exponents to include negative exponents, work with scientific notation for very small numbers, and perform arithmetic in scientific notation. The conceptual groundwork laid here is essential.
- **Grade 8, Exponents and Scientific Notation:** Students formally define all integer exponents, apply exponent laws, and compute with scientific notation. This unit's introduction will be revisited and deepened significantly.
- **Science (all grades):** Powers of 10 and scientific notation are the language of physical measurement — from nanometers to light-years. Fluency with these concepts supports physics, chemistry, biology, and earth science learning throughout middle and high school.
