# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 5, Lesson 4

## Topic
Powers of 10 (10¹, 10², 10³ and Beyond — Patterns in Zeros and Place Value)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Write and evaluate powers of 10 from 10⁰ through 10⁹ using exponent notation.
- Explain the pattern: the exponent in a power of 10 tells exactly how many zeros follow the 1.
- Match each power of 10 to its corresponding place on the extended place-value chart.
- State the value of 10⁰ as 1 and explain why, using the decreasing-exponent pattern.
- Convert fluently between exponential notation (10ⁿ) and standard form for powers of 10.
- Describe why powers of 10 are especially useful for describing the place-value system.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to evaluate exponential expressions for any base (Lesson 3 of this unit).
- Knowledge of the place-value chart through billions (Lessons 1-2).
- Understanding that each place is 10 times the place to its right (Lessons 1-2 and Unit 4).

## Materials Needed
- Extended place-value chart (billions through ones) already on the board from Lessons 1-2
- Powers-of-10 table handout or drawn on whiteboard (see Direct Instruction)
- Whiteboard and markers
- Paper and pencil
- Index cards for exit ticket

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Recall exponent evaluation and connect to base-10 patterns.

Write on the board:
1. Evaluate: 10² = ___
2. Evaluate: 10³ = ___
3. Evaluate: 10⁴ = ___
4. What is the relationship between each answer and the one before it?

Give the student 2 minutes.

Then ask: "What do you notice about 10², 10³, 10⁴? How are they related to each other?" (Each is 10 times the previous one.)

Say: "In Lesson 3, you learned that exponents are shorthand for repeated multiplication. Today we focus on one special base — the number 10. When the base is 10, something very beautiful appears."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build the complete powers-of-10 table, discover the zeros pattern, and connect each power to the place-value chart.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Power of 10:** Any number that can be expressed as 10 raised to a whole-number exponent (10⁰, 10¹, 10², ...).
- **Exponent (in powers of 10):** Tells how many times 10 is used as a factor; also equals the number of zeros in the resulting number.
- **10⁰:** Ten to the zero power = 1.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Build the powers-of-10 table.**

Draw this table on the board column by column, inviting the student to help fill in the blanks:

| Exponential Form | Repeated Multiplication | Standard Form | Number of Zeros |
|-----------------|------------------------|---------------|-----------------|
| 10⁰ | (special case) | 1 | 0 |
| 10¹ | 10 | 10 | 1 |
| 10² | 10 × 10 | 100 | 2 |
| 10³ | 10 × 10 × 10 | 1,000 | 3 |
| 10⁴ | 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 | 10,000 | 4 |
| 10⁵ | 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 | 100,000 | 5 |
| 10⁶ | 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 | 1,000,000 | 6 |
| 10⁷ | 10 × 10 × ... (7 times) | 10,000,000 | 7 |
| 10⁸ | 10 × 10 × ... (8 times) | 100,000,000 | 8 |
| 10⁹ | 10 × 10 × ... (9 times) | 1,000,000,000 | 9 |

Say: "Do you see the pattern? The exponent equals the number of zeros in the standard form. 10⁶ has 6 zeros — it is one million. 10⁹ has 9 zeros — it is one billion."

**Step 2 — Address 10⁰ = 1.**

Say: "10⁰ is a special case. Look at the pattern going downward: we get the next entry by dividing by 10."
- 10³ = 1,000
- 10² = 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100
- 10¹ = 100 ÷ 10 = 10
- 10⁰ = 10 ÷ 10 = 1

Say: "Following the pattern, 10⁰ must equal 1. And it has 0 zeros — just the digit 1. This makes sense with our rule: the exponent tells the number of zeros."

**Step 3 — Match each power of 10 to the place-value chart.**

Return to the extended place-value chart on the board. Label each place with its corresponding power of 10:

```
| Billions | Hundred-M | Ten-M | Millions | Hund-T | Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones |
| 10⁹      | 10⁸       | 10⁷   | 10⁶      | 10⁵    | 10⁴   | 10³       | 10²      | 10¹  | 10⁰  |
```

Say: "Every place in the whole number system corresponds to a power of 10. The ones place is 10⁰. The tens place is 10¹. The billions place is 10⁹. The exponent is simply how many places you are to the left of the ones."

Ask: "If I told you a number is in the ten-billions place, what power of 10 would that be?" (10¹⁰.)

**Step 4 — Powers of 10 as a compact description of place.**

Write: 7 × 10⁵ = 7 × 100,000 = 700,000

Say: "When we write 7 in the hundred-thousands place, we are really saying 7 × 10⁵. Expanded form is already using powers of 10, in disguise."

Write the expanded form of 304,020:
- 3 × 10⁵ + 0 × 10⁴ + 4 × 10³ + 0 × 10² + 2 × 10¹ + 0 × 10⁰
- Simplified: 3 × 100,000 + 4 × 1,000 + 2 × 10
- = 300,000 + 4,000 + 20

Say: "This is called 'expanded form using powers of 10.' It shows exactly how our number system works."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Write 10⁷ in standard form. How many zeros does it have?
- 10⁷ = 10,000,000 (ten million). It has 7 zeros.

**Example B:** Write 100,000 as a power of 10.
- Count the zeros: 5 zeros → 10⁵.

**Example C:** What power of 10 corresponds to the millions place?
- The millions place holds values of 1,000,000 = 10⁶ (six zeros). So the millions place corresponds to 10⁶.

**Example D:** Write the expanded form of 5,030,000 using powers of 10.
- 5 × 10⁶ + 3 × 10⁴
- (The 5 is in the millions place = 10⁶; the 3 is in the ten-thousands place = 10⁴)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these together.

1. Fill in the blank: 10⁵ = ___ (standard form)
   - **Answer:** 100,000

2. Write as a power of 10: 10,000
   - **Answer:** 10⁴

3. What place does 10⁸ correspond to on the place-value chart?
   - **Answer:** The hundred-millions place

4. Write the expanded form of 6,004,000 using powers of 10.
   - **Answer:** 6 × 10⁶ + 4 × 10³

5. A student says 10⁰ = 0. What is the error and what is the correct value?
   - **Answer:** 10⁰ = 1, not 0. Using the pattern, 10¹ = 10, and 10 ÷ 10 = 1, so 10⁰ = 1. The exponent 0 does not mean the result is 0.

6. Which power of 10 has the value one trillion (1,000,000,000,000)?
   - **Answer:** Count 12 zeros → 10¹²

**Facilitator tip:** For problem 4, make sure the student uses the place-value chart rather than guessing. Have them write the number in the chart first, identify which places have non-zero digits, then read off the corresponding powers.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Write each as a standard-form number: 10², 10⁵, 10⁸.
2. Write each as a power of 10: 1,000; 100,000,000; 1,000,000.
3. What place does 10⁶ correspond to?
4. Write the expanded form of 2,500,000 using powers of 10.
5. True or false: 10³ = 3 × 10. Explain.

**Stretch:**
1. A scientist measures a cell and reports its diameter as "one ten-thousandth of a millimeter." Write this measurement using a power of 10 in the denominator (as a fraction: 1/10,000 = 1/10⁴).
2. The population of a city is 8 × 10⁵ people. Write this in standard form and in word form.
3. Explain in your own words why the exponent in a power of 10 always equals the number of zeros in the standard form. (Hint: think about what 10¹ × 10 does to the number of zeros.)

**Challenge:**
1. Is there a power of 10 that equals 50,000? Explain why or why not.
2. If 10ⁿ = 10,000,000,000 (ten billion), what is n? How did you determine this?
3. Using only powers of 10 and multiplication, write the expanded form of 909,000,009. Compare this to the standard expanded form — are both correct representations?

### Interleaved Review
*(From Units 1-4)*

- **Answer:** 3 × 2 1/4 = 3 × 9/4 = 27/4 = 6 3/4 cups

**Review 2 (Unit 4 — Decimals):** Calculate 3.6 ÷ 4.
- **Answer:** 3.6 ÷ 4 = 0.9

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Write 10⁶ in standard form.
2. Write 100,000,000 as a power of 10.
3. What place corresponds to 10⁴ on the place-value chart?

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Rebuild the powers-of-10 table step by step, having the student fill in each row.
- Focus on the zeros-counting pattern as the main rule, and confirm it for each value 10¹ through 10⁹.
- For the place-value connection, count together: "The ones place is 10⁰ (zero places from ones). The tens place is 10¹ (one place left). The hundreds place is 10² (two places left)." Have the student count each step from the ones place.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Proceed to Lesson 5 (Multiplying Whole Numbers by Powers of 10).
- Preview: "You now know that 10³ = 1,000. Next, we will figure out: what happens when you multiply any whole number by 1,000? Or by 10,000? The pattern is elegant."

## Extension (Optional)
**Powers of 10 in Real Life:**

Using the table of powers of 10, match each measurement to its approximate power of 10:

| Quantity | Approximate Value |
|----------|------------------|
| Number of seconds in a minute | 60 ≈ 10¹ |
| Number of steps in a typical school day | ~10,000 ≈ 10⁴ |
| Number of grains of sand on a beach | ~10 billion = 10¹⁰ |
| Distance from Earth to Moon in meters | ~384,000,000 ≈ 10⁸·⁵ |

| Age of the universe in years | ~14 billion ≈ 10¹⁰ |

For each, find the nearest power of 10 and discuss whether it is above or below that power. What does it mean that some numbers fall "between" two powers of 10?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The key insight of this lesson is the bridge between exponents (Lesson 3) and the place-value chart (Lessons 1-2). The powers-of-10 table IS the place-value chart, written in a different language.
- Do not skip Step 4 (expanded form with powers of 10). While the notation may feel abstract, writing expanded form with powers of 10 is the conceptual foundation for scientific notation in Lesson 9. Students who understand it now will find scientific notation much more accessible.
- The 10⁰ = 1 fact will come up repeatedly. Do not skip it or label it "just a rule." The decreasing-exponent pattern (divide by 10 each step going down) is the right explanation for this level.
- The lesson is short enough that the student may be ready to begin Lesson 5 in the same session if time permits. Use your judgment based on the exit ticket result.
- Real-world tip: the distance chart in the extension shows that many quantities are not exact powers of 10 — they fall between powers. This is a great entry point for scientific notation in Lesson 9: how do we handle 3.84 × 10⁸ rather than exactly 10⁸?

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice
1. 100,000
2. 10⁴
3. Hundred-millions place (10⁸ = 100,000,000)
4. 6 × 10⁶ + 4 × 10³
5. 10⁰ = 1. The student confused 10⁰ with 10 × 0 = 0. The exponent 0 means zero factors of 10, which by the pattern gives 1.
6. 10¹² (1,000,000,000,000 has 12 zeros)

### Independent Practice — On-Level
1. 10² = 100; 10⁵ = 100,000; 10⁸ = 100,000,000
2. 1,000 = 10³; 100,000,000 = 10⁸; 1,000,000 = 10⁶
3. 10⁶ corresponds to the millions place
4. 2,500,000 = 2 × 10⁶ + 5 × 10⁵
5. False. 10³ = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000. The expression 3 × 10 = 30, which is very different. The exponent 3 means "use 10 as a factor three times," not "multiply 10 by 3."

### Independent Practice — Stretch
1. 1/10⁴ = 1/10,000 (one ten-thousandth)
2. Standard: 800,000; Word form: eight hundred thousand
3. Each time we multiply 10¹ by another 10, we get 10², and the number of zeros goes from 1 to 2. Multiplying by 10 is the same as appending a zero in the ones place. So each additional factor of 10 adds exactly one more zero to the result. After n factors of 10, there are exactly n zeros — matching the exponent.

### Independent Practice — Challenge
1. No. 50,000 = 5 × 10,000 = 5 × 10⁴. For a number to equal a power of 10, it must be a 1 followed only by zeros. 50,000 has a 5 in the first position, not a 1. Powers of 10 are always: 1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, ... — they always start with exactly one 1 digit.
2. 10,000,000,000 has 10 zeros, so n = 10. Count the zeros to find n.
3. Using powers of 10: 909,000,009 = 9 × 10⁸ + 9 × 10⁶ + 9 × 10⁰ (or: + 9 × 1). Standard expanded form: 900,000,000 + 9,000,000 + 9. Both are correct; the powers-of-10 version uses exponent notation to name each place, while the standard version writes the full place value.
