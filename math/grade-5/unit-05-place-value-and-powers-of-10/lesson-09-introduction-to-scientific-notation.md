# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 5, Lesson 9

## Topic
Introduction to Scientific Notation (Writing Very Large Numbers Compactly)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Explain why scientific notation is useful for writing very large (and very small) numbers.
- State the two requirements of scientific notation: the coefficient must be at least 1 and less than 10; the second factor must be a power of 10.
- Convert a number in standard form to scientific notation by identifying the coefficient and counting the decimal-place shift.
- Convert a number in scientific notation to standard form.
- Identify whether a given expression is in correct scientific notation or not.
- Read and write examples from astronomy, biology, and demographics using scientific notation.

**Note:** At this introductory level, students read and write scientific notation. They do NOT perform arithmetic in scientific notation. That extension occurs in Grade 8.

## Prerequisites
- Fluency multiplying and dividing by powers of 10 (Lessons 5-7).
- Ability to write any power of 10 in standard form (Lesson 4).
- Understanding of the extended place-value chart through billions (Lessons 1-2).
- Ability to name all digit place values on the chart.

## Materials Needed
- Extended place-value chart on board

- Index cards for exit ticket
- Optional: printed list of real-world large numbers (distances, populations, biology) for reference

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Recall the place-value meaning of very large numbers and motivate the need for compact notation.

Write on the board:
1. The distance from Earth to the nearest star (Proxima Centauri) is approximately 40,000,000,000,000 km. Count the digits. How hard is this to read and write?
2. The number of stars in the Milky Way galaxy is approximately 300,000,000,000. Write this in word form.
3. A human hair is about 0.00007 m wide. How many zeros are in this number?

Discuss.

Say: "Scientists deal with numbers like these every day. Writing them in full is tedious, error-prone, and hard to compare. Today we learn the standardized shorthand that scientists and mathematicians use — scientific notation."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce scientific notation from scratch, emphasizing the two-part structure and the two-direction conversion process.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Scientific notation:** A way of writing a number as the product of two factors: a × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ a < 10 and n is a whole number. Example: 3.2 × 10⁶.
- **Coefficient (in scientific notation):** The first factor (a); it must be at least 1 and less than 10. It contains all the significant digits.
- **Exponent (in scientific notation):** The whole number n in 10ⁿ; it tells how many places the decimal point has moved (or how many times you multiply by 10) to recover the standard form.
- **Standard form:** The full written-out version of the number (e.g., 3,200,000).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Introduce the form and the two requirements.**

Write on the board:

```
a × 10ⁿ

where: 1 ≤ a < 10  (a is at least 1 and less than 10)
       n is a whole number
```

Say: "Every number in scientific notation has two parts. The first part (a) contains all the meaningful digits, written so there is exactly one digit before the decimal point. The second part (10ⁿ) tells us the scale — how large the number really is."

Write examples:
- 3.2 × 10⁶ ← coefficient is 3.2 (between 1 and 10) ✓
- 4.67 × 10⁸ ← coefficient is 4.67 ✓
- 1.0 × 10⁹ ← coefficient is 1.0 ✓ (exactly 1 is allowed since 1 ≤ a)
- 9.99 × 10⁴ ← coefficient is 9.99 ✓

Write a non-example:
- 46 × 10⁵ ← coefficient is 46, which is NOT less than 10 ✗ (not in proper scientific notation)
- 0.5 × 10⁷ ← coefficient is 0.5, which is NOT ≥ 1 ✗ (not in proper scientific notation)

**Step 2 — Converting from standard form to scientific notation.**

Write: 5,700,000

Say: "Step 1: Find the first non-zero digit. Here, it is 5. Place the decimal point right after it: 5.7"

Say: "Step 2: Count how many places you moved the decimal point from its original position (after the last digit of the whole number) to its new position (after the first digit). In 5,700,000 the decimal was at the end (5,700,000.) and moved to 5.7 — that is 6 places to the left."

Write: 5,700,000 = 5.7 × 10⁶

Verify: 5.7 × 10⁶ = 5.7 × 1,000,000 = 5,700,000 ✓

**Step 3 — Converting from scientific notation to standard form.**

Write: 4.35 × 10⁷

Say: "The exponent 7 tells us to multiply by 10⁷ — to shift the decimal 7 places to the right."

4.35 × 10⁷: move decimal 7 places right.

4.35 → 43,500,000

Write: 4.35 × 10⁷ = 43,500,000

Say: "We fill in zeros as placeholders where there are no digits to the right of the 5."

**Step 4 — Practice identifying correct scientific notation.**

Write a series of expressions and ask the student to say whether each is in correct scientific notation:

1. 7.4 × 10⁵ → ✓ (coefficient between 1 and 10)
2. 12 × 10³ → ✗ (12 is not less than 10; correct form: 1.2 × 10⁴)
3. 3.0 × 10⁹ → ✓
4. 0.6 × 10⁸ → ✗ (0.6 is not ≥ 1; correct form: 6 × 10⁷)
5. 9.999 × 10² → ✓ (9.999 is between 1 and 10)

**Step 5 — Real-world connections.**

Write on the board:

| Quantity | Standard Form | Scientific Notation |
|----------|--------------|---------------------|
| World population | 8,000,000,000 | 8 × 10⁹ |
| Distance: Earth to Sun | 150,000,000 km | 1.5 × 10⁸ km |
| Stars in Milky Way | 300,000,000,000 | 3 × 10¹¹ |
| US national debt (approx.) | $35,000,000,000,000 | $3.5 × 10¹³ |
| Number of cells in human body | 37,000,000,000,000 | 3.7 × 10¹³ |

Say: "Scientific notation is not just a math exercise — it is the language astronomers, biologists, and economists use daily. Knowing how to read and write it connects math to everything you study in science."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Write 420,000,000 in scientific notation.
- Coefficient: 4.2 (first non-zero digit with decimal after it)
- Count places: 4.20000000 — the decimal moves 8 places left to create 4.2
- Answer: 4.2 × 10⁸

**Example B:** Write 6.09 × 10⁵ in standard form.
- Move decimal 5 places right: 6.09 → 609,000
- Answer: 609,000

**Example C:** Is 53 × 10⁴ in correct scientific notation? If not, rewrite it.
- 53 is not between 1 and 10. Not correct.
- Rewrite: 53 × 10⁴ = 5.3 × 10 × 10⁴ = 5.3 × 10⁵
- Correct scientific notation: 5.3 × 10⁵

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these together.

1. Write 67,000 in scientific notation.
   - **Answer:** 6.7 × 10⁴

2. Write 3.04 × 10⁶ in standard form.
   - **Answer:** 3,040,000

3. Write 900,000,000 in scientific notation.
   - **Answer:** 9 × 10⁸ (the coefficient is exactly 9, or 9.0)

4. Is 80 × 10³ in correct scientific notation? Rewrite if needed.
   - **Answer:** No. 80 is not less than 10. Correct form: 8 × 10⁴ (since 80 × 10³ = 8 × 10 × 10³ = 8 × 10⁴ = 80,000)

5. The distance from Earth to Mars is approximately 225,000,000 km. Write this in scientific notation.
   - **Answer:** 2.25 × 10⁸ km

6. Jupiter has a mass of approximately 1.898 × 10²⁷ kilograms. Write a sentence that reads this number aloud.
   - **Answer:** "Jupiter's mass is approximately 1.898 times 10 to the twenty-seventh power kilograms." (Note: this is a very large number — students need not convert to standard form, but they should be able to state that it means 1.898 × 10²⁷.)

**Facilitator tip:** For problem 6, simply reading the scientific notation aloud fluently is the goal. Students do not need to write out the full standard form of 10²⁷.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Write in scientific notation: 48,000
2. Write in scientific notation: 7,300,000,000
3. Write in standard form: 5.6 × 10⁵
4. Write in standard form: 1.02 × 10⁷
5. A website gets about 24,000,000 visitors per year. Write this in scientific notation.

**Stretch:**
1. The diameter of the Milky Way is approximately 100,000 light-years. Write this in scientific notation.
2. Order these from least to greatest: 4.5 × 10⁶; 3.9 × 10⁷; 4.5 × 10⁵. Explain how you can compare numbers in scientific notation without converting to standard form.
3. Is 9.9 × 10⁴ greater than or less than 100,000? Explain.
4. Rewrite in correct scientific notation: a) 300 × 10² b) 0.45 × 10⁶

**Challenge:**
1. The Andromeda galaxy is approximately 2.537 × 10²² kilometers from Earth. Convert this to standard form (you may abbreviate by writing "about 25 followed by 21 zeros" rather than writing all digits). How many more zeros does Andromeda's distance have than the Earth-Sun distance (1.5 × 10⁸)?
2. Explain: if two numbers are both in scientific notation, how can you tell which is larger just by looking at the exponents? What if the exponents are the same?
3. A researcher says "the sample contains 2.4 × 10⁵ bacteria." Another researcher says "the sample contains 240,000 bacteria." Are they saying the same thing? Prove it.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Write 850,000 in scientific notation.
2. Write 3.7 × 10⁴ in standard form.
3. Why is 50 × 10³ NOT in correct scientific notation? What is the correct form?

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Focus on the conversion process, step by step: (1) identify the first non-zero digit; (2) place the decimal after it; (3) count the shift; (4) write the power of 10.
- Do not move on to ordering or comparing scientific notation; secure the basic conversion first.
- Practice with round numbers (1,000,000; 50,000,000; etc.) before moving to numbers like 3,450,000.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Proceed to Lesson 10 (Mixed Practice and Real-World Applications).
- Preview: "The next lesson brings together everything from this unit — large numbers, powers of 10, and scientific notation — through real-world problems in science and everyday life."

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- This lesson is explicitly introductory. The goal is reading and writing scientific notation, not computation. Do not introduce addition, subtraction, multiplication, or division of scientific notation expressions — that is Grade 8 content.
- The coefficient rule (1 ≤ a < 10) is the most important constraint to drill. Students frequently write 43 × 10⁵ and think they are done. Check every conversion for coefficient validity.
- For comparing numbers in scientific notation (Stretch problem 2): the key insight is that you look at the exponents first. A larger exponent means a larger number (when coefficients are both between 1 and 10). When exponents are equal, compare the coefficients directly. This is a natural extension that well-prepared students can handle.
- The notation 1.0 × 10⁹ is perfectly valid (coefficient is exactly 1). Students sometimes think they need a non-trivial decimal part; reassure them that 1.0, 2.0, etc. are fine.
- Connect to the reading from Lesson 2: when the student already knows that 8,000,000,000 is "eight billion," expressing it as 8 × 10⁹ should feel like natural shorthand, not a new mystery.

### Independent Practice — Stretch
1. 100,000 light-years = 1 × 10⁵ light-years
2. Order: 4.5 × 10⁵ < 4.5 × 10⁶ < 3.9 × 10⁷. To compare without converting: first look at exponents (5 < 6 < 7); when exponents differ, the larger exponent means the larger number. When exponents are equal, compare coefficients directly (e.g., 4.5 × 10⁶ vs. 6.2 × 10⁶: compare 4.5 and 6.2).
3. 9.9 × 10⁴ = 99,000. Since 99,000 < 100,000, the number is less than 100,000.
4. a) 300 × 10² = 30,000 = 3 × 10⁴ (correct scientific notation); b) 0.45 × 10⁶ = 450,000 = 4.5 × 10⁵

### Independent Practice — Challenge
1. 2.537 × 10²² in standard form: a 25 followed by 21 digits (2,537 followed by 18 zeros). Earth-Sun distance: 1.5 × 10⁸ km. Andromeda's exponent is 22; Earth-Sun exponent is 8. Difference: 22 − 8 = 14. Andromeda is 10¹⁴ times farther from Earth than the Sun is — an almost incomprehensibly large difference. In terms of zeros: Andromeda has 21 zeros in standard form (after the significant digits); Earth-Sun has 6. So Andromeda has 15 more zeros.
2. If both numbers are in scientific notation, the number with the larger exponent is always larger (because 10^(larger n) > 10^(smaller n) and both coefficients are between 1 and 10 — the largest possible coefficient, 9.99..., is still less than 10¹). When exponents are equal, compare the coefficients directly (both are between 1 and 10, so it is a straightforward decimal comparison).
3. 2.4 × 10⁵ = 2.4 × 100,000 = 240,000. Yes, they are saying the same thing. Both represent exactly 240,000 bacteria.
