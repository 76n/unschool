# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 5, Lesson 3

## Topic
Understanding Exponents (Repeated Multiplication, Base and Exponent)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Define "exponent," "base," and "power" using precise vocabulary.
- Explain that an exponent tells how many times the base is used as a factor in repeated multiplication.
- Write and evaluate exponential expressions with whole-number bases and exponents (e.g., 2⁴ = 16, 3³ = 27, 5² = 25).
- Distinguish between exponential notation and multiplication notation (2⁴ ≠ 2 × 4).
- Read exponential expressions correctly using language such as "two to the fourth power" or "three squared."
- Connect the concept of exponents to the place-value patterns studied in Lessons 1-2.

## Prerequisites
- Fluency with whole-number multiplication (Grade 4 and earlier).
- Understanding that multiplication is repeated addition (earlier grades).
- Familiarity with the place-value chart and the ×10 pattern between places (Lessons 1-2).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers
- Paper and pencil
- Index cards for exit ticket
- Optional: grid paper (for drawing arrays to represent small squares and cubes)

**Standards:** 5.NBT.2 (exponent notation as preparation for powers of 10)

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect repeated multiplication to prior knowledge and motivate the need for a shorthand notation.

Write on the board and ask the student to compute:
1. 2 × 2 = ___
2. 2 × 2 × 2 = ___
3. 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = ___
4. 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = ___
5. 10 × 10 = ___
6. 10 × 10 × 10 = ___

Give the student about 2 minutes.

Say: "There is a pattern here: you are multiplying the same number by itself over and over. Mathematicians noticed this happens so often that they invented a shorthand to write it more compactly. Today you learn that shorthand — and it is called exponential notation."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce exponent notation from the ground up, building understanding of what an exponent means before introducing the notation.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Exponent:** A number that tells how many times the base is multiplied by itself. Written as a small raised number to the upper right of the base.
- **Base:** The number being repeatedly multiplied in an exponential expression.
- **Power:** The result of evaluating an exponential expression; also used to refer to the expression itself (e.g., "5 to the third power").
- **Squared:** The special name for an exponent of 2 (because it gives the area of a square with that side length). 5² is read "5 squared."
- **Cubed:** The special name for an exponent of 3 (because it gives the volume of a cube with that side length). 5³ is read "5 cubed."
- **Evaluate:** To find the numerical value of an expression.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Introduce the notation through a concrete story.**

Say: "Suppose bacteria in a dish double every hour. You start with 1 bacterium."
- After 1 hour: 2 bacteria (2)
- After 2 hours: 2 × 2 = 4 bacteria
- After 3 hours: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 bacteria
- After 4 hours: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 bacteria
- After 5 hours: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32 bacteria

Say: "Writing '2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2' is long. Mathematicians write it as 2⁵ instead."

Draw on the board:
```
  5  ← exponent (how many 2s are multiplied together)
 ↑
2   ← base (the number being multiplied)
```

Write: 2⁵ means 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32

**Step 2 — Clarify the critical distinction.**

Write side by side:
- 2⁵ = 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32 (five 2s multiplied together)
- 2 × 5 = 10 (2 added five times, or 2 groups of 5)

Say: "These are very different. The exponent 5 tells you how many 2s to MULTIPLY TOGETHER. The × 5 tells you how many groups of 2 to ADD. Do not mix them up."

Repeat this contrast with another example: 3⁴ vs. 3 × 4.
- 3⁴ = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81
- 3 × 4 = 12

Say: "3 to the fourth power is 81, not 12. The exponent is NOT a multiplication partner — it is a count of how many factors."

**Step 3 — Practice reading exponential expressions.**

Write these on the board:
- 4² → "four squared" or "four to the second power" = 4 × 4 = 16
- 2³ → "two cubed" or "two to the third power" = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8
- 6² → "six squared" = 6 × 6 = 36
- 3⁴ → "three to the fourth power" = 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81
- 10³ → "ten cubed" or "ten to the third power" = 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000

**Step 4 — Special cases.**

Write:
- 5¹ = 5 (any base to the first power equals the base itself — there is only one factor)
- 5⁰ = 1 (any non-zero base to the zero power equals 1 — the student need not prove this yet; note the pattern: 5³ = 125, 5² = 25, 5¹ = 5, so 5⁰ = 5 ÷ 5 = 1)

Say: "We will use these special cases more when we get to powers of 10 in the next lesson. For now, just remember: anything to the first power is itself, and anything to the zero power is 1."

**Step 5 — Connecting to area and volume (optional visual).**

Draw a square with side length 4.
- Area = 4 × 4 = 4² = 16 square units.

Draw a cube with side length 3.
- Volume = 3 × 3 × 3 = 3³ = 27 cubic units.

Say: "This is why we call 4² 'four squared' and 3³ 'three cubed' — the geometry gives us those names."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Write 5 × 5 × 5 × 5 in exponential notation, then evaluate.
- Base: 5; Exponent: 4 (four 5s)
- Exponential notation: 5⁴
- Evaluate: 5 × 5 = 25; 25 × 5 = 125; 125 × 5 = 625
- 5⁴ = 625

**Example B:** Evaluate 2⁷.
- 2¹ = 2
- 2² = 4
- 2³ = 8
- 2⁴ = 16
- 2⁵ = 32
- 2⁶ = 64
- 2⁷ = 128

**Example C:** A student says 4³ = 4 × 3 = 12. Find the error and give the correct answer.
- Error: The student multiplied 4 × 3 instead of computing 4 × 4 × 4.
- 4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these problems together.

1. Write in exponential notation: 7 × 7 × 7 = ___
   - **Answer:** 7³

2. Evaluate: 3⁴ = ___
   - **Answer:** 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = 81

3. Evaluate: 6² = ___
   - **Answer:** 36

4. Write as repeated multiplication, then evaluate: 2⁶ = ___
   - **Answer:** 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 64

5. Which is larger: 4² or 2⁴? By how much?
   - **Answer:** 4² = 16; 2⁴ = 16. They are equal!

6. A student writes 5³ = 15. Is this correct? If not, give the correct answer.
   - **Answer:** Incorrect. 5³ = 5 × 5 × 5 = 125, not 15. The student multiplied 5 × 3.

**Facilitator tip:** After problem 5, pause and discuss why 4² = 2⁴. Ask: "Does the order of the base and exponent matter in general?" (No — swapping base and exponent usually gives a different result; this is a coincidence for 4 and 2.) Try 2³ vs. 3² to confirm: 8 vs. 9, different.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Evaluate: 9² = ___
2. Evaluate: 4³ = ___
3. Write in exponential notation: 8 × 8 × 8 × 8 × 8 = ___
4. A student says that 3⁵ = 3 × 5 = 15. Explain the error and calculate the correct answer.
5. Which is greater: 10² or 3⁴?

**Stretch:**
1. Evaluate: 2⁸ = ___
2. Fill in the blank: ___ ³ = 64. What is the base?
3. Write both as exponential expressions, then evaluate and compare: "six squared" vs. "two to the sixth power."
4. Is 2⁵ × 2 the same as 2⁶? Show why or why not.

**Challenge:**
1. Find a pattern: Fill in the table for 2ⁿ from n = 0 to n = 10. What do you notice about consecutive entries?

   | n | 2ⁿ |
   |---|-----|
   | 0 | 1 |
   | 1 | 2 |
   | ... | ... |

2. Can you find two different whole numbers a and b (where a ≠ b) such that a^b = b^a? Try to find all examples where both a and b are less than 10.
3. The number 64 can be written as an exponential expression in at least three different ways using integer bases and exponents. Find them all.

### Interleaved Review
*(From Units 1-4)*

**Review 1 (Unit 2 — Multiplying Fractions):** Calculate 3/4 × 2/5. Simplify your answer.
- **Answer:** (3 × 2) / (4 × 5) = 6/20 = 3/10

**Review 2 (Unit 4 — Decimals):** Calculate 0.7 × 0.9.
- **Answer:** 7 × 9 = 63; two decimal places → 0.63

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Evaluate: 5² = ___
2. Evaluate: 2⁵ = ___
3. A student claims 4² = 8 because 4 × 2 = 8. Explain in one sentence what the student got wrong.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct (including a clear explanation on question 3).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the bacteria story and rebuild the concept from repeated multiplication. Write out every factor explicitly before introducing the shorthand.
- Spend additional time on the 2ⁿ table (n = 1 to 6) to build familiarity with what exponential growth looks like.
- Practice only with small, well-known bases (2, 3, 4, 5) before moving to the base-10 work in Lesson 4.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Proceed to Lesson 4 (Powers of 10).
- Preview: "Next lesson, we apply this same idea to just one base: 10. And when the base is 10, something remarkable happens with place value."

## Extension (Optional)
**Exponential Patterns Investigation:**

Build the following tables and look for patterns:
- Powers of 2: 2¹ through 2¹⁰
- Powers of 3: 3¹ through 3⁶
- Powers of 5: 5¹ through 5⁵

Questions to explore:
1. What is the last digit (ones digit) of each power of 2? Does it ever repeat?
2. What is the last digit of each power of 5? What pattern do you see?
3. Which grows faster: powers of 2 or powers of 3? What does this tell you about the bases?
4. What is 2¹⁰ (1,024)? Is it close to 1,000? How close in percentage terms?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- This lesson introduces a genuinely new concept for most Grade 5 students. Do not rush through it. The goal is understanding, not speed.
- The most important thing to prevent is the 2⁴ = 2 × 4 = 8 error. This error is extremely common and deeply persistent. Address it explicitly and return to it every time you introduce a new exponential expression.
- A useful physical model for small powers: draw dots on the board.
  - 2¹ = a row of 2 dots
  - 2² = a 2×2 array of dots = 4 dots
  - 2³ = imagine a 2×2×2 cube of dots = 8 dots
  - This visual helps students see why "squaring" and "cubing" have geometric names.
- For the special cases (n¹ and n⁰), simply establish the results and reinforce them through the lesson's pattern. Do not attempt a formal proof at this level.
- The connection to place value (hinted at in Step 4) will be fully developed in Lesson 4. This lesson should end with the student feeling confident about exponent notation on any base, setting up the base-10 specialization.
