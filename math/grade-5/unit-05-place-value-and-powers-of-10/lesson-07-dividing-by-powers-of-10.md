# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 5, Lesson 7

## Topic
Dividing by Powers of 10 (Whole Numbers and Decimals)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Divide any whole number or decimal by 10, 100, 1,000, or any power of 10 and find the quotient using place-value reasoning.
- Describe what happens when dividing by a power of 10: every digit shifts to a lower place value (toward smaller value), or equivalently, the decimal point appears to move to the left.
- Use the inverse relationship between multiplication and division to check division results.
- Explain WHY dividing by 10ⁿ shifts digits n places to the right.
- Apply division-by-powers-of-10 to real-world measurement and unit-conversion contexts.

## Prerequisites
- Fluency multiplying whole numbers and decimals by powers of 10 (Lessons 5-6).
- Understanding that division is the inverse of multiplication (Grade 4).
- Knowledge of the x10/÷10 pattern between adjacent place values (Lessons 1-4 and Unit 4).

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review multiplication by powers of 10, then use the inverse relationship to set up today's concept.

Write on the board:
1. 73 × 10 = ___
2. 73 × 100 = ___
3. 73 × 1,000 = ___
4. If 73 × 100 = 7,300, then 7,300 ÷ 100 = ___

Give the student 2-3 minutes.

Say: "Every multiplication by a power of 10 has an inverse division. If multiplying by 10 shifts digits LEFT, what do you predict dividing by 10 does? Today we prove that prediction and extend it to any number."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build the division-by-powers-of-10 pattern as the inverse of multiplication, using the place-value chart.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Inverse operation:** An operation that undoes another; multiplication and division are inverses.
- **Digit shift (rightward):** When dividing by a power of 10, each digit moves to a lower (smaller) place value — one place to the right for each factor of 10.
- **Place-value decrease:** Dividing by 10 makes each digit's contribution to the total value 10 times smaller.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Show division as the inverse of multiplication.**

Write the multiplication and division fact families:
- 5 × 10 = 50, so 50 ÷ 10 = 5
- 5 × 100 = 500, so 500 ÷ 100 = 5
- 5 × 1,000 = 5,000, so 5,000 ÷ 1,000 = 5

Say: "Each division undoes the multiplication. Multiplying by 10 shifted 5 one place left. Dividing by 10 shifts it one place right — back to where it started."

**Step 2 — Show the digit shift on the place-value chart for whole-number division.**

Write 4,200 in the chart:
```
Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
      |    4      |    2     |  0   |  0
```

Divide by 10 (shift 1 right):
```
Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
      |           |    4     |  2   |  0
```

Result: 420

Divide 4,200 by 100 (shift 2 right):
```
Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
      |           |          |  4   |  2
```

Result: 42

Divide 4,200 by 1,000 (shift 3 right):
```
Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths
      |           |          |      |  4   | . |  2
```

Result: 4.2

Say: "When we shift far enough right, the digits cross the decimal point and become decimal values. 4,200 ÷ 1,000 = 4.2 because the 4 moved from thousands to ones and the 2 moved from hundreds to tenths."

**Step 3 — Division with decimals: digits move further right.**

Write: 4.2 ÷ 10

Chart:
```
Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths
  4  | . |   2    |
```

Shift 1 right:
```
Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths
     | . |   4    |    2
```

Result: 0.42

Say: "We write a 0 in the ones place so the number does not appear to start with a decimal point. 4.2 ÷ 10 = 0.42."

Continue: 4.2 ÷ 100 = 0.042

Chart shift of 2 places right:
```
Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths
     | . |   0    |    4       |    2
```

Result: 0.042 (placeholder zero in tenths needed because the 4 only reached hundredths)

**Step 4 — The decimal-movement shortcut (for division).**

Say: "Just as multiplying by 10ⁿ appears to move the decimal right n places, dividing by 10ⁿ appears to move the decimal LEFT n places."

Examples:
- 700 ÷ 10 = 70 (decimal moved 1 left: 700. → 70.)
- 700 ÷ 1,000 = 0.7 (decimal moved 3 left: 700. → 0.700 → 0.7)
- 35.6 ÷ 100 = 0.356 (decimal moved 2 left: 35.6 → 0.356)

**Step 5 — Verifying with inverse multiplication.**

Always check: if 35.6 ÷ 100 = 0.356, then 0.356 × 100 should equal 35.6. ✓

Say: "The inverse relationship is your built-in answer checker."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Calculate 8,500 ÷ 1,000.
- Shift 3 right: 8,500 → 8.5
- Check: 8.5 × 1,000 = 8,500 ✓

**Example B:** Calculate 0.6 ÷ 100.
- Shift 2 right: 0.6 → 0.006
- Check: 0.006 × 100 = 0.6 ✓

**Example C:** A scientist divides a 3.5 cm sample into 1,000 equal pieces. How long is each piece?
- 3.5 ÷ 1,000 = 0.0035 cm
- Each piece is 0.0035 cm (3.5 thousandths of a centimeter).

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work together, using the place-value chart for the first two problems.

1. Calculate 6,000 ÷ 10 using the place-value chart. Show the digit shift.
   - **Answer:** 600

2. Calculate 6,000 ÷ 1,000.
   - **Answer:** 6

3. Calculate 45 ÷ 100.
   - **Answer:** 0.45

4. Calculate 0.3 ÷ 10.
   - **Answer:** 0.03

5. Calculate 7,800 ÷ 10⁴.
   - **Answer:** 7,800 ÷ 10,000 = 0.78

6. Verify problem 5 using inverse multiplication: 0.78 × 10,000 = ___
   - **Answer:** 0.78 × 10,000 = 7,800 ✓

**Facilitator tip:** Stress the inverse-check habit with every division result. Students who always check their division with multiplication develop strong number sense and self-correction skills.

## Extension (Optional)
**Thinking Small: Microscopic Measurements**

Scientists who study cells and molecules need to divide by very large powers of 10. Use your skills to solve these:

1. A red blood cell is about 0.007 mm in diameter. A scientist divides this measurement by 1,000 to convert to micrometers (µm). What is the diameter in mm ÷ 1,000?
2. A DNA strand is about 0.000002 mm wide. Divide by 1,000,000 to find the width in nanometers (nm).
3. A wavelength of green light is 0.00055 mm. Divide by 1,000 to express in micrometers.

After computing, write each answer in standard form and identify what place value the significant digit ended up in.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The most common error in this lesson is dividing in the wrong direction — students shift digits left instead of right. Use the inverse multiplication check every time to catch this immediately.
- Zeros that appear in results deserve careful attention. In 45 ÷ 100 = 0.45, the zero before the decimal is a conventional placeholder (we write 0.45, not .45). It does not represent a digit shift — it is simply a notation standard. Mention this briefly.
- Students sometimes think 0.3 ÷ 10 = 3 (they removed the zero and decimal). Use the chart to show why this is wrong: the 3 shifts from tenths to hundredths, giving 0.03 — a smaller number, not a larger one. Division by 10 always makes numbers smaller (when the divisor is > 1).
- The molecular-scale extension connects powerfully to science and motivates why tiny decimal numbers matter. If the student is curious about units like micrometers and nanometers, use this as an entry point to explain metric prefixes as powers of 10.
