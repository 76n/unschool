# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 5, Lesson 6

## Topic
Multiplying Decimals by Powers of 10 (Moving the Decimal Point)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Multiply a decimal by 10, 100, 1,000, or any power of 10 and find the product using place-value reasoning.
- Describe what happens when multiplying a decimal by a power of 10: every digit shifts to a higher place value (or equivalently, the decimal point appears to move to the right).
- Predict how many places the decimal point will move based on the power of 10 used.
- Convert between decimals and larger numbers using powers-of-10 multiplication (e.g., 0.0045 × 10,000 = 45).
- Apply decimal × power-of-10 multiplication to real-world measurement contexts.

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of decimal place values through thousandths (Unit 4, Lesson 1).
- The ×10/÷10 relationship between decimal places (Unit 4 and Lessons 1-4 of this unit).
- Fluency multiplying whole numbers by powers of 10 (Lesson 5 of this unit).
- Ability to read and write decimals in standard and expanded form.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review decimal place values and connect to the whole-number digit-shift pattern from Lesson 5.

Write on the board:
1. What is 47 × 1,000?
2. What place is the digit in 0.3 occupying?
3. What place is the digit in 0.03 occupying?
4. What is 10 times the value of 0.03?

Give the student 2-3 minutes.

Say: "In Lesson 5, multiplying by a power of 10 shifted every digit to the left — toward higher place values. Decimals live on the right side of the decimal point, in lower place values. Today we apply the same shift to numbers with decimal parts. The rule is exactly the same — and the results are just as predictable."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Extend the digit-shift model to decimals, using the expanded place-value chart.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Decimal point:** The boundary between the whole-number part and the fractional part of a number; it stays fixed; digits shift relative to it.
- **Digit shift (decimal context):** When multiplying by 10, each digit moves one place to the left (toward greater value), which makes the decimal point appear to move one place to the right relative to the digits.
- **Apparent decimal movement:** An alternative description of multiplication by powers of 10: the decimal point seems to move right when multiplying and left when dividing. (We prefer the digit-shift language, but both are useful.)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Draw the extended place-value chart.**

Draw the chart on the board:

```
Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths
   1000   |   100    |  10  |   1  | . |  0.1   |   0.01     |  0.001
```

Write 3.456 in the chart:

```
Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths
          |          |      |  3   | . |   4    |     5      |     6
```

**Step 2 — Multiply 3.456 × 10.**

Say: "Multiplying by 10 shifts every digit one place to the left."

After shift:

```
Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths
          |          |  3   |  4   | . |   5    |     6      |
```

Result: 34.56

Say: "Every digit moved left one place. The 3 that was in the ones place is now in the tens. The 4 went from tenths to ones. The 5 went from hundredths to tenths. The 6 went from thousandths to hundredths. The decimal point did NOT move — the digits moved."

Alternatively: "If you think of it as the decimal point moving, it moved one place to the right relative to the digits."

**Step 3 — Multiply 3.456 × 100.**

Shift all digits 2 places left:

```
Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths
          |    3     |  4   |  5   | . |   6    |            |
```

Result: 345.6

**Step 4 — Multiply 3.456 × 1,000.**

Shift all digits 3 places left:

```
Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths
    3     |    4     |  5   |  6   | . |        |            |
```

Result: 3,456 (a whole number)

Say: "When the exponent is large enough, the decimal part disappears entirely — all digits have moved to the left of the decimal point."

**Step 5 — What if more places are needed than digits?**

Write: 0.045 × 100

Chart:
```
Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths
  0  | . |   0    |     4      |      5
```

Shift 2 places left:
```
Tens | Ones | . | Tenths | Hundredths | Thousandths
     |  4   | . |   5    |            |
```

Result: 4.5

Say: "The two leading zeros in 0.045 had no digit value — only the 4 and 5 matter. When we shift them left 2 places, we land at 4.5. The zeros to the left of the 4 simply disappear (they are not needed as placeholders in the final answer)."

**Step 6 — Describe the decimal-point movement shortcut.**

Say: "Instead of drawing the chart every time, some people find it easier to say: multiply by 10ⁿ = move the decimal point n places to the right."

Write side by side:
- 5.307 × 10 = 53.07 (decimal moved 1 right)
- 5.307 × 100 = 530.7 (decimal moved 2 right)
- 5.307 × 1,000 = 5,307 (decimal moved 3 right — becomes a whole number)
- 5.307 × 10,000 = 53,070 (decimal moved 4 right — need a placeholder zero)

Say: "When the decimal point moves past all the written digits, we add zeros as placeholders."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Calculate 0.007 × 1,000.
- Shift 3 places left (or move decimal 3 right): 0.007 → 7
- Answer: 7

**Example B:** Calculate 12.5 × 10².
- 10² = 100: move decimal 2 right: 12.5 → 1,250
- Answer: 1,250

**Example C:** A microscopic organism is 0.003 cm long. If you line up 10,000 of them end to end, what is the total length?
- 0.003 × 10,000 = 0.003 × 10⁴
- Move decimal 4 right: 0.003 → 30
- Total length: 30 cm

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these together.

1. Calculate 4.7 × 10 using the place-value chart.
   - **Answer:** 47

2. Calculate 0.56 × 100.
   - **Answer:** 56

3. Calculate 2.04 × 1,000.
   - **Answer:** 2,040

4. Calculate 0.0008 × 10⁴.
   - **Answer:** 8

5. A recipe uses 0.25 liters of orange juice. If a chef makes 100 servings, how many liters does the chef need?
   - **Answer:** 0.25 × 100 = 25 liters

6. A student says: "0.6 × 1,000 = 0.6000 because I added three zeros at the end." What is wrong with this reasoning? What is the correct answer?
   - **Answer:** Adding zeros after the decimal (making 0.6000) does not change the value of the number — 0.6000 = 0.6. The correct approach is to shift the digit 6 three places to the left: 0.6 becomes 600. The correct answer is 600.

**Facilitator tip:** Problem 6 addresses a very common error. Students sometimes confuse "append zeros to a decimal" (which changes nothing) with the digit shift. Ensure the student can articulate the difference.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Calculate: 3.8 × 10 = ___
2. Calculate: 0.45 × 100 = ___
3. Calculate: 7.002 × 1,000 = ___
4. Calculate: 0.0009 × 10,000 = ___
5. A caterpillar moves 0.08 meters per second. How far does it move in 100 seconds?

**Stretch:**
1. Fill in the blank: 0.006 × ___ = 6. What power of 10 fills the blank?
2. A student measures a bacterium as 0.0025 cm wide. How wide is this in millimeters? (Hint: 1 cm = 10 mm, so multiply by 10.)
3. A film grain is 0.0004 cm in diameter. How many film grains laid end to end would make 4 cm? (Set up the equation and solve using powers of 10.)
4. Calculate: 0.00030 × 10⁵. Does the trailing zero matter in the original number?

**Challenge:**
1. What power of 10 must you multiply 0.000012 by to get the whole number 12?
2. A student computes 3.5 × 1,000 = 350. Explain the error and give the correct answer.
3. Explain why multiplying by 10 "moves the decimal point right" is an equivalent way of describing what happens, even though the decimal point itself does not actually move.

- **Answer:** 1/4 ÷ 3 = 1/4 × 1/3 = 1/12

**Review 2 (Unit 4 — Decimals):** Calculate 5.4 − 2.87. Show your work.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Calculate: 0.07 × 100 = ___
2. Calculate: 4.005 × 1,000 = ___
3. In your own words, what happens to every digit in a number when you multiply by 10²?

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct (including a clear description on question 3).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the place-value chart with decimal columns. Show the digit-shift step by step for 0.1 × 10, then 0.1 × 100, asking the student to trace where the 1 goes each time.
- If the student is using the decimal-movement shortcut and making errors, abandon the shortcut temporarily and return to the chart.
- Practice only with numbers that have a single non-zero digit (0.003, 0.04, 0.5) before moving to multi-digit decimals.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Proceed to Lesson 7 (Dividing by Powers of 10).
- Preview: "Division is the inverse of multiplication. If multiplying by 10 moves the decimal right, what do you predict dividing by 10 will do?"

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The decimal-movement shortcut (move the point n places right) is the most commonly taught rule at this level, but it should be understood as a description of what happens to the digits relative to the decimal point — not a magic trick. Prioritize the digit-shift language and connect both framings.
- The worked examples include a scientific context (organism length) to preview the real-world applications in Lesson 10 and scientific notation in Lesson 9. These contexts motivate why we need to multiply very small decimals by large powers of 10.
- Problem 4 in guided practice (2.04 × 1,000 = 2,040) requires the student to introduce a placeholder zero. The 2 becomes thousands, the 0 stays hundreds, the 4 becomes tens, and then there is nothing in the ones place — so 0 is written. Confirm this on the chart.
- The extension (unit conversion) is an excellent bridge to science class. If the student studies measurement, encourage this extension.

### Independent Practice — Stretch
1. 0.006 × 1,000 = 6. The blank is 10³ = 1,000.
2. 0.0025 cm × 10 = 0.025 mm
3. Set up: 0.0004 × n = 4. Solve: n = 4 ÷ 0.0004 = 4 ÷ (4 × 10⁻⁴) = 10,000. So 10,000 grains would make 4 cm. Alternatively: 0.0004 × 10,000 = 4. ✓
4. 0.00030 × 10⁵ = 30. The trailing zero in 0.00030 is the same as 0.0003; both give 30 when multiplied by 10⁵. The trailing zero does not affect the value.

### Independent Practice — Challenge
1. 0.000012 × 10⁶ = 12. The blank power of 10 is 10⁶ = 1,000,000.
2. The student shifted the digits only 2 places instead of 3. 3.5 × 1,000 = 3,500, not 350. 350 would be the result of 3.5 × 100 = 350 (2 shifts). Correct: 3.5 × 1,000 = 3,500.
3. When we multiply a number by 10, every digit moves to the next higher place value. The decimal point marks the boundary between the ones and tenths places — it does not move. But when each digit moves one place to the LEFT relative to a fixed reference, it is equivalent to saying the boundary (decimal point) moved one place to the RIGHT relative to the digits. It is the same shift described from two different perspectives: the digits move left, or equivalently, the decimal point moves right. Both descriptions produce the correct numerical answer.
