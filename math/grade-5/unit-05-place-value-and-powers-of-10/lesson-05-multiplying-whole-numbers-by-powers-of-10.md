# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 5, Lesson 5

## Topic
Multiplying Whole Numbers by Powers of 10

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Multiply any whole number by 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, or any power of 10 and explain the result using place-value reasoning.
- Describe what happens on a place-value chart when a whole number is multiplied by a power of 10: every digit shifts one place to the left for each factor of 10.
- Connect the shift in place value to the number of zeros appended when multiplying a whole number ending in non-zero digits by a power of 10.
- Explain WHY multiplying by 100 appends two zeros (by connecting it to multiplying by 10 twice).
- Use powers-of-10 multiplication to solve practical problems involving large quantities.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to evaluate powers of 10 and name corresponding place values (Lesson 4).
- Understanding of expanded form using powers of 10 (Lesson 4).
- Whole-number multiplication fluency (Grade 4 and earlier).
- The ×10 digit-shift pattern between adjacent places (Lessons 1-2 and Unit 4).

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate the ×10 and ÷10 place-value connection and prepare for today's generalization.

Write on the board:
1. What is 5 × 10?
2. What is 5 × 100?
3. What is 5 × 1,000?
4. What is 5 × 10,000?
5. Describe the pattern: what is happening to each digit of 5 with each multiplication?

Give the student 2-3 minutes. Discuss the pattern.

Say: "You can already compute these. Today we are going to understand exactly WHY this pattern works — using the place-value chart — and then extend it to any whole number, not just single-digit numbers."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Ground the "multiply by a power of 10 = shift digits left" rule firmly in place-value understanding.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Digit shift:** What happens on the place-value chart when a number is multiplied or divided by a power of 10; each digit moves to a new place position.
- **Place holder (zero):** A zero digit that is written in a place to hold that position, ensuring other digits are in their correct places (e.g., the zeros in 3,400 show that the 3 is in the thousands place and the 4 is in the hundreds place).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Show the shift on the place-value chart using a single-digit number.**

Write 6 on the place-value chart (in the ones column).

```
Millions | Hund-T | Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
         |        |       |           |          |      |  6
```

Multiply by 10:

```
Millions | Hund-T | Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
         |        |       |           |          |  6   |  0
```

Say: "The 6 shifted one place to the left — from the ones place to the tens place. Its value went from 6 to 60. We write a 0 in the ones place as a placeholder."

Multiply by 10 again (now 6 × 100 = 60 × 10):

```
Millions | Hund-T | Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
         |        |       |           |    6     |  0   |  0
```

Say: "Multiply by 10 twice = multiply by 100. The 6 shifted two places left. We have two placeholder zeros."

Continue: 6 × 1,000 (shift three places), 6 × 10,000 (shift four places).

**Step 2 — Show the shift with a multi-digit number.**

Write 34 on the place-value chart:

```
Hundreds | Tens | Ones
         |  3   |  4
```

Multiply 34 × 10:

```
Hundreds | Tens | Ones
    3    |  4   |  0
```

Say: "Every digit shifts one place to the left. The 3 went from tens to hundreds, the 4 went from ones to tens. We write a 0 to fill the ones place."

Multiply 34 × 100 (34 × 10 × 10):

```
Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
      |           |    3     |  4   |  0  ← Wait, 34 × 100...
```

Let's redo correctly:
```
Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
      |           |    3     |  4   |  0  (this is 34 × 10 = 340)
```
Then shift once more:
```
Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
      |     3     |    4     |  0   |  0  (this is 34 × 100 = 3,400)
```

Say: "Both digits shifted two places to the left. Two placeholder zeros are added."

**Step 3 — Connect to powers of 10.**

Write the rule explicitly:
- Multiplying by 10¹ = shift each digit 1 place left → append 1 zero
- Multiplying by 10² = shift each digit 2 places left → append 2 zeros
- Multiplying by 10³ = shift each digit 3 places left → append 3 zeros
- Multiplying by 10ⁿ = shift each digit n places left → append n zeros

**Step 4 — Extend to non-round whole numbers.**

Write: 247 × 10³

On the chart:
```
Millions | Hund-T | Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
         |        |       |           |  2       |  4   |  7
```
Shift 3 places left:
```
Millions | Hund-T | Ten-T | Thousands | Hundreds | Tens | Ones
         |  2     |  4    |  7        |  0       |  0   |  0
```

Result: 247,000

Say: "247 × 10³ = 247,000. Each of the three digits shifted three places. Three placeholder zeros were added."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Calculate 56 × 10⁴.
- 56 × 10⁴: shift both digits 4 places left, add 4 zeros.
- 56 × 10,000 = 560,000

**Example B:** Calculate 309 × 100.
- 100 = 10²: shift 2 places, add 2 zeros.
- 309 × 100 = 30,900

**Example C:** A factory produces 475 cars per day. How many cars does it produce in 1,000 days?
- 475 × 1,000 = 475 × 10³ = 475,000 cars

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
Work through these together, using the place-value chart for the first two problems.

1. Calculate 38 × 100 using the place-value chart. Show the digit shift.
   - **Answer:** 38 × 100 = 3,800 (digits 3 and 8 shift 2 places left; two zeros added)

2. Calculate 124 × 1,000.
   - **Answer:** 124,000

3. Calculate 7 × 10⁵.
   - **Answer:** 7 × 100,000 = 700,000

4. Calculate 450 × 10²
   - **Answer:** 450 × 100 = 45,000. Notice: 450 already has one zero. Two more zeros are appended, giving 45,000. The student should write this as: shift all digits (4, 5, 0) two places left.

5. A bookshelf holds 85 books. A warehouse holds 10,000 bookshelves. How many books can the warehouse hold?
   - **Answer:** 85 × 10,000 = 85 × 10⁴ = 850,000 books

**Facilitator tip:** Problem 4 often causes confusion when students try to "just add zeros" — adding two zeros to 450 gives 45,000, which is correct, but a student might think "I already have zeros so I skip them." Use the chart to show that ALL digits shift, including the zero that was already there.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Calculate: 72 × 10 = ___
2. Calculate: 72 × 100 = ___
3. Calculate: 72 × 10,000 = ___
4. Calculate: 306 × 1,000 = ___
5. Describe in words what happens to a number when you multiply it by 10³.

**Stretch:**
1. A light-year is approximately 9,461,000,000,000 kilometers. An astronomer is measuring a distance of 4 light-years. Before computing, estimate: is the answer closer to 40 billion km, 40 trillion km, or 40 quadrillion km? Then calculate 4 × 9,461,000,000,000 by thinking of it as 4 × 9.461 × 10¹². (Tip: you are approximating; you do not need to multiply precisely.)
2. Fill in the blank: 36 × ___ = 36,000,000. What power of 10 fills the blank?
3. If 8 × 10ⁿ = 800,000, what is n?

**Challenge:**
1. Explain why the rule "multiply by 10 means append a zero" works for 34 × 10 = 340 but would be misleading for 34.5 × 10. What is 34.5 × 10, and what really happened?
2. A number is multiplied by 10⁴ and the result is 2,700,000. What was the original number? Explain your reasoning.
3. The pattern for multiplying by powers of 10 says "shift n places left." Is there a number for which shifting left by 3 places is the same as multiplying by 1,000? Always? Write three examples and check.

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Calculate: 93 × 1,000 = ___
2. Calculate: 6 × 10⁴ = ___
3. On a place-value chart, what happens to each digit when you multiply by 10²?

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the place-value chart and physically trace the digit shift, step by step, for multiplying by 10 once, then twice, then three times.
- Start with single-digit whole numbers before multi-digit ones.
- Ask repeatedly: "Where does this digit go? Which place is it in now?" Until the shift is visible and not just a rule.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Proceed to Lesson 6 (Multiplying Decimals by Powers of 10).
- Preview: "Everything you just learned about whole numbers works exactly the same way for decimals. In the next lesson, we will discover what happens when the decimal point is involved."

## Extension (Optional)
**Big Number Estimation:**

Estimate each of the following by finding the nearest power of 10:
1. The number of days in a year (365) — nearest power of 10?
2. 365 × 10³ = how many days in a millennium?
3. A national park covers 4,000 square kilometers. How many square meters is that? (1 km² = 10⁶ m²). Write as a power-of-10 multiplication.
4. The Earth has a population of about 8 billion. If each person speaks at 150 words per minute, how many words are spoken on Earth every minute? Express using powers of 10 (8 × 10⁹ × 150 ≈ 8 × 10⁹ × 1.5 × 10² ≈ 12 × 10¹¹ = 1.2 × 10¹²).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

- The place-value chart is essential for this lesson. Students should trace digit movements physically — a pencil moving from column to column — until the pattern is internalized.
- The critical insight to reinforce: we are not "adding zeros"; we are shifting every digit to a higher place value, which requires zeros to fill the vacated places. These zeros are placeholders, not just decorations.
- Problem 4 in guided practice (450 × 10²) is intentionally chosen because it forces a decision about a digit that is already zero. Use this to firmly establish that ALL digits shift, not just non-zero ones.
- The extension problem is a wonderful real-world application connecting powers-of-10 multiplication to unit conversion (km to m, km² to m²). If time permits, it provides good practice and connects to science.

### Independent Practice — Stretch
1. 4 × 9,461,000,000,000 ≈ 4 × 9 × 10¹² ≈ 36 × 10¹² ≈ 3.6 × 10¹³ ≈ 36 trillion km. The answer is closest to 40 trillion km.
2. 36 × 1,000,000 = 36,000,000. The blank is 10⁶ (1,000,000).
3. 8 × 10ⁿ = 800,000 = 8 × 100,000 = 8 × 10⁵. So n = 5.

### Independent Practice — Challenge
1. "Append a zero" works for 34 × 10 because 34 is a whole number — adding a zero gives 340, which is correct. But 34.5 × 10 is NOT 34.50 or 345.0 appended with an extra zero. 34.5 × 10 = 345: the digits 3, 4, and 5 each shift one place to the left, and the decimal point stays between the ones and tenths place. The "append a zero" rule breaks down for decimals because the decimal point position matters. What really happened: each digit shifted one place toward higher value, and the decimal point (which marks the boundary between whole and fractional parts) now falls after the 345.
2. 2,700,000 ÷ 10⁴ = 2,700,000 ÷ 10,000 = 270. The original number was 270. (Shifting 4 places right gives 270. Confirmation: 270 × 10,000 = 2,700,000.)
3. Yes, for any whole number, multiplying by 1,000 always shifts each digit 3 places left. Examples: 5 × 1,000 = 5,000 ✓; 72 × 1,000 = 72,000 ✓; 304 × 1,000 = 304,000 ✓. This is true for all whole numbers because the place-value system is consistent.
