# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 6

## Topic
Simplifying Before Multiplying (Cross-Canceling)

## Learning Objectives
- Students will identify common factors between any numerator and any denominator across two fractions before multiplying.
- Students will apply cross-canceling to simplify fractions before multiplying, producing smaller numbers and a product already in simplest form.
- Students will explain why cross-canceling works: dividing a numerator and a denominator by the same factor is equivalent to dividing the eventual product by 1.
- Students will recognize the rule: you may only cancel a numerator with a denominator (cross-diagonal), never two numerators or two denominators with each other.
- Students will verify that cross-canceling and the standard algorithm produce the same answer by solving problems both ways.

## Prerequisites
- Fluency with the fraction multiplication algorithm: a/b x c/d = (a x c)/(b x d) (Lesson 5).
- Ability to simplify fractions to lowest terms using the GCF (Unit 1, Lesson 6).
- Understanding of factors and common factors.
- Comfort with dividing whole numbers to find factors.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Colored pencils or markers (two colors, for showing cancellation lines on the board).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.
- Optional: factor chart or multiplication table for reference.

**Standards:** 5.NF.4 — Apply and extend previous understandings of multiplication to multiply a fraction or whole number by a fraction.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Remind students that simplifying after multiplying can involve large numbers, motivating the desire for a shortcut.

1. Write on the board: "5/6 x 3/10 = ?"
2. Ask students to solve using the algorithm from Lesson 5. Give them 1 minute.
3. Collect answers. Expected work: (5 x 3)/(6 x 10) = 15/60.
4. Ask: "Now simplify 15/60. What is the GCF of 15 and 60?" (15.) "So 15/60 = 1/4."
5. Say: "That worked, but we ended up with 15 and 60 — pretty big numbers — and then we had to find the GCF of 15 and 60, which is not always obvious. What if there were a way to make the numbers smaller BEFORE we multiply, so we never get big numbers in the first place? That is what today's lesson is about."
6. Write on the board: "Today's goal: Learn to simplify before multiplying — a shortcut called cross-canceling."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Cross-canceling** — the process of dividing a numerator of one fraction and a denominator of the other fraction by their common factor before multiplying; this simplifies the computation and guarantees the product is already in lowest terms.
- **Common factor** — a number that divides evenly into two or more numbers.
- **Cancel** — to divide both a numerator and a denominator by the same factor, which is equivalent to dividing by 1 and does not change the value of the expression.
- **Cross-diagonal** — the pairing of a numerator from one fraction with the denominator of the other fraction (not from the same fraction).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Revisit the Warm-Up problem with cross-canceling.**

Write on the board: 5/6 x 3/10.

"Before we multiply, let's look for common factors between any numerator and any denominator from the OTHER fraction."

"Look at the first numerator, 5, and the second denominator, 10. Do they share a common factor?" (Yes, 5.) "Divide both by 5: the 5 becomes 1 and the 10 becomes 2."

Draw a diagonal line through 5 and write 1 above it. Draw a diagonal line through 10 and write 2 below it.

"Now look at the second numerator, 3, and the first denominator, 6. Do they share a common factor?" (Yes, 3.) "Divide both by 3: the 3 becomes 1 and the 6 becomes 2."

Draw a diagonal line through 3 and write 1 above it. Draw a diagonal line through 6 and write 2 below it.

"Now multiply the simplified numbers: 1/2 x 1/2 = (1 x 1)/(2 x 2) = 1/4."

"We got 1/4 — the same answer as before — but we never had to deal with 15 or 60. The biggest number we worked with was 2."

**Step 2 — Explain WHY cross-canceling works.**

"When we divide a numerator and a denominator by the same number, we are dividing the overall fraction by that number over that same number — which equals 1. Dividing by 1 does not change the value."

Write on the board:

"5/6 x 3/10 = (5 x 3)/(6 x 10)"

"If we divide the 5 in the numerator product and the 10 in the denominator product both by 5, we get: (1 x 3)/(6 x 2) — same as dividing top and bottom of 15/60 by 5."

"If we then divide the 3 in the numerator and the 6 in the denominator both by 3, we get: (1 x 1)/(2 x 2) = 1/4. Same as dividing 3/12 by 3. We are just simplifying earlier."

"Think of it this way: the numerators will all be multiplied together to form the final numerator, and the denominators will all be multiplied together to form the final denominator. Any factor that appears in a numerator AND in a denominator can be divided out — whether we do it before or after multiplying."

**Step 3 — Establish the rule: cross-diagonal only.**

Write on the board in a box:

**Rule: You may cancel a numerator with a denominator (from the same fraction or the other fraction). You may NOT cancel two numerators with each other or two denominators with each other.**

"Why not? Because two numerators are both part of the final numerator — dividing both of them would change the value. Same for two denominators. Canceling only works when one factor is in the numerator and the other is in the denominator — that way, you are dividing top and bottom by the same number."

Give a wrong example: "Suppose someone tries to cancel the 3 and the 6 in 3/5 x 2/6 by saying 'both are divisible by 3, so change the problem to 1/5 x 2/2.' That gives 2/10 = 1/5. But the correct answer is 3/5 x 2/6 = 6/30 = 1/5. In this case it happened to work — but only because the 3 and 6 were a numerator-denominator pair (3 is the first numerator, 6 is the second denominator). The key is WHERE the numbers sit, not just that they share a factor."

Now show a true error: "What about 3/8 x 5/6? Someone might try to cancel the 3 and the 6 by saying 'both are in the top,' but 3 is a numerator and 6 is a denominator — so that IS a valid cross-cancel. 3 and 6 share a factor of 3, so we divide: 3 becomes 1, 6 becomes 2. Result: 1/8 x 5/2 = 5/16. Check: 3/8 x 5/6 = 15/48 = 5/16. Correct."

**Step 4 — Model a multi-step cross-cancel: 3/8 x 4/9.**

Write on the board: 3/8 x 4/9.

"Look for cross-diagonal common factors."

"First numerator (3) and second denominator (9): common factor is 3. Divide both: 3 becomes 1, 9 becomes 3."

"Second numerator (4) and first denominator (8): common factor is 4. Divide both: 4 becomes 1, 8 becomes 2."

"Now multiply: 1/2 x 1/3 = 1/6."

"Let's verify without cross-canceling: 3/8 x 4/9 = 12/72. GCF(12, 72) = 12. 12/72 = 1/6. Same answer, but 12 and 72 are much harder to work with than 1 and 2."

**Step 5 — Model when there is only one cross-cancel opportunity: 2/5 x 3/4.**

Write on the board: 2/5 x 3/4.

"Look for common factors cross-diagonally."

"First numerator (2) and second denominator (4): common factor is 2. Divide both: 2 becomes 1, 4 becomes 2."

"Second numerator (3) and first denominator (5): common factor? GCF(3, 5) = 1. No common factor. Leave them as they are."

"Now multiply: 1/5 x 3/2 = 3/10."

"Verify: 2/5 x 3/4 = 6/20 = 3/10. Confirmed."

"Cross-canceling is optional — you can always multiply first and simplify later. But when it works, it keeps numbers small and saves time."

**Step 6 — Emphasize: cross-canceling is optional, not required.**

"You will always get the correct answer if you multiply first and simplify afterward. Cross-canceling is a shortcut that makes the arithmetic easier. Use it when you spot common factors quickly. If you do not see any, just multiply and simplify at the end."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — 3/8 x 4/9 (with cross-canceling)**
1. Cross-cancel: 3 and 9 share factor 3 → 3 becomes 1, 9 becomes 3.
2. Cross-cancel: 4 and 8 share factor 4 → 4 becomes 1, 8 becomes 2.
3. Multiply simplified fractions: 1/2 x 1/3 = 1/6.
**Answer: 3/8 x 4/9 = 1/6**

**Example A (verification) — 3/8 x 4/9 (without cross-canceling)**
1. Multiply numerators: 3 x 4 = 12.
2. Multiply denominators: 8 x 9 = 72.
3. Product: 12/72.
4. Simplify: GCF(12, 72) = 12. 12/72 = 1/6.
**Answer: 3/8 x 4/9 = 1/6 (same result)**

**Example B — 5/6 x 3/10 (with cross-canceling)**
1. Cross-cancel: 5 and 10 share factor 5 → 5 becomes 1, 10 becomes 2.
2. Cross-cancel: 3 and 6 share factor 3 → 3 becomes 1, 6 becomes 2.
3. Multiply simplified fractions: 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4.
**Answer: 5/6 x 3/10 = 1/4**

**Example C — 2/5 x 3/4 (with cross-canceling)**
1. Cross-cancel: 2 and 4 share factor 2 → 2 becomes 1, 4 becomes 2.
2. Check: 3 and 5 share no common factor. Leave as is.
3. Multiply simplified fractions: 1/5 x 3/2 = 3/10.
**Answer: 2/5 x 3/4 = 3/10**

**Example D — 4/9 x 3/8 (with cross-canceling)**
1. Cross-cancel: 4 and 8 share factor 4 → 4 becomes 1, 8 becomes 2.
2. Cross-cancel: 3 and 9 share factor 3 → 3 becomes 1, 9 becomes 3.
3. Multiply simplified fractions: 1/3 x 1/2 = 1/6.
**Answer: 4/9 x 3/8 = 1/6**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. For each, students first identify cross-cancel opportunities, then multiply.

1. 2/3 x 3/8 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Look cross-diagonally. Do 2 and 8 share a common factor? Do 3 and 3 share a common factor? Cancel before you multiply."

2. 4/5 x 5/12 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "The 5s jump out — one is a numerator, the other is a denominator. What happens when you cancel them? Then check the 4 and 12."

3. 6/7 x 1/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Is there a cross-cancel between 6 and 3? Between 1 and 7? How does this simplify the problem?"

4. 3/10 x 5/9 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "There are two cross-cancels here. Find both before multiplying. What is the GCF of 3 and 9? What is the GCF of 5 and 10?"

5. 7/8 x 2/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Is there a cross-cancel available? Check all four cross-diagonal pairs. If there is only one, that is fine — cancel what you can and multiply."

> **Facilitator Tip:** Use two different colored markers on the board to show cross-cancel pairs — for example, draw the first cancellation in blue and the second in red. This visual distinction helps students see that each cancellation is an independent step and that canceled numbers are paired cross-diagonally, not within the same fraction.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — Cross-Cancel and Multiply
Use cross-canceling to simplify before multiplying. Show your cancellation work and write the final product.

1. 2/9 x 3/4 = ?
2. 5/8 x 4/15 = ?
3. 3/10 x 2/9 = ?
4. 6/7 x 7/12 = ?

### Part B — Side-by-Side Comparison
Solve each problem TWO ways: (a) multiply first, then simplify, and (b) cross-cancel first, then multiply. Confirm that both methods give the same answer.

5. 4/9 x 3/10 = ?
6. 5/12 x 6/25 = ?

### Part C — Reasoning and Application
7. Kenji solved 3/8 x 4/9 by canceling the 3 and the 4, writing 1/8 x 1/9 = 1/72. Is Kenji correct? If not, explain his mistake and find the correct answer.
8. A recipe uses 3/4 cup of sugar. Maya wants to make 2/3 of the recipe. How much sugar does she need? Use cross-canceling to simplify your computation.
9. Determine whether cross-canceling is possible for 3/7 x 2/5. If yes, show the cancellation. If not, explain why and solve using the standard algorithm.
10. Create your own fraction multiplication problem where cross-canceling is possible. Show the cross-cancel steps and the final answer.

### Answer Key

**Part A**
1. 2/9 x 3/4: Cross-cancel 2 and 4 (GCF 2) → 1/9 x 3/2. Cross-cancel 3 and 9 (GCF 3) → 1/3 x 1/2 = 1/6. **Answer: 1/6.**
2. 5/8 x 4/15: Cross-cancel 5 and 15 (GCF 5) → 1/8 x 4/3. Cross-cancel 4 and 8 (GCF 4) → 1/2 x 1/3 = 1/6. **Answer: 1/6.**
3. 3/10 x 2/9: Cross-cancel 3 and 9 (GCF 3) → 1/10 x 2/3. Cross-cancel 2 and 10 (GCF 2) → 1/5 x 1/3 = 1/15. **Answer: 1/15.**
4. 6/7 x 7/12: Cross-cancel 7 and 7 (GCF 7) → 6/1 x 1/12. Cross-cancel 6 and 12 (GCF 6) → 1/1 x 1/2 = 1/2. **Answer: 1/2.**

**Part B**
5. 4/9 x 3/10:
   - (a) Multiply first: (4 x 3)/(9 x 10) = 12/90. GCF(12, 90) = 6. 12/90 = 2/15.
   - (b) Cross-cancel: 4 and 10 share GCF 2 → 2/9 x 3/5. 3 and 9 share GCF 3 → 2/3 x 1/5 = 2/15.
   - Both methods give **2/15.** **Answer: 2/15.**
6. 5/12 x 6/25:
   - (a) Multiply first: (5 x 6)/(12 x 25) = 30/300. GCF(30, 300) = 30. 30/300 = 1/10.
   - (b) Cross-cancel: 5 and 25 share GCF 5 → 1/12 x 6/5. 6 and 12 share GCF 6 → 1/2 x 1/5 = 1/10.
   - Both methods give **1/10.** **Answer: 1/10.**

**Part C**
7. Kenji is NOT correct. He canceled the 3 and 4 with each other, but 3 is a numerator and 4 is also a numerator — you cannot cancel two numerators. The correct cross-cancels are: 3 (first numerator) with 9 (second denominator), GCF 3, giving 1 and 3; and 4 (second numerator) with 8 (first denominator), GCF 4, giving 1 and 2. Correct computation: 1/2 x 1/3 = 1/6. **Correct answer: 3/8 x 4/9 = 1/6.**
8. 2/3 x 3/4: Cross-cancel 3 and 3 (GCF 3) → 2/1 x 1/4. Cross-cancel 2 and 4 (GCF 2) → 1/1 x 1/2 = 1/2. **Answer: Maya needs 1/2 cup of sugar.**
9. Cross-canceling is NOT possible for 3/7 x 2/5. Check: GCF(3, 5) = 1 and GCF(2, 7) = 1. No cross-diagonal pair shares a common factor greater than 1. Solve using the standard algorithm: (3 x 2)/(7 x 5) = 6/35. GCF(6, 35) = 1; already in simplest form. **Answer: 6/35.**
10. Answers will vary. Example: 4/9 x 3/8. Cross-cancel: 4 and 8 (GCF 4) → 1/9 x 3/2. Cross-cancel: 3 and 9 (GCF 3) → 1/3 x 1/2 = 1/6. **Answer: 1/6.** (Accept any valid problem where at least one cross-diagonal pair shares a common factor, with correct cancellation and final product shown.)

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket
Solve the following problems. For Problems 1 and 2, use cross-canceling. For Problem 3, solve using whichever method you prefer.

**Problem 1:** Use cross-canceling to solve 4/5 x 5/8. Show your cancellation steps.
**Problem 2:** Use cross-canceling to solve 6/7 x 1/3. Show your cancellation steps.
**Problem 3:** Deshawn says that in 2/5 x 4/7, you can cancel the 2 and the 4 because they share a common factor of 2. Is Deshawn correct? Explain.

**Scoring:**
- 3 out of 3 correct with cancellation work and reasoning shown = Mastery demonstrated.
- 2 out of 3 correct = Approaching mastery; review the missed concept.
- 0-1 out of 3 correct = Reteach needed with guided examples.

**Exit Ticket Answers:**
1. 4/5 x 5/8: Cross-cancel 5 and 5 (GCF 5) → 4/1 x 1/8. Cross-cancel 4 and 8 (GCF 4) → 1/1 x 1/2 = 1/2. **Answer: 1/2.**
2. 6/7 x 1/3: Cross-cancel 6 and 3 (GCF 3) → 2/7 x 1/1 = 2/7. (No other cross-cancel available: GCF(1, 7) = 1.) **Answer: 2/7.**
3. Deshawn is NOT correct. The 2 is a numerator (first fraction) and the 4 is also a numerator (second fraction). You cannot cancel two numerators with each other — you can only cancel a numerator with a denominator. The correct approach: check cross-diagonal pairs. GCF(2, 7) = 1 and GCF(4, 5) = 1. No cross-canceling is possible. Multiply directly: (2 x 4)/(5 x 7) = 8/35. **Answer: 8/35. No cross-canceling is possible for this problem.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Review the concept using color-coded arrows on the board: draw an arrow from each numerator to the opposite denominator to show which pairs can be checked for common factors. Practice identifying cross-cancel opportunities on 3-4 problems before attempting to solve.
- Provide a reference card: "1. Check: first numerator and second denominator — common factor? 2. Check: second numerator and first denominator — common factor? 3. Divide each pair by its GCF. 4. Multiply the simplified numbers."
- Have the student solve 2-3 problems both ways (with and without cross-canceling) to see that the answers match. This builds trust in the technique.
- If the error is canceling two numerators or two denominators, use a concrete analogy: "The numerators are on the same team (top). The denominators are on the same team (bottom). You can only trade between teams — one from the top, one from the bottom."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may serve as peer tutors, explaining cross-canceling to classmates who are still developing the skill.

## Extension

- **Speed Round — Cancel vs. No Cancel:** Give students 6 problems and a 3-minute timer. For each problem, they decide: "Is cross-canceling possible? If yes, use it. If no, multiply directly." The goal is to build fluency in recognizing when the shortcut applies. Sample set: (a) 2/3 x 3/4, (b) 1/7 x 3/5, (c) 5/9 x 3/10, (d) 4/11 x 2/3, (e) 8/9 x 3/4, (f) 7/10 x 5/14.
- **Triple Fraction Challenge (Preview):** "Can cross-canceling work with three fractions? Try: 2/3 x 3/4 x 4/5. Cancel as many cross-diagonal pairs as you can, then multiply." (Answer: after canceling, 1/1 x 1/1 x 2/5 = 2/5. Direct multiplication: (2 x 3 x 4)/(3 x 4 x 5) = 24/60 = 2/5.) This previews future work with multiple fractions.
- **Error Analysis Worksheet:** Give students 5 solved problems, some with correct cross-canceling and some with mistakes (e.g., canceling two numerators, using the wrong GCF, or canceling within the same fraction). Students must identify and correct each error.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Canceling two numerators or two denominators.** This is the most critical error in this lesson. For example, in 3/8 x 4/9, a student cancels the 3 and 4 (both numerators) to get 1/8 x 1/9. Reinforce the rule: "One from the top, one from the bottom — always." Use the color-coded arrow method on the board to make the cross-diagonal pairing visual.
- **Canceling within the same fraction.** A student may see 4/8 and simplify it to 1/2 before multiplying. While simplifying 4/8 to 1/2 does produce a correct result, it is a different technique (simplifying a single fraction) rather than cross-canceling. It is not wrong, but clarify the distinction so students understand both strategies.
- **Using the wrong common factor.** A student cancels 6 and 9 by dividing by 2 instead of 3, getting 3/9 and 9/9 instead of 2/3 and 3/3. Encourage students to find the GCF, not just any factor. If they cancel by a smaller factor, they may need to cancel again.
- **Canceling by a factor that is not actually shared.** A student sees 3/8 x 2/5 and tries to cancel 3 and 5 (no common factor) or 2 and 8 (valid — GCF is 2). Remind students: "Check that both numbers actually divide by the factor you are using."
- **Believing cross-canceling is required.** Some students may feel they MUST cross-cancel and become stuck when no common factors exist. Emphasize: "Cross-canceling is optional. If you do not see common factors, just multiply and simplify at the end. Both approaches are correct."

### Pacing Advice
- The Warm-Up is crucial for motivation — students need to experience the pain of simplifying large numbers (like 15/60) before they appreciate the cross-canceling shortcut. Do not skip it.
- Step 3 (the cross-diagonal rule) deserves careful attention. Spend 2-3 minutes on this, including the wrong-example demonstration. The most persistent errors in this lesson come from canceling in the wrong direction.
- Part B of Independent Practice (side-by-side comparison) is the most valuable exercise for building understanding. If time is short, assign Part B and Part C, and reduce Part A to just 2 problems.
- If students master cross-canceling quickly, the Triple Fraction Challenge in the Extension is an excellent stretch that previews work with multiple fractions and reinforces the logic of why cross-canceling works.
- This lesson pairs well with a brief review at the start of the next lesson, since cross-canceling is a skill that improves with spaced practice.
