# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 9

## Topic
Scaling and Comparison: Is the Product Bigger or Smaller?

## Learning Objectives
- Students will predict whether a product will be greater than, less than, or equal to a given factor based on the size of the other factor relative to 1.
- Students will explain why multiplying by a fraction less than 1 produces a product less than the other factor (the product is a "part of" the factor).
- Students will explain why multiplying by a fraction equal to 1 (such as 3/3 or 5/5) leaves the other factor unchanged.
- Students will explain why multiplying by a fraction greater than 1 (improper fractions and mixed numbers) produces a product greater than the other factor.
- Students will compare expressions using >, <, or = without computing the exact products.
- Students will use number line reasoning to visualize how scaling stretches or shrinks a quantity.

## Prerequisites
- Fluency multiplying fractions by whole numbers (Lessons 1-2), fractions by fractions (Lessons 3-5), and mixed numbers (Lessons 7-8).
- Understanding of improper fractions and their relationship to 1 (e.g., 7/4 > 1 because 7 > 4).
- Ability to determine whether a fraction is less than, equal to, or greater than 1 by comparing its numerator and denominator.
- Familiarity with number lines and plotting fractions on them (Grade 3-4 review).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Number line handouts (0 to 30, marked in whole numbers, with space to annotate jumps).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.
- Colored markers or pencils (optional, for number line annotations).

**Standards:** 5.NF.5 — Interpret multiplication as scaling (resizing) by comparing the size of a product to the size of one factor on the basis of the size of the other factor, without performing the indicated multiplication. 5.NF.5a — Comparing the size of a product to the size of one factor on the basis of the size of the other factor, without performing the indicated multiplication. 5.NF.5b — Explaining why multiplying a given number by a fraction greater than 1 results in a product greater than the given number; explaining why multiplying a given number by a fraction less than 1 results in a product smaller than the given number; and relating the principle of fraction equivalence a/b = (n x a)/(n x b) to the effect of multiplying a/b by 1.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Build intuition for the idea that multiplying does not always "make things bigger" — it depends on what you multiply by.

1. Write three expressions on the board, side by side:
   - **A:** 2 x 12
   - **B:** 1/2 x 12
   - **C:** 1 x 12
2. Ask: "Without computing, which expression gives the largest answer? The smallest?" Give students 30 seconds to think, then take a quick poll by show of hands.
3. Compute together: A = 24, B = 6, C = 12.
4. Ask: "What happened? Multiplying 12 by 2 made it bigger. Multiplying 12 by 1 kept it the same. Multiplying 12 by 1/2 made it smaller. Why?"
5. Pause for student responses. Guide toward: "It depends on whether we multiply by something greater than 1, equal to 1, or less than 1."
6. Say: "Today we are going to explore this pattern deeply. By the end of the lesson, you will be able to predict whether a product is bigger or smaller WITHOUT computing."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Scaling** — resizing a quantity by multiplying it by a factor; the factor determines whether the quantity grows, shrinks, or stays the same.
- **Scale factor** — the number you multiply by; a scale factor greater than 1 enlarges, a scale factor equal to 1 preserves, and a scale factor less than 1 reduces.
- **Fraction less than 1** — a fraction whose numerator is less than its denominator (e.g., 3/4, 5/8, 1/6). Also called a proper fraction.
- **Fraction equal to 1** — a fraction whose numerator equals its denominator (e.g., 3/3, 5/5, 8/8). These are equivalent forms of 1.
- **Fraction greater than 1** — a fraction whose numerator is greater than its denominator (e.g., 5/4, 7/3). Also called an improper fraction; can also be written as a mixed number greater than 1.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Establish the three cases with a number line.**

Draw a number line from 0 to 30 on the board. Mark the point 24 clearly.

Say: "Let's think about 24 as our starting quantity. I am going to multiply 24 by different fractions, and we are going to see what happens."

Write: **3/4 x 24**. Ask: "Is 3/4 less than 1, equal to 1, or greater than 1?" (Less than 1.)

Compute: 3/4 x 24 = 72/4 = 18. Plot 18 on the number line. Draw an arrow from 24 to 18 and label it "shrinks."

Write: **4/4 x 24**. Ask: "Is 4/4 less than 1, equal to 1, or greater than 1?" (Equal to 1.)

Compute: 4/4 x 24 = 96/4 = 24. The point stays at 24. Write "stays the same."

Write: **5/4 x 24**. Ask: "Is 5/4 less than 1, equal to 1, or greater than 1?" (Greater than 1.)

Compute: 5/4 x 24 = 120/4 = 30. Plot 30 on the number line. Draw an arrow from 24 to 30 and label it "grows."

Circle the three results: 18, 24, 30. Say: "The starting number was always 24. The only thing that changed was the fraction we multiplied by. When the fraction was less than 1, the product was less than 24. When it was equal to 1, the product equaled 24. When it was greater than 1, the product was greater than 24."

**Step 2 — State the general rule.**

Write on the board:

> **Scaling Rule:**
> - If the scale factor < 1, then the product < the other factor.
> - If the scale factor = 1, then the product = the other factor.
> - If the scale factor > 1, then the product > the other factor.

Say: "This is the key insight of today's lesson. You do not need to compute to know whether the answer will be bigger or smaller — you just need to compare the scale factor to 1."

**Step 3 — Recognize fractions equal to 1.**

Write on the board: 3/3, 5/5, 8/8, 12/12.

Ask: "Are these fractions less than, equal to, or greater than 1?" (All equal to 1.)

Say: "Multiplying by any of these is the same as multiplying by 1. The number does not change. For example: 3/3 x 15 = 45/3 = 15. This connects to equivalent fractions — when we multiply a fraction by 3/3, we are not changing its value, we are just writing it in an equivalent form."

Write: 2/5 x 3/3 = 6/15. "2/5 and 6/15 are equivalent fractions. Multiplying by 3/3 simply renamed the fraction."

**Step 4 — Practice the reasoning without computing.**

Write on the board: "Without computing, decide: Is 5/6 x 24 more or less than 24?"

Think-aloud: "5/6 — is the numerator less than, equal to, or greater than the denominator? 5 < 6, so 5/6 < 1. Since we are multiplying 24 by something less than 1, the product must be less than 24."

Write: **5/6 x 24 < 24.** "I did not need to compute 5/6 x 24 to know this."

Now write: "Is 7/5 x 10 more or less than 10?"

Think-aloud: "7/5 — numerator 7 is greater than denominator 5, so 7/5 > 1. The product must be greater than 10."

Write: **7/5 x 10 > 10.**

**Step 5 — Comparing two expressions without computing.**

Write on the board: "Without computing, fill in >, <, or =."

**5/8 x 20 ___ 20**

Ask students: "5/8 < 1, so 5/8 x 20 < 20." Write <.

**9/9 x 7 ___ 7**

Ask students: "9/9 = 1, so 9/9 x 7 = 7." Write =.

**4/3 x 15 ___ 15**

Ask students: "4/3 > 1, so 4/3 x 15 > 15." Write >.

**Step 6 — Comparing two products to each other.**

Write on the board: "Without computing, which is greater: 3/4 x 16 or 5/4 x 16?"

Think-aloud: "Both expressions multiply 16 by something. 3/4 < 1 and 5/4 > 1. So 3/4 x 16 < 16 and 5/4 x 16 > 16. That means 3/4 x 16 < 5/4 x 16."

Write: **3/4 x 16 < 5/4 x 16.**

Say: "When two expressions share the same factor, the one with the larger scale factor gives the larger product. You can compare the scale factors directly."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Predicting without computing: 2/3 x 18**
1. Compare 2/3 to 1: numerator 2 < denominator 3, so 2/3 < 1.
2. Since the scale factor is less than 1, the product is less than 18.
3. Verification: 2/3 x 18 = 36/3 = 12. Indeed 12 < 18.
**Prediction: 2/3 x 18 < 18. Confirmed.**

**Example B — Predicting without computing: 7/7 x 35**
1. Compare 7/7 to 1: numerator 7 = denominator 7, so 7/7 = 1.
2. Since the scale factor equals 1, the product equals 35.
3. Verification: 7/7 x 35 = 245/7 = 35. Correct.
**Prediction: 7/7 x 35 = 35. Confirmed.**

**Example C — Predicting without computing: 3/2 x 14**
1. Compare 3/2 to 1: numerator 3 > denominator 2, so 3/2 > 1.
2. Since the scale factor is greater than 1, the product is greater than 14.
3. Verification: 3/2 x 14 = 42/2 = 21. Indeed 21 > 14.
**Prediction: 3/2 x 14 > 14. Confirmed.**

**Example D — Comparing two expressions: 4/5 x 30 ___ 6/5 x 30**
1. Both expressions multiply 30 by a fraction.
2. 4/5 < 1 (since 4 < 5), so 4/5 x 30 < 30.
3. 6/5 > 1 (since 6 > 5), so 6/5 x 30 > 30.
4. Therefore 4/5 x 30 < 6/5 x 30.
5. Verification: 4/5 x 30 = 24. 6/5 x 30 = 36. Indeed 24 < 36.
**Answer: 4/5 x 30 < 6/5 x 30. Confirmed.**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Students attempt each problem first, then discuss.

1. Without computing, decide: Is 3/8 x 40 greater than, less than, or equal to 40?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Compare the scale factor 3/8 to 1. Is 3 less than, equal to, or greater than 8? What does that tell you about the product?"

2. Without computing, decide: Is 6/6 x 25 greater than, less than, or equal to 25?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What kind of fraction is 6/6? What happens when you multiply any number by 1?"

3. Without computing, decide: Is 9/4 x 12 greater than, less than, or equal to 12?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Is 9/4 greater than 1? How do you know? What does that tell you about the product?"

4. Fill in >, <, or = without computing: 2/5 x 35 ___ 35.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Think about the scale factor 2/5. Is it less than 1? If so, what symbol goes in the blank?"

5. Fill in >, <, or = without computing: 7/6 x 18 ___ 5/6 x 18.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Both expressions multiply 18 by a fraction. Compare 7/6 and 5/6 — which is the larger scale factor? The larger scale factor gives the larger product."

6. On a number line from 0 to 20, show where 15 is located. Then predict (without computing) approximately where 3/5 x 15 and 6/5 x 15 would land. Explain your reasoning.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "3/5 is less than 1, so 3/5 x 15 should be somewhere to the left of 15. 6/5 is greater than 1, so 6/5 x 15 should be somewhere to the right of 15. Mark both with arrows."

> **Facilitator Tip:** Some students may want to compute to "check." Encourage this after they make their prediction — but emphasize that the goal of this lesson is to reason without computing. Say: "First predict, then verify. The prediction is the important part." If students can predict correctly but then make a computation error, they still demonstrate understanding of the standard. The conceptual reasoning is the priority.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — Predict Without Computing (On-Level)
For each problem, write >, <, or = in the blank. Do NOT compute the product. Explain your reasoning in one sentence.

1. 4/7 x 21 ___ 21
2. 8/8 x 50 ___ 50
3. 11/10 x 30 ___ 30
4. 1/3 x 27 ___ 27
5. 2 1/4 x 16 ___ 16

### Part B — Compare Two Expressions (Stretch)
Fill in >, <, or = without computing either product. Explain your reasoning.

6. 3/5 x 40 ___ 7/5 x 40
7. 5/3 x 12 ___ 2/3 x 12
8. 4/4 x 18 ___ 6/6 x 18
9. 3/8 x 24 ___ 3/8 x 32

### Part C — Explanation and Number Line (Challenge)
10. Marcus says: "When you multiply, the answer is always bigger." Is Marcus correct? Write a clear explanation using at least two specific examples — one where multiplication makes the number bigger and one where it makes the number smaller. Use the word "scaling" in your explanation.

11. Draw a number line from 0 to 20. Mark the point 12 on the number line. Without computing, place approximate points for each product and label them:
    - A: 1/2 x 12
    - B: 3/3 x 12
    - C: 5/4 x 12
    After placing your points, compute each product to check your placement.

12. A baker has 10 cups of sugar. She uses 3/4 of the sugar for one recipe and then uses 5/4 of the original amount for a second (larger) recipe. Without computing, explain which recipe uses more sugar than the original 10 cups and which uses less. Then compute both amounts to verify.

13. Without computing, place the correct symbol (>, <, or =) and explain your reasoning:
    - 2/3 x 5/8 ___ 5/8
    - 8/5 x 2/3 ___ 2/3

### Answer Key

**Part A**
1. 4/7 x 21 **<** 21. Reasoning: 4/7 < 1 (since 4 < 7), so the product is less than 21.
2. 8/8 x 50 **=** 50. Reasoning: 8/8 = 1, so the product equals 50.
3. 11/10 x 30 **>** 30. Reasoning: 11/10 > 1 (since 11 > 10), so the product is greater than 30.
4. 1/3 x 27 **<** 27. Reasoning: 1/3 < 1 (since 1 < 3), so the product is less than 27.
5. 2 1/4 x 16 **>** 16. Reasoning: 2 1/4 > 1 (any mixed number with a whole part of 1 or more is greater than 1), so the product is greater than 16.

**Part B**
6. 3/5 x 40 **<** 7/5 x 40. Reasoning: Both expressions share the factor 40. Since 3/5 < 7/5, the product 3/5 x 40 is smaller. (Also: 3/5 < 1 makes the product less than 40, while 7/5 > 1 makes the product greater than 40.)
7. 5/3 x 12 **>** 2/3 x 12. Reasoning: Both share the factor 12. Since 5/3 > 2/3, the product 5/3 x 12 is larger. (Also: 5/3 > 1 enlarges 12, while 2/3 < 1 shrinks 12.)
8. 4/4 x 18 **=** 6/6 x 18. Reasoning: 4/4 = 1 and 6/6 = 1. Both are multiplying 18 by 1, so both products equal 18.
9. 3/8 x 24 **<** 3/8 x 32. Reasoning: Both expressions use the same scale factor 3/8. Since 24 < 32, taking 3/8 of the smaller number gives a smaller result. (Verification: 3/8 x 24 = 9 and 3/8 x 32 = 12. Indeed 9 < 12.)

**Part C**
10. Marcus is incorrect. Multiplying does not always make a number bigger — it depends on the scale factor. For example, 3 x 8 = 24, which is bigger than 8 — this is scaling UP because 3 > 1. But 1/2 x 8 = 4, which is smaller than 8 — this is scaling DOWN because 1/2 < 1. When you multiply by a fraction less than 1, you are taking a part of the number, so the product shrinks. When you multiply by a number greater than 1, you are scaling the number up, so the product grows. Multiplying by exactly 1 keeps the number the same. (Accept any two valid examples and a clear explanation using "scaling.")

11. Number line placement:
    - A: 1/2 x 12 — should be placed at 6 (to the left of 12, about halfway to 0). Computation: 1/2 x 12 = 6. Correct.
    - B: 3/3 x 12 — should be placed at 12 (same spot). Computation: 3/3 x 12 = 12. Correct.
    - C: 5/4 x 12 — should be placed at 15 (to the right of 12). Computation: 5/4 x 12 = 60/4 = 15. Correct.

12. The first recipe uses 3/4 of 10 cups. Since 3/4 < 1, she uses less than 10 cups — the first recipe uses less sugar than the original amount. The second recipe uses 5/4 of 10 cups. Since 5/4 > 1, she uses more than 10 cups — the second recipe uses more sugar than the original amount. Verification: 3/4 x 10 = 30/4 = 7 1/2 cups (less than 10). 5/4 x 10 = 50/4 = 12 1/2 cups (more than 10). Confirmed.

13. First comparison: 2/3 x 5/8 **<** 5/8. Reasoning: The scale factor is 2/3, which is less than 1 (since 2 < 3). Multiplying 5/8 by a number less than 1 produces a product less than 5/8. (Verification: 2/3 x 5/8 = 10/24 = 5/12. Is 5/12 < 5/8? Yes — 5/12 = 10/24 and 5/8 = 15/24, and 10/24 < 15/24.)

    Second comparison: 8/5 x 2/3 **>** 2/3. Reasoning: The scale factor is 8/5, which is greater than 1 (since 8 > 5). Multiplying 2/3 by a number greater than 1 produces a product greater than 2/3. (Verification: 8/5 x 2/3 = 16/15. Is 16/15 > 2/3? Yes — 16/15 = 32/30 and 2/3 = 20/30, and 32/30 > 20/30.)

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket
Answer the following three problems. You do NOT need to compute — use reasoning about scale factors.

**Problem 1:** Fill in >, <, or =: 5/9 x 36 ___ 36. Explain your reasoning in one sentence.

**Problem 2:** Fill in >, <, or =: 7/4 x 20 ___ 3/4 x 20. Explain your reasoning in one sentence.

**Problem 3:** Mia has 16 feet of rope. She cuts a piece that is 2/5 of the total length. Without computing, is the piece she cut longer or shorter than 16 feet? How do you know?

**Scoring:**
- 3 out of 3 correct with valid reasoning = Mastery demonstrated.
- 2 out of 3 correct = Solid understanding; review the missed item.
- 1 out of 3 correct or correct answers but no reasoning = Partial understanding; reteach the scaling rule.
- 0 out of 3 correct = Reteach needed.

**Exit Ticket Answers:**
1. 5/9 x 36 **<** 36. Reasoning: 5/9 < 1 because 5 < 9, so the product is less than 36.
2. 7/4 x 20 **>** 3/4 x 20. Reasoning: Both expressions multiply 20 by a fraction. Since 7/4 > 3/4, the product 7/4 x 20 is greater. (Alternatively: 7/4 > 1 enlarges 20 while 3/4 < 1 shrinks 20, so the first product is larger.)
3. The piece is shorter than 16 feet. Since 2/5 < 1 (the numerator 2 is less than the denominator 5), multiplying 16 by 2/5 gives a product less than 16.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the number line model from Step 1 of Direct Instruction. Use a concrete example: draw a number line, mark 20, then physically show where 1/2 x 20, 1 x 20, and 3/2 x 20 land. Let the student point to where the product goes.
- Provide a reference card with the three rules: "Less than 1 = shrinks. Equal to 1 = stays the same. Greater than 1 = grows." Have students tape this to their desk.
- Use a sorting activity: give the student 10 index cards with expressions like "2/3 x 15," "5/5 x 15," "7/4 x 15." Have them sort the cards into three piles: "Product < 15," "Product = 15," and "Product > 15" by examining only the scale factor.
- Pair the student with a peer tutor to practice 5 additional comparison problems before the unit assessment.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may create a "Scaling Quiz" with 5 comparison problems for a classmate, including an answer key.

## Extension

- **Scaling Exploration with Patterns:** Choose a starting number (e.g., 20). Multiply it by 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, 4/4, 5/4, 6/4, 7/4, and 8/4. Record all products. Graph the results with the fraction on the x-axis and the product on the y-axis. What pattern do you notice? Where does the product equal the starting number? Where does the product cross from "less than" to "greater than"?
- **Real-World Scaling:** Research three situations where scaling by a fraction less than 1 happens in real life (e.g., a map scale of 1/1000, a recipe cut in half, a model car that is 1/24 the size of the real car). Write one paragraph explaining each example and how multiplication by a fraction less than 1 is used.
- **Challenge — Double Scaling:** If you multiply 48 by 3/4 and then multiply the result by 4/3, what do you get? Try it. Now multiply 48 by 2/5 and then by 5/2. What happens? Can you explain why multiplying by a fraction and then by its reciprocal always brings you back to the original number?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Assuming multiplication always makes numbers bigger.** This is the central misconception this lesson addresses. Students who have years of whole-number experience may deeply believe "multiply = bigger." Use the number line visualization and concrete examples (3/4 x 24 = 18) to challenge this belief. Repetition across multiple examples is key.
- **Confusing the roles of numerator and denominator when determining if a fraction is less than, equal to, or greater than 1.** Some students may think 3/5 > 1 because "3 and 5 are both bigger than 1." Redirect: "Compare the numerator to the denominator. If the top is less than the bottom, the fraction is less than 1."
- **Not recognizing fractions equal to 1 in disguised forms.** Students may not immediately see that 8/8 or 12/12 equal 1. Practice several examples and connect to the concept of equivalent fractions: "Any number divided by itself is 1."
- **Difficulty with mixed numbers as scale factors.** When the scale factor is a mixed number like 2 1/4, some students may not realize it is greater than 1. Remind them: "Any mixed number with a whole part of 1 or more is always greater than 1."
- **Trying to compare expressions that do not share a common factor.** Problem 9 in Part B (3/8 x 24 vs. 3/8 x 32) requires comparing the other factor when the scale factor is the same. Students may not recognize this reasoning pattern. Prompt: "The scale factor is the same in both — so which starting number is bigger?"

### Pacing Advice
- This lesson is conceptually rich but computationally light. The emphasis should be on discussion, reasoning, and explanation rather than on performing calculations. If students are eager to compute, let them verify after predicting, but keep the focus on the "why."
- The number line in Step 1 is the single most important visual in this lesson. Take time to draw it carefully and annotate it clearly. If possible, display it for the remainder of the lesson so students can refer back to it.
- If students grasp the three cases quickly (less than 1, equal to 1, greater than 1), move through the Guided Practice at a conversational pace and allocate more time to Part C of Independent Practice, which asks for deeper explanations.
- For students who struggle, spend extra time on Guided Practice problems 1-4 (single-expression comparisons) before introducing two-expression comparisons (problems 5-6). The two-expression problems require a second layer of reasoning that some students may need scaffolding for.
- The Extension activities are intentionally varied: the graphing activity suits visual/analytical learners, the real-world activity suits verbal/contextual learners, and the reciprocal challenge suits students who enjoy algebraic patterns.
