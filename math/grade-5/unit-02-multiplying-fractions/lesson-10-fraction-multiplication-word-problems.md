# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 10

## Topic
Word Problems with Fraction Multiplication

## Learning Objectives
- Students will identify key phrases in word problems that signal multiplication: "of," "each," "times as much," "per," and "for every."
- Students will draw diagrams (bar models, area models, or number lines) to represent word problems before solving them.
- Students will solve one-step word problems involving multiplication of fractions and mixed numbers in real-world contexts (recipes, area, distance, time, money).
- Students will solve multi-step word problems that combine fraction multiplication with addition or subtraction.
- Students will express answers with appropriate labels and check reasonableness using estimation.

## Prerequisites
- Fluency multiplying a fraction by a whole number (Lessons 1-2), a fraction by a fraction (Lessons 3-5), and mixed numbers (Lessons 7-8).
- Ability to simplify fractions and convert between improper fractions and mixed numbers.
- Understanding of scaling: when multiplying by a fraction less than 1, the product is smaller than the other factor (Lesson 9).
- Familiarity with area as length x width (Lesson 4 area model work).
- Experience reading and interpreting word problems from prior units and grades.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.
- Blank paper for student-drawn diagrams.
- Colored pencils (optional, for labeling diagrams).

**Standards:** 5.NF.6 — Solve real world problems involving multiplication of fractions and mixed numbers, e.g., by using visual fraction models or equations to represent the problem.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of identifying operations in word problems and introduce the idea that "of" often signals multiplication.

1. Write two sentences on the board:
   - **A:** "Sam ate 3/4 of a pizza."
   - **B:** "Sam ate 3/4 of 2 pizzas."
2. Ask: "In sentence A, what does '3/4 of a pizza' mean?" (He ate 3 out of 4 equal slices of one pizza.)
3. Ask: "In sentence B, what does '3/4 of 2 pizzas' mean? Is it the same as sentence A?" (No — he ate 3/4 of the total amount, which is 2 pizzas. So he ate 3/4 x 2 = 6/4 = 1 1/2 pizzas.)
4. Say: "Notice the word 'of.' In math, 'of' almost always means multiply. '3/4 of 2' means 3/4 x 2."
5. Write a third sentence: **C:** "A recipe uses 2/3 cup of sugar. Maria makes 1 1/2 times the recipe."
6. Ask: "What operation do we need?" (Multiplication: 2/3 x 1 1/2 or 1 1/2 x 2/3.)
7. Say: "Today we are going to practice finding the multiplication hidden inside word problems, drawing a picture to understand the situation, and solving carefully."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **"Of" as multiplication** — in fraction word problems, the word "of" typically signals multiplication (e.g., "1/3 of 12" means 1/3 x 12).
- **"Times as much/many"** — indicates multiplication (e.g., "3 times as many" means multiply by 3; "2/3 times as much" means multiply by 2/3).
- **Bar model** — a rectangular diagram divided into parts to represent fractional relationships; used to visualize "part of a whole" problems.
- **Multi-step problem** — a word problem that requires more than one operation (e.g., multiply and then add) to reach the final answer.
- **Reasonableness** — checking whether an answer makes sense, often by estimating before or after computing.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Teach a three-step approach for fraction word problems.**

Write on the board:

> **Problem-Solving Steps:**
> 1. **Read and Identify:** Read the problem. Circle the key numbers. Underline the question. Identify key phrases ("of," "each," "times as much").
> 2. **Draw a Diagram:** Sketch a bar model, area model, or number line to represent the situation.
> 3. **Write an Equation and Solve:** Translate the diagram into a multiplication equation. Compute, simplify, and label your answer.

Say: "We are going to use these three steps for every word problem today. The diagram step is the most important — it helps you understand what the problem is really asking before you start computing."

**Step 2 — Model a one-step problem: recipe context.**

Write on the board: "A cookie recipe calls for 3/4 cup of butter. Deshawn is making 2/3 of the recipe. How much butter does he need?"

**Read and Identify:** Circle 3/4 and 2/3. Underline "How much butter does he need?" Key phrase: "2/3 of the recipe" — "of" signals multiplication.

**Draw a Diagram:** Draw a bar labeled "3/4 cup" divided into 3 equal parts. Shade 2 of the 3 parts. Say: "Deshawn is using 2 out of every 3 parts of the recipe. So we need 2/3 of 3/4."

**Write and Solve:** 2/3 x 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2. Deshawn needs 1/2 cup of butter.

**Reasonableness check:** "Deshawn is making less than the full recipe (2/3 < 1), so he should need less than 3/4 cup. Is 1/2 less than 3/4? Yes. Reasonable."

Write: **Answer: Deshawn needs 1/2 cup of butter.**

**Step 3 — Model a one-step problem: area context.**

Write on the board: "A rectangular garden plot is 4 1/2 meters long and 2/3 of a meter wide. What is the area of the garden?"

**Read and Identify:** Circle 4 1/2 and 2/3. Underline "What is the area?" Key phrase: area = length x width.

**Draw a Diagram:** Sketch a rectangle labeled 4 1/2 m along the top and 2/3 m along the side.

**Write and Solve:** 4 1/2 x 2/3. Convert: 4 1/2 = 9/2. Multiply: 9/2 x 2/3 = 18/6 = 3. The area is 3 square meters.

**Reasonableness check:** "4 1/2 x 2/3 — we are multiplying about 4.5 by about 0.67. That should give roughly 3. The answer checks out."

Write: **Answer: The area of the garden is 3 square meters.**

**Step 4 — Model a multi-step problem.**

Write on the board: "Jada has 3/4 of a pound of trail mix. She eats 1/3 of it on Monday and another 1/4 of the original amount on Tuesday. How much trail mix does she have left?"

**Read and Identify:** Circle 3/4, 1/3, and 1/4. Underline "How much does she have left?" This is multi-step: we need to find how much she ate on each day, add those amounts, then subtract from the total.

**Draw a Diagram:** Draw a bar representing 3/4 pound. Divide it to show 1/3 of it eaten on Monday and 1/4 of it eaten on Tuesday. The remaining portion is what is left.

**Write and Solve:**

Monday: 1/3 x 3/4 = 3/12 = 1/4 pound.

Tuesday: 1/4 x 3/4 = 3/16 pound.

Total eaten: 1/4 + 3/16. Find a common denominator: 1/4 = 4/16. So 4/16 + 3/16 = 7/16 pound.

Trail mix left: 3/4 - 7/16. Convert: 3/4 = 12/16. So 12/16 - 7/16 = 5/16 pound.

**Reasonableness check:** "She started with 3/4 pound and ate some on two days, so the remainder should be less than 3/4. 5/16 is less than 3/4 (which is 12/16). She ate about 1/3 + 1/4 = about 7/12 of the total, leaving roughly 5/12. Our answer of 5/16 is in the right ballpark. Reasonable."

Write: **Answer: Jada has 5/16 of a pound of trail mix left.**

**Step 5 — Emphasize diagrams as a thinking tool.**

Say: "Notice that in every problem, the diagram helped us see what to do. For the recipe problem, the bar model showed us taking a part of a part. For the area problem, the rectangle showed us length times width. For the multi-step problem, the bar model helped us see three pieces: Monday's portion, Tuesday's portion, and the leftover. Always draw before you compute."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Recipe (one-step)**
Problem: A recipe calls for 3/4 cup of butter. Deshawn is making 2/3 of the recipe. How much butter does he need?
1. Key phrase: "2/3 of the recipe" means multiply by 2/3.
2. Equation: 2/3 x 3/4.
3. Compute: 2/3 x 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2.
4. Check: 2/3 < 1, so the result should be less than 3/4. 1/2 < 3/4. Reasonable.
**Answer: 1/2 cup of butter.**

**Example B — Area (one-step)**
Problem: A rectangular garden is 4 1/2 m long and 2/3 m wide. What is the area?
1. Key phrase: area = length x width.
2. Equation: 4 1/2 x 2/3 = 9/2 x 2/3.
3. Compute: 9/2 x 2/3 = 18/6 = 3.
4. Check: About 4.5 x 0.67 is about 3. Reasonable.
**Answer: 3 square meters.**

**Example C — Multi-step**
Problem: Jada has 3/4 lb of trail mix. She eats 1/3 of it Monday and 1/4 of the original amount Tuesday. How much is left?
1. Monday: 1/3 x 3/4 = 3/12 = 1/4 lb.
2. Tuesday: 1/4 x 3/4 = 3/16 lb.
3. Total eaten: 1/4 + 3/16 = 4/16 + 3/16 = 7/16 lb.
4. Remaining: 3/4 - 7/16 = 12/16 - 7/16 = 5/16 lb.
5. Check: remainder < 3/4. 5/16 < 12/16. Reasonable.
**Answer: 5/16 of a pound of trail mix left.**

**Example D — Distance (one-step)**
Problem: Maya walks 3/5 of a mile to school each way. She walks to school and back home. How far does she walk in total?
1. Key phrase: "each way" — she walks the distance twice.
2. Equation: 2 x 3/5 = 6/5 = 1 1/5.
3. Check: She walks less than 1 mile each way, so the round trip should be less than 2 miles. 1 1/5 < 2. Reasonable.
**Answer: Maya walks 1 1/5 miles in total.**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Students attempt each problem first (including drawing a diagram), then discuss.

1. A recipe calls for 2/3 cup of milk. Tomas is making 3/4 of the recipe. How much milk does he need?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What key phrase tells you to multiply? Draw a bar showing 2/3 cup. Now shade 3/4 of that bar. Set up the equation and solve."

2. A ribbon is 5/6 of a yard long. Keisha uses 1/2 of the ribbon for a craft project. How much ribbon does she use?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What does '1/2 of' tell you? Draw the ribbon as a bar and cut it in half. Write the equation."

3. A park is 1 3/4 miles long and 2/5 of a mile wide. What is the area of the park in square miles?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "This is an area problem — length times width. Convert the mixed number first, then multiply."

4. Ms. Rivera has 24 students. On Monday, 3/4 of them turned in homework. On Tuesday, 5/6 of them turned in homework. How many MORE students turned in homework on Tuesday than on Monday?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "This is multi-step. First find 3/4 of 24, then find 5/6 of 24, then subtract. Draw a diagram showing both groups."

5. A painter can paint 2 1/2 walls per hour. She works for 3/4 of an hour. How many walls can she paint?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What key phrase signals multiplication? Convert 2 1/2 to an improper fraction and multiply by 3/4."

> **Facilitator Tip:** When students draw diagrams, do not insist on one specific type. A bar model, an area model, a number line, or even a simple labeled sketch all serve the purpose. The goal is that students visualize the problem before writing an equation. If a student skips the diagram, gently redirect: "Before you start computing, can you draw a quick picture so I can see what the problem looks like?" Over time, many students will internalize the visual and be able to reason without drawing, but at this stage, the practice of drawing is building essential problem-solving habits.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — One-Step Word Problems (On-Level)
For each problem, draw a diagram, write an equation, and solve. Show all work and label your answer.

1. A smoothie recipe uses 3/4 cup of yogurt. Anaya is making 1/3 of the recipe for a small serving. How much yogurt does she need?

2. Marcus jogged 4/5 of a mile on Saturday. On Sunday, he jogged 3/4 of the distance he covered on Saturday. How far did he jog on Sunday?

3. A rectangular sticker is 2 1/2 inches long and 1 1/4 inches wide. What is the area of the sticker?

4. A bag of rice weighs 3/4 of a pound. Mr. Chen buys 6 bags. What is the total weight of the rice?

### Part B — Multi-Step Word Problems (Stretch)
5. Emma had 2/3 of a gallon of lemonade. She drank 1/4 of it and gave 1/3 of the original amount to her brother. How much lemonade does she have left?

6. A hiking trail is 3 1/2 miles long. On the first day, Kayla hiked 2/5 of the trail. On the second day, she hiked 1/4 of the trail. How many miles does she still need to hike to finish the trail?

7. Luis earns $12 per hour. On Monday, he worked 3/4 of an hour. On Wednesday, he worked 1 1/2 hours. How much did he earn in total for both days?

8. A carpenter has a board that is 5 1/4 feet long. She cuts off a piece that is 2/3 of the total length. How long is the piece she cut? How long is the remaining piece?

### Part C — Challenge
9. A recipe for a large cake uses 2 1/3 cups of flour and 1 1/4 cups of sugar. Priya wants to make 3/4 of the recipe. How much flour and how much sugar does she need? What is the total amount of flour and sugar combined?

10. A rectangular field is 6 2/3 meters long and 3 3/4 meters wide. A gardener wants to plant flowers in 1/2 of the field and vegetables in 1/3 of the field. What is the area planted with flowers? What is the area planted with vegetables? What area is left unplanted?

### Answer Key

**Part A**
1. 1/3 x 3/4 = 3/12 = 1/4. **Answer: Anaya needs 1/4 cup of yogurt.**

2. 3/4 x 4/5 = 12/20 = 3/5. **Answer: Marcus jogged 3/5 of a mile on Sunday.**

3. 2 1/2 x 1 1/4. Convert: 5/2 x 5/4 = 25/8 = 3 1/8. **Answer: The area of the sticker is 3 1/8 square inches.**

4. 6 x 3/4 = 18/4 = 9/2 = 4 1/2. **Answer: The total weight is 4 1/2 pounds.**

**Part B**
5. Emma starts with 2/3 gallon.
   She drank: 1/4 x 2/3 = 2/12 = 1/6 gallon.
   She gave away: 1/3 x 2/3 = 2/9 gallon.
   Total removed: 1/6 + 2/9. Common denominator 18: 3/18 + 4/18 = 7/18 gallon.
   Remaining: 2/3 - 7/18 = 12/18 - 7/18 = 5/18 gallon.
   **Answer: Emma has 5/18 of a gallon of lemonade left.**

6. Trail is 3 1/2 miles = 7/2 miles.
   Day 1: 2/5 x 7/2 = 14/10 = 7/5 = 1 2/5 miles.
   Day 2: 1/4 x 7/2 = 7/8 miles.
   Total hiked: 7/5 + 7/8. Common denominator 40: 56/40 + 35/40 = 91/40 = 2 11/40 miles.
   Remaining: 7/2 - 91/40 = 140/40 - 91/40 = 49/40 = 1 9/40 miles.
   **Answer: Kayla still needs to hike 1 9/40 miles.**

7. Monday earnings: 12 x 3/4 = 36/4 = 9 dollars.
   Wednesday earnings: 12 x 1 1/2 = 12 x 3/2 = 36/2 = 18 dollars.
   Total: 9 + 18 = 27 dollars.
   **Answer: Luis earned $27 in total.**

8. Board is 5 1/4 feet = 21/4 feet.
   Piece cut: 2/3 x 21/4 = 42/12 = 7/2 = 3 1/2 feet.
   Remaining piece: 21/4 - 7/2 = 21/4 - 14/4 = 7/4 = 1 3/4 feet.
   Check: 3 1/2 + 1 3/4 = 5 1/4. Confirmed.
   **Answer: The cut piece is 3 1/2 feet long. The remaining piece is 1 3/4 feet long.**

**Part C**
9. Flour needed: 3/4 x 2 1/3 = 3/4 x 7/3 = 21/12 = 7/4 = 1 3/4 cups.
   Sugar needed: 3/4 x 1 1/4 = 3/4 x 5/4 = 15/16 cups.
   Total combined: 1 3/4 + 15/16 = 28/16 + 15/16 = 43/16 = 2 11/16 cups.
   **Answer: Priya needs 1 3/4 cups of flour and 15/16 cup of sugar. The total combined is 2 11/16 cups.**

10. Total area of the field: 6 2/3 x 3 3/4. Convert: 20/3 x 15/4. Cross-cancel: 20 and 4 share factor 4 (giving 5/3 x 15/1). Then 15 and 3 share factor 3 (giving 5/1 x 5/1). Multiply: 25/1 = 25 square meters.
    Flowers (1/2 of the field): 1/2 x 25 = 25/2 = 12 1/2 square meters.
    Vegetables (1/3 of the field): 1/3 x 25 = 25/3 = 8 1/3 square meters.
    Total planted: 12 1/2 + 8 1/3 = 25/2 + 25/3. Common denominator 6: 75/6 + 50/6 = 125/6 = 20 5/6 square meters.
    Unplanted: 25 - 20 5/6 = 25/1 - 125/6 = 150/6 - 125/6 = 25/6 = 4 1/6 square meters.
    **Answer: Flowers area = 12 1/2 sq m. Vegetables area = 8 1/3 sq m. Unplanted area = 4 1/6 sq m.**

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket
Solve the following two problems. Draw a diagram for at least one of them. Show all work.

**Problem 1:** A juice box contains 3/4 cup of juice. Mia drinks 2/3 of the juice box. How much juice does she drink?

**Problem 2:** A rectangular poster is 2 1/4 feet wide and 1 2/3 feet tall. What is the area of the poster? (Multi-step check: if the poster costs $2 per square foot, how much does the poster cost?)

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct with work shown and diagram for at least one = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct or correct answers but no diagram or work shown = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

**Exit Ticket Answers:**
1. 2/3 x 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2. **Answer: Mia drinks 1/2 cup of juice.**
2. Area: 2 1/4 x 1 2/3. Convert: 9/4 x 5/3. Cross-cancel: 9 and 3 share factor 3 (giving 3/4 x 5/1). Multiply: 15/4 = 3 3/4. **Answer: The area of the poster is 3 3/4 square feet.** Cost: 3 3/4 x 2 = 15/4 x 2 = 30/4 = 15/2 = $7.50. **Answer: The poster costs $7.50.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Identify which step is causing difficulty: is it understanding the word problem (Step 1), drawing the diagram (Step 2), or computing (Step 3)? Targeted reteaching depends on the source of the struggle.
- If the student cannot identify the operation, provide a "Key Phrase Reference Sheet" listing common phrases and their operations: "of" = multiply, "how much left" = subtract, "total" = add, "each" = multiply, "times as much" = multiply.
- If the student cannot draw a diagram, model 2-3 bar diagrams side-by-side with the student, narrating your thinking as you draw. Then have the student draw the diagram for a new problem while you watch and coach.
- If the student can set up the equation but makes computation errors, the issue is with prior lessons (Lessons 1-8). Provide targeted computation practice on the specific operation type that is causing errors.
- Pair the student with a peer tutor for 3-4 additional word problems before the unit assessment.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may write their own multi-step word problem involving fraction multiplication for a classmate to solve.

## Extension

- **Create Your Own Word Problem Book:** Write four original word problems that use fraction multiplication — one about cooking/recipes, one about distance/travel, one about area/measurement, and one about money. Each problem must require at least two steps. Include a diagram and full solution for each. Exchange books with a classmate and solve each other's problems.
- **Recipe Scaling Challenge:** Find a simple recipe (or use one provided by the facilitator). Scale the recipe to make 1/2, 3/4, 1 1/2, and 2 1/3 batches. Create a table showing the original amounts and the scaled amounts for each ingredient. Which scaling factor produced amounts that are easiest to measure in a real kitchen? Explain why.
- **Real-World Investigation:** Measure the length and width of three rectangular objects in the classroom (a book, a desk, a window) to the nearest 1/4 inch or 1/4 foot. Calculate the area of each object. Then answer: "If you wanted to cover 2/3 of each surface with paper, how much paper would you need for each?" Show your work with diagrams.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Not identifying the correct operation.** Students may add fractions instead of multiplying. For example, in "3/4 of 2/3 cup," a student may write 3/4 + 2/3 instead of 3/4 x 2/3. Redirect by asking: "Does 'of' mean 'plus' or 'times'? If you have 1/2 of 10, do you add 1/2 + 10 or multiply 1/2 x 10?"
- **Skipping the diagram.** Students may jump straight to computation and set up the wrong equation. Insist on the diagram step, especially for multi-step problems. Say: "If you cannot draw it, you probably do not fully understand what the problem is asking."
- **Forgetting to include units or labels.** Students may write "3 3/4" as the area without specifying "square feet." Require labels on every answer. A good habit: write the label as part of the equation setup (e.g., "Area = 2 1/4 ft x 1 2/3 ft = ? sq ft").
- **Misinterpreting multi-step problems.** In Problem 5 of Part B, students may compute 1/4 of 2/3 and 1/3 of 2/3, but then forget to subtract from the original. Or they may compute the fractions eaten but subtract from 1 instead of from 2/3. The diagram is the best defense against this error — if the bar model is drawn correctly, each piece of the problem is visible.
- **Computing "1/3 of the trail" as a fraction of what is left rather than a fraction of the whole.** In Problem 6, some students may compute Day 2 as 1/4 of the remaining distance after Day 1 rather than 1/4 of the whole trail. Careful reading and a diagram that shows both days as parts of the whole trail will prevent this.

### Pacing Advice
- This lesson is word-problem heavy. Students who are strong computationally but weak at reading comprehension may need extra time on the Read and Identify step. Consider reading the first two Guided Practice problems aloud as a class.
- If time is limited, prioritize Guided Practice problems 1-3 (one-step) and problems 5-6 of Independent Practice (multi-step). These cover the core of the standard.
- The diagram requirement may slow students down initially, but it pays dividends in accuracy. Do not rush past it. For students who resist drawing, emphasize: "Professional mathematicians and engineers always sketch before they solve."
- Multi-step problems (Part B and Part C) are significantly harder than one-step problems. If most of the class struggles with Part A, spend the remainder of the period on one-step problems and assign multi-step problems as a follow-up or homework activity.
- For students who finish early, the Extension activities — especially the Recipe Scaling Challenge — provide authentic, engaging practice that extends the lesson naturally into real-world application.
