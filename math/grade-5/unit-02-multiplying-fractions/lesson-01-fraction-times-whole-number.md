# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 1

## Topic
Fraction x Whole Number (Repeated Addition)

## Learning Objectives
- Students will interpret multiplication of a fraction by a whole number as repeated addition (e.g., 3 x 2/5 = 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5).
- Students will compute the product of a fraction and a whole number using the shortcut: multiply the numerator by the whole number, keep the denominator.
- Students will rewrite a whole number as a fraction over 1 (e.g., 3 = 3/1) to apply the fraction multiplication algorithm.
- Students will convert improper fraction results to mixed numbers in simplest form.

## Prerequisites
- Solid understanding of what a fraction represents (parts of a whole, points on a number line).
- Fluency with whole-number multiplication facts through 12 x 12.
- Ability to convert between improper fractions and mixed numbers (Grade 4 review).
- Experience adding fractions with like denominators (Unit 1, Lesson 1).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Fraction strips or fraction circle manipulatives (1 set per student).
- Grid paper or number line handouts (optional, for visual modeling).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NF.4 — Apply and extend previous understandings of multiplication to multiply a fraction by a whole number.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of repeated addition with whole numbers and connect it to fraction language.

1. Write on the board: "4 x 3 = ?"
2. Ask: "What does 4 x 3 mean as repeated addition?" (3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12.)
3. Now write: "4 x 1/5 = ?"
4. Ask: "If 4 x 3 means 'four groups of 3,' then what does 4 x 1/5 mean?" (Four groups of 1/5, which is 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5.)
5. Have students use fraction strips to lay out four 1/5 pieces end to end. Ask: "How many fifths do we have?" (4/5.)
6. Tell students: "Today we are going to multiply fractions by whole numbers. The idea is exactly the same as repeated addition — just with fractional groups."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Repeated addition** — expressing multiplication as adding a number to itself multiple times (e.g., 3 x 2/5 = 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5).
- **Numerator** — the top number of a fraction; tells how many parts are counted.
- **Denominator** — the bottom number of a fraction; tells how many equal parts make up the whole.
- **Improper fraction** — a fraction where the numerator is greater than or equal to the denominator (e.g., 7/4).
- **Mixed number** — a number consisting of a whole number and a proper fraction (e.g., 1 3/4).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Build the concept with repeated addition.**
Write on the board: 3 x 2/5.
"This means 3 groups of 2/5. Let's add them up."
Write: 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5.
"The denominators are all the same — fifths. So we add the numerators: 2 + 2 + 2 = 6. The answer is 6/5."
Lay out fraction strips to confirm: three groups of two-fifths pieces line up to show 6/5, which is one whole and one-fifth.

**Step 2 — Discover the shortcut.**
"Look at what we did: we added the numerator 2 three times. That is the same as multiplying: 3 x 2 = 6. The denominator stays at 5."
Write the rule on the board:
- **Whole Number x Fraction: multiply the whole number by the numerator; keep the denominator.**
- n x a/b = (n x a)/b

**Step 3 — Write the whole number as a fraction.**
"We can also think of the whole number 3 as 3/1. Now both numbers are fractions."
Write: 3/1 x 2/5 = (3 x 2)/(1 x 5) = 6/5.
"This uses the standard fraction multiplication algorithm — multiply numerators, multiply denominators. You will use this algorithm throughout the unit."

**Step 4 — Convert the improper fraction to a mixed number.**
"Is 6/5 greater than 1 whole? Yes, because 6 > 5. Divide 6 by 5: 6 / 5 = 1 remainder 1. So 6/5 = 1 1/5."
Remind students: to convert an improper fraction, divide the numerator by the denominator. The quotient is the whole number; the remainder is the new numerator over the original denominator.

**Step 5 — Model a second example: 4 x 3/8.**
- Repeated addition: 3/8 + 3/8 + 3/8 + 3/8 = 12/8.
- Shortcut: 4 x 3 = 12, denominator stays 8. So 12/8.
- As a fraction problem: 4/1 x 3/8 = 12/8.
- Simplify: 12/8 = 3/2 (divide numerator and denominator by 4), or convert to mixed number: 12 / 8 = 1 remainder 4, so 12/8 = 1 4/8 = 1 1/2.

### Worked Examples

**Example A — 3 x 2/5**
Repeated addition: 2/5 + 2/5 + 2/5 = 6/5
Shortcut: (3 x 2)/5 = 6/5
As fractions: 3/1 x 2/5 = 6/5
Convert: 6/5 = 1 1/5
**Answer: 6/5 = 1 1/5**

**Example B — 4 x 3/8**
Shortcut: (4 x 3)/8 = 12/8
As fractions: 4/1 x 3/8 = 12/8
Simplify: 12/8 = 3/2 = 1 1/2
**Answer: 12/8 = 1 1/2**

**Example C — 5 x 2/3**
Shortcut: (5 x 2)/3 = 10/3
As fractions: 5/1 x 2/3 = 10/3
Convert: 10 / 3 = 3 remainder 1, so 10/3 = 3 1/3
**Answer: 10/3 = 3 1/3**

**Example D — 6 x 1/4**
Shortcut: (6 x 1)/4 = 6/4
As fractions: 6/1 x 1/4 = 6/4
Simplify: 6/4 = 3/2 = 1 1/2
**Answer: 6/4 = 1 1/2**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Students attempt each problem first, then discuss.

1. 2 x 3/7 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Write this as repeated addition first: 3/7 + 3/7. Now use the shortcut — multiply the numerator by 2."

2. 5 x 1/6 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is 5 x 1? Keep the denominator at 6. Is your answer improper? If so, convert it."

3. 3 x 4/9 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Write 3 as 3/1 and multiply across. Can you simplify the result?"

4. 4 x 2/5 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Use the shortcut to get the product. Then convert the improper fraction to a mixed number."

5. 7 x 2/8 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "After multiplying, check — can you simplify before or after converting to a mixed number?"

> **Facilitator Tip:** If students are comfortable with the shortcut, encourage them to practice writing the whole number as n/1 and multiplying across. This builds the habit they will need when multiplying fraction x fraction in Lessons 3-5.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — Computation (On-Level)
Solve each problem. Express your answer as a mixed number in simplest form where applicable.

1. 2 x 4/9 = ?
2. 3 x 5/8 = ?
3. 6 x 2/7 = ?
4. 4 x 3/10 = ?

### Part B — Repeated Addition Connection and Word Problems (Stretch)
5. Write 5 x 3/4 as a repeated addition expression. Then compute the product and convert to a mixed number.
6. A recipe calls for 2/3 cup of flour. If you want to make 4 batches, how much flour do you need in total? Express your answer as a mixed number.
7. A painter uses 3/8 of a gallon of paint for each room. He paints 6 rooms. How many gallons of paint does he use? Express your answer as a mixed number in simplest form.
8. Maria runs 3/4 of a mile each day. How many miles does she run in 5 days? Express your answer as a mixed number.

### Part C — Challenge
9. A box contains ribbon that is 7/12 of a yard long. You need 8 pieces of ribbon that length. How many yards of ribbon do you need altogether? Express your answer as a mixed number in simplest form.
10. True or false: 6 x 2/5 = 2 x 6/5. Explain your reasoning using the commutative property or by computing both sides.
11. Without computing, determine whether 9 x 2/7 is greater than, less than, or equal to 2. Explain your reasoning, then verify by computing.

### Answer Key

**Part A**
1. 2 x 4/9 = 8/9
2. 3 x 5/8 = 15/8 = 1 7/8
3. 6 x 2/7 = 12/7 = 1 5/7
4. 4 x 3/10 = 12/10 = 6/5 = 1 1/5

**Part B**
5. 5 x 3/4 = 3/4 + 3/4 + 3/4 + 3/4 + 3/4 = 15/4 = 3 3/4
6. 4 x 2/3 = 8/3 = 2 2/3 cups of flour
7. 6 x 3/8 = 18/8 = 9/4 = 2 1/4 gallons of paint
8. 5 x 3/4 = 15/4 = 3 3/4 miles

**Part C**
9. 8 x 7/12 = 56/12 = 14/3 = 4 2/3 yards of ribbon
10. True. 6 x 2/5 = 12/5 and 2 x 6/5 = 12/5. Both equal 12/5. This works because multiplication is commutative and associative: 6 x 2/5 = (6 x 2)/5 = 12/5, and 2 x 6/5 = (2 x 6)/5 = 12/5.
11. 9 x 2/7 is greater than 2. Reasoning: 2/7 is a little more than 1/4 (since 2/8 = 1/4 and 2/7 > 2/8). So 9 x 2/7 is a little more than 9 x 1/4 = 9/4 = 2 1/4. Verification: 9 x 2/7 = 18/7 = 2 4/7, which is indeed greater than 2.

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket
Solve the following two problems. Show your work, including the repeated addition form for Problem 1.

**Problem 1:** 3 x 4/5 = ?
**Problem 2:** 7 x 3/10 = ? (express as a mixed number in simplest form)

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct with work shown = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct or correct answer but work missing = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Pull students into a small group and use fraction strip manipulatives to physically model 2 x 3/5 and 4 x 1/3 as repeated addition.
- Provide a step-by-step reference card: (1) Write the whole number as n/1, (2) Multiply numerators, (3) Multiply denominators, (4) Simplify or convert.
- Pair the student with a peer tutor for additional guided practice before Lesson 2.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may create their own word problems involving fraction x whole number for a classmate to solve.

## Extension

- **Fraction Multiplication Patterns:** Compute 1 x 2/5, 2 x 2/5, 3 x 2/5, 4 x 2/5, and 5 x 2/5. Write the products as improper fractions and as mixed numbers. What pattern do you notice in the numerators? What happens when the product equals a whole number?
- **Real-World Connection:** A water bottle holds 3/8 of a liter. If you drink 5 full bottles in a day, how many liters of water did you drink? Is that more or less than 2 liters? Write a recommendation about how many bottles someone should drink to reach 2 liters.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Multiplying both the numerator and the denominator by the whole number.** For example, writing 3 x 2/5 = 6/15 instead of 6/5. Redirect by asking: "Did the size of each piece change? If we still have fifths, the denominator stays 5." Demonstrate with fraction strips.
- **Forgetting to convert improper fractions.** Some students will leave 12/5 as their final answer. Remind them that answers should be expressed as mixed numbers unless otherwise directed.
- **Simplification errors.** For example, writing 12/8 = 1 4/8 but not simplifying 4/8 to 1/2. Build the habit: after converting, check whether the remaining fraction can be simplified.
- **Not recognizing the problem as multiplication.** In word problems, students may not see that "5 groups of 2/3" means 5 x 2/3. Practice identifying key phrases: "groups of," "each," "every."

### Pacing Advice
- If students grasp the Warm-Up quickly, move directly into the first worked example and spend more time on Guided Practice.
- If many students struggle with converting improper fractions to mixed numbers, pause and reteach this prerequisite skill with 2-3 additional examples before continuing.
- The repeated addition model is the bridge from Unit 1 (fraction addition) to Unit 2 (fraction multiplication). Spend enough time here so students truly see multiplication as a shortcut for repeated addition — this conceptual link is critical for later lessons.
- For students who finish Independent Practice early, direct them to the Extension activity or have them illustrate one of their word problems with a diagram.
