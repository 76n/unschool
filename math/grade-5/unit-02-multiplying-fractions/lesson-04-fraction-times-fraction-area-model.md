# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 4

## Topic
Fraction x Fraction: Area Model

## Learning Objectives
- Students will represent fraction multiplication using an area model drawn on grid paper.
- Students will find the product of two fractions by counting overlap squares out of total squares in a unit square.
- Students will connect the area model to finding the area of a rectangle with fractional side lengths.
- Students will simplify products to lowest terms.
- Students will recognize and articulate the emerging pattern: multiply the numerators to get the new numerator, multiply the denominators to get the new denominator.

## Prerequisites
- Conceptual understanding of fraction x fraction from paper folding (Lesson 3).
- Understanding that "a/b of c/d" means a/b x c/d.
- Familiarity with area as length x width for whole-number rectangles.
- Ability to simplify fractions (Unit 1, Lesson 6).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Grid paper (1-cm or 1/4-inch squares; at least 4 sheets per student).
- Colored pencils (two different colors per student).
- Rulers (for drawing neat unit squares).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NF.4b — Find the area of a rectangle with fractional side lengths by tiling it with unit squares of the appropriate unit fraction side lengths, and show that the area is the same as would be found by multiplying the side lengths. Multiply fractional side lengths to find areas of rectangles, and represent fraction products as rectangular areas.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review the paper-folding model from Lesson 3 and transition to the more precise area model on grid paper.

1. Write on the board: "1/2 x 1/3 = ?"
2. Ask: "Who remembers what we did with paper folding yesterday? What was the answer?" (1/6.)
3. Say: "Today we are going to use grid paper instead of folding paper. Grid paper gives us perfectly equal squares, so we can count more precisely. The idea is the same — we shade one fraction in one direction, the other fraction in the other direction, and the overlap tells us the product."
4. Quick check: Draw a 2 x 3 grid on the board (a rectangle with 2 columns and 3 rows). Ask: "How many small squares are in this rectangle?" (6.) "If I shade 1 column out of 2, and 1 row out of 3, how many squares are in the overlap?" (1.) "So 1/2 x 1/3 = 1/6. Same answer, cleaner picture."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Unit square** — a square with side lengths of 1 unit, representing one whole. Its area is 1 square unit.
- **Area model** — a rectangular diagram used to represent multiplication; for fractions, one side length is one fraction and the other side length is the other fraction.
- **Horizontal shading** — shading rows to represent one fraction.
- **Vertical shading** — shading columns to represent the other fraction.
- **Overlap region** — the area where both shadings intersect; this represents the product.
- **Tiling** — covering an area completely with smaller unit squares without gaps or overlaps.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Establish the unit square.**

Draw a square on grid paper (projected or on the board) that is 6 squares wide and 6 squares tall. Say: "This square represents 1 whole. Its area is 1 square unit. Every time we multiply two fractions, we will start by drawing a unit square."

Explain: "We choose the size of our unit square based on the denominators. If the denominators are 2 and 3, we need a square that can be divided into 2 equal parts one way and 3 equal parts the other way. So we draw a 2 x 3 grid — or a 3 x 2 grid — inside our unit square."

**Step 2 — Model: 1/2 x 1/3.**

Draw a rectangle divided into 2 columns and 3 rows (a 2 x 3 grid, 6 total squares).

Shade the first column with Color A (blue) — this represents 1/2.

Shade the first row with Color B (red) — this represents 1/3.

Ask: "Total small squares?" (6.) "Overlap squares (where both colors appear)?" (1.)

Write: **1/2 x 1/3 = 1/6.**

"This also means: a rectangle with side lengths 1/2 and 1/3 has an area of 1/6 square unit."

**Step 3 — Model: 2/3 x 3/5.**

Draw a rectangle divided into 3 columns and 5 rows (a 3 x 5 grid, 15 total squares).

Shade 2 out of 3 columns with Color A — this represents 2/3.

Shade 3 out of 5 rows with Color B — this represents 3/5.

Ask: "Total small squares?" (15.) "Overlap squares?" Count together: 2 columns x 3 rows of overlap = 6 squares. (6.)

Write: **2/3 x 3/5 = 6/15.**

"Can we simplify? GCF of 6 and 15 is 3. So 6/15 = 2/5."

Write: **2/3 x 3/5 = 6/15 = 2/5.**

Point to the model: "Look at the overlap — it covers 6 out of 15 squares, which is the same as 2 out of every 5 squares. That matches 2/5."

**Step 4 — Model: 3/4 x 2/3.**

Draw a rectangle divided into 4 columns and 3 rows (a 4 x 3 grid, 12 total squares).

Shade 3 out of 4 columns with Color A — this represents 3/4.

Shade 2 out of 3 rows with Color B — this represents 2/3.

"Total squares?" (12.) "Overlap squares?" 3 columns x 2 rows = 6. (6.)

Write: **3/4 x 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2.**

"Let's double-check with the pattern we started to notice in Lesson 3. Numerator x numerator: 3 x 2 = 6. Denominator x denominator: 4 x 3 = 12. Product: 6/12 = 1/2. It matches!"

**Step 5 — Name the pattern explicitly.**

Write on the board in a box:

"To multiply two fractions: **Multiply the numerators. Multiply the denominators. Simplify if possible.**"

"a/b x c/d = (a x c) / (b x d)"

Say: "The area model shows us WHY this works. The total number of squares in the grid is always denominator x denominator. The number of overlap squares is always numerator x numerator. So the fraction of the whole that is double-shaded is (numerator x numerator) / (denominator x denominator)."

"In the next lesson, we will practice using this rule without drawing the model every time. But for today, keep drawing — make sure the model and the rule give you the same answer."

**Step 6 — Connect to area.**

Draw a rectangle labeled 3/4 on one side and 2/3 on the other. Say: "In geometry, area = length x width. If a rectangle has side lengths of 3/4 unit and 2/3 unit, its area is 3/4 x 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2 square unit. The area model is not just a trick for multiplying — it IS the area."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — 1/2 x 1/3**
1. Draw a unit square divided into 2 columns and 3 rows (6 total squares).
2. Shade 1 column (Color A) for 1/2.
3. Shade 1 row (Color B) for 1/3.
4. Overlap: 1 square out of 6 total.
5. Product: 1/6.
6. Check with algorithm: (1 x 1)/(2 x 3) = 1/6.
**Answer: 1/2 x 1/3 = 1/6**

**Example B — 2/3 x 3/5**
1. Draw a unit square divided into 3 columns and 5 rows (15 total squares).
2. Shade 2 columns (Color A) for 2/3.
3. Shade 3 rows (Color B) for 3/5.
4. Overlap: 2 x 3 = 6 squares out of 15 total.
5. Product: 6/15 = 2/5.
6. Check with algorithm: (2 x 3)/(3 x 5) = 6/15 = 2/5.
**Answer: 2/3 x 3/5 = 6/15 = 2/5**

**Example C — 3/4 x 2/3**
1. Draw a unit square divided into 4 columns and 3 rows (12 total squares).
2. Shade 3 columns (Color A) for 3/4.
3. Shade 2 rows (Color B) for 2/3.
4. Overlap: 3 x 2 = 6 squares out of 12 total.
5. Product: 6/12 = 1/2.
6. Check with algorithm: (3 x 2)/(4 x 3) = 6/12 = 1/2.
**Answer: 3/4 x 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

For each problem, students draw an area model on grid paper, shade with two colors, count the overlap, and write the product. Then verify using the multiplication pattern.

1. 1/3 x 1/4 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Draw a 3-by-4 grid. Shade 1 column out of 3 for 1/3, and 1 row out of 4 for 1/4. How many overlap squares? How many total?"

2. 2/5 x 1/2 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What size grid do you need? 5 columns and 2 rows. Shade 2 columns and 1 row. Count the overlap."

3. 3/4 x 1/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Shade 3 out of 4 columns and 1 out of 3 rows. Can you simplify the product?"

4. 2/3 x 2/5 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "This time you are shading more than one section in each direction. How many overlap squares do you count?"

5. 3/5 x 2/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "After you find the product from the model, verify: does the 'multiply tops, multiply bottoms' rule give the same answer?"

> **Facilitator Tip:** Encourage students to use a consistent system: always shade the first fraction as columns (vertical) and the second fraction as rows (horizontal). This prevents confusion about which shading represents which fraction. Walk around to check that students are counting total squares correctly — a common error is counting only the shaded squares rather than ALL squares in the grid.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — Area Model Practice
Draw an area model for each problem. Shade, count, and write the product in simplest form.

1. 1/4 x 1/3 = ?
2. 2/3 x 1/4 = ?
3. 1/2 x 3/5 = ?
4. 3/4 x 2/5 = ?

### Part B — Model and Verify
Draw the area model AND use the algorithm (multiply numerators, multiply denominators) for each problem. Confirm that both methods give the same answer.

5. 4/5 x 1/2 = ?
6. 2/3 x 3/4 = ?
7. 3/5 x 2/3 = ?
8. A garden bed is 3/4 meter long and 2/5 meter wide. What is the area of the garden bed? Draw an area model to show your answer.

### Part C — Reasoning and Patterns
9. Anika says 2/3 x 3/5 = 5/8 because she added the numerators (2 + 3 = 5) and added the denominators (3 + 5 = 8). Use an area model to show why Anika's answer is incorrect. What is the correct answer?
10. Look at your answers to problems 6 and 7 above. Notice that 2/3 x 3/4 and 3/4 x 2/3 give the same product. Does 3/5 x 2/3 give the same product as 2/3 x 3/5? What property of multiplication does this demonstrate?
11. Without drawing a model, predict: Is 1/3 x 1/5 greater than, less than, or equal to 1/5? Explain your reasoning. Then multiply to check.

### Answer Key

**Part A**
1. 1/4 x 1/3: Grid = 4 columns x 3 rows = 12 squares. Overlap = 1 x 1 = 1. **Answer: 1/12.**
2. 2/3 x 1/4: Grid = 3 columns x 4 rows = 12 squares. Overlap = 2 x 1 = 2. **Answer: 2/12 = 1/6.**
3. 1/2 x 3/5: Grid = 2 columns x 5 rows = 10 squares. Overlap = 1 x 3 = 3. **Answer: 3/10.**
4. 3/4 x 2/5: Grid = 4 columns x 5 rows = 20 squares. Overlap = 3 x 2 = 6. **Answer: 6/20 = 3/10.**

**Part B**
5. 4/5 x 1/2: Grid = 5 columns x 2 rows = 10 squares. Overlap = 4 x 1 = 4. Model: 4/10 = 2/5. Algorithm: (4 x 1)/(5 x 2) = 4/10 = 2/5. **Answer: 2/5.**
6. 2/3 x 3/4: Grid = 3 columns x 4 rows = 12 squares. Overlap = 2 x 3 = 6. Model: 6/12 = 1/2. Algorithm: (2 x 3)/(3 x 4) = 6/12 = 1/2. **Answer: 1/2.**
7. 3/5 x 2/3: Grid = 5 columns x 3 rows = 15 squares. Overlap = 3 x 2 = 6. Model: 6/15 = 2/5. Algorithm: (3 x 2)/(5 x 3) = 6/15 = 2/5. **Answer: 2/5.**
8. Area = 3/4 x 2/5. Grid = 4 columns x 5 rows = 20 squares. Overlap = 3 x 2 = 6. Model: 6/20 = 3/10. **Answer: The garden bed has an area of 3/10 square meter.**

**Part C**
9. Anika is incorrect. Area model for 2/3 x 3/5: Grid = 3 columns x 5 rows = 15 squares. Overlap = 2 x 3 = 6. Product = 6/15 = 2/5. Anika's answer of 5/8 does not match the model. The correct method is to multiply numerators (2 x 3 = 6) and multiply denominators (3 x 5 = 15), not add them. **Correct answer: 2/3 x 3/5 = 6/15 = 2/5.**
10. 3/5 x 2/3 = 6/15 = 2/5, and 2/3 x 3/5 = 6/15 = 2/5. Yes, they give the same product. This demonstrates the **commutative property** of multiplication — the order in which you multiply does not change the product.
11. 1/3 x 1/5 is less than 1/5, because multiplying 1/5 by 1/3 (a number less than 1) gives a smaller result — you are taking only one-third of 1/5. Check: 1/3 x 1/5 = (1 x 1)/(3 x 5) = 1/15. Since 1/15 < 1/5, the prediction is correct. **Answer: Less than 1/5.**

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket
Complete the following problems. Show your area model or algorithm work.

**Problem 1:** Draw an area model for 1/4 x 2/3. Write the product in simplest form.
**Problem 2:** Use the algorithm (multiply numerators, multiply denominators) to solve 3/5 x 1/4. Simplify your answer.
**Problem 3:** A rectangular piece of fabric is 2/3 yard long and 1/2 yard wide. What is the area of the fabric? Show your work.

**Scoring:**
- 3 out of 3 correct with models/work shown = Mastery demonstrated.
- 2 out of 3 correct = Approaching mastery; review the missed concept.
- 0-1 out of 3 correct = Reteach needed with additional area model practice.

**Exit Ticket Answers:**
1. 1/4 x 2/3: Grid = 4 columns x 3 rows = 12 squares. Overlap = 1 x 2 = 2. Product = 2/12 = 1/6. **Answer: 1/6.**
2. 3/5 x 1/4 = (3 x 1)/(5 x 4) = 3/20. GCF of 3 and 20 is 1, so 3/20 is already in simplest form. **Answer: 3/20.**
3. 2/3 x 1/2 = (2 x 1)/(3 x 2) = 2/6 = 1/3. **Answer: The area of the fabric is 1/3 square yard.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Reteach using pre-drawn grids where the student only needs to shade and count (removing the burden of drawing the grid accurately).
- Pair the student with a peer tutor to work through 3 additional area model problems side by side.
- Review Lesson 3 paper-folding activities if the student does not understand what the overlap region represents.
- Provide a step-by-step reference card: "1. Draw a grid (denominator x denominator). 2. Shade columns for the first fraction. 3. Shade rows for the second fraction. 4. Count overlap / total. 5. Simplify."

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may create a set of 3 area model problems with an answer key and trade with a partner.

## Extension

- **Area Model Art:** Students create a poster with four area models for four different fraction multiplication problems of their choosing. Each model should be neatly drawn and labeled with the multiplication sentence, the overlap count, the total count, and the simplified product. Display posters in the classroom.
- **Find the Missing Factor:** Give students problems with one factor missing: "? x 1/3 = 2/15." Students use an area model to work backward and find the missing fraction. (Answer: 2/5, because a 5-by-3 grid with 2 overlap squares in 15 total means 2 columns out of 5 were shaded.)
- **Area Challenge:** "A rectangular garden is 3/4 meter by 4/5 meter. A square stepping stone is 1/4 meter by 1/4 meter. How many stepping stones would fit across the length of the garden? Across the width? What fraction of the garden does one stepping stone cover?" (Length: 3 stones. Width: 16/5 — this reveals that non-whole-number answers are possible and previews more complex area reasoning.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Drawing the wrong grid size.** For 2/3 x 3/5, students may draw a 2 x 3 grid instead of a 3 x 5 grid. Reinforce: "The grid dimensions come from the DENOMINATORS, not the numerators. The denominators tell you how many equal parts make up the whole."
- **Counting only shaded squares instead of total squares.** Some students count only the colored squares for the denominator of their answer. Remind them: "The total number of squares in the entire grid is the denominator."
- **Confusing which fraction is rows and which is columns.** This does not affect the product (commutative property), but it can confuse students if they are not consistent. Establish a class convention and stick with it.
- **Not simplifying.** After finding the overlap fraction, students may forget to simplify. Build the habit: "After you write your fraction, always ask — do the top and bottom share a common factor?"
- **Adding numerators and denominators instead of multiplying.** This error (e.g., 2/3 x 3/5 = 5/8) persists from addition habits. The area model is the best corrective — the overlap region clearly shows 6 out of 15 squares, not 5 out of 8.

### Pacing Advice
- The first two worked examples (Step 2 and Step 3) are the most important. Take time to let students draw along with you. If students are slow to draw grids, provide pre-printed grid templates with unit squares already outlined.
- If the class is moving quickly and the pattern is clicking, you may combine Guided Practice problems 4 and 5 into a single partner activity and use the saved time for Part C of Independent Practice, which deepens reasoning.
- If many students struggle with the grid drawing, consider doing all of Guided Practice as a whole class (students draw on their own grid paper while you model on the board) before releasing them for Independent Practice.
- This lesson pairs naturally with Lesson 5 (the formal algorithm). Students who finish early can preview the algorithm by solving problems with just the "multiply tops, multiply bottoms" rule and checking against their area models.
