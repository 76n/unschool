# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 3

## Topic
Fraction x Fraction: Conceptual Introduction

## Learning Objectives
- Students will model the product of two fractions using paper folding and shading.
- Students will interpret "a/b of c/d" as multiplication (a/b x c/d).
- Students will explain why multiplying two fractions that are each less than 1 produces a product less than either fraction.
- Students will count overlap regions in a folded model to determine the product of two fractions.
- Students will begin to connect the concrete folding model to the multiplication algorithm (numerator x numerator over denominator x denominator).

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of what a fraction represents (parts of a whole).
- Ability to multiply a fraction by a whole number (Lesson 1) and a whole number by a fraction (Lesson 2).
- Familiarity with equivalent fractions and simplifying fractions (Unit 1).
- Comfort with basic multiplication facts.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Rectangular sheets of plain paper (at least 4 per student for folding activities).
- Colored pencils or crayons (two different colors per student).
- Grid paper (optional, for students who want to verify their folded models).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NF.4a — Interpret the product (a/b) x q as a parts of a partition of q into b equal parts; equivalently, as the result of a sequence of operations a x q / b.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate the concept of "fraction of" from Lesson 2 and bridge toward taking a fraction of a fraction.

1. Write on the board: "What is 1/2 of 8?"
2. Students solve independently. (Answer: 4.)
3. Now write: "What is 1/2 of 1?" Students solve. (Answer: 1/2.)
4. Now write: "What is 1/2 of 1/2?"
5. Give students 1 minute to think or discuss with a partner. Accept predictions. Some students may say 1/4, others may be unsure.
6. Say: "Today we are going to learn what happens when we take a fraction OF a fraction. The word 'of' in math means multiply. So '1/2 of 1/2' means 1/2 x 1/2. Let's find out the answer — and it might surprise you."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fraction of a fraction** — taking a fractional part of a quantity that is itself a fraction (e.g., "2/3 of 3/4" means 2/3 x 3/4).
- **Overlap region** — the area of a folded and shaded paper where both colors appear; this region represents the product.
- **Product** — the result of multiplying two numbers.
- **Less than either factor** — when both factors are less than 1, the product is smaller than each factor individually.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Start with the simplest case: 1/2 x 1/2.**

Hold up a rectangular sheet of paper. Say: "This whole sheet represents 1."

Fold the paper in half horizontally. Open it and shade the top half with Color A (e.g., blue). Say: "I have shaded 1/2 of the paper."

Now fold the paper in half vertically. Open it and shade the left half with Color B (e.g., red). Say: "I have shaded 1/2 going the other direction."

Ask: "Where do both colors overlap?" (The top-left quarter.) "How many total sections does the paper have now?" (4.) "How many sections have both colors?" (1.) "So 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4."

Write on the board: **1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4.**

Ask: "Is 1/4 bigger or smaller than 1/2?" (Smaller.) "When we took half of a half, we got something even smaller. Keep that in mind."

**Step 2 — Model the anchor problem: 2/3 x 3/4.**

Take a new sheet of paper. Say: "We want to find 2/3 of 3/4. Let's start with 3/4."

Fold the paper into 4 equal columns (fold in half vertically, then in half again). Open it and shade 3 of the 4 columns with Color A. Say: "I have shaded 3/4 of the paper."

Now fold the paper into 3 equal rows (fold into thirds horizontally). Open it and shade 2 of the 3 rows with Color B. Say: "I am taking 2/3 of the paper in this direction — 2/3 OF the 3/4."

Ask: "How many total small rectangles does the paper have?" (Count: 4 columns x 3 rows = 12 total rectangles.)

"How many rectangles have BOTH colors — the overlap?" (Students count the overlap region: 2 rows x 3 columns = 6 rectangles.)

Write on the board: **2/3 x 3/4 = 6/12.**

Ask: "Can we simplify 6/12?" (Yes. 6/12 = 1/2.)

Point to the paper: "Look — exactly half the paper is double-shaded. So 2/3 x 3/4 = 1/2. That makes sense!"

**Step 3 — Highlight the key insight.**

Write on the board in a box:

"When you multiply two fractions that are each less than 1, the product is LESS than either fraction."

Ask: "Is 1/2 less than 2/3?" (Yes.) "Is 1/2 less than 3/4?" (Yes.) "So our product, 1/2, is less than both of the fractions we multiplied. This is different from whole numbers, where multiplication makes things bigger."

Give an everyday example: "If you eat 2/3 of 3/4 of a pizza, you eat less than 3/4 of a pizza — you only eat a part of that part."

**Step 4 — Connect folding to the algorithm (preview).**

Return to the anchor problem. Point to the paper and say:

"Look at the numerators we multiplied: 2 x 3 = 6. That is the number of overlap rectangles."

"Look at the denominators we multiplied: 3 x 4 = 12. That is the total number of rectangles on the paper."

Write: 2/3 x 3/4 = (2 x 3)/(3 x 4) = 6/12 = 1/2.

"Do you see the pattern? When we multiply fractions, we multiply the numerators to get the new numerator, and multiply the denominators to get the new denominator. We will practice this pattern more in the next lessons — for now, let's make sure we understand WHY it works by folding more paper."

**Step 5 — Model one more example: 1/3 x 1/2.**

Take a new sheet. Fold into 2 columns; shade 1 column with Color A (showing 1/2). Fold into 3 rows; shade 1 row with Color B (showing 1/3).

"Total rectangles?" (6.) "Overlap rectangles?" (1.) "So 1/3 x 1/2 = 1/6."

Verify with the algorithm: (1 x 1)/(3 x 2) = 1/6. Confirmed.

### Worked Examples

**Example A — 1/2 x 1/2**
1. Fold paper into 2 columns, shade 1 column (1/2).
2. Fold paper into 2 rows, shade 1 row (1/2).
3. Total sections: 2 x 2 = 4.
4. Overlap sections: 1 x 1 = 1.
5. Product: 1/4.
**Answer: 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4**

**Example B — 2/3 x 3/4**
1. Fold paper into 4 columns, shade 3 columns (3/4).
2. Fold paper into 3 rows, shade 2 rows (2/3).
3. Total sections: 3 x 4 = 12.
4. Overlap sections: 2 x 3 = 6.
5. Product: 6/12 = 1/2.
**Answer: 2/3 x 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2**

**Example C — 1/3 x 1/2**
1. Fold paper into 2 columns, shade 1 column (1/2).
2. Fold paper into 3 rows, shade 1 row (1/3).
3. Total sections: 3 x 2 = 6.
4. Overlap sections: 1 x 1 = 1.
5. Product: 1/6.
**Answer: 1/3 x 1/2 = 1/6**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. For each problem, students fold and shade paper first, then count overlap regions and write the product.

1. 1/2 x 1/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Fold your paper into 3 columns and shade 1 for 1/3. Then fold into 2 rows and shade 1 for 1/2. How many total sections? How many overlap?"

2. 3/4 x 1/2 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Start by showing 1/2 on your paper. Then take 3/4 of it. How many sections overlap out of the total?"

3. 1/4 x 2/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Fold into 3 columns, shade 2 for 2/3. Then fold into 4 rows, shade 1 for 1/4. Count the overlap and the total."

4. 2/3 x 2/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "This time both fractions are the same. Fold into 3 columns and shade 2. Fold into 3 rows and shade 2. What do you notice about the overlap?"

5. 3/4 x 2/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "After you fold and shade, check — does the pattern hold? Do you get the same answer by multiplying numerators and multiplying denominators?"

> **Facilitator Tip:** Circulate while students fold. The most common difficulty is folding paper into equal thirds. Show students how to estimate thirds by folding the paper into an S-shape, or provide pre-creased paper for students who struggle with fine motor tasks. Encourage students to label each section of their paper with the fraction it represents before counting overlap.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — Paper Folding (Concrete)
For each problem, fold and shade a sheet of paper (or draw the folded model on grid paper). Record the total sections, overlap sections, and the product.

1. 1/2 x 1/4 = ?
2. 1/3 x 3/4 = ?
3. 2/3 x 1/4 = ?

### Part B — Drawing Models (Pictorial)
Draw a rectangle for each problem. Divide it into the correct number of sections, shade using two colors, and find the product by counting the overlap.

4. 3/4 x 1/3 = ?
5. 1/2 x 2/5 = ?
6. 2/3 x 3/5 = ?

### Part C — Reasoning and Connection
7. Marcus says that 1/2 x 1/3 = 2/6. Keisha says the answer is 1/6. Who is correct? Explain using a model or the pattern you noticed today.
8. Without folding paper, predict: Will 3/4 x 2/5 be greater than 2/5 or less than 2/5? Explain your reasoning, then check by drawing a model or using the pattern.
9. Complete the pattern:
   - 1/2 x 1/2 = ___
   - 1/3 x 1/3 = ___
   - 1/4 x 1/4 = ___
   - What do you notice? Write one sentence describing the pattern.

### Answer Key

**Part A**
1. 1/2 x 1/4: Total sections = 2 x 4 = 8. Overlap = 1 x 1 = 1. **Answer: 1/8.**
2. 1/3 x 3/4: Total sections = 3 x 4 = 12. Overlap = 1 x 3 = 3. **Answer: 3/12 = 1/4.**
3. 2/3 x 1/4: Total sections = 3 x 4 = 12. Overlap = 2 x 1 = 2. **Answer: 2/12 = 1/6.**

**Part B**
4. 3/4 x 1/3: Total sections = 4 x 3 = 12. Overlap = 3 x 1 = 3. **Answer: 3/12 = 1/4.**
5. 1/2 x 2/5: Total sections = 2 x 5 = 10. Overlap = 1 x 2 = 2. **Answer: 2/10 = 1/5.**
6. 2/3 x 3/5: Total sections = 3 x 5 = 15. Overlap = 2 x 3 = 6. **Answer: 6/15 = 2/5.**

**Part C**
7. Both give the same value: 2/6 = 1/6? No — 2/6 simplifies to 1/3, not 1/6. Actually, 1/2 x 1/3: overlap = 1 out of 6 total sections = 1/6. Keisha is correct. Marcus may have added the numerators (1 + 1 = 2) instead of multiplying (1 x 1 = 1). **Answer: Keisha is correct. 1/2 x 1/3 = 1/6.**
8. 3/4 x 2/5 will be less than 2/5, because we are taking only 3/4 of 2/5 (a part of it). Since 3/4 is less than 1, the product must be smaller than 2/5. Check: 3/4 x 2/5 = 6/20 = 3/10. Is 3/10 < 2/5? Convert 2/5 = 4/10. Yes, 3/10 < 4/10. **Answer: The product is less than 2/5.**
9. 1/2 x 1/2 = 1/4; 1/3 x 1/3 = 1/9; 1/4 x 1/4 = 1/16. **Pattern: When you multiply a unit fraction by itself, the product is 1 over the square of the denominator. The products get smaller and smaller.**

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket
Use a model (paper fold or drawing) to solve the following. Show your work.

**Problem 1:** 1/3 x 1/4 = ?
**Problem 2:** 2/3 x 1/2 = ?
**Problem 3:** True or false: 3/4 x 1/2 is greater than 3/4. Explain your reasoning.

**Scoring:**
- 3 out of 3 correct with models or clear reasoning = Mastery demonstrated.
- 2 out of 3 correct = Approaching mastery; review the missed concept.
- 0-1 out of 3 correct = Reteach needed with additional concrete activities.

**Exit Ticket Answers:**
1. 1/3 x 1/4 = 1/12. (Total sections: 3 x 4 = 12. Overlap: 1 x 1 = 1.)
2. 2/3 x 1/2 = 2/6 = 1/3. (Total sections: 3 x 2 = 6. Overlap: 2 x 1 = 2.)
3. False. 3/4 x 1/2 = 3/8, which is less than 3/4. Multiplying by 1/2 (a fraction less than 1) makes the result smaller, not larger.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Work one-on-one with the student using pre-folded paper models. Have the student physically shade and count sections for 3-4 additional problems (e.g., 1/2 x 1/2, 1/3 x 1/2, 1/4 x 1/3, 2/3 x 1/4).
- Provide a reference card: "Fraction x Fraction: Shade one fraction in rows, the other in columns. Count the overlap out of the total."
- Revisit Lesson 2 ("fraction of a whole number") to reinforce the "of means multiply" concept before returning to fraction-of-a-fraction.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may create their own folding problem and trade with a partner to solve.

## Extension

- **Fraction Folding Challenge:** Give students the product (e.g., "the answer is 1/6") and ask them to find at least two different multiplication problems that produce that product using fractions less than 1. They should verify each answer with a paper fold. (Possible answers: 1/2 x 1/3, 1/3 x 1/2, 1/6 x 1/1.)
- **Real-World Connection:** "A recipe calls for 3/4 of a cup of sugar, but you want to make only 1/2 of the recipe. How much sugar do you need?" Students model with paper folding, then write the multiplication sentence and solve. (1/2 x 3/4 = 3/8 cup.)
- **Pattern Exploration:** Students investigate: "Is 2/3 x 3/4 the same as 3/4 x 2/3?" They fold paper for both and compare. This previews the commutative property of fraction multiplication.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Expecting the product to be larger.** Students' whole-number intuition tells them multiplication makes things bigger. Address this directly and repeatedly: "When you multiply by a number less than 1, you get a smaller result. You are taking a PART of something."
- **Confusing multiplication with addition.** Some students may add numerators and add denominators (e.g., writing 1/2 x 1/3 = 2/5). The paper-folding model directly contradicts this: the overlap region does not match 2/5 of the total. Use the visual to correct.
- **Miscounting sections on folded paper.** Uneven folds lead to unequal sections and incorrect counts. Provide grid paper as a backup so students can draw models if their folds are imprecise.
- **Not simplifying the product.** Remind students to check if the numerator and denominator share a common factor (e.g., 6/12 should be simplified to 1/2).

### Pacing Advice
- The paper folding activity for 2/3 x 3/4 is the heart of this lesson. Do not rush it. If students need extra time folding, reduce Independent Practice to Part A and Part C only, and assign Part B as homework.
- If students grasp the folding model quickly and are already noticing the "multiply tops, multiply bottoms" pattern, acknowledge it but do not skip to the algorithm yet. Say: "Great observation! We will prove that the shortcut always works in the next two lessons. For now, keep building models so you understand WHY it works."
- This lesson may span two sessions for some groups. A natural breakpoint is after Guided Practice — resume the next session with a quick review fold, then move into Independent Practice.
