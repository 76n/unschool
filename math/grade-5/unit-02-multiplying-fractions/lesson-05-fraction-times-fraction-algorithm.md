# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 2, Lesson 5

## Topic
Fraction x Fraction: The Algorithm

## Learning Objectives
- Students will state and apply the fraction multiplication algorithm: a/b x c/d = (a x c)/(b x d).
- Students will connect each step of the algorithm to the area model reasoning developed in Lesson 4.
- Students will multiply a variety of fractions (unit fractions, non-unit fractions, and fractions whose products require simplification).
- Students will simplify products to lowest terms using the GCF.
- Students will explain why fraction multiplication does NOT require common denominators (unlike fraction addition and subtraction).

## Prerequisites
- Ability to represent fraction multiplication using an area model (Lesson 4).
- Understanding that the overlap region in an area model equals (numerator x numerator) / (denominator x denominator) (Lesson 4).
- Fluency with basic multiplication facts through 12 x 12.
- Ability to find the GCF of two numbers and simplify fractions (Unit 1, Lesson 6).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Grid paper (optional, for students who want to verify with area models).
- Colored pencils (optional, for area model verification).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NF.4 — Apply and extend previous understandings of multiplication to multiply a fraction or whole number by a fraction.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate the area model reasoning from Lesson 4 and set the stage for formalizing the algorithm.

1. Write on the board: "2/3 x 3/5 = ?"
2. Ask: "Who can describe how we would solve this with an area model?" (Draw a 3 x 5 grid, shade 2 columns for 2/3, shade 3 rows for 3/5, count overlap: 6 out of 15.)
3. Write: "Overlap squares = 2 x 3 = 6. Total squares = 3 x 5 = 15. Product = 6/15 = 2/5."
4. Now ask: "What if I told you that you could get the same answer WITHOUT drawing the grid? Look at the numbers: where does the 6 come from? Where does the 15 come from?"
5. Guide students to articulate: "We multiplied the numerators (2 x 3 = 6) and multiplied the denominators (3 x 5 = 15)."
6. Say: "Today we are going to formalize that pattern as our official algorithm for multiplying fractions. You will no longer need to draw the model every time — but you should always be able to explain WHY the algorithm works by connecting it back to the model."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Algorithm** — a step-by-step procedure for solving a type of problem; here, the rule for multiplying two fractions.
- **Numerator** — the top number of a fraction; tells how many parts are counted.
- **Denominator** — the bottom number of a fraction; tells how many equal parts make up the whole.
- **Greatest Common Factor (GCF)** — the largest number that divides evenly into both the numerator and denominator; used to simplify fractions.
- **Simplest form (lowest terms)** — a fraction in which the numerator and denominator share no common factor other than 1.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — State the algorithm formally.**

Write on the board in a box:

**Fraction Multiplication Algorithm:**
a/b x c/d = (a x c) / (b x d)

"To multiply two fractions: multiply the numerators to get the new numerator, multiply the denominators to get the new denominator. Then simplify if possible."

**Step 2 — Connect the algorithm to the area model.**

Draw a quick sketch of a 3 x 5 grid on the board (the area model for 2/3 x 3/5 from the Warm-Up).

Point to the grid and say:

"The denominator of the first fraction (3) tells us how many columns. The denominator of the second fraction (5) tells us how many rows. So the total number of squares is denominator x denominator = 3 x 5 = 15. That is the denominator of our product."

"The numerator of the first fraction (2) tells us how many columns are shaded. The numerator of the second fraction (3) tells us how many rows are shaded. So the number of overlap squares is numerator x numerator = 2 x 3 = 6. That is the numerator of our product."

"The algorithm is not a magic trick — it is a shortcut for counting overlap squares in an area model."

**Step 3 — Apply the algorithm to unit fractions.**

Write: 1/4 x 1/3.

"Multiply the numerators: 1 x 1 = 1. Multiply the denominators: 4 x 3 = 12. Product: 1/12."

"Does this need simplifying? GCF of 1 and 12 is 1. Already in simplest form."

"Area model check: a 4 x 3 grid has 12 squares; shading 1 column and 1 row gives 1 overlap square. That confirms 1/12."

**Step 4 — Apply the algorithm to non-unit fractions.**

Write: 3/4 x 2/5.

"Multiply the numerators: 3 x 2 = 6. Multiply the denominators: 4 x 5 = 20. Product: 6/20."

"Simplify: GCF of 6 and 20 is 2. Divide both by 2: 6/20 = 3/10."

"Area model check: a 4 x 5 grid has 20 squares; shading 3 columns and 2 rows gives 6 overlap squares. 6/20 = 3/10. Confirmed."

**Step 5 — Apply the algorithm when the product simplifies to a familiar fraction.**

Write: 2/3 x 3/8.

"Multiply the numerators: 2 x 3 = 6. Multiply the denominators: 3 x 8 = 24. Product: 6/24."

"Simplify: GCF of 6 and 24 is 6. Divide both by 6: 6/24 = 1/4."

"Notice something interesting — the 3 in the numerator and the 3 in the denominator ended up canceling out. We will explore this idea more in the next lesson."

**Step 6 — Address the common denominators misconception.**

Write on the board in a box:

**Important: You do NOT need common denominators to multiply fractions.**

"When we add or subtract fractions, we need common denominators because we are combining same-sized pieces. But when we multiply fractions, we are finding a part of a part. The area model does not require the fractions to have the same denominator — we use one fraction for the columns and the other for the rows. The grid handles different denominators naturally."

Give a contrasting example: "Compare 1/3 + 1/4 with 1/3 x 1/4."
- "To add: we need a common denominator. 1/3 + 1/4 = 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12."
- "To multiply: we just multiply across. 1/3 x 1/4 = (1 x 1)/(3 x 4) = 1/12."
- "Different operations, different rules."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — 1/4 x 1/3**
1. Multiply numerators: 1 x 1 = 1.
2. Multiply denominators: 4 x 3 = 12.
3. Product: 1/12.
4. Simplify: GCF(1, 12) = 1. Already in simplest form.
**Answer: 1/4 x 1/3 = 1/12**

**Example B — 3/4 x 2/5**
1. Multiply numerators: 3 x 2 = 6.
2. Multiply denominators: 4 x 5 = 20.
3. Product: 6/20.
4. Simplify: GCF(6, 20) = 2. Divide both by 2: 6/20 = 3/10.
**Answer: 3/4 x 2/5 = 6/20 = 3/10**

**Example C — 2/3 x 3/8**
1. Multiply numerators: 2 x 3 = 6.
2. Multiply denominators: 3 x 8 = 24.
3. Product: 6/24.
4. Simplify: GCF(6, 24) = 6. Divide both by 6: 6/24 = 1/4.
**Answer: 2/3 x 3/8 = 6/24 = 1/4**

**Example D — 5/6 x 3/10**
1. Multiply numerators: 5 x 3 = 15.
2. Multiply denominators: 6 x 10 = 60.
3. Product: 15/60.
4. Simplify: GCF(15, 60) = 15. Divide both by 15: 15/60 = 1/4.
**Answer: 5/6 x 3/10 = 15/60 = 1/4**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Students apply the algorithm, then simplify to lowest terms.

1. 1/2 x 1/5 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Multiply the numerators: 1 x 1. Multiply the denominators: 2 x 5. Does the product need simplifying?"

2. 2/3 x 1/4 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is 2 x 1? What is 3 x 4? Write the product and check for common factors."

3. 3/5 x 2/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Multiply across: 3 x 2 and 5 x 3. Now simplify — what is the GCF of the numerator and denominator?"

4. 4/5 x 5/8 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Multiply across first. Then look at the product — the GCF may be larger than you expect. Check carefully."

5. 3/7 x 2/9 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "After you multiply, is the product already in simplest form, or can you simplify?"

> **Facilitator Tip:** After each problem, ask a student to explain what the multiplication would look like as an area model — even though they are not drawing it. For example, for 3/5 x 2/3: "We would have a 5-by-3 grid (15 squares), and the overlap would be 3 columns by 2 rows = 6 squares, giving 6/15 = 2/5." This verbal connection reinforces conceptual understanding while building fluency with the algorithm.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Part A — Algorithm Practice
Use the fraction multiplication algorithm. Show your multiplication and simplify each answer to lowest terms.

1. 1/3 x 1/5 = ?
2. 2/5 x 1/4 = ?
3. 3/8 x 2/3 = ?
4. 4/7 x 1/2 = ?

### Part B — Products That Need Simplifying
Multiply and simplify to lowest terms. Show the GCF you used to simplify.

5. 3/4 x 2/9 = ?
6. 5/6 x 2/5 = ?
7. 4/9 x 3/8 = ?
8. 2/3 x 3/10 = ?

### Part C — Reasoning and Application
9. Jamal says you need to find a common denominator before multiplying 2/3 x 1/4. His classmate Rosa says you do not. Who is correct? Explain why, and then solve the problem.
10. A rectangular sticker is 3/4 inch wide and 2/5 inch tall. What is the area of the sticker? Show your work.
11. Compute 2/3 x 3/4 and 3/4 x 2/3. What do you notice? What property of multiplication does this demonstrate?
12. Without computing, determine whether 5/8 x 2/7 is greater than, less than, or equal to 5/8. Explain your reasoning. Then multiply to verify.

### Answer Key

**Part A**
1. 1/3 x 1/5 = (1 x 1)/(3 x 5) = 1/15. GCF(1, 15) = 1; already in simplest form. **Answer: 1/15.**
2. 2/5 x 1/4 = (2 x 1)/(5 x 4) = 2/20. GCF(2, 20) = 2; 2/20 = 1/10. **Answer: 1/10.**
3. 3/8 x 2/3 = (3 x 2)/(8 x 3) = 6/24. GCF(6, 24) = 6; 6/24 = 1/4. **Answer: 1/4.**
4. 4/7 x 1/2 = (4 x 1)/(7 x 2) = 4/14. GCF(4, 14) = 2; 4/14 = 2/7. **Answer: 2/7.**

**Part B**
5. 3/4 x 2/9 = (3 x 2)/(4 x 9) = 6/36. GCF(6, 36) = 6; 6/36 = 1/6. **Answer: 1/6.**
6. 5/6 x 2/5 = (5 x 2)/(6 x 5) = 10/30. GCF(10, 30) = 10; 10/30 = 1/3. **Answer: 1/3.**
7. 4/9 x 3/8 = (4 x 3)/(9 x 8) = 12/72. GCF(12, 72) = 12; 12/72 = 1/6. **Answer: 1/6.**
8. 2/3 x 3/10 = (2 x 3)/(3 x 10) = 6/30. GCF(6, 30) = 6; 6/30 = 1/5. **Answer: 1/5.**

**Part C**
9. Rosa is correct. You do NOT need common denominators to multiply fractions. Common denominators are required for addition and subtraction (to combine same-sized pieces), but multiplication uses the algorithm a/b x c/d = (a x c)/(b x d). Solving: 2/3 x 1/4 = (2 x 1)/(3 x 4) = 2/12 = 1/6. **Answer: Rosa is correct; 2/3 x 1/4 = 1/6.**
10. Area = 3/4 x 2/5 = (3 x 2)/(4 x 5) = 6/20 = 3/10. **Answer: The area of the sticker is 3/10 square inch.**
11. 2/3 x 3/4 = 6/12 = 1/2. 3/4 x 2/3 = 6/12 = 1/2. Both products are the same. This demonstrates the **commutative property** of multiplication — the order of the factors does not change the product.
12. 5/8 x 2/7 is less than 5/8, because multiplying 5/8 by a fraction less than 1 (2/7 < 1) produces a smaller result — you are taking only 2/7 of 5/8. Verification: 5/8 x 2/7 = (5 x 2)/(8 x 7) = 10/56 = 5/28. Since 5/28 < 5/8, the prediction is correct. **Answer: Less than 5/8.**

## Assessment Check

### Exit Ticket
Solve the following problems using the fraction multiplication algorithm. Show your work and simplify to lowest terms.

**Problem 1:** 2/5 x 1/3 = ?
**Problem 2:** 3/8 x 4/9 = ?
**Problem 3:** Explain in one or two sentences why you do NOT need to find a common denominator when multiplying fractions.

**Scoring:**
- 3 out of 3 correct with work and explanation shown = Mastery demonstrated.
- 2 out of 3 correct = Approaching mastery; review the missed concept.
- 0-1 out of 3 correct = Reteach needed with area model support.

**Exit Ticket Answers:**
1. 2/5 x 1/3 = (2 x 1)/(5 x 3) = 2/15. GCF(2, 15) = 1; already in simplest form. **Answer: 2/15.**
2. 3/8 x 4/9 = (3 x 4)/(8 x 9) = 12/72. GCF(12, 72) = 12; 12/72 = 1/6. **Answer: 1/6.**
3. Sample response: "When we multiply fractions, we are finding a part of a part, not combining same-sized pieces. The algorithm multiplies numerators and denominators directly, so the denominators do not need to match. Common denominators are only needed for addition and subtraction."

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Return to the area model from Lesson 4. Have the student draw the grid, shade, and count for 2-3 problems, then write the algorithm steps alongside each model to see the direct connection.
- Provide a step-by-step reference card: "1. Multiply the numerators. 2. Multiply the denominators. 3. Find the GCF. 4. Divide both by the GCF to simplify."
- Pair the student with a peer tutor to work through 3 additional problems side by side, alternating between model and algorithm.
- If the error is specifically in simplifying (the multiplication is correct but the simplification is wrong), review GCF-finding strategies from Unit 1, Lesson 6.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may create a set of 4 fraction multiplication problems with an answer key and trade with a partner.

## Extension

- **Multiplication vs. Addition Challenge:** Create a two-column poster. On the left, solve 2/3 + 3/4 (requires common denominators). On the right, solve 2/3 x 3/4 (does not require common denominators). Explain in your own words why the two operations require different approaches.
- **Missing Factor Puzzles:** Find the missing fraction: "? x 2/3 = 2/15." Solve by thinking: what fraction times 2/3 gives 2/15? Use the algorithm backward. (Answer: 1/5, because (1 x 2)/(5 x 3) = 2/15.)
- **Simplification Scavenger Hunt:** Given a list of 8 fraction multiplication problems, predict BEFORE multiplying which products will need simplifying and which are already in simplest form. Then compute to check your predictions. (Hint: if a numerator and a denominator from opposite fractions share a common factor, the product will need simplifying.)

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Adding numerators and/or denominators instead of multiplying.** For example, writing 2/3 x 1/4 = 3/7 (adding across). Redirect by asking: "Does 3/7 match the area model? A 3-by-4 grid has 12 squares, and the overlap is 2 x 1 = 2. The answer should be 2/12, not 3/7."
- **Finding common denominators before multiplying.** Students may carry over the addition/subtraction habit and convert 2/3 x 1/4 to 8/12 x 3/12 before multiplying. While this eventually yields the correct answer (24/144 = 1/6), it creates unnecessarily large numbers. Emphasize: "Multiplication does not require common denominators. Multiply the fractions as they are."
- **Forgetting to simplify.** Students may leave answers as 6/24 or 10/30. Build the habit: "After multiplying, always ask — do the numerator and denominator share a common factor?"
- **Incorrect GCF leading to partial simplification.** For example, simplifying 12/72 to 6/36 (dividing by 2 instead of by 12). Encourage students to find the GCF first, or to simplify in steps until no more common factors remain.
- **Confusing this algorithm with whole-number x fraction from Lessons 1-2.** Some students may try to multiply only one numerator. Remind them: "When both numbers are fractions, multiply BOTH numerators and BOTH denominators."

### Pacing Advice
- This lesson is algorithm-focused and moves faster than Lessons 3-4 because the conceptual groundwork has been laid. If students are confident with the area model connection, spend less time on Step 2 and more time on Guided Practice.
- If many students are struggling with simplification rather than with the multiplication itself, pause and do a brief GCF review (3-4 minutes) before moving to Independent Practice.
- Part B of Independent Practice is the most important section for this lesson — it builds fluency with simplification after multiplication. If time is limited, prioritize Part B over Part A, since Part A problems are simpler.
- For students who finish early, the Extension activity "Simplification Scavenger Hunt" directly previews Lesson 6, where students will learn to simplify before multiplying (cross-canceling).
