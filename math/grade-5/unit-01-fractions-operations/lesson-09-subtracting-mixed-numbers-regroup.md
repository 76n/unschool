# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic

Subtracting Mixed Numbers (With Regrouping)

## Learning Objectives

- Students will recognize when regrouping (borrowing) is necessary in mixed-number subtraction.
- Students will regroup a mixed number by borrowing 1 from the whole number and adding it to the fraction part.
- Students will subtract mixed numbers with regrouping for both like and unlike denominators.
- Students will express all answers in simplest form.

## Prerequisites

- Subtracting mixed numbers without regrouping (Lesson 8)
- Understanding of improper fractions and converting between mixed numbers and improper fractions
- Finding least common denominators
- Simplifying fractions (Lesson 6)

## Materials Needed

- Whiteboard and markers
- Fraction strips or base-ten-style fraction blocks
- Student math journals
- Pencils
- Printed practice worksheet (Independent Practice problems)
- Exit ticket slips
- Number line display (optional, for visual modeling)

**Standards:** 5.NF.1

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review converting whole numbers into fractional form and set up the concept of "borrowing" a whole to make the fraction part larger.

1. Write on the board: "Rewrite the number 1 as a fraction with a denominator of 4."
   - Answer: 4/4
2. "Rewrite 1 as a fraction with a denominator of 6."
   - Answer: 6/6
3. "Rewrite 1 as a fraction with a denominator of 3."
   - Answer: 3/3
4. Now write: "Rewrite 5 1/4 so that the fraction part is larger. Hint: borrow 1 from the 5."
   - 5 1/4 = 4 + 1 + 1/4 = 4 + 4/4 + 1/4 = 4 5/4.
5. Say: "Today we are going to learn why this trick matters. Sometimes when we subtract mixed numbers, the fraction part is too small to subtract from, so we need to borrow."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary

- **Regroup (borrow):** To take 1 whole from the whole-number part and convert it into a fraction to add to the existing fraction part, making the fraction larger so subtraction is possible.
- **Improper fraction (temporary):** During regrouping, the fraction part will temporarily be greater than 1 (e.g., 5/4) — this is intentional and part of the process.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Show why regrouping is needed.**

Write: 5 1/4 - 2 3/4

Ask: "Can we subtract 3/4 from 1/4?" (No — 1/4 is smaller than 3/4.)

"This is like when we subtract whole numbers and need to borrow. For example, in 52 - 27, we can't subtract 7 from 2, so we borrow from the tens place. Same idea here — we borrow from the whole number."

**Step 2: Teach the regrouping process step by step.**

Write the key example on the board:

5 1/4 - 2 3/4

- Step A: Notice that 1/4 < 3/4, so we need to regroup.
- Step B: Borrow 1 from the 5. 5 becomes 4. The borrowed 1 = 4/4.
- Step C: Add the borrowed fraction to the existing fraction: 4/4 + 1/4 = 5/4.
- Step D: Rewrite: 5 1/4 = **4 5/4**.
- Step E: Now subtract: 4 5/4 - 2 3/4.
  - Wholes: 4 - 2 = 2.
  - Fractions: 5/4 - 3/4 = 2/4 = 1/2.
- Answer: **2 1/2**

**Step 3: Model an example with unlike denominators.**

Write: 6 1/3 - 2 3/4

- First, find the LCD of 3 and 4. LCD = 12.
- Convert: 6 4/12 - 2 9/12.
- Notice: 4/12 < 9/12, so regroup.
- Borrow 1 from 6: 6 becomes 5. Borrowed 1 = 12/12. Add: 12/12 + 4/12 = 16/12.
- Rewrite: 6 4/12 = 5 16/12.
- Subtract: 5 16/12 - 2 9/12.
  - Wholes: 5 - 2 = 3.
  - Fractions: 16/12 - 9/12 = 7/12.
- Check: GCF of 7 and 12 is 1 — already simplified.
- Answer: **3 7/12**

**Step 4: Model another example and simplify the result.**

Write: 8 1/6 - 3 2/3

- LCD of 6 and 3 is 6. Convert: 8 1/6 - 3 4/6.
- Notice: 1/6 < 4/6, so regroup.
- Borrow 1 from 8: 8 becomes 7. Borrowed 1 = 6/6. Add: 6/6 + 1/6 = 7/6.
- Rewrite: 8 1/6 = 7 7/6.
- Subtract: 7 7/6 - 3 4/6.
  - Wholes: 7 - 3 = 4.
  - Fractions: 7/6 - 4/6 = 3/6 = 1/2.
- Answer: **4 1/2**

**Step 5: Summarize the process.**

Write on the board:
"To subtract mixed numbers with regrouping:
1. Find a common denominator if needed.
2. Compare the fractions — if the top fraction is smaller, regroup.
3. Borrow 1 from the whole number and add it to the fraction (as denominator/denominator).
4. Subtract whole numbers and fractions.
5. Simplify."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** 7 1/5 - 4 3/5
- 1/5 < 3/5 → regroup. Borrow 1: 7 becomes 6. 5/5 + 1/5 = 6/5.
- 6 6/5 - 4 3/5. Wholes: 2. Fractions: 6/5 - 3/5 = 3/5.
- Answer: **2 3/5**

**Example B:** 9 1/8 - 5 1/2
- LCD = 8. Convert: 9 1/8 - 5 4/8.
- 1/8 < 4/8 → regroup. Borrow 1: 9 becomes 8. 8/8 + 1/8 = 9/8.
- 8 9/8 - 5 4/8. Wholes: 3. Fractions: 9/8 - 4/8 = 5/8.
- Answer: **3 5/8**

**Example C:** 4 1/4 - 1 5/6
- LCD = 12. Convert: 4 3/12 - 1 10/12.
- 3/12 < 10/12 → regroup. Borrow 1: 4 becomes 3. 12/12 + 3/12 = 15/12.
- 3 15/12 - 1 10/12. Wholes: 2. Fractions: 15/12 - 10/12 = 5/12.
- Answer: **2 5/12**

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

Solve the following problems. Show all work. Write answers in simplest form.

1. 5 1/4 - 2 3/4
2. 7 1/3 - 3 5/6

**Scoring:**
- 2/2 correct with work shown = Mastery
- 1/2 correct = Approaching mastery
- 0/2 correct = Needs reteaching

### If Mastery Is Not Met

- Use fraction strip manipulatives to physically model the regrouping. Build 5 1/4 with strips. Show that you cannot remove 3/4 from the 1/4 strip. Then "break apart" one whole strip into four 1/4 strips, giving you 4 wholes and 5/4. Now remove 3/4.
- Provide a visual regrouping guide that shows the borrowing step in a box diagram (similar to borrowing in whole-number subtraction).
- Practice regrouping without subtracting first: "Rewrite 3 1/5 with regrouping" (= 2 6/5), "Rewrite 6 2/8 with regrouping" (= 5 10/8). Then attempt full subtraction.

### If Mastery Is Met

- Challenge: "I subtracted two mixed numbers with regrouping and got 4 5/8. The number I subtracted was 6 7/8. What was the original number?" (Answer: 11 1/2, since 4 5/8 + 6 7/8 = 10 12/8 = 10 + 1 4/8 = 11 1/2.)
- Have the student create a "Regrouping or Not?" sorting activity: write 6 mixed-number subtraction problems and label each as "regroup" or "no regroup" before solving.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors

- **Subtracting the larger fraction from the smaller without regrouping.** Students may write 5 1/4 - 2 3/4 = 3 2/4, reasoning that 3/4 - 1/4 = 2/4 and ignoring which fraction is on top. Emphasize that we always subtract the second number from the first — if the fraction part of the first is too small, we must regroup.
- **Forgetting to decrease the whole number by 1 after borrowing.** Students borrow 1 and add it to the fraction but leave the whole number unchanged. Use a visual check: circle the whole number, cross it out, write the new number above it.
- **Incorrect borrowing amount.** Students may borrow 1 and write it as the wrong fraction (e.g., borrowing 1 but writing 5/4 when the denominator is 6). Remind: "Borrow 1, which equals denominator/denominator."
- **Not converting to a common denominator before deciding whether to regroup.** Students must compare fractions with the same denominator to know if regrouping is needed.

### Pacing Advice

- Spend significant time on Step 2 (the detailed regrouping process). This is the conceptual crux of the lesson. Use multiple representations: fraction strips, number line, and numerical.
- If students grasp the like-denominator regrouping quickly (Step 2), move to unlike denominators (Step 3) with confidence. If not, do one more like-denominator example before proceeding.
- Guided Practice problems 1-2 (like denominators) should confirm understanding before problems 3-5 (unlike denominators) add the extra step.
- If time is tight, assign the ★★ and ★★★ problems as homework, but ensure all students complete at least the ★ On-Level problems in class.
