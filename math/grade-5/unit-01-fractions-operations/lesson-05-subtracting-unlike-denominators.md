# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 5

## Topic
Subtracting Fractions with Unlike Denominators

## Learning Objectives
- Students will subtract two fractions with unlike denominators by finding the LCD, converting to equivalent fractions, and subtracting the numerators.
- Students will express differences in simplest form.
- Students will compare the process for subtracting unlike fractions with the process for adding unlike fractions and identify what stays the same and what changes.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to add fractions with unlike denominators using the 4-step process (Lesson 4).
- Ability to find the least common denominator of two fractions (Lesson 3).
- Ability to generate equivalent fractions (Lesson 2).
- Ability to subtract fractions with like denominators (Lesson 1).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Fraction strips (1 set per student).
- "4-Step Process" chart from Lesson 4 (updated with "add or subtract" in Step 3).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NF.1 — Add and subtract fractions with unlike denominators (including mixed numbers) by replacing given fractions with equivalent fractions in such a way as to produce an equivalent sum or difference of fractions with like denominators.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review the 4-step addition process from Lesson 4 and prime students to see that subtraction follows the same structure.

1. Write on the board: 1/3 + 1/4 = ?
2. Ask students to solve it using the 4-step process. (LCD = 12; 4/12 + 3/12 = 7/12.)
3. Now write underneath: 1/3 - 1/4 = ?
4. Ask: "What would change in our process if the operation were subtraction instead of addition?" Give 30 seconds of think time.
5. Take responses. Guide students toward the insight: "The only thing that changes is Step 3 — instead of adding the numerators, we subtract them. Everything else stays exactly the same."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Difference** — the result of subtracting one number from another.
- **Unlike denominators** — fractions with different denominators.
- **LCD (Least Common Denominator)** — the smallest number that both denominators divide into evenly.
- **Simplest form** — a fraction whose numerator and denominator share no common factor other than 1.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Present the updated 4-step process.**
Post or update the process chart:

1. Find the LCD of the two denominators.
2. Rewrite each fraction as an equivalent fraction with the LCD.
3. Subtract the numerators. Keep the denominator the same.
4. Simplify the difference if possible.

"Notice that the only change from our addition process is the word 'subtract' in Step 3. Steps 1, 2, and 4 are identical."

**Step 2 — Model the anchor problem: 3/4 - 1/3.**
- Step 1: LCD of 4 and 3. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12. Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12. LCD = 12.
- Step 2: 3/4 = 9/12 and 1/3 = 4/12.
- Step 3: 9/12 - 4/12 = 5/12.
- Step 4: 5 and 12 share no common factor other than 1. Final answer: 5/12.

**Step 3 — Verify with fraction strips.**
Lay out 3/4 using fraction strips (three 1/4 pieces). Show that this is the same as 9/12 (nine 1/12 pieces). Remove 4/12 (four 1/12 pieces, equivalent to 1/3). Count what remains: 5 twelfths. Confirm the answer: 5/12.

**Step 4 — Model a problem where simplifying is needed: 5/6 - 1/4.**
- Step 1: LCD of 6 and 4. Multiples of 6: 6, 12. Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12. LCD = 12.
- Step 2: 5/6 = 10/12 and 1/4 = 3/12.
- Step 3: 10/12 - 3/12 = 7/12.
- Step 4: 7 and 12 share no common factor other than 1. Final answer: 7/12.

**Step 5 — Model a problem where simplifying is needed: 3/4 - 1/6.**
- Step 1: LCD of 4 and 6 = 12.
- Step 2: 3/4 = 9/12 and 1/6 = 2/12.
- Step 3: 9/12 - 2/12 = 7/12.
- Step 4: 7/12 is in simplest form. Final answer: 7/12.

### Worked Examples

**Example A — 3/4 - 1/3**
1. LCD of 4 and 3 = 12
2. 3/4 = 9/12; 1/3 = 4/12
3. 9/12 - 4/12 = 5/12
4. 5/12 is in simplest form.
**Answer: 5/12**

**Example B — 5/6 - 1/4**
1. LCD of 6 and 4 = 12
2. 5/6 = 10/12; 1/4 = 3/12
3. 10/12 - 3/12 = 7/12
4. 7/12 is in simplest form.
**Answer: 7/12**

**Example C — 7/8 - 1/6**
1. LCD of 8 and 6 = 24
2. 7/8 = 21/24; 1/6 = 4/24
3. 21/24 - 4/24 = 17/24
4. 17/24 is in simplest form.
**Answer: 17/24**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Students attempt each problem first, then discuss.

1. 1/2 - 1/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is the LCD of 2 and 3? Convert both fractions, then subtract."

2. 3/5 - 1/4 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is the LCD of 5 and 4? Remember to convert both fractions."

3. 2/3 - 1/4 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "After subtracting, check: is your answer in simplest form?"

4. 5/6 - 1/3 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Is 6 already a multiple of 3? Do you need to convert both fractions?"

5. 7/10 - 1/4 = ?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What is the LCD of 10 and 4? Be careful with the conversion."

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket
Solve the following two problems. Show all four steps of your work.

**Problem 1:** 2/3 - 1/5 = ?
**Problem 2:** 5/8 - 1/3 = ?

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct with all steps shown = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct or correct answer but steps missing = Partial understanding; review the process.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Identify the specific step causing difficulty (LCD, conversion, subtraction of numerators, or simplification).
- If the issue is specific to subtraction (e.g., the student added instead of subtracted), highlight Step 3 on the process chart and practice three problems where the student circles the operation sign before solving.
- Pair the student with a partner for structured practice using the 4-step process card.
- Provide additional problems with smaller denominators (halves, thirds, fourths) before introducing larger ones.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may create a "How to Add and Subtract Unlike Fractions" mini-poster that could be displayed in the classroom.

## Extension (Optional)
- **Mixed Operations Challenge:** Give students a set of 8 problems that randomly alternate between addition and subtraction of unlike fractions. The key skill is reading the operation sign carefully.
- **Fraction Difference Puzzle:** "I am thinking of two fractions with different denominators. Their difference is 1/6. What could the two fractions be?" Find at least three different pairs.
- **Real-World Connection:** A recipe calls for 3/4 cup of milk, but you have already poured 1/3 cup. How much more milk do you need to add?

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Subtracting the smaller numerator from the larger, regardless of order.** For example, computing 1/3 - 1/2 as 1/6 by subtracting 2 from 3 in the denominators, or confusing which numerator goes first after converting. Emphasize: "The first fraction is the one you start with. Subtract the second fraction from it."
- **Carrying over addition habits.** Some students accidentally add the numerators instead of subtracting because they have just spent a full lesson on addition. Remind them to circle the operation sign before computing.
- **Negative results confusion.** If a student sets up a problem like 1/4 - 1/3 and gets a negative numerator, address it: "In this lesson, we always subtract a smaller fraction from a larger one. Check that the first fraction is larger. If not, reread the problem." (Negative fractions are beyond Grade 5 scope.)
- **Forgetting to simplify.** Same as in Lesson 4 — encourage students to always check for common factors.

### Pacing Advice
- Because the process is nearly identical to Lesson 4, students should move through Direct Instruction more quickly. Plan to spend more time on Guided and Independent Practice.
- If most students demonstrate confidence during the Warm-Up, abbreviate Direct Instruction to one worked example and go straight into Guided Practice.
- Use the Interleaved Review problems to check whether students can switch between operations (addition vs. subtraction). If students consistently add when they should subtract (or vice versa), consider a brief "operation sort" activity where they categorize problems by operation before solving.
- This lesson completes the core skill sequence of Unit 1. Consider using the next session for mixed review and assessment preparation.
