# Math — Grade 5 — Unit 1, Lesson 3

## Topic
Finding the Least Common Denominator (LCD)

## Learning Objectives
- Students will find the least common multiple (LCM) of two denominators using the list-multiples method.
- Students will identify the least common denominator (LCD) of two fractions.
- Students will explain why a common denominator is needed before adding or subtracting fractions with unlike denominators.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to generate equivalent fractions by multiplying numerator and denominator by the same number (Lesson 2).
- Ability to add and subtract fractions with like denominators (Lesson 1).
- Fluency with multiplication facts through 12 x 12.
- Understanding of the term "multiple" (e.g., multiples of 3 are 3, 6, 9, 12, ...).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Fraction strips (1 set per student).
- Two different-colored markers or pencils for listing multiples.
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 5.NF.1 — Add and subtract fractions with unlike denominators (including mixed numbers) by replacing given fractions with equivalent fractions in such a way as to produce an equivalent sum or difference of fractions with like denominators.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review the concept of multiples and build readiness for the list-multiples method.

1. Write on the board: "List the first six multiples of 4." Have students write their answers on personal whiteboards. (4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24.)
2. Now: "List the first six multiples of 6." (6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36.)
3. Ask: "Do the two lists share any numbers?" (Yes — 12 and 24.)
4. Ask: "What is the smallest number that appears in both lists?" (12.)
5. Tell students: "That number — 12 — is called the Least Common Multiple, or LCM. Today we will use the LCM to find the Least Common Denominator for fractions."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Multiple** — the product of a whole number and a counting number (e.g., multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, ...).
- **Least Common Multiple (LCM)** — the smallest number that is a multiple of two (or more) given numbers.
- **Least Common Denominator (LCD)** — the LCM of the denominators of two or more fractions. It is the smallest denominator that allows us to rewrite both fractions with like denominators.
- **Unlike denominators** — fractions whose denominators are different (e.g., 1/3 and 1/4).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Motivate with a concrete problem.**
Hold up fraction strips for 1/3 and 1/4. Place them next to each other.
Ask: "Can we add these two pieces directly? Why or why not?" (No — the pieces are different sizes, so we cannot just count them together.)
"We need to find a common-size piece — a common denominator — so we can combine them fairly."

**Step 2 — Introduce the list-multiples method.**
"To find the LCD of 1/3 and 1/4, we find the LCM of 3 and 4."
- Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, **12**, 15, 18, 21, **24**, ...
- Multiples of 4: 4, 8, **12**, 16, 20, **24**, ...
- Common multiples: 12, 24, ...
- The smallest is 12, so the LCD is 12.

**Step 3 — Show a second example.**
"Find the LCD of 1/6 and 1/8."
- Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, **24**, 30, 36, 42, **48**, ...
- Multiples of 8: 8, 16, **24**, 32, 40, **48**, ...
- Common multiples: 24, 48, ...
- The LCD is 24.

**Step 4 — Connect LCD back to equivalent fractions.**
"Once we know the LCD of 1/3 and 1/4 is 12, we rewrite each fraction with denominator 12."
- 1/3 = (1 x 4)/(3 x 4) = 4/12
- 1/4 = (1 x 3)/(4 x 3) = 3/12
"Now both fractions have like denominators, and we could add or subtract them." (Full addition/subtraction will be the focus of Lessons 4 and 5.)

**Step 5 — Discuss why we want the *least* common denominator.**
"Could we use 24 instead of 12? Yes — 24 is also a common multiple. But 12 is simpler to work with. Using the LCD keeps numbers smaller and makes simplifying easier."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — LCD of 1/3 and 1/4**
- Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, ...
- Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, ...
- LCD = 12
- Rewrite: 1/3 = 4/12 and 1/4 = 3/12

**Example B — LCD of 1/6 and 1/8**
- Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, ...
- Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24, ...
- LCD = 24
- Rewrite: 1/6 = 4/24 and 1/8 = 3/24

**Example C — LCD of 2/5 and 3/10**
- Multiples of 5: 5, 10, ...
- Multiples of 10: 10, ...
- LCD = 10
- Rewrite: 2/5 = 4/10 (3/10 already has denominator 10)

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

Work through these problems together. Students attempt each problem first, then discuss.

1. Find the LCD of 1/2 and 1/5.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "List the first five multiples of 2, then the first five multiples of 5. Where do the lists first overlap?"

2. Find the LCD of 1/4 and 1/6.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Keep listing multiples of 4 and 6 until you find a match."

3. Find the LCD of 2/3 and 5/9.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Look at the multiples of 3 and 9. Is 3 already a factor of 9?"

4. Rewrite 1/4 and 1/6 as equivalent fractions with the LCD you found in Problem 2.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "What do you multiply each denominator by to reach the LCD?"

5. Rewrite 2/3 and 5/9 as equivalent fractions with the LCD you found in Problem 3.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "One of these fractions may already have the LCD as its denominator."

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### ★ On-Level
1. Find the LCD of 1/3 and 1/5.
2. Find the LCD of 1/4 and 1/10.
3. Rewrite 1/3 and 1/5 using the LCD from Problem 1.
4. Rewrite 1/4 and 1/10 using the LCD from Problem 2.

### ★★ Stretch
5. Find the LCD of 3/8 and 1/6.
6. Rewrite 3/8 and 1/6 using the LCD from Problem 5.
7. Jasmine says the LCD of 1/4 and 1/8 is 32 because 4 x 8 = 32. Is she correct? What is the actual LCD? Explain.

### ★★★ Challenge
8. Find the LCD of 1/4, 1/6, and 1/3 (three fractions). Rewrite all three with the LCD.
9. Without listing multiples, can you find the LCD of 1/5 and 1/7? Explain your strategy.

### Interleaved Review
*(Reviewing: Like denominators — Lesson 1; Equivalent fractions — Lesson 2)*
10. 5/8 + 1/8 = ?
11. Write two fractions equivalent to 3/5.

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket
Solve the following two problems. Show your work.

**Problem 1:** Find the LCD of 1/3 and 1/8.
**Problem 2:** Rewrite 1/3 and 1/8 as equivalent fractions using the LCD.

**Scoring:**
- Both problems correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- LCD correct but conversion has an error = Partial understanding; review equivalent fractions.
- LCD incorrect = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Reteach the list-multiples method one-on-one using color-coded lists: write multiples of the first denominator in blue and multiples of the second in red, then circle matches.
- Provide a number line from 1 to 50 and have students highlight multiples of each denominator.
- Review equivalent fractions (Lesson 2) if the conversion step is the issue.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Students may begin the Extension activity below.
- Students may attempt finding LCDs with larger denominators (e.g., LCD of 7 and 9).

## Extension (Optional)
- **LCD Bingo:** Create bingo cards where each square has a pair of denominators (e.g., "3 and 5"). The caller announces an LCD (e.g., "15"). Students cover the matching square. First to get five in a row wins.
- **Predict and Check:** Before listing multiples, have students predict the LCD. Is it always the product of the two denominators? When is it smaller? Explore cases like 4 and 8, 3 and 9, and 6 and 10.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Using the product instead of the LCM.** Students often multiply the two denominators (e.g., LCD of 4 and 6 = 24 instead of 12). While the product always works as a common denominator, it is not always the *least*. Encourage students to list multiples and find the smallest overlap. Emphasize that using the LCD keeps numbers smaller and simpler.
- **Stopping too early when listing multiples.** Some students list only 2-3 multiples and give up if there is no match. Remind them to keep going until they find the first common multiple.
- **Confusing LCM with GCF.** Reinforce that the LCM is the *smallest shared multiple* (we are looking for a number both denominators go into), not the *greatest common factor*.
- **Forgetting to convert both fractions.** After finding the LCD, some students convert only one fraction. Remind them that both fractions need to be rewritten with the new denominator.

### Pacing Advice
- The list-multiples method is the primary strategy at this level. More advanced methods (prime factorization) can be introduced later in the year if time allows, but are not required for 5.NF.1.
- If students find the Warm-Up easy, spend less time there and more on Step 4 (converting fractions using the LCD), which is the bridge to Lessons 4 and 5.
- If many students struggle with listing multiples, pause and do a quick fluency drill: call out a number and have students rapid-fire list its first five multiples.
- Do not rush this lesson. Solid understanding of LCD is essential for the addition and subtraction lessons that follow.

## Answer Key

### Guided Practice
1. LCD of 1/2 and 1/5: Multiples of 2: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10; Multiples of 5: 5, 10. LCD = 10.
2. LCD of 1/4 and 1/6: Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12; Multiples of 6: 6, 12. LCD = 12.
3. LCD of 2/3 and 5/9: Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9; Multiples of 9: 9. LCD = 9.
4. 1/4 = 3/12 and 1/6 = 2/12 (using LCD = 12 from Problem 2).
5. 2/3 = 6/9 and 5/9 = 5/9 (using LCD = 9 from Problem 3; 5/9 already has the LCD).

### Independent Practice

**★ On-Level**
1. LCD of 1/3 and 1/5: Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15; Multiples of 5: 5, 10, 15. LCD = 15.
2. LCD of 1/4 and 1/10: Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20; Multiples of 10: 10, 20. LCD = 20.
3. 1/3 = 5/15 and 1/5 = 3/15.
4. 1/4 = 5/20 and 1/10 = 2/20.

**★★ Stretch**
5. LCD of 3/8 and 1/6: Multiples of 8: 8, 16, 24; Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24. LCD = 24.
6. 3/8 = 9/24 and 1/6 = 4/24.
7. Jasmine is not correct. The LCD of 1/4 and 1/8 is 8, not 32. Since 8 is already a multiple of 4 (4 x 2 = 8), the LCD is just 8. The product (32) is a common denominator but not the least one.

**★★★ Challenge**
8. LCD of 1/4, 1/6, and 1/3: Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12; Multiples of 6: 6, 12; Multiples of 3: 3, 6, 9, 12. LCD = 12. Rewrite: 1/4 = 3/12, 1/6 = 2/12, 1/3 = 4/12.
9. Since 5 and 7 share no common factors other than 1 (they are relatively prime), the LCD is their product: 5 x 7 = 35. When two denominators share no common factor, the LCD equals the product of the two numbers.

11. Two fractions equivalent to 3/5: 6/10 and 9/15 (accept any correct equivalents).
