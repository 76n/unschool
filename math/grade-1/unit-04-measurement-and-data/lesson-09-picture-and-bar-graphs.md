# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 4, Lesson 9

## Topic
Picture Graphs and Bar Graphs

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read and interpret a picture graph where each symbol represents 1
- Read and interpret a bar graph by finding the height of each bar
- Create a simple bar graph from tally chart data
- Explain what a graph shows using complete sentences

## Prerequisites
- Record and read data using tally marks and tally charts (Lesson 8)
- Count and compare quantities within 20 (Unit 1)
- Understand "more" and "fewer" when comparing groups (Unit 1)

## Materials Needed
- Paper and pencil
- Crayons or colored pencils
- Grid paper or graph paper (1-inch squares)
- Pre-made picture graph and bar graph examples (drawn or printed)
- Tally chart from Lesson 8 (or a new one prepared for this lesson)
- Small stickers or stamps (optional, for creating picture graphs)
- Ruler or straightedge (for drawing neat bars)

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce picture graphs and bar graphs as visual ways to show the same data from a tally chart.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Picture graph (pictograph):** A graph that uses small pictures or symbols to show data — each picture stands for 1
- **Bar graph:** A graph that uses bars (colored rectangles) to show data — the taller the bar, the greater the number
- **Axis:** The lines along the bottom and side of a bar graph that help us read the numbers and labels
- **Title:** The name at the top of a graph that tells us what the data is about

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Introduce picture graphs.**
Draw a simple picture graph on paper using the ice cream data from the warm-up:

**Our Favorite Ice Cream Flavor**

| Flavor | Pictures |
|--------|----------|
| Vanilla | (cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone) |
| Chocolate | (cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone)(cone) |
| Strawberry | (cone)(cone)(cone)(cone) |

Each (cone) = 1 person

"This is called a picture graph. Instead of tally marks, we draw a small picture for each person. Each little ice cream cone stands for 1 person."

"How many people chose vanilla? Let's count the cones: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8. Eight people!"

"Notice that the pictures are lined up in neat rows. That makes it easy to SEE which row is longest — that is the most popular flavor."

**Step 2: Introduce bar graphs.**
Draw a simple bar graph using the same data on grid paper:

"Now I am going to show the SAME data a different way — with a bar graph."

Draw the axes: categories (Vanilla, Chocolate, Strawberry) along the bottom, numbers (0 through 10) up the left side.

"Along the bottom, I write the flavor names. Up the side, I write numbers — these help me know how tall to make each bar."

Color in bars: Vanilla up to 8, Chocolate up to 10, Strawberry up to 4.

"For vanilla, 8 people chose it, so I color the bar up to the line for 8. For chocolate, I go all the way up to 10. For strawberry, only up to 4."

"Now I can LOOK at the bars and instantly see which is tallest. Chocolate has the tallest bar — it is the most popular!"

**Step 3: Demonstrate building a bar graph from tally data.**
"Let me show you how to turn a tally chart INTO a bar graph, step by step."

1. "First, I draw my two lines — one along the bottom, one going up."
2. "Along the bottom, I write each category from my tally chart."
3. "Up the side, I write numbers starting at 0. I go as high as my biggest number."
4. "Now I read my tally chart. Vanilla = 8, so I color the vanilla bar up to 8."
5. "Chocolate = 10, so I color up to 10."
6. "Strawberry = 4, so I color up to 4."
7. "Last, I give my graph a title: Our Favorite Ice Cream Flavor."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** Reading a picture graph.

**Pets in Our Class**

| Pet | Pictures |
|-----|----------|
| Dog | (paw)(paw)(paw)(paw)(paw)(paw) |
| Cat | (paw)(paw)(paw)(paw)(paw)(paw)(paw) |
| Fish | (paw)(paw)(paw) |

Each (paw) = 1 student

"How many students have a dog? I count 6 paw prints. How many have a cat? 7. How many have a fish? 3. Which pet is most popular? Cat, because it has the most pictures — 7."

**Example B:** Reading a bar graph.
A bar graph titled "Favorite Sport" shows: Soccer bar reaches 5, Basketball bar reaches 8, Baseball bar reaches 3.

"Which sport is the most popular? I look for the tallest bar. Basketball reaches up to 8 — that is the tallest. So basketball is the most popular. Baseball has the shortest bar at 3, so it is the least popular."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student reads graphs and builds a bar graph with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Answer questions about a picture graph.**
Present a picture graph:

**Favorite Fruit**

| Fruit | Pictures |
|-------|----------|
| Apple | (star)(star)(star)(star)(star) |
| Banana | (star)(star)(star)(star)(star)(star)(star) |
| Orange | (star)(star)(star) |
| Grapes | (star)(star)(star)(star)(star)(star) |

Each (star) = 1 person

1. "How many people chose apple?" (5)
2. "How many people chose banana?" (7)
3. "Which fruit was chosen the MOST?" (Banana)
4. "Which fruit was chosen the LEAST?" (Orange)
5. "How many people were asked in all?" (5 + 7 + 3 + 6 = 21)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student struggles to count the symbols, suggest lining up a finger under each picture and counting aloud.*

**Activity 2: Build a bar graph from a tally chart.**
Give the student this tally chart and grid paper:

| Color | Tallies | Total |
|-------|---------|-------|
| Red | ~~|||||~~ | | 6 |
| Blue | | | | | 4 |
| Green | ~~|||||~~ | | | | 9 |
| Yellow | | | | 3 |

"Use grid paper to create a bar graph of this data. Remember: labels on the bottom, numbers on the side, color each bar to the right height, and give it a title."

Walk the student through each step. Check that:
- Numbers on the side go from 0 to at least 9
- Bars reach the correct height (Red = 6, Blue = 4, Green = 9, Yellow = 3)
- The graph has a title

*Facilitator prompt: "What is the tallest number you need on your graph? That tells you how high your number line needs to go."*

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
Use this bar graph to answer questions. The bar graph "Favorite Animal" shows: Dog = 5, Cat = 3, Bird = 2, Fish = 4.

1. How many people chose dog? (5)
2. How many people chose cat? (3)
3. Which animal was chosen the most? (Dog)
4. Which animal was chosen the least? (Bird)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
Use this picture graph to answer questions.

**Books We Read This Week**

| Day | Pictures |
|-----|----------|
| Monday | (book)(book)(book) |
| Tuesday | (book)(book)(book)(book)(book) |
| Wednesday | (book)(book) |
| Thursday | (book)(book)(book)(book) |
| Friday | (book)(book)(book)(book)(book)(book) |

Each (book) = 1 book

1. How many books were read on Friday? (6)
2. On which day were the MOST books read? (Friday)
3. On which day were the FEWEST books read? (Wednesday)
4. How many books were read on Monday and Tuesday combined? (3 + 5 = 8)
5. Create a bar graph on grid paper using this same data. Label the days on the bottom and numbers on the side. Give it a title. (Bar graph with bars: Mon = 3, Tue = 5, Wed = 2, Thu = 4, Fri = 6.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
1. Here is a tally chart. Create both a picture graph AND a bar graph for this data.

   | Vehicle | Tallies | Total |
   |---------|---------|-------|
   | Car | ~~|||||~~ | | | | 9 |
   | Truck | ~~|||||~~ | 6 |
   | Bus | | | | 3 |
   | Bike | ~~|||||~~ | | 7 |

   (Student creates a picture graph with symbols and a bar graph with correctly scaled bars.)

2. "How many vehicles were counted in all?" (9 + 6 + 3 + 7 = 25)
3. "How many more cars than buses were counted?" (9 - 3 = 6 more)
4. "Why might a bar graph be easier to read than a tally chart when there are a lot of items?" (Answers vary; e.g., "You can see which bar is tallest without counting," or "The bars make it easy to compare.")
5. "If 2 more bikes were counted, how tall would the bike bar be?" (7 + 2 = 9, so the bar would go up to 9.)

## Exit Ticket (2-3 minutes)

1. A picture graph shows 5 stars for Pizza and 3 stars for Tacos. Each star = 1 person. How many more people chose pizza than tacos? (5 - 3 = 2 more)
2. A bar graph shows the Apples bar reaching 7 and the Bananas bar reaching 4. Which has more? How many more? (Apples has more; 7 - 4 = 3 more.)
3. What goes along the bottom of a bar graph? (The category names / labels.)

## Teacher Notes

**Common Errors:**
- **Misreading bar height:** Students may read the label at the bottom of the bar instead of where the bar ends at the top. Teach them to trace a finger from the top of the bar straight across to the number line.
- **Uneven bar widths:** When creating bar graphs, students may draw bars of different widths, making visual comparison misleading. Use grid paper so each bar fills the same number of columns.
- **Forgetting the zero line:** Students sometimes start their number scale at 1 instead of 0. Emphasize that the numbers on the side always start at 0.
- **Picture graph symbols not aligned:** If symbols are not lined up in neat rows or columns, the graph is hard to read. Encourage using grid paper for picture graphs too.

**Tips:**
- Use the same data set across tally chart, picture graph, and bar graph so students see that all three represent the same information — just in different visual forms.
- Let the student color the bars with different colors for each category. This makes the graph visually appealing and reinforces category distinctions.
- Connect to prior learning: "How many more ___ than ___?" questions use the subtraction skills from Unit 1.
- If the student enjoys this, let them choose their own survey question and collect data from family members to graph.

**Look-Fors:**
- Can the student read a picture graph by counting symbols accurately?
- Can the student read a bar graph by finding where the top of the bar meets the number scale?
- Does the student include all necessary parts of a bar graph (title, labels, number scale, bars)?
- Can the student translate data from a tally chart into a bar graph with correct bar heights?
