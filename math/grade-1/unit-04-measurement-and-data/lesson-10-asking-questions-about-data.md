# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 4, Lesson 10

## Topic
Asking and Answering Questions About Data

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Answer "how many" questions about a data display (tally chart, picture graph, or bar graph)
- Answer "how many more" and "how many fewer" comparison questions using data
- Compare categories within a data display to identify the most, fewest, and equal groups
- Formulate simple questions that can be answered by looking at a data display

## Prerequisites
- Read and interpret tally charts (Lesson 8)
- Read and interpret picture graphs and bar graphs (Lesson 9)
- Add and subtract within 20 fluently (Unit 1)

## Materials Needed
- Paper and pencil
- Crayons or colored pencils
- Pre-made data displays (tally charts, picture graphs, bar graphs — at least 2-3 different examples)
- Grid paper (for student-created graphs)
- Index cards or slips of paper (for writing questions)
- Small objects for a whole-unit data project (colored cubes, stickers, or similar)

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Teach students to ask and answer different types of questions about data displays, including comparison questions using "how many more" and "how many fewer."

### Key Vocabulary
- **How many:** A question that asks for the count in one category
- **How many more:** A question that asks for the DIFFERENCE between two categories — how much bigger one group is than another
- **How many fewer:** A question that asks for the difference from the other direction — how much smaller one group is
- **Compare:** To look at two or more things and decide which is greater, which is less, or if they are equal

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Model the three types of questions.**
Use the playground bar graph from the warm-up.

"When we look at a graph, we can ask three kinds of questions."

**Type 1 — "How many?"**
"How many people chose the slide? I look at the slide bar. It reaches up to 4. So 4 people chose the slide."

**Type 2 — "Which has the most / fewest?"**
"Which activity was the most popular? I look for the tallest bar. Swings is tallest at 6. Swings is the most popular. Which was the least popular? The shortest bar is monkey bars at 3."

**Type 3 — "How many more / fewer?"**
"How many MORE people chose swings than monkey bars?"

"Swings has 6. Monkey bars has 3. I need to find the difference. I can subtract: 6 - 3 = 3. So 3 more people chose swings than monkey bars."

"I can also think of it this way: if I start at 3 and count up to 6, I count 3 more. The answer is the same."

"I could also ask it the other way: How many FEWER people chose monkey bars than swings? The answer is still 3, because the difference is the same. We just describe it from the other direction."

**Step 2: Demonstrate finding "how many more" by comparing bars.**
Point to the graph.

"Here is a trick: I can hold my finger at the top of the monkey bars bar (3) and count UP to the top of the swings bar (6). 4, 5, 6 — that is 3 hops. So swings has 3 more."

"Or I can just subtract: the bigger number minus the smaller number. 6 - 3 = 3."

Try another pair: "How many more people chose sandbox (5) than slide (4)?" 5 - 4 = 1. "Just 1 more person."

**Step 3: Model writing your own questions about a graph.**
"Now here is the fun part — YOU get to be the question writer! Look at the graph and think of a question someone else could answer."

Model writing questions on index cards:
- "How many people chose the sandbox?" (Answer: 5.)
- "Which two activities are closest in number?" (Answer: Sandbox and swings, or slide and sandbox, depending on the student's reasoning — accept any pair that is 1 apart.)
- "How many fewer people chose monkey bars than sandbox?" (Answer: 5 - 3 = 2 fewer.)

"A good data question can be answered by LOOKING at the graph. If the answer is not in the graph, it is not a data question."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** A picture graph shows: Cats = 7, Dogs = 5, Rabbits = 2.
- "How many more cats than rabbits?" 7 - 2 = 5.
- "How many fewer dogs than cats?" 7 - 5 = 2.

**Example B:** A tally chart shows: Red = 8, Blue = 8, Green = 4.
- "Do any colors have the SAME number?" Yes — red and blue both have 8.
- "How many more red than green?" 8 - 4 = 4 more.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student answers and writes questions about data displays with facilitator support.

**Activity 1: Answer questions about a bar graph.**
Present a bar graph titled "Our Favorite Season" with bars: Spring = 5, Summer = 9, Fall = 3, Winter = 7.

1. "How many people chose summer?" (9)
2. "Which season was chosen the LEAST?" (Fall, with 3.)
3. "How many MORE people chose summer than fall?" (9 - 3 = 6 more.)
4. "How many FEWER people chose spring than winter?" (7 - 5 = 2 fewer.)
5. "How many people were asked in all?" (5 + 9 + 3 + 7 = 24.)

*Facilitator prompt: For "how many more" questions, if the student is unsure, say: "Find the number for each category. Now subtract the smaller from the bigger. The answer tells us how many more."*

**Activity 2: Write your own questions.**
Using the same bar graph, ask the student to write 3 questions that can be answered by the graph. Then have them answer their own questions.

Examples the student might write:
- "How many people chose spring?" (5)
- "Which season is the most popular?" (Summer)
- "How many more people chose winter than fall?" (7 - 3 = 4)

*Facilitator prompt: If the student writes a question that cannot be answered from the graph (e.g., "Why do people like summer?"), say: "That is a great question, but can we answer it just by looking at the bars? A data question needs to have an answer that is IN the graph."*

## Independent Practice

### Tier 1 (Approaching)
Use this bar graph: "Favorite Drink" — Milk = 6, Juice = 4, Water = 8.

1. How many people chose milk? (6)
2. How many people chose water? (8)
3. Which drink was chosen the MOST? (Water)
4. Which drink was chosen the LEAST? (Juice)
5. How many more people chose water than juice? (8 - 4 = 4 more)

### Tier 2 (On Level)
Use this picture graph:

**Shapes We Found**

| Shape | Pictures |
|-------|----------|
| Circle | (dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot) |
| Square | (dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot) |
| Triangle | (dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot)(dot) |
| Rectangle | (dot)(dot)(dot) |

Each (dot) = 1 shape

1. How many triangles were found? (9)
2. How many circles and squares combined? (7 + 5 = 12)
3. How many MORE triangles than rectangles? (9 - 3 = 6 more)
4. How many FEWER squares than circles? (7 - 5 = 2 fewer)
5. Write one question of your own about this graph and answer it. (Answers vary; must be answerable from the graph data.)

### Tier 3 (Advanced)
**Whole-Unit Data Project:** The student designs and carries out a mini data investigation.

1. "Think of a question you want to answer. Write it down." (Example: "What is our family's favorite fruit?")
2. "Collect data by asking at least 4 people. Record their answers in a tally chart." (Student creates a tally chart with categories and tallies.)
3. "Create a bar graph from your tally chart data. Include a title, labels, and a number scale." (Student produces a complete bar graph.)
4. "Write 3 questions about your graph. At least one must be a 'how many more' or 'how many fewer' question. Then answer all 3." (Student writes and answers three questions.)
5. "Write one sentence that summarizes what you learned from your data." (Example: "Most people in my family like apples best, and nobody chose grapes.")

## Teacher Notes

**Common Errors:**
- **Confusing "how many" with "how many more":** Students may answer a "how many more" question by simply stating the larger number instead of finding the difference. For example, asked "How many more soccer than swimming?" a student might say "8" (the soccer count) instead of "5" (the difference). Reinforce: "'How many more' means we need to find the DIFFERENCE — subtract the smaller from the bigger."
- **Adding instead of subtracting for comparison questions:** Some students add the two numbers when asked "how many more." If this happens, use concrete objects: "Put 8 cubes here for soccer and 3 cubes here for swimming. Now match them up, one to one. How many soccer cubes have no partner? That is how many MORE."
- **Writing questions that cannot be answered from the data:** Students may write "why" questions or opinion questions. Redirect: "Can you answer that by looking at the bars? A data question has an answer you can find right in the graph."

**Tips:**
- The "how many more / fewer" concept is essentially subtraction in a real-world context. Connect explicitly: "This is just like the subtraction we learned in Unit 1, but now we are using it to compare groups in a graph."
- The Tier 3 data project is an excellent opportunity for the student to take ownership. Let them choose any topic that interests them. The process of asking, collecting, organizing, graphing, and interpreting is more important than the specific topic.
- If working with a group or family, have students exchange their graphs and questions — answering someone else's questions makes the activity more engaging.
- This lesson serves as a capstone for the data strand of Unit 4. Consider displaying the student's completed data project as a celebration of their learning.

**Look-Fors:**
- Can the student distinguish between "how many" and "how many more/fewer" questions?
- Does the student subtract (rather than add) to find differences between categories?
- Can the student write questions that are answerable from a given data display?
- Does the student use complete thoughts when explaining what a graph shows?
- In the data project (Tier 3), does the student follow the full cycle: question, collect, organize, display, interpret?
