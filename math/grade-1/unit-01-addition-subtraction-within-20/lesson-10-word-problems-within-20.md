# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 1, Lesson 10

## Topic
Word Problems Within 20

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Solve addition and subtraction word problems with numbers up to 20
- Identify the operation needed from context clues in the problem
- Solve "start unknown" and "change unknown" problems (not just "result unknown")
- Write a number sentence that matches each word problem

## Prerequisites
- All addition and subtraction strategies within 20 (Lessons 1-8)
- Understanding of the equal sign (Lesson 9)
- Word problem basics (Kindergarten Unit 2, Lesson 8)

## Materials Needed
- Counting objects (available for modeling)
- Double ten-frame
- Paper and pencil

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Computation review across strategies.

1. "9 + 7 = ___?" (16)
2. "14 − 8 = ___?" (6)
3. "True or false: 6 + 5 = 5 + 6?" (True)
4. "___ + 7 = 15" (8)

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Extend word problems to include all problem types within 20.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Result unknown:** "How many altogether?" or "How many left?" — the answer is the result
- **Change unknown:** "How many more were added/removed?" — the answer is what changed
- **Start unknown:** "How many did they start with?" — the answer is the beginning amount

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Review result-unknown problems (familiar).**
"Sara has 8 stickers. She gets 7 more. How many does she have now?"

Model: 8 + 7 = 15. "The answer is the result of adding. We do this type a lot."

**Step 2: Introduce change-unknown problems.**
"Tom has 6 marbles. He finds some more and now has 13. How many did he find?"

Think aloud: "He started with 6 and ended with 13. I need to find what changed."

Write: 6 + ___ = 13. "What plus 6 makes 13? Think addition... 6 + 7 = 13. He found 7 marbles."

Model with objects: start with 6, add until you reach 13, count what you added.

**Step 3: Introduce start-unknown problems.**
"Some birds were on a tree. 5 flew away and now there are 8 left. How many birds were there at first?"

Think aloud: "Some number minus 5 equals 8. ___ − 5 = 8."

"Think addition: 5 + 8 = 13. So there were 13 birds at first."

Model: place 8 objects (the ones remaining), add back the 5 that left → 13 total.

**Step 4: Comparison problems (preview).**
"Mia has 12 books. Jake has 7 books. How many more does Mia have than Jake?"

"This is COMPARISON subtraction — not 'taking away.' We find the difference: 12 − 7 = 5. Mia has 5 more."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1 (result unknown):** "There are 9 red balloons and 6 blue balloons. How many balloons in all?" 9 + 6 = 15.

**Example 2 (change unknown):** "Kai had 15 cards. He gave some to his friend and now has 9. How many did he give away?" 15 − ___ = 9. Think: 9 + ___ = 15. Answer: 6.

**Example 3 (start unknown):** "After buying 8 apples, Maya has 17 apples. How many did she have before?" ___ + 8 = 17. Answer: 9.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student solves varied word problem types.

**Problem 1 (result unknown):**
"A farmer has 8 chickens and 9 ducks. How many birds does the farmer have?"
Student: 8 + 9 = 17. ✓

**Problem 2 (change unknown):**
"Ella had 14 crayons. She lost some and now has 8. How many did she lose?"
Ask: "What's the number sentence?" (14 − ___ = 8)
Student solves: 8 + ___ = 14 → 6. ✓

**Problem 3 (start unknown):**
"Leo ate 4 cookies. Now there are 9 cookies left. How many were there before?"
Student: ___ − 4 = 9. Think: 4 + 9 = 13. ✓

**Problem 4 (comparison):**
"Ava read 15 pages. Ben read 8 pages. How many more pages did Ava read than Ben?"
Student: 15 − 8 = 7. ✓

*Facilitator prompt: For change-unknown and start-unknown problems, if the student doesn't know where to start, say: "What do you know? What don't you know? Can you draw it?"*

## Independent Practice (15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Solve a mix of word problem types.

**★ On-level:**
1. "There are 7 fish in a pond. 6 more swim in. How many fish are in the pond?" ___ Number sentence: ___ (13; 7+6=13)
2. "A dog has 12 treats. It eats 5. How many are left?" ___ Number sentence: ___ (7; 12−5=7)
3. "Jake has 8 toy cars. He gets some more and now has 14. How many did he get?" ___ (6; 8+___=14)

**★★ Stretch:**
4. "Some children were on a bus. 7 got off and now there are 9 on the bus. How many were on the bus at first?" ___ (16; ___−7=9)
5. "Lily picked 13 flowers. Emma picked 8. How many more did Lily pick?" ___ (5; 13−8=5)
6. Make up your own word problem for 9 + 5 = 14.

**★★★ Challenge:**
7. "There were some apples in a basket. Mom put in 6 more. Then Dad took out 3. Now there are 11 apples. How many were there at the start?" ___ (8; ___+6−3=11 → ___+3=11 → 8)
8. "Write a change-unknown word problem and solve it."

### Interleaved Review
- "18 − 9 = ___?" (9) *(Lesson 8)*
- "Is 4 + 7 = 12 true or false?" (False — 4+7=11) *(Lesson 9)*
- "Make 10: 8 + 6 = ___?" (14) *(Lesson 5)*

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess word problem solving across types.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. "Ryan has 9 stickers. He buys 7 more. How many stickers does he have now?" (16; 9+7=16)
2. "There are 16 birds. 9 fly away. How many are left?" (7; 16−9=7)
3. "Emma had some pencils. She gave 5 to her friend and now has 8. How many did she start with?" (13; ___−5=8 → 5+8=13)

**Mastery = All 3 correct, with correct number sentences.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Identify which problem TYPE is difficult (result, change, or start unknown).
- For change/start unknown: always model with objects. Acting out the story makes the unknown concrete.
- Create a problem-type reference card with examples of each.

### If Mastery Is Met
The student is ready for the Unit Assessment.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**Problem types matter.** Most word problems students encounter are "result unknown" (add these, what's the total?). But real math requires all types. Change-unknown and start-unknown problems develop algebraic thinking — the student is essentially solving for an unknown.

**Drawing helps.** Bar models (a visual representation showing two parts making a whole) can help students organize word problems. For first graders, a simple part-part-whole diagram works: draw a box divided into two parts for the addends and one box for the total.

**Comparison problems are the hardest type.** "How many more?" doesn't involve physical adding or taking away — it involves finding a difference. Many students need multiple exposures before this feels natural. If it's too hard now, it can be revisited later.

**Two-step problems (Challenge #7) are a preview.** Don't worry if the student can't solve them — they're not a Grade 1 standard. But students who can are showing strong reasoning.
