# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 5, Lesson 4

## Topic
Comparing 2D and 3D Shapes — Flat vs. Solid, Relationship Between Faces and 2D Shapes

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Distinguish between 2D (flat) shapes and 3D (solid) shapes.
- Identify the 2D shape of each face on a 3D solid.
- Match 3D shapes to the 2D shapes that make up their faces.
- Explain why a sphere cannot be traced to make a flat shape (it has no flat face).

## Prerequisites
- Knows names and attributes of 2D shapes: circle, triangle, rectangle, square (Lessons 1-2)
- Knows names and attributes of 3D shapes: cube, cone, cylinder, sphere (Lesson 3)
- Understands the term face as a flat side of a 3D shape

## Materials Needed
- 3D solid models: cube, cone, cylinder, sphere
- 2D shape cutouts: circles, squares, rectangles
- Paper and crayon or pencil for tracing
- Whiteboard and dry-erase marker
- Sticky note for exit ticket

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Activate vocabulary from Lessons 1-3 and transition thinking from 2D to 3D.

Place a square cutout and a cube on the table. Ask: "What is the same about these two things?"

Guide the student to notice: "They both have the square shape." Then ask: "What is different?"

Key distinctions to draw out:
- The cutout is flat; you cannot stack things inside it. The cube is solid; you can stack things on top of it.
- The cutout is 2D — it has a shape but no depth. The cube is 3D — it has length, width, and height.

Say: "Today we are going to see how 2D shapes and 3D shapes are connected to each other."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Show students that 3D shapes are made of 2D faces, and connect the two kinds of shapes.

### Key Vocabulary
- **2D shape:** A flat shape — it has length and width but no depth. You can draw it on paper.
- **3D shape:** A solid shape — it has length, width, and height. You can hold it in your hand.
- **Face:** A flat side of a 3D shape. Faces are always 2D shapes.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete.**
Hold up the cube. Say: "Let's trace one face of the cube to see what 2D shape it makes."

Press one face of the cube flat against a piece of paper. Trace around the edge with a pencil. Lift the cube. Ask: "What shape did we trace?" (A square.) "Every single face on a cube is a square!"

Repeat with the cylinder. Press one circular end against paper and trace it. Ask: "What 2D shape did we trace?" (A circle.) "A cylinder has two circular faces."

Hold up the sphere. Try to press it against paper. Ask: "Can we trace the sphere?" (No — it is round all over. There is no flat part to press down.) "The sphere has no flat faces, so we cannot make a 2D tracing of any of its sides."

Hold up the cone. Press the circular base against paper and trace. Ask: "What 2D shape did we trace?" (A circle.) "The cone has one circular face on its bottom. Its other side is curved — no flat face."

**Step 2 — Pictorial.**
Draw a simple table on the whiteboard:

| 3D Shape | Face Shape(s) |
|----------|--------------|
| Cube | Square (6 faces) |
| Cylinder | Circle (2 faces) |
| Cone | Circle (1 face) |
| Sphere | None (0 faces) |

Point to each row and say it together. Then say: "So when we look at a cube, we see squares hiding in it. When we look at a cylinder, we see circles hiding in it."

**Step 3 — Abstract.**
Ask: "If I see a 3D shape and all its flat faces are squares, what 3D shape is it?" (Cube.) "If I see a 3D shape with two flat circular faces, what could it be?" (Cylinder.) "What if there is only one flat circular face and a pointy vertex at the top?" (Cone.)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "I traced the face of a 3D shape and got a square. What 3D shape could it have come from?"
- A square face → cube.
- Answer: Cube.

**Example 2:** "I traced the face of a 3D shape and got a circle. What 3D shapes have a circular face?"
- Cylinder (two circles) and cone (one circle).
- Answer: Could be a cylinder or a cone.

**Example 3:** "A student says, 'A sphere is just a big circle.' Is the student right? Explain."
- A circle is flat — 2D. A sphere is solid — 3D. You can hold a sphere in your hand; you cannot hold a circle in your hand the same way. The sphere has no flat faces to trace as a circle.
- Answer: No. A circle is flat and 2D; a sphere is a solid 3D shape.

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect 3D shapes to their 2D faces through tracing and matching.

**Activity: Trace and Match**
Have the student trace the faces of the cube (one face), the cylinder (both faces), and the cone (the bottom face). After tracing, the student names the 2D shape traced.

Then lay out three 2D shape cutouts: a circle, a square, and a rectangle. Place the four 3D solids next to them. Ask:

1. "Which 3D shape has faces that match this square cutout?" (Cube.)
2. "Which 3D shapes have faces that match this circle?" (Cylinder and cone.)
3. "Is there any 3D shape that matches the rectangle cutout?" (No — none of our four shapes have rectangular faces. A rectangular prism would, but we are not studying that this lesson. Accept this as an interesting discovery.)
4. "The sphere — which 2D shape does it match?" (None — it has no flat faces.)

Discuss: "What is the most important difference between a 2D shape and a 3D shape?" (A 3D shape is solid and takes up space; a 2D shape is flat and lives on paper.)

---

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice connecting 2D and 3D shapes independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. A cube has flat faces shaped like ___. (Squares.)
2. A cylinder has flat faces shaped like ___. (Circles.)
3. A sphere has ___ flat faces. (Zero.)
4. I traced a face of a 3D shape and got a circle. Name two 3D shapes this could be.
5. True or false: A flat drawing of a square is the same as a cube.
6. True or false: The faces of a cube are squares.
7. Which 3D shape has the most flat faces? (Cube — 6 faces.)
8. I can trace a 2D shape from a face of a cylinder or a cone. What 2D shape would I trace from both of them?

**Challenge:**
9. A student holds up a shape with two flat, circular faces and a curved middle section. She can roll it on its side. Name the shape and describe why she knows it is not a cube.
10. Draw a cube and a cylinder side by side. Under each, write the names of the 2D shapes found in its faces.

### Interleaved Review
11. (From Unit 2 — addition) A cube has 6 faces. A cylinder has 2 faces. How many faces are there altogether?
12. (From Unit 3 — subtraction) A shape has 6 faces. I color 4 of them red. How many faces are not red?
13. (From Lesson 1 of this unit — 2D shapes) Name a 2D shape that has 4 sides. Name a 2D shape that has 3 sides.

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. What 2D shape do you find on the faces of a cube?
2. Name a 3D shape that has zero flat faces.
3. What is the difference between a 2D shape and a 3D shape?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Square.
2. Sphere.
3. A 2D shape is flat; a 3D shape is solid and takes up space.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct (accept reasonable descriptions for #3).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Use the tracing activity again as a reteach: press the cube face down, trace it, confirm it is a square. If the 2D vs. 3D distinction is unclear, use the holding demonstration — hold a physical block and a paper cutout, ask "which one can I drop into a box?" (the solid 3D block).

### If Mastery Is Met
Say: "You understand how 2D and 3D shapes are connected! Next, we will put shapes together to create brand-new shapes."

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. **3D Shape Trace Book:** Trace each face of the cube on a piece of paper. Cut out all six square tracings. Challenge: can you fold and tape them together to make a cube shape (a net)?
2. **Connections Chart:** Create a chart with two columns — "2D Shape" and "3D Shape with That Face." Fill in as many connections as you can. (Circle → cylinder, cone; Square → cube.)

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Tracing is the most powerful concrete activity in this lesson. Make sure the student actually presses the 3D object down and traces — do not skip this step for the sake of time.
- When students trace a cone's circular base, some may expect the cone to leave a triangle or cone-shaped trace. Ask them to predict first, then trace. The surprise is memorable.
- The rectangle connection is an interesting teaching moment: none of our four 3D shapes have rectangular faces, but a rectangular prism does. You can mention this if the student asks, without teaching it in detail.
- Revisit the 2D vs. 3D distinction in Lesson 5's warm-up to ensure it is solidly retained before students move into composing shapes.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:** 1. Cube. 2. Cylinder and cone. 3. No (none of our four 3D shapes have rectangular faces). 4. None (sphere has no flat faces).

**Independent Practice:**
1. Squares.
2. Circles.
3. Zero.
4. Cylinder and cone.
5. False (a flat square is 2D; a cube is 3D and solid).
6. True.
7. Cube (6 flat faces).
8. Circle.
9. Cylinder — it has two flat circular faces and a curved surface; a cube has square flat faces and no curved surfaces.
10. Cube: square faces. Cylinder: circular faces.

**Interleaved Review:**
11. 6 + 2 = 8 faces.
12. 6 − 4 = 2 faces not red.
13. 4-sided: rectangle or square. 3-sided: triangle.

**Exit Ticket:** 1. Square. 2. Sphere. 3. A 2D shape is flat and can be drawn on paper; a 3D shape is solid and takes up space.
