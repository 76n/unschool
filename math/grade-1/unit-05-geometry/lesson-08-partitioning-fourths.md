# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 5, Lesson 8

## Topic
Partitioning Circles and Rectangles into Four Equal Shares (Fourths and Quarters)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Fold a circle or rectangle into four equal shares.
- Use the words fourth, fourths, and quarter to describe four equal shares.
- Use the phrase fourth of or quarter of to describe one share.
- Explain that four equal shares make one whole.
- Compare a half and a fourth and recognize that a fourth is smaller than a half.

## Prerequisites
- Understands that two equal shares are called halves (Lesson 7)
- Can fold and verify equal halves using paper circles and rectangles
- Understands that equal means the same size

## Materials Needed
- Paper circles (at least 3 per student)
- Paper rectangles (at least 3 per student)
- Crayons (four colors — one for each fourth, or two colors for contrast)
- Pencil or marker
- Two identical paper rectangles for the comparison activity
- Sticky note for exit ticket

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review halves and build toward the idea of more equal pieces.

Hold up a paper rectangle. Fold it into two equal halves. Open it. Ask: "What do we call these two equal pieces?" (Halves.) "How many halves make a whole?" (Two.)

Then ask: "What if four people want to share this rectangle fairly? We need four equal pieces — not two. How could we make four equal pieces from this rectangle?" Let the student think and respond freely.

Say: "Today we will fold shapes into four equal pieces. We have a special name for four equal pieces: fourths — or we also say quarters."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce fourths through folding, naming, and verifying equality, and compare fourths to halves.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fourth:** One of four equal shares of a whole.
- **Fourths:** All four equal shares together.
- **Quarter:** Another word for one fourth — one of four equal shares.
- **Quarters:** Another word for all four equal shares.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete.**
Pick up a paper rectangle. Fold it in half the short way (fold the two long sides together). Open it and check: "Two halves — good." Do NOT unfold. Now fold it in half again the same direction (fold the two open ends together). Open it out fully. Say: "I folded it in half, then in half again. Now how many equal pieces do I have?" Count the sections: "One, two, three, four. Four equal pieces! Each piece is called one fourth — or one quarter. The whole rectangle is split into four equal fourths."

Ask: "Are all four pieces the same size?" (Yes.) "How do you know?" Re-fold and check: fold one piece on top of another — they match. "Good — because they match, they are equal. Equal pieces = fourths."

Pick up a paper circle. Fold it in half — fold the two sides together. Do not open. Now fold in half again — fold the two new ends together. Open fully. Count the sections: "One, two, three, four — four equal parts!" "Each part is one fourth of the circle."

**Step 2 — Compare halves and fourths with the same shape.**
Take two identical paper rectangles. Fold one into halves (two pieces). Fold the other into fourths (four pieces). Open both.

Tear one half off the first rectangle. Tear one fourth off the second rectangle. Hold them side by side. Ask: "Which piece is bigger — the half or the fourth?" (The half is bigger.) "Why?" "Because we split the rectangle into only two pieces for halves — two big pieces. We split it into four pieces for fourths — four smaller pieces. More pieces means each piece is smaller!"

Write on the whiteboard: "A fourth is SMALLER than a half."

**Step 3 — Pictorial.**
Draw a rectangle on the whiteboard. Draw one vertical line through the middle (halves). Say: "Two halves." Now draw a horizontal line across the middle too. Count the four sections: "Now we have four fourths." Color each fourth a different color. Label each: "one fourth."

Draw a circle. Draw one line through the center (halves). Then draw a second line through the center, perpendicular to the first. Count four sections: "Four fourths of the circle."

**Step 4 — Abstract.**
Say: "If I split a shape into four equal pieces, each piece is called one ___." (Fourth or quarter.) "Four fourths make one ___." (Whole.) "Which is smaller — a half or a fourth?" (A fourth.) "Why?" (Because you split into more pieces, each piece is smaller.)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "I fold a circle in half and then in half again. How many equal pieces do I have? What is each piece called?"
- Folded in half twice: 4 equal pieces. Each piece is one fourth (or one quarter).
- Answer: 4 pieces; each is one fourth.

**Example 2:** "Four friends want to share a rectangular pizza fairly. How should they cut it? What will each person get?"
- Cut the rectangle into four equal pieces. Each person gets one fourth (one quarter) of the pizza.
- Answer: Cut into 4 equal pieces. Each person gets one fourth.

**Example 3:** "Is a fourth bigger than a half, or smaller? Explain."
- Smaller — when you cut a shape into more pieces, each piece gets smaller.
- Answer: A fourth is smaller than a half.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice folding and verifying fourths and comparing them to halves.

**Activity: Fold, Count, Color**

1. Give the student a paper rectangle. Say: "Fold it in half, then fold it in half again. Open it. Count the equal pieces. How many?" (Four.) "Color each fourth a different color." Ask: "What is each colored piece called?" (One fourth or one quarter.)

2. Give the student a paper circle. Say: "Fold it in half. Now fold it in half again. Open it. How many equal pieces?" (Four.) "Color each piece a different color."

3. Hold up the rectangle with four fourths visible. Ask: "If I color one fourth, how many fourths are NOT colored?" (Three.) "If I color two fourths, how many are NOT colored?" (Two.) "If I color all four fourths, what do I have?" (The whole rectangle.)

4. Ask the comparison question: "Which takes up more space — one fourth of this rectangle, or one half?" (One half.) "Which takes up less space?" (One fourth.) Confirm with the physical pieces if needed.

---

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply knowledge of fourths independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Fold a paper circle into four equal shares. What is each share called? (One fourth or one quarter.)
2. How many fourths make one whole? (Four.)
3. True or false: A fourth is bigger than a half.
4. I split a rectangle into four equal pieces and colored three of them. How many pieces are colored? How many are NOT colored? (Three colored, one not colored.)
5. Another name for one fourth is one ___. (Quarter.)
6. I have one fourth of a circle. My friend has one half of the same circle. Who has the bigger piece? (The friend — one half is bigger than one fourth.)
7. Draw a rectangle and divide it into four equal parts. Color two of the parts.
8. True or false: Four fourths make one whole.

**Challenge:**
9. Draw a circle divided into fourths two different ways. Show that both ways give you four equal pieces. (Hint: you can draw the lines at different angles — as long as they go through the center point, the pieces will be equal.)
10. I have a rectangle divided into fourths. I eat two fourths. Is that the same as eating one half? Explain. (Yes — two fourths = two equal pieces out of four; one half = one equal piece out of two; both describe the same amount of the whole when the shape is the same size.)

### Interleaved Review
11. (From Lesson 7 — halves) Two equal pieces of a circle are called ___. (Halves.)
12. (From Unit 2 — addition) I colored 1 fourth and then 1 more fourth. How many fourths did I color in all? (Two fourths.)
13. (From Unit 3 — subtraction) A rectangle has 4 fourths. I shaded 3 of them. How many are not shaded? (One fourth.)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. I fold a rectangle in half and then in half again. How many equal pieces do I have?
2. What do we call one of four equal pieces?
3. Which is smaller — one half or one fourth?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Four equal pieces.
2. One fourth (or one quarter).
3. One fourth is smaller.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to the physical folding. Fold the rectangle in halves, then fold it again into fourths. Count: "One, two, three, four." Say: "Four equal pieces — we call them fourths." Compare the half piece to the fourth piece side by side. Revisit at the Lesson 9 warm-up.

### If Mastery Is Met
Say: "You can split shapes into fourths! In our next lesson, we will use halves and fourths in real-world sharing situations."

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Halves vs. Fourths Poster:** Fold one paper rectangle into halves and another identical rectangle into fourths. Cut out the pieces. Paste the pieces on a sheet of paper labeled "Halves" and "Fourths." Write: "Halves: 2 equal pieces. Fourths: 4 equal pieces. A fourth is smaller than a half."
2. **The Folding Challenge:** Can you fold a rectangle into eighths (8 equal pieces)? How? (Fold in half three times.) What do you notice about the size of each piece compared to a fourth?

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The comparison between halves and fourths is the most important conceptual development in this lesson. Use identical pieces of paper for the comparison so the student cannot argue that one shape was bigger to start with.
- The phrase "quarter" is more familiar to many students because of money (a quarter coin = 25 cents = one quarter of a dollar). If the student knows about coins, use this connection briefly.
- Do not introduce the fraction notation 1/4 in this lesson. Fraction notation is a Grade 2 concept. Use words only: one fourth, one quarter, fourth of, quarter of.
- When drawing circles divided into fourths, the two lines through the center should be roughly perpendicular (forming four roughly equal pie slices). Precise angles are not required — the goal is the concept of four equal shares.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:** 1. Four fourths (or quarters); each is one fourth (one quarter). 2. Four fourths of the circle. 3. Four − 1 = 3 not colored; four − 2 = 2 not colored; four − 4 = 0 not colored (the whole). 4. One half is larger; one fourth is smaller.

**Independent Practice:**
1. One fourth (or one quarter).
2. Four.
3. False (a fourth is SMALLER than a half).
4. Three colored; one not colored.
5. Quarter.
6. The friend (one half is bigger than one fourth).
7. Accept any valid drawing of a rectangle with four equal parts, two colored.
8. True.
9. Accept two valid circle drawings with lines through the center in different directions.
10. Yes — two fourths of the same shape equals one half of that shape.

**Interleaved Review:**
11. Halves.
12. Two fourths.
13. One fourth is not shaded.

**Exit Ticket:** 1. Four. 2. One fourth (or one quarter). 3. One fourth is smaller.
