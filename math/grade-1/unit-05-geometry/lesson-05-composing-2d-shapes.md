# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 5, Lesson 5

## Topic
Composing 2D Shapes — Combining Triangles, Rectangles, and Squares to Make New Shapes

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Combine two or more 2D shapes to create a new composite shape.
- Name the new shape that results from combining two smaller shapes.
- Identify which smaller shapes were used to make a composite shape.
- Understand that the smaller shapes still exist inside the composite shape.

## Prerequisites
- Knows the names, sides, and corners of circles, triangles, rectangles, and squares (Lessons 1-2)
- Can name 2D shapes in different orientations and sizes
- Understands vocabulary: side, corner, flat

## Materials Needed
- Pattern blocks (at least two sets — triangles, squares, rhombuses, trapezoids, hexagons)
- 2D shape cutouts: multiple triangles, squares, rectangles in different sizes
- Blank paper and pencil or crayon for tracing or drawing
- Whiteboard and dry-erase marker
- Sticky note for exit ticket

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Prime spatial thinking by exploring how shapes fit together.

Place two triangle pattern blocks on the table. Say: "If I push these two triangles together so their longest sides are touching, what new shape do we get?"

Let the student explore by pushing the blocks together. After they form the new shape, ask: "What shape is this?" (It should look like a square or a parallelogram — accept both and identify which one matches.)

Say: "Today we are going to combine shapes — put them together — to make brand new shapes. This is called composing shapes."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model composing shapes using pattern blocks, then connect to pictures and abstract descriptions.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Compose:** To put smaller shapes together to make a new, larger shape.
- **Composite shape:** The new shape made from combining two or more smaller shapes.
- **Half-circle:** A shape that looks like a circle cut in half — a flat bottom and a curved top.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete.**
Place two triangle pattern blocks on the table. Arrange them so their longest sides (hypotenuses) touch each other. Say: "I am composing — I am putting shapes together. Now I have a new shape. What shape did we make?" (A square or rectangle, depending on triangles used.) "We made a rectangle! We used two triangles to make a rectangle."

Now try: place a square and two triangles. Put one triangle on the left, the square in the middle, and one triangle on the right, all joined. Ask: "What large shape does this remind you of?" (Depending on the arrangement, students may see a large rectangle or a pentagon. Accept observations and guide them to the rectangle idea.)

Try: two small squares pushed together. Ask: "What shape did we make?" (A rectangle.)

Try: three triangles arranged to form a larger triangle. Ask: "What did we make?" (A bigger triangle.)

Remind the student: "Even though we made a new shape, the original shapes are still there inside it. The triangles did not disappear — they became part of the new shape."

**Step 2 — Pictorial.**
On the whiteboard, draw two triangles side by side with their longest sides touching. Draw a dotted line down the middle. Say: "Here are two triangles. When we put them together with their longest sides touching, we get this rectangle." Trace the outer outline of the rectangle.

Draw a second example: two squares side by side. "Two squares make a longer rectangle."

Draw a third example: a small square on top of a rectangle. "A square on top of a rectangle makes a house shape — like a rectangle with a square on top." Explain that composite shapes do not always have a standard name.

**Step 3 — Abstract.**
Ask: "Two triangles put together by their longest sides make a ___." (Rectangle or square, depending on triangle type.) "Two squares put together side by side make a ___." (Rectangle.) "What shapes would you need to make a bigger triangle?" (Two or more smaller triangles.)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "I used two pattern block triangles and pushed their long sides together. What shape did I most likely make?"
- Two triangles, long sides touching → a square (if equal-leg triangles) or a parallelogram.
- Answer: Square (using right-triangle pattern blocks) or parallelogram (using equilateral triangles). Accept either with explanation.

**Example 2:** "I made a shape that looks like a large rectangle. I used two equal shapes. What shapes did I probably use?"
- Could be: two squares, or two smaller rectangles.
- Answer: Two squares or two smaller rectangles.

**Example 3:** "I have a composite shape that looks like a square with a triangle sitting on top of it. What two shapes make it up?"
- A triangle and a square.
- Answer: One triangle on top of one square.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Compose and decompose shapes together using pattern blocks.

**Activity: Make That Shape**
Challenge the student with each of the following. Use pattern blocks to build the answer:

1. "Use two triangles to make one rectangle. Touch their long sides together."
2. "Use two squares to make one rectangle."
3. "Use four small squares to make one big square." (Arrange 2 rows of 2.)
4. "Use three triangles to make one larger triangle." (Arrange them to fit together.)
5. "Can you make a shape that looks like a house using pattern blocks? What shapes did you use?" (A square with a triangle on top.)

After each build, ask:
- "What new shape did you make?"
- "What shapes are hiding inside it?"
- "How many pieces did you use?"

---

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice composing and identifying composite shapes independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Two triangles are pushed together at their long sides. What new shape is made?
2. Two squares are placed side by side. What new shape is made?
3. I see a large shape that looks like a rectangle. It has a dotted line down the middle. The dotted line splits it into two equal pieces. What shape is each piece?
4. How many small triangles would it take to make a large triangle shape? (Use pattern blocks to find out — answer: 4 small equilateral triangles make 1 large equilateral triangle.)
5. I made a shape by putting one square and one triangle together. Describe what the composite shape looks like.
6. True or false: When two triangles are combined to make a square, the triangles disappear.
7. What two shapes are hiding inside a rectangle that has a line drawn down its middle from top to bottom? (Two squares, if it is a square-ish rectangle, or two smaller rectangles.)
8. Name two different ways to make a rectangle using pattern blocks.

**Challenge:**
9. Using pattern blocks, find two different ways to fill in a large triangle outline. Describe each way. (Example: three small triangles, or one trapezoid and one triangle.)
10. Can you make a hexagon using pattern blocks? How many triangles do you need? (Six equilateral triangles fill a hexagon.)

### Interleaved Review
11. (From Unit 1 — counting) How many sides does a triangle have? How many corners? Write the numbers.
12. (From Unit 2 — addition) I used 3 triangles and 2 squares to build a picture. How many shapes did I use in all?
13. (From Unit 3 — subtraction) I had 6 pattern blocks. I used 4 of them. How many pattern blocks are left?

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Two triangles are pushed together at their long sides. What shape do they make?
2. Two squares are put side by side. What shape do they make?
3. True or false: When you compose shapes, the smaller shapes inside disappear.

**Expected Answers:**
1. A rectangle (or square, depending on triangle size).
2. A rectangle.
3. False — the smaller shapes are still there inside the composite shape.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to the physical blocks. Use two identical triangles and physically push them together. Ask the student to name the new shape. Emphasize: "The triangles are still here — look, I can pull them apart again." Revisit the exit ticket idea at the start of Lesson 6.

### If Mastery Is Met
Say: "Great work composing 2D shapes! Next, we will compose 3D shapes — using solid shapes to build even bigger solid structures."

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Tangram Puzzle:** If tangram pieces are available, challenge the student to use all seven tangram pieces to make a large square. This is a classic spatial reasoning challenge appropriate for above-level students.
2. **Shape Artwork:** Use pattern block tracings on paper to create a picture — a house, a rocket ship, an animal — made entirely of triangles, squares, and rectangles. Label each shape used.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The most important conceptual move in this lesson is that the component shapes do not disappear inside the composite shape. When students pull apart the pattern blocks to reveal the triangles again, that is a powerful moment. Do not skip it.
- Pattern block triangles are equilateral (all sides equal). Two equilateral triangles pushed together at their long sides make a rhombus (diamond), not a rectangle. If you are using equilateral triangle pattern blocks, note this. Two right-triangle cutouts pushed together at their long sides make a rectangle. Use whichever you have and discuss what you observe.
- It is fine if the composite shape does not have a standard name (like the "house shape"). Encourage students to describe the composite shape even when it does not have a name.
- Challenge students to find more than one way to make the same composite shape — this is important mathematical flexibility.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:** 1. Rectangle (or parallelogram/rhombus depending on block type). 2. Rectangle. 3. Large square. 4. Larger triangle (students may need to experiment — accept any answer found through manipulation). 5. A square with a triangle on top (a "house" shape).

**Independent Practice:**
1. Rectangle (or parallelogram/rhombus — see note above).
2. Rectangle.
3. Two squares (if the rectangle is square proportions) or two smaller rectangles.
4. 4 small triangles (when using equilateral pattern block triangles).
5. Looks like a "house" — a square base with a triangle roof.
6. False.
7. Two smaller rectangles (or two squares).
8. Accept: two squares; one square and two triangles; four small squares; other valid combinations.
9. Accept: six small triangles; two trapezoids; one hexagon (trivial but worth noting); other valid combinations.
10. Six equilateral triangles fill a regular hexagon.

**Interleaved Review:**
11. Triangle: 3 sides, 3 corners.
12. 3 + 2 = 5 shapes.
13. 6 − 4 = 2 pattern blocks left.

**Exit Ticket:** 1. Rectangle (or rhombus/parallelogram — see teacher note). 2. Rectangle. 3. False.
