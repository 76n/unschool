# Facilitator Guide — Grade 1 Math, Unit 5: Geometry

**Grade:** 1
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 5 — Geometry
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 4-5 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided

---

## Unit Overview

In this unit, students explore the world of shapes — both flat (2D) shapes and solid (3D) shapes. Students begin by identifying and describing 2D shapes such as circles, triangles, rectangles, and squares using attributes like the number of sides and corners. They then move into 3D shapes — cubes, cones, cylinders, and spheres — exploring faces, edges, and vertices.

Students learn to compose new shapes by combining smaller shapes, building spatial reasoning that supports later work in area and fractions. The unit closes with partitioning: students divide circles and rectangles into two and four equal shares, introducing the foundational language of halves and fourths.

This unit matters because geometry builds spatial reasoning, a predictor of later math success. Hands-on work with physical shapes and real-world objects makes abstract ideas concrete and memorable for 6- and 7-year-olds.

**Standards Addressed:**
- **1.G.1** — Distinguish between defining attributes (e.g., triangles are closed and three-sided) versus non-defining attributes (e.g., color, orientation, overall size); build and draw shapes to possess defining attributes.
- **1.G.2** — Compose two-dimensional shapes (rectangles, squares, trapezoids, triangles, half-circles, and quarter-circles) or three-dimensional shapes (cubes, right rectangular prisms, right circular cones, and right circular cylinders) to create a composite shape, and compose new shapes from the composite shape.
- **1.G.3** — Partition circles and rectangles into two and four equal shares, describe the shares using the words halves, fourths, and quarters, and use the phrases half of, fourth of, and quarter of. Describe the whole as two of, or four of the shares. Understand that decomposing into more equal shares creates smaller shares.

## Materials Needed for This Unit

**Shape Manipulatives**
- Pattern blocks (at least one set per student — hexagons, trapezoids, rhombuses, triangles, squares, thin rhombuses)
- 2D shape cutouts: circles, triangles, rectangles, squares (laminated cardstock, various sizes and colors)
- 3D solid models: cube, cone, cylinder, sphere (wooden or plastic classroom solids, or household objects)

**Household / Real-World Objects for 3D Shapes**
- Cube: dice, small wooden block, tissue box
- Cone: party hat, ice cream cone (cardboard model)
- Cylinder: toilet paper roll, soup can, paper towel roll
- Sphere: tennis ball, orange, marble

**Composing Shapes**
- Pattern blocks (reused from above)
- Tangram pieces (optional but valuable for Lessons 5-6)
- Blank paper and crayons for tracing shapes

**Partitioning**
- Paper circles (several per student for folding — Lessons 7-8)
- Paper rectangles (several per student for folding — Lessons 7-8)
- Crayons or colored pencils (two colors per student)
- Scissors (for teacher-guided cutting in Lesson 9)
- Real food examples if available: a round cracker, a graham cracker or rectangle-shaped food

**General**
- Whiteboard and dry-erase markers (or chalkboard and chalk)
- Sticky notes for exit tickets
- Recording sheet or math notebook for each student

## Facilitation Tips

1. **Lead with physical objects before pictures or words.** For every new shape, hand the student a physical object or model first. Let them hold it, count the sides or corners with their finger, and describe it in their own words before you introduce the formal name or definition. This is especially important for 3D shapes, which are harder to visualize from a picture.

2. **Use consistent shape language every day.** Refer to sides and corners (not edges or angles) when talking about 2D shapes. For 3D shapes, introduce faces, edges, and vertices in Lesson 3 and use these terms consistently from then on. Repetition of precise vocabulary builds confidence.

3. **Avoid color and size as shape identifiers.** Students commonly believe that a large red triangle and a small blue triangle are different shapes because they look so different. Always show multiple sizes, orientations, and colors of each shape to reinforce that the defining attributes are sides and corners, not color or size.

4. **Model folding before asking the student to fold.** In Lessons 7 and 8, folding is the primary concrete tool for understanding equal shares. Demonstrate a fold on a large piece of paper visible to the student, then do it together, and only then have the student do it independently. Emphasize that both parts must match exactly when you fold one on top of the other.

5. **Connect geometry to the classroom and home environment.** Before Lesson 3, do a brief "shape hunt" — ask the student to find real objects in the room that are shaped like a cube, cylinder, or sphere. This 2-minute activity makes 3D shapes immediately relevant and gives you objects to reference during instruction.

6. **Use the "Is it always true?" question throughout the unit.** When students make generalizations — "all triangles look like a mountain" — test the claim together. Draw a very flat triangle or a very tall triangle. Ask: "Is this still a triangle? How do you know?" This builds flexible thinking about attributes.

## Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Correction |
|---------------|------------|
| A shape is not a "real" triangle if it is tilted or oddly proportioned | Show triangles in many orientations and sizes. Count sides (3) and corners (3) each time to confirm it is a triangle. |
| Squares and rectangles are completely different shapes | Explain that a square is a special rectangle where all four sides are the same length. Sort squares into the rectangle group to show they belong there. |
| A 3D shape is the same as a 2D shape (e.g., calling a sphere a "circle") | Hold up the sphere next to a paper circle. Ask: "Can you pick up the circle? Can you hold the sphere in your hand?" Distinguish flat (2D) from solid (3D). |
| "Half" means any two pieces, not two equal pieces | After cutting or folding, have the student overlap the two pieces to check if they match. If they do not match, the pieces are not halves. |
| Fourths are only made by making 4 cuts in the same direction (like 4 vertical strips) | Show multiple correct ways to make 4 equal shares — 4 vertical strips, 4 horizontal strips, or 2 cuts crossing in the middle. Confirm equality by checking that all pieces match. |
| The whole stays the same size whether it is cut in halves or fourths | Use a real paper rectangle: one folded into halves (2 big pieces) and one folded into fourths (4 small pieces). Ask which piece is bigger. The fourths piece is smaller — more pieces means each piece is smaller. |
| Composing shapes — believes the shape identity changes based on position | When two triangles are pushed together to make a rectangle, confirm the triangles are still triangles inside. The new shape is a rectangle, but the triangles have not disappeared. |

## Assessment Guidance

**Formative Assessment (ongoing, every lesson)**
- Exit tickets are provided at the end of every lesson. These take 3 minutes and consist of 2-3 targeted problems. Review exit tickets immediately after the session. If fewer than 2 of 3 are correct, reteach the concept in the next session warm-up before moving on.
- During Guided Practice, observe whether the student self-corrects, needs prompting, or needs full reteaching. Note any consistent errors for differentiation.

**Summative Assessment**
- The Unit 5 Assessment (see `unit-assessment.md`) covers all three standards: 1.G.1, 1.G.2, and 1.G.3.
- Administer after Lesson 10 when student has completed all review.
- Allow 30-40 minutes without time pressure.
- Mastery benchmark: 26/30 points (87%). Students below mastery on any standard section should receive targeted review before Unit 6.

## Differentiation at a Glance

**Below Level**
- Provide a shape reference card (name + picture + attributes) for the student to keep at their workspace throughout the unit.
- Allow extra time during exit tickets; ask the student to explain their thinking aloud rather than writing.
- For composing shapes (Lessons 5-6), pre-outline the target composite shape so the student snaps pattern blocks inside the outline.
- For partitioning (Lessons 7-9), use physical folding and do not require the student to draw partitioned shapes independently — let them fold, then trace the fold line.

**On Level**
- Follow lesson plans as written.
- Encourage students to explain their reasoning using vocabulary (sides, corners, faces, edges, vertices, halves, fourths).

**Above Level**
- Introduce the concept of a trapezoid and half-circle as additional 2D shapes in Lessons 1-2.
- Challenge the student to find more than one way to compose the same target shape (Lesson 5).
- In Lessons 7-8, ask: "Can you split this shape into fourths in two different ways and show that both ways are fair?"
- Ask extension questions: "If we cut into 8 equal pieces, would each piece be bigger or smaller than a fourth? Why?"
