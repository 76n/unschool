# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 5, Lesson 9

## Topic
Equal Shares Practice — Halves and Fourths in Real-World Contexts (Sharing Fairly)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Apply the concepts of halves and fourths to real-world sharing situations.
- Determine whether a given partition creates equal or unequal shares.
- Use the language half of, fourth of, quarter of, halves, and fourths in context.
- Explain that the same whole divided into more pieces gives smaller pieces.
- Solve simple sharing problems involving splitting shapes or objects into halves or fourths.

## Prerequisites
- Understands halves: two equal shares of a whole (Lesson 7)
- Understands fourths: four equal shares of a whole (Lesson 8)
- Can fold circles and rectangles into halves and fourths and verify equality

## Materials Needed
- Paper circles and rectangles (2-3 of each per student)
- Crayons
- Scissors (teacher-guided cutting in this lesson)
- Real or pretend food objects if available (round cracker, rectangular graham cracker, etc.)
- Whiteboard and dry-erase marker
- Sticky note for exit ticket

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review vocabulary and connect halves and fourths to familiar sharing experiences.

Tell this story: "Four friends — Max, Ava, Eli, and Zoe — found one round pizza. They want to share it so everyone gets the same amount. How should they cut the pizza?"

Let the student answer: "Into four equal pieces — four fourths." Ask: "What do we call each piece?" (One fourth or one quarter.) "If two friends shared the pizza instead of four, how many equal pieces would they cut?" (Two pieces — two halves.)

Say: "Today we are going to practice sharing shapes and objects fairly using what we know about halves and fourths."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model fair-sharing problems and connect the math vocabulary to practical situations.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Fair share:** A portion that is the same size as every other share — an equal share.
- **Half of:** One equal share when something is divided into two parts.
- **Fourth of / Quarter of:** One equal share when something is divided into four parts.
- **Whole:** The entire object or shape before it is divided.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete.**
Present a paper rectangle as a "sandwich." Say: "This is a sandwich. Two people want to share it equally. How should we cut it?"

Fold and crease the rectangle in half. Ask: "How many pieces?" (Two.) "Are they equal?" (Yes.) "What is each piece called?" (One half.) "Each person gets one half of the sandwich."

Now say: "Same sandwich, but now four people want to share it equally. How should we cut it?"

Fold the rectangle in half, then in half again. Open it. Ask: "How many pieces?" (Four.) "Are they equal?" (Yes.) "What is each piece called?" (One fourth or one quarter.) "Each person gets one fourth of the sandwich."

Ask: "When four people share the sandwich, each person gets one ___." (Fourth.) "When two people share it, each person gets one ___." (Half.) "Which share is bigger — a half or a fourth?" (A half — because fewer pieces means each piece is bigger.)

**Step 2 — Real-world story problems.**
Tell Story 1: "A round pie is cut into two equal pieces. Each piece is called ___." (One half.) "If I eat one half, how much pie is left?" (One half.)

Tell Story 2: "A rectangular chocolate bar has four equal sections. Each section is called ___." (One fourth.) "If I eat two sections, how many fourths are left?" (Two fourths — which is the same as one half.)

Tell Story 3: "A student says she cut a circle into four pieces, but one piece is much bigger than the others. Are these fourths?" (No — fourths must all be equal.)

**Step 3 — Abstract.**
Ask: "When you divide a whole into 2 equal pieces, each is one ___." (Half.) "When you divide into 4 equal pieces, each is one ___." (Fourth.) "Which requires more cuts?" (Fourths — more cuts, more pieces.) "Which gives you bigger pieces?" (Halves — fewer pieces, each is bigger.) "What do all fourths together make?" (One whole.)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "There is one rectangular brownie. Sam and Ana want equal shares. How should they cut it? What will each get?"
- Cut into two equal pieces. Each person gets one half of the brownie.
- Answer: Two halves. Each person gets one half.

**Example 2:** "There is one round pancake. Four children want equal shares. What should each piece be called?"
- Cut into four equal pieces. Each piece is one fourth (one quarter).
- Answer: Four fourths. Each child gets one fourth.

**Example 3:** "A circle is split into two pieces. One piece is more than half the circle. Is this a fair split? Why not?"
- No — fair sharing means equal pieces. One piece is larger than the other, so the shares are not equal.
- Answer: No — not a fair split. The pieces are not equal.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Solve real-world sharing problems together using paper models.

**Activity: Story Problems with Paper Models**

Work through each story problem below. For each one, the student folds or draws to show the answer.

1. "Two friends share a rectangular cracker equally. Fold a paper rectangle to show how. What does each friend get?" (One half each.)

2. "Four children share a round cookie equally. Fold a paper circle to show how. What does each child get?" (One fourth or one quarter each.)

3. "A sandwich is cut into two pieces. One piece is bigger than the other. Is this fair? How could we fix it?" (Not fair — cut more carefully so both pieces are equal.)

4. "A pizza is cut into four fourths. Maya eats two fourths. How many fourths are left for others?" (Two fourths.)

5. "Kenji has one half of a circle and his sister has one fourth of the same circle. Who has more?" (Kenji — one half is bigger than one fourth.)

After each problem, ask: "How do you know the shares are fair (or not fair)?" Encourage the student to use the words equal, half, fourth.

---

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply halves and fourths to sharing problems and shape partitioning independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Three friends want to share a rectangle. If it is split into halves, can all three share equally? (No — halves makes two pieces, not three.)
2. Two friends share a circle equally. What is each piece called?
3. Four children share a rectangle equally. What is each piece called?
4. Draw a circle. Divide it into two equal halves. Color one half.
5. Draw a rectangle. Divide it into four equal fourths. Color one fourth.
6. Maria ate one fourth of a pizza. Leo ate one half of the same pizza. Who ate more? Why?
7. True or false: Two fourths of a rectangle is the same amount as one half of the same rectangle.
8. A whole has four equal pieces. Each piece is one fourth. If I eat three fourths, how many fourths are left?

**Challenge:**
9. A circle is split into four equal pieces. Rosa takes one fourth. Then Rosa takes one more fourth. How much has she taken in all? Write the answer two ways: as fourths and as halves. (Two fourths — or the same as one half.)
10. Dad has a rectangular cake. He wants to give two children one fourth each. How much of the cake will be left after they both take their pieces? (Two fourths, which is the same as one half.)

### Interleaved Review
11. (From Lesson 1 — 2D shapes) What 2D shape has no sides and no corners? (Circle.)
12. (From Unit 2 — addition) I had a rectangle cut into 4 fourths. I added 2 more rectangles cut into 4 fourths each. How many fourths are there in all? (4 + 4 + 4 = 12 fourths.)
13. (From Unit 3 — subtraction) A pizza has 4 equal slices. I ate 1 slice. How many slices are left? (3 slices — three fourths.)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. Two friends share a pizza equally. What is each person's share called?
2. Four children share a rectangle equally. What is each child's share called?
3. Which gives each person a bigger piece: sharing between two people (halves) or sharing between four people (fourths)? Why?

**Expected Answers:**
1. One half.
2. One fourth (or one quarter).
3. Sharing between two people (halves) gives a bigger piece — because fewer people share, each person gets more.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Use the two identical rectangles from Lesson 8: one cut into halves, one cut into fourths. Hold a half next to a fourth. Ask: "Which is bigger?" If the student confuses which is bigger, re-read the sizes aloud: "Two pieces in total for halves — so each is bigger. Four pieces in total for fourths — so each is smaller."

### If Mastery Is Met
Say: "Excellent! You can solve real-world sharing problems using halves and fourths. In our last lesson, we will review everything we have learned about geometry."

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Fair or Not Fair?:** Draw three rectangles. In the first, draw a fair split into halves. In the second, draw a fair split into fourths. In the third, draw an unfair split (unequal pieces). Label each: "Fair: halves," "Fair: fourths," "Not fair."
2. **Sharing Story:** Write or dictate a short story about four friends sharing a pizza or sandwich fairly. Use the words fourth and quarter in the story.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The key real-world connection in this lesson is that more people sharing means smaller pieces for each person. This is a counterintuitive idea for young learners (more pieces sounds like more, but means less per person). Use physical pieces to demonstrate this concretely.
- Story problem 1 from Guided Practice is a good critical thinking moment: three people cannot share fairly using halves (only two pieces). Ask the student how they could share fairly among three people. (They would need thirds — three equal pieces.) You do not need to formally teach thirds, but the question prompts thinking beyond halves and fourths.
- The connection that two fourths = one half is an important insight. If it arises naturally, confirm it with physical pieces. Do not force it if it does not come up.
- When using real food (crackers, etc.) for this lesson, food handling should follow standard safety practices.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:** 1. One half each. 2. One fourth (one quarter) each. 3. Not fair — fix by cutting equally. 4. Two fourths are left. 5. Kenji has more (one half > one fourth).

**Independent Practice:**
1. No — halves creates only two pieces; three people cannot share halves equally.
2. One half.
3. One fourth (one quarter).
4. Accept any valid circle with a line through the center and one half colored.
5. Accept any valid rectangle with two perpendicular lines and one fourth colored.
6. Leo ate more — one half is bigger than one fourth.
7. True — two fourths of the same rectangle equals one half of that rectangle.
8. One fourth is left (4 − 3 = 1).
9. Two fourths total; two fourths = one half.
10. Two fourths are left, which is the same as one half.

**Interleaved Review:**
11. Circle.
12. 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 fourths.
13. 4 − 1 = 3 slices (three fourths) left.

**Exit Ticket:** 1. One half. 2. One fourth (one quarter). 3. Sharing between two people (halves) gives a bigger piece — fewer total pieces means each piece is larger.
