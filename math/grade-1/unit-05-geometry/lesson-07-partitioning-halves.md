# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 5, Lesson 7

## Topic
Partitioning Circles and Rectangles into Two Equal Shares (Halves)

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Fold a circle or rectangle into two equal shares.
- Use the words half and halves to describe two equal shares.
- Use the phrase half of to describe one share of a whole.
- Explain that two halves together make one whole.
- Distinguish between two equal shares (halves) and two unequal pieces.

## Prerequisites
- Recognizes circles and rectangles (Lessons 1-2)
- Understands the concept of equal — two things that match exactly
- Understands the concept of sharing fairly from everyday life

## Materials Needed
- Paper circles (at least 3 per student — pre-cut circles from paper)
- Paper rectangles (at least 3 per student — cut from plain paper)
- Crayons (two colors — one for each half)
- Pencil or marker
- Scissors (for the teacher to demonstrate cutting along fold lines)
- Real food or food pictures if available (a sandwich cut in half, a cookie, a cracker)
- Sticky note for exit ticket

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build intuition for equal sharing before introducing formal vocabulary.

Tell this story: "Imagine you and a friend have one cookie. You want to share it fairly so you each get the same amount. How would you split the cookie?"

Let the student respond freely. Guide toward: "You would cut it into two pieces that are the same size — two equal pieces."

Ask: "What if one piece was big and one piece was small — would that be fair?" (No.) "We want both pieces to match. We call this equal."

Say: "Today we are going to learn the math word for two equal pieces: halves."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Introduce halves through folding, naming, and verifying equality.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Partition:** To split a shape into equal shares.
- **Equal shares:** Pieces that are exactly the same size.
- **Half:** One of two equal shares of a whole.
- **Halves:** Two equal parts together. (Half is one; halves are both.)
- **Whole:** The entire shape before it is split.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete.**
Pick up a paper circle. Say: "This is a whole circle." Hold it up.

Fold the circle carefully so the two curved edges match perfectly. Crease the fold. Say: "I folded the circle into two equal parts. Each part is called a half." Point to one half: "This is one half of the circle." Point to the other half: "This is the other half of the circle. Together, the two halves make the whole circle."

Unfold. Ask: "Are both pieces the same size?" Fold one half back on top of the other and check: "When I put one half on top of the other, they match perfectly. That tells me they are equal. They are halves."

Now pick up a rectangle. Fold it from left to right so the short edges meet. Crease. Say: "I folded the rectangle into two halves. Each piece is one half of the rectangle."

Then fold a second rectangle so it is folded from top to bottom (a different direction). Show both folded rectangles. Ask: "Did I fold this one into halves too?" Unfold and check — yes, both pieces match. "A rectangle can be folded into halves in more than one way."

Show a circle that has been folded incorrectly — fold it so one piece is bigger than the other. Ask: "Are these two halves?" Unfold and check: "The pieces don't match. These are NOT halves. They are just two unequal pieces."

**Step 2 — Pictorial.**
Draw a rectangle on the whiteboard. Draw a line down the middle to split it into two equal pieces. Color one half with a crayon and leave the other white. Say: "The colored part is one half. The uncolored part is the other half. Together they make the whole rectangle."

Draw a circle on the whiteboard. Draw a line through the center. Color one half. Label: "one half" and "one half" in each section.

Draw a rectangle split unevenly — one piece much bigger than the other. Ask: "Is this split into halves?" (No.) "How can you tell?" (The pieces are not equal — one is bigger.)

**Step 3 — Abstract.**
Say: "If I split a shape into two equal pieces, each piece is called one ___." (Half.) "Two halves make one ___." (Whole.) "If I have one half and one half, how many halves do I have?" (Two.) "What do two halves make?" (One whole.)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "I folded a circle in half. I colored one half red. How much of the circle is red?"
- One half of the circle.
- Answer: One half.

**Example 2:** "I have a rectangle. I split it into two equal pieces. I give one piece to my sister. What fraction of the rectangle does my sister have?"
- She has one of two equal pieces: one half.
- Answer: One half.

**Example 3:** "A student splits a rectangle into two pieces. One piece is very big and one piece is very small. Are these halves? Why or why not?"
- No — the pieces are not equal, so they are not halves.
- Answer: No. Halves must be equal — the same size.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice folding and verifying equal halves together.

**Activity: Fold and Check**

1. Give the student a paper circle. Say: "Fold this circle into two equal halves. Unfold it and trace the fold line. Color one half with a crayon. Now unfold and lay both halves flat. Do they match? Good — those are halves!"

2. Give the student a paper rectangle. Say: "Fold this rectangle into two equal halves the short way (fold the long sides together). Open it and draw the fold line. Color one half."

3. Give the student a second paper rectangle. Say: "This time, fold it into halves the long way (fold the short sides together). Open it and draw the fold line. Color one half." Ask: "Did we make halves both times? How do you know?" (Both times the two pieces match when folded on top of each other.)

4. Ask: "Are the halves in example 2 and example 3 the same shape?" (No — folding the different ways makes different-shaped halves, but both are still halves.) "What matters is that the two pieces are equal — they match."

5. Show a pre-folded circle that is folded unevenly (off-center fold). Ask: "Is this folded into halves?" The student unfolds and checks. (No — the pieces do not match.)

---

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice identifying and creating halves independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. Fold a paper circle into two equal halves. Color one half blue. What do we call the blue part? (One half of the circle.)
2. True or false: Two halves of a circle make a whole circle.
3. A rectangle is cut into two pieces. Both pieces are the same size. Are they halves? (Yes.)
4. A rectangle is cut into two pieces. One piece is big and one piece is small. Are they halves? (No.)
5. I have one half of a cookie. My friend has one half of the same cookie. Do we have equal amounts? (Yes.)
6. Draw a rectangle and draw a line through the middle to split it into two equal halves. Color one half.
7. How many halves make one whole? (Two.)
8. True or false: There is only one way to fold a rectangle into halves. (False — you can fold it two ways.)

**Challenge:**
9. Can you fold a rectangle into halves two different ways and show that both ways make equal pieces? Fold it, open it, then re-fold a different way. Draw both folded versions.
10. I have a rectangle. I colored one half. My friend says, "You colored MORE than half." Who is right? If both halves are equal, explain why the friend is wrong.

### Interleaved Review
11. (From Lesson 1 — 2D shapes) Name the 2D shape that has zero sides and zero corners. (Circle.)
12. (From Unit 2 — addition) I have 2 halves. How many halves make 1 whole? Write a number sentence: ___ + ___ = 1 whole. (1 half + 1 half = 1 whole.)
13. (From Unit 3 — subtraction) I had a whole sandwich. I ate one half. How many halves are left? (One half.)

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. I fold a circle into two equal pieces. What is each piece called?
2. How many halves make one whole?
3. A rectangle is cut into two pieces. One is bigger than the other. Are they halves? Why or why not?

**Expected Answers:**
1. A half (one half of the circle).
2. Two halves make one whole.
3. No — halves must be equal. Both pieces need to be the same size.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to the folding activity. Fold a circle together, unfold it, and overlap the two pieces to show they match. Explicitly connect: "Equal means the same size. Halves are always equal." Revisit at the start of Lesson 8.

### If Mastery Is Met
Say: "You understand halves — two equal shares that make a whole. Next, we will learn about fourths — splitting a shape into four equal pieces!"

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Half-and-Half Art:** Fold a circle and rectangle each in half. Color one half one color and one half another color. Label each section "one half." Display as a reminder poster for the word halves.
2. **Real-World Halves:** Find three things in your home that can be split into two equal halves (a banana, a piece of paper, a sandwich). Draw each one split in half and label it.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- The key idea is that halves must be equal. Students often assume any two pieces are "halves." Always verify by overlapping or folding one piece on top of the other.
- When folding, emphasize the act of checking: "How do we KNOW these are halves? We put one piece on top of the other and they match."
- Show multiple orientations of the fold line. A horizontal line and a vertical line both create halves of a rectangle. A diagonal line through the center of a rectangle also creates halves, but the resulting triangular shapes can confuse students — introduce this only for above-level students.
- Avoid using the fraction notation 1/2 in this lesson. Grade 1 standards use the words half, halves, and half of. Fraction notation is a Grade 2 concept.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:** 1. Yes — halves (fold-and-check confirms equal pieces). 2. Yes — halves. 3. Yes — halves (different orientation but still equal). 4. Yes — both ways make halves because both pieces match. 5. No — pieces do not match (off-center fold).

**Independent Practice:**
1. One half.
2. True.
3. Yes.
4. No.
5. Yes.
6. Accept any valid drawing of a rectangle with a line through the middle and one half colored.
7. Two.
8. False.
9. Accept two valid different-direction folds with drawings.
10. The student who colored exactly one half is right. If the halves are equal, coloring one half means coloring exactly half — not more.

**Interleaved Review:**
11. Circle.
12. 1 half + 1 half = 1 whole.
13. One half remains.

**Exit Ticket:** 1. One half. 2. Two. 3. No — both pieces must be equal to be called halves.
