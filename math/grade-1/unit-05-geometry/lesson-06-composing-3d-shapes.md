# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 5, Lesson 6

## Topic
Composing 3D Shapes — Building Larger Shapes from Smaller 3D Shapes

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Combine two or more 3D shapes to create a new composite 3D structure.
- Name the 3D shapes used to build a composite structure.
- Describe a composite 3D structure using shape names and positional language (on top of, next to, beside, in front of).
- Recognize that 3D shapes can be composed just like 2D shapes.

## Prerequisites
- Knows names and attributes of cube, cone, cylinder, and sphere (Lesson 3)
- Understands composing 2D shapes (Lesson 5)
- Knows positional words: on top of, next to, beside, in front of, behind

## Materials Needed
- 3D solid models: cubes, cones, cylinders, spheres (at least 2 of each if possible)
- Small wooden blocks or building blocks (cubes)
- Paper and pencil for drawing or describing builds
- Whiteboard and dry-erase marker
- Sticky note for exit ticket

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Connect composing 2D shapes (Lesson 5) to the new idea of composing 3D shapes.

Ask: "In our last lesson, we put 2D shapes together to make new shapes. Can you remember: what two shapes did we put together to make a rectangle?" (Two triangles, or two squares.)

Then hold up a cylinder and a cone. Ask: "If I put this cone on TOP of this cylinder — cone on the very top — what does the whole thing look like?" Let the student describe or name it. (It looks like a rocket ship, a dunce cap on a can, etc.)

Say: "Today we are going to stack and combine 3D shapes to build composite structures — just like we did with 2D shapes, but now with solid shapes you can hold."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model building composite 3D structures and describing them using shape names and positions.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Composite structure:** Two or more 3D shapes put together to make one larger structure.
- **On top of:** Positioned directly above and touching.
- **Next to / beside:** Positioned to the left or right and touching.
- **Stack:** To place one shape directly on top of another.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Concrete.**
Build Structure 1: Place a cylinder on the table. Place a cone on top of the cylinder so it sits on the cylinder's circular face. Say: "I put a cone on top of a cylinder. The cone is on top of the cylinder. Together, they look like a rocket or a tower. What 3D shapes did I use?" (A cylinder and a cone.)

Build Structure 2: Place two cubes side by side, touching. Say: "I put two cubes next to each other. Now I have a wider structure. How many cubes did I use?" (Two.) "Both cubes are still cubes, but together they make a bigger rectangular block shape."

Build Structure 3: Stack two cubes — one directly on top of the other. Say: "Two cubes, one on top of the other. I stacked them. Now I have a taller structure. Both cubes are still cubes inside the structure."

Build Structure 4: Place a large cylinder flat, then stack a small cube on top. Say: "Can you describe this structure? What is on the bottom? What is on top?" (Cylinder on bottom, cube on top.)

**Step 2 — Pictorial.**
On the whiteboard, draw a simple outline of a cylinder with a cone on top (like a rocket). Label: "cylinder" at the bottom, "cone" at the top. Say: "We can describe a 3D composite structure with a drawing and labels."

Draw a second structure: two squares (representing cubes) side by side with a third square on top of the left one. Label each part.

**Step 3 — Abstract.**
Ask: "I have a structure. On the bottom is a cube. On top of the cube is a cone. How many 3D shapes make up this structure?" (Two.) "What are they?" (Cube and cone.)

"I have a structure made of three cubes stacked in a tower. What does the whole thing look like?" (A tall rectangular prism shape / a tower.)

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** "I placed a cone on top of a sphere. Describe this structure."
- The sphere is on the bottom. The cone sits on top of the sphere (the point of the cone faces up). Together it looks like an ice cream cone or a party hat on a ball.
- Answer: Cone on top of sphere; sphere on the bottom.

**Example 2:** "I built a structure with two cylinders side by side. How many cylinders did I use? What does the structure look like?"
- Two cylinders. Together they look like two cans or tubes sitting next to each other.
- Answer: 2 cylinders, side by side.

**Example 3:** "I have a composite structure that looks like a tower. It has three shapes stacked on top of one another: a cube at the bottom, a smaller cube in the middle, and a cone at the very top. Name the shapes from bottom to top."
- Bottom: cube. Middle: cube. Top: cone.
- Answer: Cube, cube, cone.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Build and describe composite 3D structures together.

**Activity: Build My Structure**
Take turns: the facilitator describes a structure, the student builds it. Then the student builds a structure and the facilitator describes it.

Round 1 — Facilitator describes:
"Place a cube on the table. Now place another cube on top of the first cube. Now place a cone on the very top. What does your structure look like? How many shapes did you use? Name them from bottom to top."
(Two cubes and one cone. Bottom to top: cube, cube, cone.)

Round 2 — Facilitator describes:
"Place a cylinder on the table. Place a sphere next to the cylinder on the right side. Describe your structure."
(Cylinder on the left, sphere on the right.)

Round 3 — Student builds:
The student builds any structure with three 3D shapes. The facilitator then describes it back using positional language. The student confirms whether the description is correct.

Questions to ask after each build:
- "What shapes did you use?"
- "Can you describe where each shape is?"
- "Are the shapes still their original shapes inside the structure?"

---

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Practice building and describing 3D composite structures independently.

### Difficulty Tiers

**On-level:**
1. I placed a cylinder on the table and put a cone on top. Name the two shapes I used.
2. I stacked three cubes in a tower. How many cubes did I use?
3. Describe a structure you could build with two cylinders and one cube. Where would each shape go?
4. True or false: When you put two cubes side by side, the cubes change their shape.
5. I built a structure: sphere on the bottom, cube on top of the sphere. Which shape is on top?
6. Build a structure using two 3D shapes. Write or say: what two shapes did you use? Where is each one?
7. Name a real building or object you have seen that looks like a 3D composite structure. What shapes does it remind you of?
8. True or false: A cone on top of a cylinder looks like a rocket.

**Challenge:**
9. Build the tallest possible tower using only the 3D shapes you have available. Name each shape from bottom to top and describe why you chose that shape for that position. (Example: "I put the cube at the bottom because it has flat faces and is stable. I put the cylinder on top because it also has a flat face.")
10. Can a sphere sit on top of a cube without rolling off? Try it. Explain what happens and why. (The sphere has no flat face, so it rolls off the flat top of the cube. This is a great observation about attributes.)

### Interleaved Review
11. (From Unit 2 — addition) I used 2 cubes and 3 cylinders to build a structure. How many 3D shapes did I use in all?
12. (From Unit 4 — Lesson 1, 2D shapes) A cube's flat face is a square. How many sides and corners does each square face have?
13. (From Unit 3 — subtraction) I had 8 building blocks (cubes). I used 5 to build a tower. How many blocks are left?

---

## Check for Understanding (3 minutes)
**Exit Ticket:**
1. I put a cube on the bottom and a cone on top. What two 3D shapes make up this structure?
2. True or false: When you stack two cubes, the two cubes disappear and become one new shape.
3. What word do we use to describe a shape that is positioned directly above and touching another shape?

**Expected Answers:**
1. Cube and cone.
2. False — the two cubes are still there; together they just form a taller structure.
3. On top of.

**Mastery = 3 out of 3 correct.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
Return to the concrete building activity. Stack two cubes, say: "There are still two cubes here — I can pull them apart." Ask the student to pull the cubes apart to confirm. Practice describing two or three structures. Revisit at the Lesson 7 warm-up.

### If Mastery Is Met
Say: "You can compose 3D shapes and describe your structures. Next, we will start learning about something brand new — splitting shapes into equal pieces."

---

## Extension (Optional)
1. **Architect Challenge:** Using all available 3D shapes, build the most interesting structure you can. Draw a picture of it and label every shape. Name your structure.
2. **Real-World Connection:** Look at pictures of buildings online or in a book. Try to identify which 3D shapes you can see in the buildings. Can you name the shapes? (Examples: pyramids are like cones; skyscrapers are like tall cylinders or rectangular prisms.)

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes
- Physical building in this lesson is essential. Resist the urge to skip building and go straight to describing. The hands-on work builds the spatial understanding that descriptions alone cannot provide.
- Some shapes are unstable when stacked. A sphere cannot balance on top of most shapes. Let the student discover this — it is a rich teachable moment about shape attributes (flat face vs. curved surface) and real-world design thinking.
- Positional language (on top of, next to, beside) is simultaneously geometry vocabulary and general language arts vocabulary. This lesson reinforces positional words the student likely encountered earlier, but in a new mathematical context.
- If only one copy of each 3D solid is available, plan structures carefully so each build uses different combinations.

## Answer Key

**Guided Practice:** Round 1: cube, cube, cone (3 shapes). Round 2: cylinder on left, sphere on right. Round 3: accept any valid student build and facilitator description.

**Independent Practice:**
1. Cylinder and cone.
2. Three cubes.
3. Accept any reasonable description — e.g., "one cylinder standing up, one cylinder lying on its side, and one cube on top of the standing cylinder."
4. False — the cubes keep their shape; the structure just gets wider.
5. The cube is on top.
6. Accept any valid build and description.
7. Accept any real-world connection (house = rectangle + triangle, castle tower = cylinder + cone, etc.).
8. True.
9. Accept any valid tower with shape-by-shape reasoning.
10. The sphere rolls off; it has no flat face to rest on the cube's flat top.

**Interleaved Review:**
11. 2 + 3 = 5 shapes.
12. 4 sides, 4 corners.
13. 8 − 5 = 3 blocks left.

**Exit Ticket:** 1. Cube and cone. 2. False. 3. On top of.
