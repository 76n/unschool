# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 10

## Topic
Place Value Problem Solving

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Solve word problems that require place value reasoning about tens and ones
- Apply multiple place value skills (decomposing, expanded form, comparing, ordering) in mixed problem sets
- Solve "I am a number" riddles using clues about tens, ones, and comparisons

## Prerequisites
- Grouping objects into tens (Lesson 1)
- Tens and ones on a place value chart (Lesson 2)
- Representing two-digit numbers (Lessons 3-5)
- Reading and writing numbers to 100 (Lesson 6)
- Expanded form (Lesson 7)
- Comparing two-digit numbers (Lesson 8)
- Ordering numbers on a number line (Lesson 9)

## Materials Needed
- Base-ten blocks (tens rods and ones cubes)
- Place value mat (tens | ones columns)
- Number line 0-100
- Paper and pencil

**Purpose:** Quick review pulling from multiple earlier lessons to prepare for mixed problem solving.

3. "Order from least to greatest: 81, 18, 45." (18, 45, 81) *(Lesson 9)*

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)
**Purpose:** Model how to solve word problems and riddles that require place value thinking, showing students how to identify the key information and choose the right strategy.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Word problem:** A math problem told as a story, where you figure out what to do with the numbers.
- **Riddle:** A puzzle with clues that help you figure out the answer.
- **Clue:** A piece of information that helps you solve a problem.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Model a word problem.**
Read aloud: "Mia has 3 bags of 10 crayons and 5 loose crayons. How many crayons does Mia have in all?"

"Let's think about this. What do we know? 3 bags of 10 — that's 3 tens. And 5 loose crayons — that's 5 ones. Let's build it: 3 tens rods and 5 ones cubes. 3 tens and 5 ones = 35. Mia has 35 crayons."

Emphasize: "The words 'bags of 10' tell us to think about tens. Whenever you see groups of ten in a problem, that's the tens digit!"

**Step 2: Model a comparison word problem.**
Read aloud: "Leo picked 47 apples. Ava picked 52 apples. Who picked more apples?"

"We need to compare 47 and 52. Compare tens first: 47 has 4 tens, 52 has 5 tens. 5 tens > 4 tens. So 52 > 47. Ava picked more apples."

**Step 3: Model an 'I am a number' riddle.**
Read aloud: "I am a number. I have 6 tens. My ones digit is 3 less than my tens digit. What number am I?"

"Let's use the clues one at a time:
- Clue 1: I have 6 tens. So my tens digit is 6. I'm in the 60s.
- Clue 2: My ones digit is 3 less than my tens digit. My tens digit is 6. 6 - 3 = 3. My ones digit is 3.
So I am 63!"

Write: **63**.

**Step 4: Model a riddle with a comparison clue.**
Read aloud: "I am a number between 20 and 30. I am greater than 25. My ones digit is even. What number am I?"

"Clue 1: Between 20 and 30. So my tens digit is 2 and I'm in the 20s — but not 20 or 30 themselves. I could be 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, or 29.
Clue 2: Greater than 25. That leaves 26, 27, 28, 29.
Clue 3: My ones digit is even. Even digits are 0, 2, 4, 6, 8. From the remaining numbers: 26 (ones = 6, even), 27 (ones = 7, odd), 28 (ones = 8, even), 29 (ones = 9, odd).
That leaves 26 or 28. Both work! The answer is 26 or 28."

"Sometimes a riddle can have more than one answer. That's okay!"

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** "Jake has 5 boxes of 10 marbles and 8 extra marbles. How many marbles does Jake have?" 5 tens + 8 ones = 58 marbles.

**Example B:** "I am a number. I have 4 ones. I am greater than 30 but less than 50. My tens digit is odd. What number am I?" Between 30 and 50 with an odd tens digit: 30s (tens digit 3, odd) or 40s (tens digit 4, even). Only the 30s have an odd tens digit. With 4 ones: 34.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student solves mixed place value problems with facilitator support.

1. "Emma has 7 bags of 10 stickers and 2 loose stickers. How many stickers does she have?"
   *Facilitator prompt: "How many tens? How many ones? Put them together."*
   (72 stickers)

2. "Ben has 65 baseball cards. Lily has 56 baseball cards. Who has more? How many more tens does that person have?"
   *Facilitator prompt: "Compare the tens first. What do you notice?"*
   (Ben has more; 6 tens > 5 tens, so Ben has 1 more ten)

3. "I am a number. I have 9 tens and 1 one. What number am I?"
   *Facilitator prompt: "Build it on the place value mat — 9 tens and 1 one."*
   (91)

4. "I am a number. I am between 40 and 50. My ones digit is 5 more than 2. What number am I?"
   *Facilitator prompt: "First, what digit is 5 more than 2? Now put that in the ones place."*
   (47, since 2 + 5 = 7; the number is 47)

5. "Put these in order from greatest to least: the number that is 30 + 5, the number with 7 tens and 1 one, and the number fifty-three."
   *Facilitator prompt: "First, figure out what each number is. Then compare."*
   (30 + 5 = 35, 7 tens and 1 one = 71, fifty-three = 53. Greatest to least: 71, 53, 35)

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### On-Level
1. "Rosa has 4 bags of 10 oranges and 6 extra oranges. How many oranges does Rosa have?" (46 oranges)
2. "Aiden has 83 pennies. Sofia has 78 pennies. Who has more pennies?" (Aiden; 83 > 78)
3. "I am a number. My tens digit is 5 and my ones digit is 9. What number am I?" (59)
4. "Which is greater: 40 + 7 or 50 + 2?" (50 + 2 = 52 is greater than 40 + 7 = 47)
5. "Order from least to greatest: the number with 3 tens and 0 ones, the number 13, and the number 31." (13, 30, 31)

### Stretch
6. "I am a number. I have 8 tens. I am less than 85. My ones digit is odd. What numbers could I be?" (81 or 83)
7. "Marcus has 6 bags of 10 toy cars and some loose toy cars. He has 64 toy cars in all. How many loose toy cars does he have?" (4 loose toy cars, because 60 + 4 = 64)
8. "Write a number that has 3 more tens than 25." (55, because 25 has 2 tens and 2 + 3 = 5 tens, and keeping 5 ones gives 55)

### Challenge
9. "I am a two-digit number. My tens digit and my ones digit add up to 7. I am greater than 50. What numbers could I be?" (61, since 6 + 1 = 7; or 70, since 7 + 0 = 7)
10. "Zara is thinking of a number. She says: 'My number is between 30 and 60. It has more ones than tens. The ones digit is 8.' What is Zara's number?" (Between 30 and 60 with ones digit 8: could be 38, 48, or 58. More ones than tens means ones digit > tens digit: 38 has 8 > 3, 48 has 8 > 4, 58 has 8 > 5. All three work: 38, 48, or 58.)

### Interleaved Review
*(From prior lessons)*
- "How many tens and ones in 76?" (7 tens, 6 ones) *(Lesson 2)*
- "15 - 8 = ___?" (7) *(Unit 1 review)*

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

1. "Sam has 2 bags of 10 pretzels and 9 extra pretzels. How many pretzels does Sam have?" (29 pretzels)
2. "I am a number. I have 7 tens. I am less than 75. My ones digit is 2. What number am I?" (72)

**Scoring:**
- 2/2 correct = Mastery
- 1/2 correct = Approaching mastery
- 0/2 correct = Needs reteaching

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- For word problems, return to base-ten blocks. Have the student act out each problem physically: "Here are 2 bags of 10 — that's 2 tens rods. Here are 9 extra — that's 9 ones cubes. Now count. What number is that?"
- For riddles, practice working through clues one at a time. Write each clue on a separate line. After each clue, ask: "What do we know now? What numbers are still possible?" Narrow down step by step.
- Revisit specific weak areas: if the student cannot decompose into tens and ones, return to Lesson 2; if the student cannot compare, return to Lesson 8.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Have the student write their own "I am a number" riddle with at least two clues and give it to the facilitator to solve.
- Begin the Unit 2 Assessment.

## Extension (Optional)
**Create Your Own Riddle Book:** The student writes 3-5 "I am a number" riddles on separate cards or pages. Each riddle should have at least two clues (e.g., one about tens/ones, one about comparing or ordering). The student draws base-ten block illustrations as hints. They can quiz family members or friends with their riddle book. This activity reinforces place value vocabulary and reasoning while building communication skills.

---

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors

1. **Not connecting "bags of 10" (or similar groupings) to tens.** The student counts 3 bags of 10 as 3 instead of 30. Always have them build it: "Put out 3 tens rods. That's 30, not 3. Now add the extras."

2. **Ignoring one of the clues in a riddle.** The student reads the first clue, finds an answer, and stops without checking the remaining clues. Model the habit: "Let's check — does our answer work for ALL the clues?"

3. **Confusing "between" as inclusive or exclusive.** When a riddle says "between 40 and 50," some students include 40 and 50 themselves. Clarify: "Between 40 and 50 means after 40 and before 50 — so 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 49."

4. **Struggling with multi-step problems.** The student feels overwhelmed when a problem requires more than one place value skill. Encourage them to take it one step at a time: "First, figure out each number. Then compare. Two small steps, not one big one."

### Pacing Advice
This is a review and synthesis lesson. If the student has been strong throughout the unit, it can be completed briskly in one session, with extra time spent on the extension activity. If the student has gaps from earlier lessons, use this lesson diagnostically: note which problem types cause difficulty and revisit those specific lessons before administering the unit assessment. There is no rush — it is better to solidify understanding here than to move to the assessment with shaky foundations.
