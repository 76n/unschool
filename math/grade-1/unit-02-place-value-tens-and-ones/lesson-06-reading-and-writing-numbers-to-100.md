# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 6

## Topic
Reading and Writing Numbers to 100

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Read any number from 0 to 100 correctly
- Write any number from 0 to 100 when given its name or place value description
- Understand 100 as 10 tens

## Prerequisites
- Tens and ones for numbers 10–19 (Lessons 1–3)
- Representing numbers 20–50 (Lesson 4)
- Representing numbers 51–99 (Lesson 5)
- Hundred chart familiarity (Lessons 4–5)

## Materials Needed
- Hundred chart
- Number cards 0–100
- Place value mat (tens | ones columns)
- Paper and pencil

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review reading tens and ones across the full 0–99 range.

1. "How many tens and ones in 72?" (7 tens, 2 ones) *(Lesson 5)*
2. "What number is 3 tens and 8 ones?" (38) *(Lesson 4)*
3. "How many tens and ones in 60?" (6 tens, 0 ones) *(Lesson 5)*
4. "What is the largest two-digit number?" (99) *(Lesson 5)*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Bridge from place value understanding to fluent reading and writing of number names and numerals, including the tricky teen/decade pairs and the introduction of 100.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Number name:** The word form of a number (e.g., "thirty-five")
- **Numeral:** The written form of a number using digits (e.g., 35)
- **One hundred:** 10 tens (100)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Practice reading numbers from the hundred chart.**
Point to random numbers on the chart. Student says the name aloud:
- Point to 24: "twenty-four."
- Point to 57: "fifty-seven."
- Point to 80: "eighty."
- Point to 99: "ninety-nine."

Do at least 8–10 numbers, mixing decades (40, 70) with non-decades (36, 83).

**Step 2: Dictation practice.**
Say a number name; student writes the numeral:
- "Thirty-five" → student writes 35
- "Seventy" → student writes 70
- "Ninety-nine" → student writes 99
- "Forty-two" → student writes 42

*If the student hesitates, prompt: "How many tens? Write that first. How many ones? Write that next to it."*

**Step 3: Tricky numbers — teens versus decades.**
Write 13 and 30 side by side. "These are easy to mix up. Listen carefully."
- "Thir-TEEN: 1 ten and 3 ones. The 'teen' tells you 1 ten. 13."
- "THIR-ty: 3 tens and 0 ones. The 'ty' tells you it's the tens. 30."

Repeat with:
- 14 and 40: "Four-TEEN = 1 ten, 4 ones (14). FOR-ty = 4 tens, 0 ones (40)."
- 15 and 50: "Fif-TEEN = 1 ten, 5 ones (15). FIF-ty = 5 tens, 0 ones (50)."
- 16 and 60: "Six-TEEN = 1 ten, 6 ones (16). SIX-ty = 6 tens, 0 ones (60)."

Build each pair with blocks so the student can see the difference: 13 has 1 rod and 3 cubes; 30 has 3 rods and 0 cubes.

**Step 4: Introduce 100.**
Count out 10 tens rods. Line them up. "How many tens? 10 tens. 10 tens = 100. One hundred!" Write 100. "Look — it has THREE digits now. A 1 in a brand-new place, and two zeros. This is the first three-digit number. Everything we've been learning — tens and ones — led us right here."

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** Read this number: 63. Think: 6 tens and 3 ones = "sixty-three."

**Example 2:** Write this number: "eighty-seven." Think: eighty = 8 tens, seven = 7 ones → 87.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student practices reading numbers aloud and writing numerals from dictation.

**Activity 1: Read these numbers aloud (4 minutes)**
Student reads each number and says the name:
- 47 → "forty-seven"
- 82 → "eighty-two"
- 15 → "fifteen"
- 60 → "sixty"
- 100 → "one hundred"

*Facilitator prompt: "Look at the tens digit first. What decade is it? Then read the ones."*

**Activity 2: Write these numbers from dictation (4 minutes)**
Facilitator says the name; student writes the numeral:
- "Sixty-three" → (63)
- "Nineteen" → (19)
- "Fifty" → (50)
- "Eighty-seven" → (87)
- "One hundred" → (100)

*Facilitator prompt: "How many tens do you hear? Write that digit first. How many ones? Write that next."*

**Activity 3: Teen or decade? (2 minutes)**
Quick-fire drill. Facilitator says a number; student writes it:
- "Fourteen" → (14)
- "Forty" → (40)
- "Sixteen" → (16)
- "Sixty" → (60)

*If the student writes the wrong one, stop and compare: "You wrote 40. Forty has 4 tens. But fourteen has 1 ten and 4 ones — that's 14."*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Read and write numbers across the full 0–100 range independently.

**★ On-level:**
Write the number name for each numeral:
1. 29 (twenty-nine)
2. 53 (fifty-three)
3. 70 (seventy)
4. 14 (fourteen)
5. 81 (eighty-one)
6. 100 (one hundred)

**★★ Stretch:**
7. "Write the number that is one more than 49." (50)
8. "Write the number that is one less than 80." (79)

**★★★ Challenge:**
9. "Which is larger, thirty-seven or seventy-three? How do you know?" (73 — it has 7 tens, while 37 has only 3 tens)
10. "Write all the numbers from 95 to 100." (95, 96, 97, 98, 99, 100)

### Interleaved Review
- "How many tens and ones in 56?" (5 tens, 6 ones) *(Lesson 5)*
- "What number is 4 tens and 0 ones?" (40) *(Lesson 4)*
- "9 + 7 = ___?" (16) *(Unit 1 review)*

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Assess whether the student can read, write, and reason about numbers across the full 0–100 range.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. Write the number: "fifty-six" (56)
2. Read this number: 73 ("seventy-three")
3. How many tens in 100? (10)

**Mastery = All 3 correct, with the student able to read and write without confusing teens and decades.**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student confuses teens and decades (e.g., writes 30 for thirteen), drill the pairs side by side: build 13 and 30 with blocks, say the names while pointing, and have the student repeat. Stress the phonetic cues: "-teen" = one ten, "-ty" = that many tens.
- If the student can decompose into tens and ones but struggles to write the numeral, practice the two-step process: "Say how many tens. Write that digit. Say how many ones. Write that digit right next to it."
- Use number cards: show a numeral card and have the student read it; say a name and have the student find and hold up the matching card.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 7.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**The teen/decade confusion is one of the most common and persistent errors in early math.** Students hear "fourteen" and write 40, or see 60 and say "sixteen." This is not a sign of low ability — it reflects the genuine irregularity of English number names. (In languages like Chinese, Korean, and Japanese, the names are perfectly transparent: "14" is literally said as "ten-four." English requires memorizing which sound pattern goes with which structure.) Be patient and explicit.

**Drill the contrasting pairs regularly.** Even after this lesson, continue to include teen/decade pairs in warm-ups for several weeks. Quick contrasts like "Write fourteen. Now write forty. How are they different?" keep the distinction fresh.

**100 is a preview, not a mastery target.** The student needs to know that 100 exists and that it equals 10 tens, but they do not need to fully understand three-digit place value yet. If a student asks "What comes after 100?", affirm their curiosity: "Great question! 101, 102, 103... we'll explore those later. For now, you know every number from 0 all the way to 100."

**Reading versus writing are different skills.** Some students can read 73 as "seventy-three" but struggle to write 73 when they hear "seventy-three." Other students are the reverse. This lesson practices both directions deliberately. Note which direction the student finds harder and provide extra practice there.
