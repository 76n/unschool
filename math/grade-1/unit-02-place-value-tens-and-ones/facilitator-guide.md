# Facilitator Guide — Grade 1 Math, Unit 2: Place Value — Tens and Ones

**Grade:** 1
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 2 — Place Value (Tens and Ones)
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Fully facilitated (adult present and actively guiding)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit builds the foundation for all multi-digit work by teaching students that our number system is organized in groups of ten. Students move from counting individual objects to seeing numbers as composed of tens and ones — a conceptual shift that underpins addition, subtraction, and place value understanding through all of elementary math.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Group a collection of objects into tens and leftover ones
- Represent any two-digit number using tens and ones (e.g., 47 = 4 tens and 7 ones)
- Read and write numbers from 0 to 100
- Understand expanded form (e.g., 63 = 60 + 3)
- Compare two-digit numbers using >, <, and = with place value reasoning
- Locate and order numbers on a number line to 100

**Why this matters:** Place value is the organizing principle of our entire number system. Without it, students cannot add or subtract multi-digit numbers, understand money, read clocks, or work with measurement. The tens-and-ones structure introduced here extends directly to hundreds, thousands, and beyond. Students who truly understand that 47 means "4 groups of ten and 7 more" — not just "a four and a seven" — have a massive advantage in all future math.

---

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, confirm:

- [ ] Counts to 100 by ones (or close — up to 80 is acceptable to start)
- [ ] Counts to 100 by tens (10, 20, 30, ... 100)
- [ ] Reads and writes numerals 0-20
- [ ] Understands teen numbers as "ten and some ones" (Kindergarten Unit 3)
- [ ] Adds and subtracts within 20 (Unit 1 mastery)
- [ ] Uses the Make 10 strategy (familiarity with 10 as a benchmark number)

---

## Materials Needed for This Unit

- **Counting objects (100+):** Beans, cubes, paperclips — anything small and plentiful
- **Rubber bands or small cups:** For bundling groups of 10
- **Base-ten blocks** (if available): Rods of 10, unit cubes — extremely helpful but can be improvised with bundled objects
- **Place value mat:** Two-column chart labeled "Tens" and "Ones" (draw on paper or use a folder)
- **Hundred chart** (10×10 grid numbered 1-100)
- **Number line 0-100** (hand-drawn or printed — can use a long strip of paper)
- **Number cards 0-100** (can make gradually; start with 0-50)
- **Paper and pencil**
- **Comparison symbol cards:** > < = (can cut from index cards)

| 9 | Ordering Numbers on a Number Line | 1 |
| 10 | Place Value Problem Solving | 1 |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 2 (place value chart) is foundational. Budget 2 sessions if the student needs extra time connecting physical bundles to the chart.
- Lesson 8 (comparing) can take 2 sessions — one for the concept and symbols, one for practice.
- If the student already has strong teen-number understanding from Kindergarten, Lesson 3 can be brief.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student reads 47 as "four seven" or "four and seven" | Sees digits individually, not as tens and ones | Always verbalize: "47 is 4 tens and 7 ones. That's forty-seven." Have the student build the number with bundles every time. |
| Student says 34 has "3 ones and 4 tens" (reverses positions) | Confuses tens and ones place | Use the place value mat consistently. Tens always on the left. Point to each column as you read: "3 tens (point left), 4 ones (point right)." |
| Student thinks 17 is "1 and 7" rather than "1 ten and 7 ones" | Teen numbers not connected to place value | Build 17 physically: bundle 10, plus 7 loose. "See? 1 ten and 7 ones. Sev-en-teen. Hear the 'teen'? That means 'ten plus.'" |
| Student thinks 60 > 53 only because "6 is bigger than 5" | Correct answer, wrong reasoning (or sometimes wrong when ones digit is large) | Test with: "Is 29 greater than 31?" If student says yes (because 9 > 1), the tens-place reasoning hasn't connected. Reteach comparison starting with tens. |
| Student writes 30 + 4 = 7 | Drops the zero, treats 30 as 3 | Expanded form confusion. Build with blocks: 3 rods of ten + 4 units = 34, not 7. "30 is thirty — that's 3 tens. 30 + 4 = 34." |

## Conversation Starters

- "How many tens can you make from 36 objects?"
- "If I have 5 tens and 2 ones, what number is that?"
- "Which is bigger: 48 or 52? How do you know without counting?"
- "What number comes right after 39? How do you know?" (Hint: the ones go back to 0 and the tens go up by 1)
- "If you have 7 tens and 0 ones, what number is that? What if you add 1 more one?"

---

## When to Seek Additional Help

Consider seeking additional support if:

- After 3+ weeks, the student cannot consistently group objects into tens and tell how many tens and ones
- The student confuses the tens and ones positions after repeated practice with the place value mat
- Counting to 50 by ones is unreliable (indicates a prerequisite gap)
- The student consistently treats two-digit numbers as two separate digits with no place value meaning

---

## What Comes Next

After this unit, students move to **Unit 3: Addition and Subtraction Within 100 (Introduction)**, where they use place value understanding to add and subtract two-digit numbers. The tens-and-ones decomposition from this unit is the engine for those strategies.
