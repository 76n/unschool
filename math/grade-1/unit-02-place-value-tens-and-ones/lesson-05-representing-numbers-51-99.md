# Math — Grade 1 — Unit 2, Lesson 5

## Topic
Representing Numbers 51–99

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this lesson, the student will be able to:
- Represent numbers 51–99 using tens and ones
- Extend the place value pattern from numbers 20–50 to the full two-digit range
- Recognize that 9 tens and 9 ones (99) is the largest two-digit number

## Prerequisites
- Tens and ones for numbers 10–19 (Lessons 1–3)
- Representing numbers 20–50 (Lesson 4)
- Familiarity with the hundred chart (Lesson 4)

## Materials Needed
- 100 counting objects (cubes, beans, or craft sticks)
- Rubber bands (for bundling)
- Place value mat (tens | ones columns)
- Base-ten blocks (tens rods and ones cubes)
- Hundred chart

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Review place value for numbers 20–50 and decade number patterns.

1. "How many tens and ones in 34?" (3 tens, 4 ones) *(Lesson 4)*
2. "What number is 5 tens and 0 ones?" (50) *(Lesson 4)*
3. "How many tens and ones in 40?" (4 tens, 0 ones) *(Lesson 4)*
4. "What comes after 49 on the hundred chart?" (50) *(Lesson 4)*

---

## Direct Instruction (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Extend place value understanding into the 51–99 range, showing that the tens-and-ones pattern continues all the way to the largest two-digit number.

### Key Vocabulary
- **Sixty:** 6 tens (60)
- **Seventy:** 7 tens (70)
- **Eighty:** 8 tens (80)
- **Ninety:** 9 tens (90)
- **Two-digit:** A number that uses exactly two places (tens and ones), from 10 to 99

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1: Build 56.**
Count out 56 objects. Bundle into 5 groups of 10 with 6 left over. Place 5 tens rods and 6 ones cubes on the mat. Write: Tens = 5, Ones = 6. "5 tens and 6 ones = fifty-six."

**Step 2: Build 70.**
Count out 70 objects. Bundle into 7 groups of 10, 0 left over. "7 tens and 0 ones = seventy. Just like 30, 40, 50 — a decade number with zero ones."

**Step 3: Build 83.**
Count out 83 objects. Bundle into 8 groups of 10 with 3 left over. "8 tens and 3 ones = eighty-three." Place on the mat and record on the chart.

**Step 4: The biggest tens digit.**
"What's the biggest number of tens we can have with just two digits? Let's count: 1 ten, 2 tens, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 — nine! 9 tens = 90." Build 9 tens rods. "Now add as many ones as we can: 9 ones. 9 tens and 9 ones = 99. That's the largest two-digit number!"

**Step 5: What comes after 99?**
"What happens if we get one more? 99 + 1... we'd have 10 tens. But 10 tens is a new group — it's called one hundred! 10 tens = 100." Show 10 tens rods grouped together. "That's a three-digit number. We'll learn more about that later — for now, just know that 99 is the biggest two-digit number."

*Don't go deep into 100 here — just plant the seed.*

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** 67 = ? tens and ? ones. Think: 6 tens and 7 ones. On the chart: Tens = 6, Ones = 7.

**Example 2:** What number is 9 tens and 2 ones? Think: 9 tens = 90, plus 2 ones = 92. The number is 92.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Student builds numbers 51–99 with hands-on materials and records on a place value chart.

**Activity 1: Build and record (5 minutes)**
For each number, student builds with base-ten blocks, places on the mat, and writes the tens and ones:
- 54 → (5 tens, 4 ones)
- 67 → (6 tens, 7 ones)
- 72 → (7 tens, 2 ones)

*Facilitator prompt: "Is this bigger or smaller than the numbers we worked with last lesson? What changed? The tens digit is bigger!"*

**Activity 2: Build and record continued (5 minutes)**
Continue with:
- 88 → (8 tens, 8 ones)
- 90 → (9 tens, 0 ones)
- 61 → (6 tens, 1 one)

*Facilitator prompt: "Find each number on the hundred chart after you build it. Do you see the pattern?"*

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)
**Purpose:** Apply tens-and-ones understanding to numbers 51–99 independently.

**★ On-level:**
1. 53 = ___ tens and ___ ones (5, 3)
2. 78 = ___ tens and ___ ones (7, 8)
3. 60 = ___ tens and ___ ones (6, 0)
4. 95 = ___ tens and ___ ones (9, 5)
5. 81 = ___ tens and ___ ones (8, 1)
6. 66 = ___ tens and ___ ones (6, 6)

**★★ Stretch:**
7. "I have 7 tens and 4 ones. What is my number?" (74)
8. "What is the smallest number with 8 in the tens place?" (80)

**★★★ Challenge:**
9. "I am 1 more than 69. What am I? How many tens and ones do I have?" (70 = 7 tens, 0 ones)
10. "List all two-digit numbers where both digits are the same." (11, 22, 33, 44, 55, 66, 77, 88, 99)

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)
**Purpose:** Verify the student can decompose numbers 51–99 into tens and ones, compose a number from a place value description, and identify the largest two-digit number.

**Exit Ticket:**

1. How many tens and ones in 84? (8 tens, 4 ones)
2. What number is 6 tens and 2 ones? (62)
3. What is the largest two-digit number? (99)

**Mastery = All 3 correct, with the student able to explain why 99 is the largest two-digit number (9 is the most tens, 9 is the most ones).**

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- If the student struggles with higher decades (70s, 80s, 90s) but handled 20–50 well, the issue may be unfamiliarity with the decade names. Practice: "6 tens = sixty, 7 tens = seventy, 8 tens = eighty, 9 tens = ninety" until automatic.
- Build numbers side by side: 53 next to 35. "Which has more tens? Which is bigger?" This highlights how the tens digit determines the size of the number.
- Use the hundred chart to count forward from 50 by ones, pointing to each number and saying its name.

### If Mastery Is Met
Move to Lesson 6 (Reading and Writing Numbers to 100).

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

**This is a milestone lesson.** After this lesson, the student understands the structure of every number from 0 to 99. Celebrate this! The student has gone from counting individual objects to understanding the entire two-digit number system. That is a huge conceptual leap.

**Students may be surprised by what happens at 99.** The preview of 100 is intentional — it creates curiosity. Some students will want to explore further ("What about 101? 200?"). Encourage the excitement but keep the focus on mastering 0–99 for now. Let them know they will learn about bigger numbers soon.

**Watch for the "sixty/sixteen" confusion.** Just as students may confuse thirteen/thirty, they may confuse sixty/sixteen, seventy/seventeen, etc. If this arises, use the same strategy: "Six-TEEN = 1 ten, 6 ones. SIX-ty = 6 tens, 0 ones." Having both numbers built side by side with blocks makes the difference vivid.

**Physical materials matter.** Even though the numbers are getting bigger (requiring more blocks), keep using the physical representations. The act of bundling 8 groups of 10 and seeing them fill the mat builds genuine understanding that no amount of abstract digit-writing can replace.
