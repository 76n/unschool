# Math — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 6

## Topic
Identifying Proportional Relationships

## Learning Objectives
- Determine whether a relationship is proportional given a table, a graph, or an equation.
- Explain why a given relationship is or is not proportional using mathematical evidence.
- Distinguish proportional relationships from additive relationships and other non-proportional patterns.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to test whether a table shows a constant ratio y/x (Lesson 2).
- Ability to identify the constant of proportionality from a table or equation (Lesson 3).
- Understanding that the graph of a proportional relationship is a straight line through the origin (Lesson 5).
- Ability to write proportional equations in the form y = kx (Lesson 4).

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Graph paper.
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 7.RP.2a — Decide whether two quantities are in a proportional relationship, e.g., by testing for equivalent ratios in a table or graphing on a coordinate plane and observing whether the graph is a straight line through the origin.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Quickly review the three representations of proportional relationships.

1. Display three items and ask the student to classify each as proportional or not proportional:
   - Table: (2, 6), (4, 12), (5, 15) — "Proportional or not?"
   - Equation: y = 3x + 2 — "Proportional or not?"
   - Graph: a straight line through (0, 0) and (3, 9) — "Proportional or not?"
2. Discuss answers: Table — proportional (6/2 = 12/4 = 15/5 = 3). Equation — not proportional (has + 2). Graph — proportional (straight line through origin).

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Additive relationship** — a relationship where the same amount is added to get from x to y (e.g., y = x + 5). This is NOT proportional.
- **Multiplicative relationship** — a relationship where x is multiplied by the same factor to get y (e.g., y = 5x). This IS proportional.
- **y-intercept** — the point where a graph crosses the y-axis. In a proportional relationship, the y-intercept is always (0, 0).

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Connect to a concrete model.**
"We have learned three ways to represent a proportional relationship: tables, equations, and graphs. Today we bring them all together and practice deciding if a relationship is proportional — no matter how it is presented."

Write the three tests on the board:

| Representation | Test for Proportionality |
|---|---|
| Table | Compute y/x for every row. If the ratio is the same every time, it is proportional. |
| Equation | If the equation has the form y = kx (no added or subtracted constant), it is proportional. |
| Graph | If the graph is a straight line AND passes through the origin (0, 0), it is proportional. |

**Step 2 — Non-examples with tables.**
Present two tables side by side:

Table A:

| x | y |
|---|---|
| 2 | 10 |
| 4 | 20 |
| 7 | 35 |

Table B:

| x | y |
|---|---|
| 2 | 10 |
| 4 | 18 |
| 7 | 31 |

"Table A: 10/2 = 5, 20/4 = 5, 35/7 = 5. Proportional."
"Table B: 10/2 = 5, 18/4 = 4.5, 31/7 ≈ 4.43. Not proportional."

"Notice that Table B looks similar — the differences between consecutive y-values might form a pattern. But proportional means the RATIO is constant, not the difference."

**Step 3 — Non-examples with equations.**
Compare:
- y = 7x — Proportional (form y = kx, k = 7).
- y = 7x + 3 — Not proportional (the + 3 means when x = 0, y = 3, not 0).
- y = x/4 — Proportional (this is the same as y = 0.25x, k = 0.25).
- y = x^2 — Not proportional (when x = 1, y = 1; when x = 2, y = 4; when x = 3, y = 9; the ratio y/x is not constant: 1, 2, 3).

**Step 4 — Non-examples with graphs.**
Draw (or describe) three graphs:
- Graph 1: Straight line through the origin — proportional.
- Graph 2: Straight line that crosses the y-axis at (0, 2) — not proportional (does not go through the origin).
- Graph 3: Curve through the origin — not proportional (not a straight line).

"Both conditions must be true: straight line AND through the origin."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Identifying from a table**

| Hours | Pay ($) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 |
| 3 | 27 |
| 5 | 45 |
| 8 | 72 |

27/3 = 9, 45/5 = 9, 72/8 = 9. The ratio is constant. Proportional. k = $9 per hour.

**Example B — Identifying from an equation**
Is y = (2/5)x proportional?

Yes. It has the form y = kx with k = 2/5. No constant is added or subtracted. When x = 0, y = 0.

**Example C — Identifying from a graph description**
A graph is a straight line passing through (0, 4) and (3, 16).

Does it pass through the origin? No — it passes through (0, 4). This is not a proportional relationship. Verification: if it were proportional, k = 16/3 ≈ 5.33, and the point (0, 0) should be on the line. But when x = 0, y = 4 (not 0). The y-intercept is 4, not 0.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

   | x | y |
   |---|---|
   | 3 | 15 |
   | 6 | 30 |
   | 9 | 45 |
   | 12 | 60 |

   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Compute y/x for each row. Are they all the same?"

2. Is the equation y = 8x - 1 proportional? Explain.
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Does it have the form y = kx? What happens when x = 0?"

3. A graph is a straight line through (0, 0) and (4, 10). Is the relationship proportional? If so, what is k?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Check: straight line? Through the origin? If yes to both, find k."

4. Alex says: "The equation y = 0 x is not proportional because nothing is happening." Is Alex correct?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Technically, y = 0 satisfies y = kx with k = 0. Is the ratio y/x constant? What does the graph look like?"

5. Classify each relationship as proportional or not proportional. Explain briefly.
   - (a) A taxi charges $2 per mile.
   - (b) A taxi charges $3 plus $2 per mile.
   - (c) The perimeter of a square is 4 times the side length.

   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Think about each: does it start at zero? Is there a fixed charge or added constant?"

- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.

- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Create a sorting activity: prepare 8-10 cards showing tables, equations, and graph descriptions. Have the student sort them into "proportional" and "not proportional" piles, explaining each decision.
- For graph identification errors, draw two graphs side by side — one through the origin, one not — and have the student physically trace each line back to see whether it hits (0, 0).
- For equation identification errors, have the student substitute x = 0 into each equation. If y is not 0, the relationship is not proportional.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Assign the Challenge problems if not yet completed.
- Preview: "We are now shifting gears to apply proportional reasoning to percents. In the next lesson, we review what percent means and how to convert between forms."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Proportional or Not? Real-World Debate:** Present scenarios and have the student argue whether each is proportional: (a) Distance and time for a car stuck in traffic; (b) Number of workers and time to finish a job; (c) Circumference and diameter of circles.
- **Error Detector:** Provide a student's work where they incorrectly classified a relationship. Have the student find and explain the error.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Thinking that all linear relationships are proportional.** A straight line with a nonzero y-intercept (e.g., y = 2x + 3) is linear but not proportional. Emphasize: proportional is a SPECIAL CASE of linear — the line must go through (0, 0).
- **Confusing a constant difference with a constant ratio.** In Table B of the instruction (y = x + 8), the difference between y and x is always 8, but the ratio y/x changes. Proportional means constant ratio, not constant difference.
- **Not checking all rows of a table.** Some students check one ratio and declare "proportional." Require checking every row.

### Pacing Advice
- This lesson synthesizes Lessons 2-5. If the student has been strong throughout, this lesson can serve as a quick consolidation and may take less than a full session.
- If the student has persistent misconceptions, this is the ideal place to slow down and address them before moving into the percent portion of the unit.
- The Guided Practice sorting activity (Problem 5) and Independent Practice Problem 4 (circle the equations) are efficient diagnostic tools — use them to identify specific gaps.
