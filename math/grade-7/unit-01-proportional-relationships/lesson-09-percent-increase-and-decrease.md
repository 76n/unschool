# Math — Grade 7 — Unit 1, Lesson 9

## Topic
Percent Increase and Decrease

## Learning Objectives
- Calculate the amount of increase or decrease given an original value and a percent change.
- Compute the new value after a percent increase or percent decrease.
- Find the percent change given the original and new values using the formula: percent change = (amount of change / original) x 100.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to find the percent of a number (Lesson 8).
- Fluency with converting between percents and decimals (Lesson 7).
- Ability to set up and solve proportional equations (Lesson 4).
- Comfort with subtraction and division of decimals.

## Materials Needed
- Whiteboard and markers (or projector).
- Calculator (optional — for multi-step decimal calculations).
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 7.RP.3 — Use proportional relationships to solve multi-step ratio and percent problems. Examples: simple interest, tax, markups and markdowns, gratuities and commissions, fees, percent increase and decrease, percent error.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review finding the percent of a number and set up the idea of change.

1. Find 25% of 80. (20)
2. Find 10% of 150. (15)
3. "A video game costs $60. The price goes up by $12. By what fraction of the original price did it increase?" (12/60 = 1/5)
4. Ask: "How would you express 1/5 as a percent?" (20%) "So the price increased by 20%."

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Percent increase** — the percent by which a value grows compared to the original. Used when the new value is greater than the original.
- **Percent decrease** — the percent by which a value shrinks compared to the original. Used when the new value is less than the original.
- **Amount of change** — the difference between the new value and the original value (always positive when describing size of change).
- **Markup** — a percent increase applied to the cost of an item to set the selling price.
- **Discount (markdown)** — a percent decrease applied to a price to reduce it.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 — Connect to a concrete model.**
"Last year, a pair of sneakers cost $80. This year, the same sneakers cost $92. Let's figure out the percent increase."

Draw a bar model: one bar for $80 (original), and an extended bar for $92. The extra piece is $12.

"The amount of change is $92 - $80 = $12."

**Step 2 — The percent change formula.**
Write on the board:

Percent change = (Amount of change / Original value) x 100

For the sneakers: (12 / 80) x 100 = 0.15 x 100 = 15% increase.

"Always divide by the ORIGINAL value — the value before the change happened."

**Step 3 — Finding the new value after an increase.**
"A shirt costs $40. The price is marked up by 30%. What is the new price?"

Method 1 (Two steps):
- Find the increase: 0.30 x 40 = $12.
- Add to original: 40 + 12 = $52.

Method 2 (One step — multiplier shortcut):
- A 30% increase means the new value is 100% + 30% = 130% of the original.
- New price = 1.30 x 40 = $52.

"The multiplier for an increase of p% is (1 + p/100)."

**Step 4 — Finding the new value after a decrease.**
"A laptop costs $600. It is on sale for 15% off. What is the sale price?"

Method 1 (Two steps):
- Find the discount: 0.15 x 600 = $90.
- Subtract from original: 600 - 90 = $510.

Method 2 (One step — multiplier shortcut):
- A 15% decrease means the new value is 100% - 15% = 85% of the original.
- Sale price = 0.85 x 600 = $510.

"The multiplier for a decrease of p% is (1 - p/100)."

### Worked Examples

**Example A — Percent increase (find the new value)**
A town's population was 12,000 last year. It increased by 8%. What is the new population?

Method 1: Increase = 0.08 x 12,000 = 960. New population = 12,000 + 960 = 12,960.
Method 2: 1.08 x 12,000 = 12,960.

**Example B — Percent decrease (find the new value)**
A jacket originally costs $85. It is discounted by 40%. What is the sale price?

Method 1: Discount = 0.40 x 85 = $34. Sale price = 85 - 34 = $51.
Method 2: 0.60 x 85 = $51.

**Example C — Finding the percent change**
A student's test score improved from 72 to 90. What is the percent increase?

Amount of change = 90 - 72 = 18.
Percent change = (18 / 72) x 100 = 0.25 x 100 = 25% increase.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

1. A bicycle costs $320. The price increases by 5%. What is the new price?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Find 5% of 320 first, then add. Or use the multiplier: 1.05 x 320."

2. A sweater is originally $55. It is marked down 20%. What is the sale price?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Find 20% of $55. Subtract from $55. Or multiply $55 by 0.80."

3. A town's population went from 8,500 to 9,350. What is the percent increase?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Find the amount of change first. Then divide by the original (8,500)."

4. A stock price dropped from $45 to $36. What is the percent decrease?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Amount of change = 45 - 36 = 9. Divide 9 by the original value (45)."

5. A store marks up the wholesale cost of a phone by 60%. The wholesale cost is $250. What is the retail price?
   - *Facilitator prompt:* "Markup means increase. Find 60% of $250 and add, or multiply by 1.60."

## Independent Practice (10 minutes)

### On-Level
1. A meal costs $24. A 15% tip is added. What is the tip amount? What is the total cost?
2. A pair of jeans originally costs $48. They are on sale for 25% off. What is the sale price?
3. A city's population grew from 15,000 to 16,800. What is the percent increase?
4. A computer was priced at $900 and is now $765. What is the percent decrease?

### Stretch
5. A store buys T-shirts for $8.50 each and marks them up by 120%. What is the selling price?
6. The price of a concert ticket increased from $45 to $54. Then it decreased from $54 to $48.60. Find the percent increase and the percent decrease. Are they the same?
7. A house was valued at $250,000. Its value increased by 6% one year, then decreased by 4% the next year. What is the value after both changes? Is the final value more or less than the original?

### Challenge
8. After a 30% discount, a jacket costs $56. What was the original price? (Hint: if the discount is 30%, you are paying 70% of the original.)
9. A store marks up an item by 50%, then offers a 50% discount on the marked-up price. Is the final price the same as the original? Prove your answer with an example.

### Interleaved Review
*(Reviewing: finding percent of a number from Lesson 8)*
10. Find 72% of 350.
11. Find 6.5% of 200.

## Check for Understanding (5 minutes)

### Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A book originally costs $18.00. It is discounted by 35%. What is the sale price?

**Problem 2:** A student's grade improved from 68 to 85. What is the percent increase? Round to the nearest tenth of a percent.

**Scoring:**
- 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated.
- 1 out of 2 correct = Partial understanding; review errors.
- 0 out of 2 correct = Reteach needed.

### If Mastery Is Not Met
- Separate the two skills: (1) finding the amount of change and (2) computing percent change. Practice each in isolation before combining.
- For students who forget to add or subtract the change from the original, use the two-step method explicitly every time. The one-step multiplier shortcut can be introduced later once the concept is solid.
- Provide a "percent change checklist": (1) Identify the original value. (2) Find the amount of change. (3) Divide by the original. (4) Multiply by 100. (5) Label as increase or decrease.

### If Mastery Is Met
- Assign the Challenge problems if not yet completed.
- Preview: "In the next lesson, we combine these skills for multi-step percent problems — like calculating tax AND tip on a restaurant bill."

## Extension (Optional)
- **Shopping Spree:** Give the student a "catalog" of 5 items with prices and various discounts (10% off, 25% off, buy one get one 50% off). Have them calculate the sale price for each item and the total savings.
- **Percent Error Connection:** Introduce percent error as a special case of percent change: percent error = (|estimated - actual| / actual) x 100. Have the student estimate the length of a table in centimeters, then measure, and compute the percent error.

## Teacher/Facilitator Notes

### Common Errors
- **Dividing by the new value instead of the original.** When finding percent change, students sometimes divide the change by the new value rather than the original. Emphasize: "You always compare the change to where you STARTED."
- **Forgetting to add/subtract after finding the percent.** A student finds "20% of 85 = 17" and writes 17 as the sale price, forgetting to subtract from 85. Remind them: the question asks for the new price, not the discount amount.
- **Thinking percent increase and decrease are symmetric.** A 50% increase followed by a 50% decrease does NOT return to the original value (Challenge Problem 9 illustrates this). This is a sophisticated idea — introduce it gently.

### Pacing Advice
- Direct Instruction has four steps. If the student understands the two-step method quickly, introduce the multiplier shortcut (Methods 2 in Steps 3-4) — it is more efficient and connects to the idea of proportional scaling.
- Guided Practice Problem 3 (finding percent change from two values) is often harder than the other direction. Allocate extra time here.
- If the student needs two sessions, split: Session 1 covers finding new values (increase and decrease), Session 2 covers finding the percent change given two values.
