# Facilitator Guide — Grade 7 Math, Unit 1: Proportional Relationships

**Grade:** 7
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 1 — Proportional Relationships
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided (facilitator leads instruction; student works independently for practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit launches Grade 7 mathematics by deepening the ratio and rate reasoning students developed in Grade 6. Students move beyond computing equivalent ratios to recognizing, representing, and analyzing proportional relationships — first through tables and equations, then through graphs. The second half of the unit applies proportional reasoning to percent problems, including percent of a number, percent increase and decrease, and multi-step percent contexts such as tax, tip, and simple interest.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Compute unit rates, including those involving fractions
- Determine whether a relationship is proportional using tables, graphs, and equations
- Identify and interpret the constant of proportionality (k) in tables, equations, graphs, and word problems
- Write and use equations in the form y = kx to represent proportional relationships
- Graph proportional relationships and recognize that the graph is a straight line through the origin
- Convert fluently among fractions, decimals, and percents
- Find the percent of a number using multiplication and proportional reasoning
- Calculate percent increase and percent decrease
- Solve multi-step percent problems involving tax, tip, discount, and simple interest

**Why this matters:** Proportional reasoning is the single most important bridge between arithmetic and algebra. Students who understand proportionality deeply will navigate linear functions, slope, and scaling with confidence. Percent fluency is essential for everyday financial literacy — every time someone calculates a tip, compares a sale price, or reads a statistic, they are using proportional reasoning.

## Prerequisite Check

Before beginning this unit, confirm:

- [ ] Can write a ratio in multiple forms (a:b, a/b, "a to b")
- [ ] Can find equivalent ratios by multiplying or dividing both terms
- [ ] Can compute a unit rate (e.g., $3.60 for 4 pounds means $0.90 per pound)
- [ ] Can use ratio tables to organize and extend ratio relationships
- [ ] Understands percent as "per 100" and can convert simple percents to fractions and decimals (e.g., 50% = 1/2 = 0.5)
- [ ] Is fluent with multiplication and division of decimals and fractions

| 8 | Percent of a Number | 1 |
| 9 | Percent Increase and Decrease | 1-2 |
| 10 | Multi-Step Percent Problems | 1-2 |
| — | Unit Assessment | Separate session |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 1 is review. If the student demonstrates quick mastery, condense to 30 minutes and begin Lesson 2 in the same session.
- Lessons 3-5 (constant of proportionality, equations, and graphing) form the conceptual core. Allow extra time here — rushing these lessons undermines everything that follows.
- Lessons 7-8 (percent review and percent of a number) can sometimes be combined into one session if the student's Grade 6 percent understanding is strong.
- Lesson 10 (multi-step percent problems) is the most challenging. Budget extra time and consider splitting it across two sessions.

## Common Misconceptions

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student confuses a ratio with a fraction (e.g., says 3:5 means "3 out of 5 total") | Conflates part-to-part ratio with part-to-whole fraction | Clarify: "3:5 means 3 compared to 5 — it might be part-to-part. 3 out of 8 total would be the fraction." Use concrete examples (3 boys to 5 girls = 3:5, but boys are 3/8 of the class). |
| Student adds a constant instead of multiplying (e.g., "y is always 3 more than x" treated as proportional) | Confuses additive relationships with multiplicative (proportional) relationships | Show a table where y = x + 3 and a table where y = 3x. Compute y/x for each row — the proportional table gives the same quotient every time; the additive table does not. |
| Student writes y = kx but reverses k (e.g., uses x/y instead of y/x) | Unclear on which quantity is the "per unit" quantity | Anchor to the unit rate: "We want y per 1 unit of x. So k = y divided by x." Use a concrete example: "$15 for 3 hours means $5 per hour, so k = 15/3 = 5." |
| Proportional graph drawn correctly but does not pass through the origin | Student plots points correctly but starts the line at the first data point | Ask: "If you buy 0 pounds of apples, how much do you pay?" Zero input produces zero output in a proportional relationship — the graph must go through (0, 0). |
| Percent problems: student computes the percent but forgets to add to or subtract from the original | Confuses "percent of" with "percent increase/decrease" | Emphasize the two-step structure: (1) find the amount of change, (2) add to or subtract from the original. Or teach the multiplier shortcut: increase of 20% means multiply by 1.20. |
| Student moves the decimal the wrong direction when converting between percents and decimals | Procedural confusion about the relationship between percent and decimal forms | Return to the definition: percent means "per 100." So 25% = 25/100 = 0.25. Dividing by 100 moves the decimal two places left. |

## Conversation Starters

- "If you earn $12 per hour, how would you figure out your pay for any number of hours? What stays the same no matter how many hours you work?"
- "A store sells apples at $2.50 per pound. Another store charges $7.00 for 3 pounds. Which is the better deal, and how do you know?"
- "Why does a proportional graph always pass through the origin? What would it mean in real life if it didn't?"
- "If a shirt is 30% off and the discount is $12, how could you figure out the original price?"
- "Is the relationship between a person's age and their height proportional? Why or why not?"

---

## When to Seek Additional Help

Consider seeking additional support if:

- The student cannot reliably find a unit rate from a simple ratio (Grade 6 prerequisite)
- Equivalent ratios are not understood after Lesson 1 reteaching — the rest of the unit depends on this skill
- The student consistently confuses additive and multiplicative relationships after explicit instruction in Lessons 2-3
- Decimal and fraction computation errors are so frequent that they mask understanding of proportional reasoning
- The student cannot convert between fractions, decimals, and percents after Lesson 7
