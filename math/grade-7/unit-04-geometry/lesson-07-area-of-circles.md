# Math -- Grade 7 -- Unit 4, Lesson 7

## Topic
Area of Circles

## Learning Objectives
- Derive the formula A = pi * r^2 through a hands-on decomposition activity.
- Apply the formula A = pi * r^2 to calculate the area of a circle given its radius or diameter.
- Solve equations to find the radius or diameter when the area is given.
- Explain in their own words why the area formula involves r-squared rather than r.

## Prerequisites
- Lesson 6: Circumference of Circles (C = pi * d = 2 * pi * r).
- Understanding of area as the number of square units covering a surface (Grade 6).
- Ability to square a number (compute r^2).
- Ability to multiply decimals.

## Materials Needed
- Compass (for drawing circles).
- Scissors.
- Ruler.
- Colored paper or plain paper.
- Glue or tape (optional, for attaching rearranged sectors).
- Calculator (optional, for checking calculations).
- Student worksheet.
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 7.G.4 -- Know the formulas for the area and circumference of a circle and use them to solve problems; give an informal derivation of the relationship between the circumference and area of a circle.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review circumference formulas and squaring numbers.

1. Find the circumference of a circle with radius 8 cm. Use pi = 3.14. (C = 2 * 3.14 * 8 = 50.24 cm)

2. Compute: 5^2 = ? (25). 9^2 = ? (81). 3.5^2 = ? (12.25).

3. A rectangle has a length of 10 cm and a width of 3.14 cm. What is its area? (10 * 3.14 = 31.4 sq cm)

Say: "Today we are going to discover the formula for the area of a circle. We will use cutting and rearranging to connect a circle to a shape we already know -- a parallelogram."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Area** -- the amount of space inside a two-dimensional shape, measured in square units.
- **Sector** -- a "pie slice" of a circle, bounded by two radii and an arc.
- **A = pi * r^2** -- the formula for the area of a circle, where r is the radius.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 -- Hands-on derivation: circle to parallelogram.**

Say: "Draw a circle on your paper using a compass. Make the radius about 8 cm so there is plenty of room to work."

Say: "Now divide the circle into equal sectors -- like cutting a pizza into slices. Try to make at least 8 slices (more is better). Cut out the circle and then cut along the lines to separate the sectors."

Say: "Now rearrange the sectors by alternating them point-up, point-down, fitting them together like teeth of a zipper."

(Demonstrate: place one sector with the point up, the next with the point down, and so on. The result approximates a parallelogram.)

Say: "What does this shape look like?" (A parallelogram, or roughly a rectangle.)

Say: "Now let us think about the dimensions of this parallelogram."
- The height of the parallelogram is the radius of the circle (r).
- The base of the parallelogram is half the circumference of the circle, because the top and bottom edges together make up the full circumference. Half the circumference = (1/2) * 2 * pi * r = pi * r.

Say: "The area of a parallelogram is base times height. So the area is:"

Write: A = base * height = (pi * r) * r = **pi * r^2**

Say: "The more slices you make, the more the shape looks like a perfect rectangle with base pi * r and height r. The area is always pi * r-squared."

**Step 2 -- Using the formula.**

Write the formula on the board: **A = pi * r^2**

Say: "To use this formula: (1) identify the radius, (2) square it, (3) multiply by pi (3.14)."

Say: "IMPORTANT: the formula uses the radius, not the diameter. If you are given the diameter, your first step is to divide by 2 to get the radius."

### Worked Examples

**Example A: Given Radius**

Find the area of a circle with a radius of 5 cm. Use pi = 3.14.

Step 1: r = 5 cm.
Step 2: r^2 = 5^2 = 25.
Step 3: A = 3.14 * 25 = **78.5 square cm**.

**Example B: Given Diameter**

Find the area of a circle with a diameter of 12 m. Use pi = 3.14.

Step 1: d = 12 m, so r = 12 / 2 = 6 m.
Step 2: r^2 = 6^2 = 36.
Step 3: A = 3.14 * 36 = **113.04 square meters**.

**Example C: Finding Radius from Area**

A circle has an area of 153.86 square cm. What is its radius? Use pi = 3.14.

Step 1: A = pi * r^2. So 153.86 = 3.14 * r^2.
Step 2: r^2 = 153.86 / 3.14 = 49.
Step 3: r = sqrt(49) = **7 cm**.

---

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity 1: Computing Area**

1. Find the area of a circle with a radius of 10 cm. Use pi = 3.14.
   - r^2 = 100. A = 3.14 * 100 = **314 sq cm**.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Always square the radius first, then multiply by pi."

2. Find the area of a circle with a diameter of 8 m. Use pi = 3.14.
   - r = 4 m. r^2 = 16. A = 3.14 * 16 = **50.24 sq m**.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "What is the first step when you are given the diameter?"

3. A circular rug has a radius of 3 feet. How many square feet of floor does it cover?
   - r^2 = 9. A = 3.14 * 9 = **28.26 square feet**.

**Activity 2: Working Backwards**

4. A circle has an area of 200.96 sq cm. Find the radius. Use pi = 3.14.
   - r^2 = 200.96 / 3.14 = 64. r = sqrt(64) = **8 cm**.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Divide the area by pi to find r-squared, then take the square root."

5. A circular garden has an area of 78.5 sq m. What is the diameter?
   - r^2 = 78.5 / 3.14 = 25. r = 5 m. d = 2 * 5 = **10 m**.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. Find the area of a circle with a radius of 7 cm. Use pi = 3.14.

2. Find the area of a circle with a diameter of 18 m. Use pi = 3.14.

3. A circular table has a diameter of 4 feet. What is the area of the table top?

4. A circle has an area of 314 sq cm. What is the radius?

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

5. A sprinkler waters a circular area with a radius of 12 m. How many square meters does it water?

6. A pizza with a diameter of 16 inches is cut into 8 equal slices. What is the area of one slice?

7. Circle A has a radius of 3 cm. Circle B has a radius of 6 cm. How many times larger is the area of Circle B compared to Circle A?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

8. A circular pool has a circumference of 62.8 m. What is its area? (Hint: find the radius from the circumference first.)

9. If the radius of a circle is doubled, what happens to the area? Explain using the formula.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** Find the area of a circle with a diameter of 14 cm. Use pi = 3.14.

**Problem 2:** A circle has an area of 50.24 sq m. What is the radius? Use pi = 3.14.

**Mastery:** 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated. 1 out of 2 = Review formula and radius vs. diameter. 0 out of 2 = Reteach with decomposition activity.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. r^2 = 49. A = 3.14 * 49 = **153.86 sq cm**.
2. r = 9 m. r^2 = 81. A = 3.14 * 81 = **254.34 sq m**.
3. r = 2 ft. r^2 = 4. A = 3.14 * 4 = **12.56 sq ft**.
4. 314 = 3.14 * r^2. r^2 = 314 / 3.14 = 100. r = **10 cm**.
5. r^2 = 144. A = 3.14 * 144 = **452.16 sq m**.
6. Total area: r = 8 in. A = 3.14 * 64 = 200.96 sq in. One slice = 200.96 / 8 = **25.12 sq in**.
7. Area A = 3.14 * 9 = 28.26 sq cm. Area B = 3.14 * 36 = 113.04 sq cm. Ratio = 113.04 / 28.26 = **4 times larger**. (Note: when the radius doubles, the area quadruples because 6^2 / 3^2 = 36/9 = 4.)
8. C = 2 * pi * r. 62.8 = 2 * 3.14 * r. 62.8 = 6.28 * r. r = 10 m. A = 3.14 * 100 = **314 sq m**.
9. Original area: A = pi * r^2. New radius = 2r. New area = pi * (2r)^2 = pi * 4r^2 = 4 * pi * r^2 = **4 times the original area**. When the radius doubles, the area is multiplied by 4 (because 2^2 = 4).

### Exit Ticket

1. r = 7 cm. r^2 = 49. A = 3.14 * 49 = **153.86 sq cm**.
2. 50.24 = 3.14 * r^2. r^2 = 50.24 / 3.14 = 16. r = **4 m**.

## Teacher Notes

- The paper-cutting decomposition activity is the most important part of this lesson. It provides an informal derivation of A = pi * r^2, which the standard explicitly requires. Do not skip it even if the student already "knows" the formula.
- The more sectors the student cuts, the closer the rearranged shape looks to a rectangle. With 8 sectors it looks like a bumpy parallelogram; with 16 sectors it looks much smoother. Discuss: "What would happen if we cut 100 slices? 1,000 slices?"
- The most common error is using the diameter instead of the radius in A = pi * r^2. If the student computes A = pi * d^2, the answer will be 4 times too large. Require labeling "r = ___" before substituting.
- Problem 7 in Tier 2 illustrates the key idea that area scales with the square of the linear dimension. When the radius doubles, the area quadruples. This concept is essential for understanding scale factors and area in later math courses.
- Problem 9 in Tier 3 asks the student to reason algebraically about doubling the radius. Encourage them to use the formula symbolically rather than plugging in specific numbers.
- Connection to Lesson 8: the next application lesson will combine circumference and area in problems involving shaded regions and composite figures. Both formulas must be solid before proceeding.
