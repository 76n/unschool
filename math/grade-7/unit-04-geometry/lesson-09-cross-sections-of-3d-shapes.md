# Math -- Grade 7 -- Unit 4, Lesson 9

## Topic
Cross-Sections of 3D Shapes

## Learning Objectives
- Define a cross-section as the 2D shape formed when a plane slices through a 3D figure.
- Identify the cross-section that results from slicing a right rectangular prism, right rectangular pyramid, and right circular cylinder with horizontal, vertical, and angled planes.
- Describe cross-sections using correct geometric vocabulary (rectangle, triangle, circle, trapezoid, etc.).
- Predict cross-section shapes before slicing and verify using physical models.

## Prerequisites
- Ability to identify 3D shapes: prisms, pyramids, cylinders, cones (Grade 6).
- Ability to identify 2D shapes: circles, rectangles, squares, triangles, trapezoids (Grade 6).
- Spatial visualization skills developed throughout this unit.

## Materials Needed
- Clay, play dough, or modeling foam (for building and slicing 3D models).
- Plastic knife, dental floss, or thin wire (for slicing models safely).
- Ruler.
- Student worksheet with diagrams of 3D shapes and cross-sections.
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.

**Standards:** 7.G.3 -- Describe the two-dimensional figures that result from slicing three-dimensional figures, as in plane sections of right rectangular prisms and right rectangular pyramids.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Review 3D shape vocabulary and activate spatial thinking.

1. Name three examples of 3D shapes you see in everyday life. (Examples: cereal box = rectangular prism, soda can = cylinder, ice cream cone = cone.)

2. If you cut a loaf of bread straight across, what shape is the cut face? (A rectangle -- the cross-section of a rectangular prism.)

3. If you cut an orange exactly in half, what shape is the cut face? (A circle.)

Say: "Today we are going to explore what happens when we slice three-dimensional shapes with flat cuts. The shape of the cut face is called a cross-section."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Cross-section** -- the two-dimensional shape that results from the intersection of a plane and a three-dimensional figure. Think of it as the shape you see when you slice through an object and look at the cut face.
- **Plane** -- a flat surface that extends infinitely in all directions. When we "slice" a 3D shape, we imagine cutting it with a plane.
- **Right rectangular prism** -- a box shape where all faces are rectangles and the edges meet at right angles.
- **Right rectangular pyramid** -- a pyramid with a rectangular base and an apex directly above the center of the base.
- **Right circular cylinder** -- a tube shape with circular bases that are directly above and below each other.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 -- Introduce cross-sections with a rectangular prism.**

Say: "Imagine a block of cheese shaped like a rectangular prism. If I cut straight down through it from top to bottom, what shape is the cut face?"

Build (or hold up) a rectangular prism from clay. Slice it vertically. Hold up the cut face.

Say: "The cross-section is a rectangle. But what if I slice it a different way?"

Demonstrate:
- **Horizontal slice** (parallel to the base): the cross-section is a **rectangle** (same shape as the base).
- **Vertical slice** (parallel to a face): the cross-section is a **rectangle**.
- **Vertical slice** (perpendicular to a long face, through the middle): also a **rectangle**, but with different dimensions.
- **Diagonal slice** (angled, corner to corner): the cross-section can be a **parallelogram**, a **triangle** (if through an edge and opposite vertex of a face), or other shapes depending on the angle.

Say: "For a rectangular prism, most cross-sections are rectangles. But angled cuts can create other shapes."

**Step 2 -- Cross-sections of a pyramid.**

Build a rectangular pyramid from clay. Demonstrate:
- **Horizontal slice** (parallel to the base): the cross-section is a **rectangle** (smaller than the base, because the pyramid narrows toward the top).
- **Vertical slice through the apex**: the cross-section is a **triangle**.
- **Vertical slice NOT through the apex**: the cross-section is a **trapezoid**.

Say: "Notice that horizontal slices of a pyramid always give a shape similar to the base, but smaller. The higher up you slice, the smaller the cross-section."

**Step 3 -- Cross-sections of a cylinder.**

Build a cylinder from clay. Demonstrate:
- **Horizontal slice** (parallel to the base): the cross-section is a **circle**.
- **Vertical slice** (perpendicular to the base, through the center): the cross-section is a **rectangle**.
- **Angled slice** (not parallel to the base and not perpendicular): the cross-section is an **ellipse** (oval).

Say: "A cylinder gives us circles, rectangles, and ellipses depending on how we cut it."

### Worked Examples

**Example A: Horizontal Slice of a Rectangular Prism**

A box (rectangular prism) is 10 cm long, 6 cm wide, and 8 cm tall. You make a horizontal cut 3 cm from the bottom.

What is the shape of the cross-section? **Rectangle**
What are its dimensions? **10 cm by 6 cm** (same as the base).

**Example B: Vertical Slice Through a Pyramid**

A square pyramid has a square base with side 8 cm. You make a vertical cut through the apex and the midpoint of one base edge.

What is the shape of the cross-section? **Triangle** (specifically, an isosceles triangle).
The base of the triangle = 8 cm (the full base edge). The height of the triangle = the height of the pyramid.

**Example C: Horizontal Slice of a Cylinder**

A cylinder has a radius of 5 cm and a height of 12 cm. You make a horizontal cut 4 cm from the top.

What is the shape of the cross-section? **Circle**
What is its radius? **5 cm** (the same as the cylinder's radius -- horizontal slices of a cylinder are all identical circles).

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity 1: Predict and Verify**

For each description, predict the cross-section shape. If clay models are available, build and slice to verify.

1. A rectangular prism (box) is sliced horizontally, parallel to the base.
   - Prediction: **Rectangle** (same shape and size as the base).
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Think about what you see when you cut a block of cheese straight across."

2. A square pyramid is sliced horizontally, halfway up.
   - Prediction: **Square** (smaller than the base).
   - **Facilitator tip:** "The pyramid gets narrower as you go up. A horizontal slice at any height gives a shape similar to the base."

3. A cylinder is sliced vertically, perpendicular to the base, through the center.
   - Prediction: **Rectangle** (width = diameter, height = cylinder height).
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Imagine slicing a can of soup in half vertically. You would see a rectangular cross-section."

**Activity 2: Matching**

Match each slice description to the resulting cross-section:

4. Vertical slice through the apex of a triangular pyramid. --> **Triangle**
5. Horizontal slice of a cylinder. --> **Circle**
6. Angled slice of a rectangular prism (not parallel to any face). --> **Parallelogram** (or other quadrilateral depending on the angle)

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. A rectangular prism is 12 cm long, 5 cm wide, and 8 cm tall. Describe the cross-section if it is sliced horizontally (parallel to the base). What are the dimensions of the cross-section?

2. A cylinder has a diameter of 10 cm and a height of 15 cm. Describe the cross-section if it is sliced vertically through the center.

3. A square pyramid with a base side of 6 cm is sliced horizontally 3 cm above the base. Is the cross-section bigger, smaller, or the same size as the base? What shape is it?

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

4. A cone (like an ice cream cone) has a circular base. Describe the cross-section for each type of slice: (a) horizontal, (b) vertical through the apex, (c) vertical NOT through the apex.

5. A triangular prism (like a tent shape) is sliced horizontally. What shape is the cross-section? What if it is sliced vertically, perpendicular to the triangular faces?

6. Can you get a circular cross-section from a rectangular prism? Explain why or why not.

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

7. A cylinder is sliced at an angle (not parallel and not perpendicular to the base). What shape is the cross-section? Why is it not a circle?

8. A rectangular prism is sliced diagonally from one edge of the top face to the opposite edge of the bottom face. Describe the cross-section shape.

---

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A right rectangular prism is sliced horizontally. What is the shape of the cross-section?

**Problem 2:** A right rectangular pyramid is sliced vertically through the apex. What is the shape of the cross-section?

**Problem 3:** Name one way to slice a cylinder to get a circular cross-section and one way to get a rectangular cross-section.

**Mastery:** 3 out of 3 correct = Mastery demonstrated. 2 out of 3 = Review the missed shape. 1 or 0 out of 3 = Reteach using clay models.

---

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. Cross-section: **rectangle**, 12 cm by 5 cm (same dimensions as the base).
2. Cross-section: **rectangle**, 10 cm wide (the diameter) and 15 cm tall (the height).
3. The cross-section is a **square**, smaller than the base. Since the pyramid narrows toward the apex, a horizontal slice above the base produces a smaller version of the base shape.
4. (a) Horizontal slice of a cone: **circle** (smaller than the base; the higher the slice, the smaller the circle). (b) Vertical slice through the apex: **triangle** (isosceles). (c) Vertical slice NOT through the apex: **a shape with a curved edge** -- specifically, a hyperbola if the slice is parallel to the axis but does not pass through the apex. At this level, accept "a shape like a narrow oval on one side."
5. Horizontal slice of a triangular prism: **triangle** (same shape as the triangular face). Vertical slice perpendicular to the triangular faces: **rectangle** (width = length of the prism, height = height of the triangle at the point where the slice intersects).
6. **No**, you cannot get a circular cross-section from a rectangular prism. All cross-sections of a rectangular prism have straight edges (they are polygons -- rectangles, triangles, trapezoids, etc.). A circle has a curved edge, which cannot result from slicing a shape with only flat faces and straight edges.
7. The cross-section is an **ellipse** (oval). It is not a circle because the angled cut stretches the circular shape in one direction, making it longer in that direction while keeping the perpendicular dimension the same.
8. The cross-section is a **rectangle** (if the slice is through opposite parallel edges) or a **parallelogram** (if the angle crosses non-parallel edges). The exact shape depends on which edges the diagonal slice connects.

### Exit Ticket

1. **Rectangle** (same shape as the base).
2. **Triangle**.
3. Circular cross-section: slice **horizontally** (parallel to the base). Rectangular cross-section: slice **vertically** through the center (perpendicular to the base).

## Teacher Notes

- This lesson is highly visual and spatial. Physical models make a dramatic difference in student understanding. If clay or play dough is available, use it. If not, use diagrams and describe the slicing process carefully.
- Students often struggle to visualize what an angled slice looks like. Start with the simplest cases (horizontal and vertical slices) and only move to angled slices for students who are ready.
- The cone cross-sections (Problem 4) go slightly beyond the standard (7.G.3 focuses on prisms and pyramids), but they are excellent for building spatial intuition. The vertical slice through the apex of a cone gives a triangle, which connects to the pyramid cross-sections.
- Problem 6 is an important conceptual question. Since a rectangular prism has only flat faces with straight edges, every possible cross-section must have straight edges (it will be a polygon). A circle cannot result from slicing a shape with flat sides. This reasoning is more important than the answer itself.
- If the student cannot visualize cross-sections even with models, try using online tools or apps that allow virtual slicing of 3D shapes. Many free geometry visualization tools are available.
- Connection to real life: MRI and CT scans create cross-sectional images of the human body. Geologists study cross-sections of the Earth. Architects draw cross-sections of buildings. These real-world connections make the topic more engaging.
