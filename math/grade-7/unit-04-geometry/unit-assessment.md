# Unit Assessment -- Grade 7 Math, Unit 4: Geometry

**Grade:** 7
**Unit:** 4 -- Geometry
**Assessment Type:** Summative (unit-level)
**Administration:** Written; facilitator reads word problems aloud if needed
**Estimated Duration:** 45-55 minutes
**Materials:** Paper, pencil, protractor, ruler. Calculator not permitted (all calculations use pi = 3.14 with manageable numbers).

## Section 1: Scale Drawings (10 points)

**1.1.** A scale drawing uses the scale 1 cm = 6 m. A building is drawn as 9 cm tall. What is the actual height of the building? (2 points)

**1.2.** A rectangular park is 120 m long and 80 m wide. An architect makes a scale drawing using the scale 1 cm = 10 m. What are the dimensions of the park on the drawing? (2 points)

**1.3.** A model airplane is 25 cm long. The actual airplane is 50 m long. What is the scale factor? Express the scale as "1 cm = ___ m." (3 points)

**1.4.** A floor plan is drawn at the scale 1 cm = 4 m. A room is 7 cm by 5 cm on the floor plan. The architect needs to redraw the room at a new scale of 1 cm = 8 m. What will the new drawing dimensions be? (3 points)

---

## Section 2: Angle Relationships (14 points)

**2.1.** Two complementary angles measure 34 degrees and x degrees. Find x. (1 point)

**2.2.** Two supplementary angles measure (3x + 10) degrees and (2x + 20) degrees. Find x and both angle measures. (3 points)

**2.3.** Two lines intersect. One of the four angles measures 55 degrees. Find the measures of the other three angles. (2 points)

**2.4.** Two vertical angles measure (4x - 8) degrees and (2x + 16) degrees. Find x and the measure of each vertical angle. (3 points)

**2.5.** A triangle has angles of (2x + 5) degrees, (x + 15) degrees, and 60 degrees. Find x and all three angle measures. Is this triangle acute, right, or obtuse? (5 points)

---

## Section 3: Circles -- Circumference and Area (16 points)

Use pi = 3.14 for all calculations.

**3.1.** Find the circumference of a circle with a diameter of 15 cm. (2 points)

**3.2.** Find the area of a circle with a radius of 9 m. (2 points)

**3.3.** A circular garden has a diameter of 20 m. How much fencing is needed to go around it? What is the area of the garden? (4 points)

**3.4.** A circle has an area of 113.04 square cm. What is the radius? What is the circumference? (4 points)

**3.5.** A rectangular piece of cardboard is 18 cm by 14 cm. A circle with a diameter of 14 cm is cut from the center of the cardboard. What is the area of the remaining cardboard? (4 points)

---

## Section 4: Cross-Sections (8 points)

**4.1.** A right rectangular prism is 10 cm long, 6 cm wide, and 8 cm tall. Describe the shape and dimensions of the cross-section if the prism is sliced horizontally (parallel to the base). (2 points)

**4.2.** A right rectangular pyramid with a square base is sliced vertically through the apex. What is the shape of the cross-section? (2 points)

**4.3.** A right circular cylinder with a radius of 5 cm and a height of 12 cm is sliced in two different ways:
- (a) Horizontally (parallel to the base). What shape is the cross-section? What is its area? Use pi = 3.14.
- (b) Vertically through the center (perpendicular to the base). What shape is the cross-section? What are its dimensions?
(4 points)

---

## Section 5: Problem Solving (12 points)

**5.1.** A student found the area of a circle with a diameter of 8 cm as follows:

> A = pi * r^2 = 3.14 * 8^2 = 3.14 * 64 = 200.96 sq cm

Identify the error, explain what went wrong, and give the correct answer. (3 points)

**5.2.** A scale drawing of a circular swimming pool uses the scale 1 cm = 3 m. On the drawing, the pool has a diameter of 6 cm.
- (a) What is the actual diameter of the pool?
- (b) What is the actual circumference of the pool?
- (c) What is the actual area of the pool?
Use pi = 3.14. (4 points)

**5.3.** In a figure, two straight lines intersect, forming four angles. One of the angles contains a triangle whose vertices are at the intersection point and two points on the lines. The triangle has one angle at the intersection measuring 40 degrees and another angle of 75 degrees.
- (a) What is the third angle of the triangle?
- (b) What is the measure of the vertical angle to the 40-degree angle at the intersection?
- (c) What is the measure of each angle adjacent to the 40-degree angle at the intersection?
(5 points)

### Section 5: Problem Solving

**5.1.** The student used the **diameter (8 cm) instead of the radius (4 cm)** in the formula. The area formula uses the radius, not the diameter.

The student's answer (200.96) is exactly 4 times the correct answer because (d)^2 = (2r)^2 = 4r^2.

| Level | Score Range | Meaning | Next Steps |

### Section-Specific Reteaching Guide

| If struggling in... | Focus on... |
|---|---|
| Section 1 (Scale Drawings) | Return to Lessons 1-2. Practice with simple scales on grid paper. Reinforce the two-step process for changing scales: convert to actual first, then convert to the new scale. Use proportions from Unit 1 if helpful. |
| Section 2 (Angle Relationships) | Return to Lessons 3-5. Start with definitions: complementary = 90, supplementary = 180. Use the memory tricks (C = Corner, S = Straight). For vertical angles, draw intersecting lines and measure with a protractor. For triangle angle sum, redo the paper-tearing activity. |
| Section 3 (Circles) | Return to Lessons 6-7. Redo the measurement activity for circumference and the paper-cutting activity for area. Provide a formula card. Require students to write "r = ___" before substituting. Practice distinguishing C = pi * d from A = pi * r^2. For shaded regions, practice the subtraction pattern: big shape minus small shape. |
| Section 4 (Cross-Sections) | Return to Lesson 9 with clay or play dough models. Start with horizontal slices (the simplest case) and build to vertical slices. Use the following sequence: rectangular prism first, then pyramid, then cylinder. |
| Section 5 (Problem Solving) | Identify which underlying skills are weak and reteach those specific lessons. Problem-solving fluency improves as the component skills become automatic. Practice multi-step problems with explicit step-by-step guidance before expecting independent problem-solving. |

## Error Analysis Guide

| Error Pattern | Example | Likely Cause | Intervention |
|--------------|---------|-------------|-------------|
| Multiplies when should divide for scale (or vice versa) | Drawing to actual: 5 cm / 3 = 1.67 m instead of 5 * 3 = 15 m | Confused about scale direction | Ask: "Should the actual measurement be bigger or smaller than the drawing?" This reality check catches the error. |
| Mixes up complementary and supplementary | Says complementary = 180 | Confuses the definitions | Use mnemonics: C for Corner (90), S for Straight (180). Repeat until automatic. |
| Forgets that vertical angles are equal | Computes vertical angles as supplementary | Confuses vertical with linear pair | Draw the X diagram. Label: "Across = equal (vertical). Side by side = sum to 180 (linear pair)." |
| Uses diameter in A = pi * r^2 | Computes A = 3.14 * d^2 | Does not distinguish r from d | Require writing "r = ___" as the first step. If given diameter, always compute r = d/2 first. |
| Confuses C and A formulas | Uses A = pi * r^2 when asked for circumference | Mixes up area and perimeter formulas | Write both formulas side by side. Circumference = distance AROUND (linear, uses 2r or d). Area = space INSIDE (squared, uses r^2). |
| Cannot identify cross-section shape | Guesses randomly | Weak spatial visualization | Use physical models. Start with the simplest case (horizontal slice) and build complexity gradually. |
| Does not convert scale before computing circle dimensions | Computes circumference using drawing dimensions | Skips scale conversion step | Require labeling: "Drawing dimension = ___. Actual dimension = ___." Always convert first. |

## Facilitator Observations

- Can the student fluidly move between drawing measurements and actual measurements using a scale?
- Does the student reliably distinguish complementary (90) from supplementary (180)?
- Does the student remember that vertical angles are equal and apply this without prompting?
- Can the student find missing angles in a triangle using the angle-sum property?
- Does the student consistently use the correct formula for circumference vs. area?
- Does the student remember to use the radius (not the diameter) in the area formula?
- Can the student visualize cross-sections, or does this require physical models?
- On multi-step problems, does the student plan their approach, or do they jump in without a strategy?
