# Facilitator Guide -- Grade 7 Math, Unit 4: Geometry

**Grade:** 7
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 4 -- Geometry
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided independence (facilitator leads instruction; student works independently for practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit introduces students to the major geometry concepts of Grade 7: scale drawings, angle relationships, properties of triangles, circles (circumference and area), and cross-sections of three-dimensional shapes. Students begin by interpreting and constructing scale drawings, then move to angle relationships -- complementary, supplementary, vertical, and adjacent angles. They prove that the interior angles of a triangle sum to 180 degrees and use this fact to find missing angles. The unit then explores circles, developing the formulas for circumference (C = pi * d = 2 * pi * r) and area (A = pi * r^2) through hands-on measurement and reasoning. Students apply these formulas to solve problems involving shaded regions and composite figures. The unit closes with cross-sections of three-dimensional shapes and a comprehensive mixed review.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Read and interpret scale drawings, determining actual lengths from a given scale
- Compute scale factors and reproduce figures at a different scale
- Identify and use complementary angle pairs (sum to 90 degrees) and supplementary angle pairs (sum to 180 degrees) to find unknown angles
- Apply properties of vertical angles (congruent) and adjacent angles on a line (sum to 180 degrees)
- Use the triangle angle-sum property (interior angles sum to 180 degrees) to find missing angles
- Calculate the circumference of a circle using C = pi * d or C = 2 * pi * r
- Calculate the area of a circle using A = pi * r^2
- Solve multi-step problems involving area and circumference, including shaded regions
- Identify the two-dimensional cross-section that results from slicing a three-dimensional figure with a plane
- Solve multi-step geometry problems that combine skills from across the unit

**Why this matters:** Geometry connects mathematics to the physical world. Scale drawings appear in architecture, maps, and engineering. Understanding angles is essential for construction, navigation, and design. Circles appear everywhere in nature and technology -- from wheels and clocks to planetary orbits. The ability to visualize how a three-dimensional object looks when sliced prepares students for science (anatomy, geology) and engineering (manufacturing, 3D printing). This unit also lays the groundwork for Grade 8 transformations, congruence, similarity, and the Pythagorean Theorem.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Scale Drawings | 1 |
| 2 | Scale Factor and Actual Dimensions | 1-2 |
| 3 | Angle Relationships: Complementary and Supplementary | 1 |
| 4 | Angle Relationships: Vertical and Adjacent | 1-2 |
| 5 | Triangles: Angle Sum | 1 |
| 6 | Circumference of Circles | 1-2 |
| 7 | Area of Circles | 1-2 |
| 8 | Area and Circumference Problem Solving | 1-2 |
| 9 | Cross-Sections of 3D Shapes | 1-2 |
| 10 | Geometry Mixed Review | 1-2 |
| -- | Unit Assessment | Separate session |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lessons 1 and 2 form a natural pair. If the student grasps scale drawings quickly in Lesson 1, begin Lesson 2 in the same session.
- Lessons 3 and 4 cover angle relationships. Lesson 3 (complementary and supplementary) is more straightforward; Lesson 4 (vertical and adjacent) introduces the visual reasoning of intersecting lines. Budget two sessions for Lesson 4 if the student needs practice with diagrams.
- Lesson 5 (triangle angle sum) is typically quick for students who are solid on supplementary angles. It can share a session with Lesson 4 if time permits.
- Lesson 6 (circumference) is a critical hands-on lesson. The measurement activity takes time. Do not rush this lesson -- the physical experience of measuring circles builds intuition for pi.
- Lesson 7 (area of circles) includes a paper-cutting decomposition activity. Allow extra time for the hands-on exploration. The formula A = pi * r^2 should emerge from the activity, not be memorized in isolation.
- Lesson 8 (problem solving) combines circumference and area. If the student is still developing fluency with the formulas, spend two sessions here with additional practice problems.
- Lesson 9 (cross-sections) is highly visual and tactile. If 3D model materials are available, allow a full session for exploration. If working from diagrams only, one session may suffice.
- Lesson 10 (mixed review) can be extended to two sessions: one for structured review and one for multi-step problem-solving.

## Lesson Summaries

| Lesson | Summary |
|--------|---------|
| 1 | Students read and interpret scale drawings, using a given scale (e.g., 1 cm = 5 m) to determine actual lengths. They measure dimensions on a scale drawing and calculate real-world measurements. |
| 2 | Students compute scale factors between a drawing and the actual object, and reproduce figures at a given scale on grid paper. They solve problems that require working in both directions (drawing to actual and actual to drawing). |
| 3 | Students define complementary angles (sum to 90 degrees) and supplementary angles (sum to 180 degrees). They identify complementary and supplementary pairs in diagrams and use these relationships to find missing angle measures. |
| 4 | Students explore intersecting lines to discover that vertical angles are congruent and that adjacent angles on a line sum to 180 degrees. They use these relationships to find unknown angles in multi-angle diagrams. |
| 5 | Students verify that the interior angles of any triangle sum to 180 degrees through measurement and a paper-tearing demonstration. They use this property to find missing angles in triangles, including multi-step problems. |
| 6 | Students measure the circumference and diameter of circular objects to discover the constant ratio pi (approximately 3.14). They develop and apply the formulas C = pi * d and C = 2 * pi * r. |
| 7 | Students decompose a paper circle into sectors and rearrange them into an approximate parallelogram to derive the area formula A = pi * r^2. They calculate the area of circles given the radius or diameter. |
| 8 | Students solve multi-step problems that require both circumference and area formulas, including finding the area of shaded regions (e.g., a square with a circle cut out) and solving for missing dimensions. |
| 9 | Students visualize and identify the two-dimensional shapes that result from slicing three-dimensional figures (prisms, pyramids, cylinders) with a plane. They use clay or play dough models when possible. |
| 10 | Students complete a comprehensive review covering all unit topics through mixed practice, error analysis, and multi-step problem-solving that combines scale drawings, angles, circles, and cross-sections. |

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Scale** | The ratio that compares a length in a drawing or model to the corresponding actual length. Example: 1 cm : 5 m. |
| **Scale factor** | The multiplier used to enlarge or reduce a figure. If a drawing uses a scale of 1:50, the scale factor from drawing to actual is 50. |
| **Complementary angles** | Two angles whose measures add up to 90 degrees. |
| **Supplementary angles** | Two angles whose measures add up to 180 degrees. |
| **Vertical angles** | Two non-adjacent angles formed by two intersecting lines. Vertical angles are always congruent (equal in measure). |
| **Adjacent angles** | Two angles that share a common vertex and a common side but do not overlap. |
| **Circumference** | The distance around a circle. |
| **Pi (pi)** | The ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter; approximately 3.14 or 22/7. Pi is the same for every circle. |
| **Radius** | The distance from the center of a circle to any point on the circle. |
| **Diameter** | The distance across a circle through its center; equal to twice the radius. |
| **Cross-section** | The two-dimensional shape that results from slicing a three-dimensional figure with a plane. |

## Facilitation Notes

### Building from Prior Grades

This unit draws heavily on Grade 6 geometry (area formulas, angle measurement, 3D shape identification) and Grade 7 Unit 1 (proportional relationships for scale drawings). The connection between proportional reasoning and scale drawings is the bridge from algebra to geometry in this course.

### Hands-On Learning Is Essential

Geometry is inherently visual and spatial. The methodology framework emphasizes the Concrete-Representational-Abstract (CRA) progression. In this unit:
- **Concrete:** Measuring circular objects, cutting paper circles, building and slicing clay models, drawing with rulers and protractors.
- **Representational:** Drawing diagrams, labeling angle measures, sketching scale drawings on grid paper.
- **Abstract:** Writing and solving equations using angle relationships and circle formulas.

Do not skip the hands-on activities, even if the student seems to understand the formulas. The physical experience builds spatial intuition that supports later geometry work.

### Pi as a Ratio

Students often memorize "pi equals 3.14" without understanding what pi is. Lesson 6 is designed to help students discover pi through measurement. Emphasize that pi is the ratio of circumference to diameter, and this ratio is the same for EVERY circle. The value 3.14 is an approximation; the true value of pi is irrational (it goes on forever without repeating).

### Equation-Solving Connections

Many geometry problems in this unit reduce to solving equations -- the same equations students mastered in Unit 3. For example, finding a missing angle in a supplementary pair (x + 65 = 180) or finding the radius from a given circumference (2 * 3.14 * r = 31.4). Remind the student that the algebra skills from Unit 3 are tools for solving geometry problems.

## Differentiation Strategies

**For the student who needs more support:**
- Provide a formula reference card listing all key formulas: C = pi * d, C = 2 * pi * r, A = pi * r^2, complementary = 90, supplementary = 180, triangle angle sum = 180.
- Use color-coding for angle pairs: highlight complementary pairs in one color, supplementary pairs in another.
- For circle calculations, break each problem into explicit steps: (1) Identify what is given (radius or diameter), (2) Choose the correct formula, (3) Substitute, (4) Calculate.
- For scale drawing problems, set up a proportion with labeled parts: drawing/actual = drawing/actual.
- Use grid paper for all scale drawing work so the student can count squares rather than measure.
- For cross-sections, always use physical models before moving to diagrams.

**For the student who needs more challenge:**
- Ask the student to create their own scale drawings of a room in their home, choosing an appropriate scale.
- Introduce problems involving arc length (fraction of a circumference) and sector area (fraction of a circle's area).
- Challenge the student to find the area of complex composite figures involving semicircles, quarter-circles, and rectangles.
- Explore the relationship between circumference and area: if the circumference doubles, what happens to the area?
- Introduce oblique cross-sections of 3D figures (cuts that are not parallel or perpendicular to the base).
- Ask "what if" questions: "If a circular garden has an area of 314 square feet, what length of fencing do you need?"

## Common Errors

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student multiplies by the scale instead of dividing (or vice versa) | Confuses the direction of scale conversion | Clarify: "Scale = drawing : actual. To find actual, multiply the drawing measurement by the scale factor. To find the drawing measurement, divide the actual measurement by the scale factor." Practice with a simple example both ways. |
| Student identifies complementary and supplementary backwards | Confuses 90 and 180 | Mnemonic: "C comes before S in the alphabet; 90 comes before 180. Complementary = 90, Supplementary = 180." Or: "A right angle makes a Corner (C = Complementary); a straight line is Straight (S = Supplementary)." |
| Student says vertical angles are supplementary | Confuses vertical and linear pair | Draw two intersecting lines and label all four angles. Measure to show that opposite (vertical) angles are equal, while side-by-side (linear pair) angles sum to 180. |
| Student uses diameter in the area formula instead of radius | Does not distinguish r from d in A = pi * r^2 | Always require the student to write "r = ___" before substituting. If given the diameter, the first step is always "r = d / 2." |
| Student computes pi * r * 2 as pi * (r * 2) = pi * r^2 | Confuses the circumference and area formulas | Write both formulas side by side: C = 2 * pi * r (multiply), A = pi * r^2 (square). Emphasize: circumference uses 2r (diameter), area uses r-squared. |
| Student forgets to square the radius in A = pi * r^2 | Computes A = pi * r instead of pi * r^2 | Require the student to write the formula, then substitute, then compute r^2 as a separate step before multiplying by pi. |
| Student cannot identify the cross-section shape | Lacks spatial visualization | Use physical models (clay, play dough). Slice slowly and hold up the cut face. Start with simple cases (horizontal slice of a rectangular prism = rectangle) before progressing to diagonal slices. |

## Assessment Guidance

The unit assessment is a 60-point summative assessment with five sections:

| Section | Skill | Points |
|---------|-------|--------|
| 1 | Scale Drawings | 10 |
| 2 | Angle Relationships | 14 |
| 3 | Circles (Circumference and Area) | 16 |
| 4 | Cross-Sections | 8 |
| 5 | Problem Solving | 12 |
| -- | **Total** | **60** |

**Mastery threshold:** 80% (48 of 60 points) AND at least 50% in each section.

If the student does not meet mastery:
- Identify the weakest section(s) and reteach the corresponding lessons.
- For scale drawings (Section 1): Return to Lessons 1-2 with grid paper and simple scales.
- For angle relationships (Section 2): Return to Lessons 3-5. Use protractors to measure and verify angle relationships physically.
- For circles (Section 3): Return to Lessons 6-7. Redo the hands-on measurement and decomposition activities. Provide a formula card and practice substitution.
- For cross-sections (Section 4): Return to Lesson 9 with physical models.
- For problem solving (Section 5): Identify which underlying skill is weak and reteach that skill first.
- Reassess the weak sections after targeted reteaching.

## Conversation Starters

- "If a map has a scale of 1 inch = 10 miles, and two cities are 3.5 inches apart on the map, how far apart are they really? What if I wanted to draw a map where 1 inch = 25 miles -- how far apart would the cities be on my map?"
- "Look at the corner of a book. What angle does it form? What two angles could add up to that corner angle?"
- "When you look at a clock, the hands form angles. At 3 o'clock, what angle do the hands form? Is that a right angle?"
- "Why do you think every pizza is a circle? What makes a circle special compared to other shapes?"
- "If you cut a cylinder of cheese straight across, what shape is the cut face? What if you cut at an angle?"
- "A bicycle wheel has a diameter of 26 inches. How far does the bike travel in one complete rotation of the wheel?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

Consider seeking additional support if:

- The student cannot use a protractor to measure angles after demonstration (prerequisite gap from Grade 6; all angle lessons depend on this skill)
- The student cannot compute with decimals reliably (prerequisite gap; all circle calculations require decimal multiplication)
- The student cannot set up or solve proportions (prerequisite gap from Unit 1; scale drawing problems depend on proportional reasoning)
- The student cannot solve basic equations like x + 65 = 180 (prerequisite gap from Unit 3; finding missing angles requires equation-solving)
- The student has significant difficulty with spatial visualization even after using physical models (cross-sections and scale drawings require spatial reasoning that some students need additional support to develop)

## Standards Alignment

This unit addresses the following standards:

- **7.G.1** -- Solve problems involving scale drawings of geometric figures, including computing actual lengths and areas from a scale drawing and reproducing a scale drawing at a different scale.
- **7.G.2** -- Draw (freehand, with ruler and protractor, and with technology) geometric shapes with given conditions. Focus on constructing triangles from three measures of angles or sides.
- **7.G.3** -- Describe the two-dimensional figures that result from slicing three-dimensional figures, as in plane sections of right rectangular prisms and right rectangular pyramids.
- **7.G.4** -- Know the formulas for the area and circumference of a circle and use them to solve problems; give an informal derivation of the relationship between the circumference and area of a circle.
- **7.G.5** -- Use facts about supplementary, complementary, vertical, and adjacent angles in a multi-step problem to write and solve simple equations for an unknown angle in a figure.
