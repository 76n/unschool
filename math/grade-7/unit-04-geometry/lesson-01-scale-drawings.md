# Math -- Grade 7 -- Unit 4, Lesson 1

## Topic
Scale Drawings

## Learning Objectives
- Explain what a scale drawing is and why scales are used.
- Read a scale and use it to determine actual lengths from a drawing.
- Measure distances on a scale drawing and convert them to real-world dimensions.
- Set up and solve proportions to find unknown lengths in scale drawing problems.

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of ratios and equivalent ratios (Unit 1 -- Proportional Relationships).
- Ability to set up and solve proportions (Unit 1).
- Ability to measure lengths with a ruler in centimeters and inches.
- Fluency with multiplication and division of decimals.

## Materials Needed
- Ruler (metric preferred).
- Grid paper.
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice problems).
- Index cards or half-sheets for Exit Ticket.
- Sample scale drawing (a simple floor plan or map -- facilitator can draw one on grid paper).

**Standards:** 7.G.1 -- Solve problems involving scale drawings of geometric figures, including computing actual lengths and areas from a scale drawing and reproducing a scale drawing at a different scale.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of ratios and proportions from Unit 1.

1. Write on the board: "The ratio of red marbles to blue marbles is 3:5. If there are 12 red marbles, how many blue marbles are there?"
   - Set up: 3/5 = 12/b. Solve: b = 20 blue marbles.

2. Write: "A recipe uses 2 cups of flour for every 3 cups of sugar. If you use 8 cups of flour, how many cups of sugar do you need?"
   - Set up: 2/3 = 8/s. Solve: s = 12 cups of sugar.

Say: "Today we are going to use ratios and proportions in a new context -- reading maps and blueprints. The math is the same; the application is different."

---

## Direct Instruction (10-15 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary
- **Scale drawing** -- a drawing that represents a real object or place, with all dimensions reduced or enlarged by the same ratio.
- **Scale** -- the ratio that compares a length in the drawing to the corresponding actual length. Example: 1 cm : 5 m means every 1 centimeter on the drawing represents 5 meters in real life.
- **Proportion** -- an equation stating that two ratios are equal.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 -- Introduce scale drawings.**

Say: "Imagine you want to draw a picture of your school building on a piece of paper. The building might be 50 meters long, but your paper is only 30 centimeters wide. You cannot draw it at full size. Instead, you draw it smaller, using a consistent ratio. That is a scale drawing."

Say: "Architects use scale drawings called blueprints to plan buildings. Cartographers use them to make maps. The key rule is: every measurement in the drawing must use the SAME scale."

**Step 2 -- Reading a scale.**

Write on the board: Scale: 1 cm = 4 m

Say: "This means that every 1 centimeter on the drawing represents 4 meters in real life. If a wall is drawn as 3 centimeters long, how long is the actual wall?"

Work through it: 3 cm on the drawing, and each cm represents 4 m. So the actual length is 3 x 4 = 12 meters.

Say: "We can also write this as a proportion: 1 cm / 4 m = 3 cm / x m. Cross-multiply: 1 * x = 4 * 3, so x = 12 meters."

**Step 3 -- Going from actual to drawing.**

Say: "Sometimes you need to go the other direction. If a room is actually 20 meters long, and the scale is 1 cm = 4 m, how long should the room be in the drawing?"

Work through it: 20 / 4 = 5 cm on the drawing.

Or by proportion: 1 cm / 4 m = x cm / 20 m. Cross-multiply: 4x = 20, so x = 5 cm.

### Worked Examples

**Example A: Drawing to Actual**

A floor plan uses the scale 1 cm = 3 m. A hallway is drawn as 7 cm long on the floor plan.

What is the actual length of the hallway?

Step 1: Identify the scale. 1 cm on the drawing = 3 m in real life.
Step 2: The hallway measures 7 cm on the drawing.
Step 3: Multiply: 7 x 3 = 21.
Step 4: The actual hallway is **21 meters** long.

**Example B: Actual to Drawing**

A park is 150 meters wide. An architect is making a scale drawing with a scale of 1 cm = 10 m.

How wide should the park be on the drawing?

Step 1: Identify the scale. 1 cm = 10 m.
Step 2: The actual width is 150 m.
Step 3: Divide: 150 / 10 = 15.
Step 4: The park should be drawn **15 cm** wide.

**Example C: Using a Proportion**

A map uses the scale 1 inch = 25 miles. Two cities are 3.5 inches apart on the map. How far apart are they in real life?

Set up: 1 in / 25 mi = 3.5 in / x mi
Cross-multiply: 1 * x = 25 * 3.5
x = **87.5 miles**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity 1: Reading a Floor Plan**

Present (draw on the board or provide a handout) a simple rectangular floor plan with three rooms. Label the scale: 1 cm = 2 m. Label the drawing dimensions: Room A is 4 cm by 3 cm, Room B is 5 cm by 3 cm, Room C is 2 cm by 3 cm.

1. What are the actual dimensions of Room A?
   - 4 x 2 = 8 m by 3 x 2 = 6 m. Room A is **8 m by 6 m**.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Multiply each drawing measurement by the scale factor."

2. What is the actual area of Room B?
   - Drawing: 5 cm by 3 cm. Actual: 10 m by 6 m. Area = 10 x 6 = **60 square meters**.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Find the actual dimensions first, then compute the area."

3. The owner wants to add a room that is actually 12 m by 8 m. What dimensions should it have on the floor plan?
   - 12 / 2 = 6 cm by 8 / 2 = 4 cm. The room should be drawn as **6 cm by 4 cm**.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "To go from actual to drawing, we divide by the scale factor."

**Activity 2: Map Proportion**

A map uses the scale 1 cm = 50 km.

4. Two towns are 4.5 cm apart on the map. How far apart are they?
   - 1/50 = 4.5/x. x = 50 x 4.5 = **225 km**.

5. A lake is 175 km across. How many centimeters across is it on the map?
   - 1/50 = x/175. 50x = 175. x = **3.5 cm**.

## Independent Practice (10-15 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. A blueprint uses the scale 1 cm = 6 m. A room is drawn as 5 cm by 4 cm. What are the actual dimensions of the room?

2. A map uses the scale 1 inch = 20 miles. Two landmarks are 6.5 inches apart on the map. How many miles apart are they?

3. An architect wants to draw a building that is 45 meters long. The scale is 1 cm = 5 m. How long should the building be on the drawing?

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

4. A soccer field is 100 meters long and 64 meters wide. On a scale drawing, the field is 20 cm long. What is the scale? How wide is the field on the drawing?

5. On a map, the scale is 1 cm = 8 km. City A and City B are 7.5 cm apart on the map. City B and City C are 4 cm apart on the map. How much farther is the actual distance from A to B than from B to C?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

6. A scale drawing of a rectangular garden uses the scale 1 cm = 3 m. On the drawing, the garden is 8 cm by 5 cm. What is the actual perimeter of the garden? What is the actual area?

7. Two different maps show the same road. On Map X (scale 1 cm = 10 km), the road is 6 cm long. On Map Y, the same road is 4 cm long. What is the scale of Map Y?

---

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A scale drawing uses the scale 1 cm = 7 m. A wall is drawn as 4 cm long. What is the actual length of the wall?

**Problem 2:** A park is 90 meters wide. Using the scale 1 cm = 15 m, how wide would the park be on a scale drawing?

**Mastery:** 2 out of 2 correct = Mastery demonstrated. 1 out of 2 = Review errors before next lesson. 0 out of 2 = Reteach with simpler scales.

---

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. Actual dimensions: 5 x 6 = 30 m by 4 x 6 = 24 m. The room is **30 m by 24 m**.
2. 6.5 x 20 = **130 miles**.
3. 45 / 5 = **9 cm**.
4. Scale: 100 m / 20 cm = 5 m per cm. Scale is **1 cm = 5 m**. Width on drawing: 64 / 5 = **12.8 cm**.
5. A to B actual: 7.5 x 8 = 60 km. B to C actual: 4 x 8 = 32 km. Difference: 60 - 32 = **28 km farther**.
6. Drawing perimeter: 2(8) + 2(5) = 26 cm. Actual perimeter: 26 x 3 = **78 m**. Alternatively: actual dimensions are 24 m by 15 m, perimeter = 2(24) + 2(15) = 78 m. Actual area: 24 x 15 = **360 square meters**.
7. The road is actually 6 x 10 = 60 km. On Map Y, 4 cm = 60 km. Scale: 60 / 4 = 15. Map Y scale is **1 cm = 15 km**.

### Exit Ticket

1. 4 x 7 = **28 meters**.
2. 90 / 15 = **6 cm**.

## Teacher Notes

- Students who struggled with proportions in Unit 1 may find it easier to think of scale problems as simple multiplication and division rather than setting up formal proportions. Both approaches are valid.
- Common confusion: students may multiply when they should divide (or vice versa). Ask: "Should the actual measurement be bigger or smaller than the drawing measurement?" This reality check catches many errors.
- If physical scale drawings are not available, draw a simple floor plan on grid paper before the lesson. Label the scale clearly.
- Extension for early finishers: "Draw a scale floor plan of your bedroom. Choose a scale that fits on one sheet of grid paper."
- This lesson connects directly to Lesson 2, where students will work with scale factors and reproduce drawings at a different scale.
