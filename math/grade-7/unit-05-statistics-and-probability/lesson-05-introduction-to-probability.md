# Math -- Grade 7 -- Unit 5, Lesson 5

## Topic
Introduction to Probability

## Learning Objectives
- Express the probability of an event as a number between 0 and 1 (or 0% to 100%).
- Place events on a likelihood scale from impossible (0) to certain (1).
- Distinguish between experimental probability and theoretical probability.
- Use the formula P(event) = favorable outcomes / total outcomes for equally likely outcomes.

## Prerequisites
- Familiarity with fractions, decimals, and percents and conversion between them (Unit 2).
- Understanding of ratios (Unit 1).
- No prior formal probability background required.

## Materials Needed
- A coin (for demonstration)
- A standard number cube (6-sided die)
- A bag with 5 colored chips/blocks: 3 red, 2 blue (or substitute with labeled paper slips)
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice)
- Index cards for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 7.SP.5 -- Understand that the probability of a chance event is a number between 0 and 1. 7.SP.7 -- Develop a probability model and use it to find probabilities of events.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate intuitive understanding of likelihood using everyday language.

Ask the student to rank these events from most likely to least likely (or assign an everyday word like "certain," "likely," "unlikely," "impossible"):

1. The sun will rise tomorrow.
2. You will roll a 7 on a standard 6-sided number cube.
3. A flipped fair coin lands on heads.
4. It will snow in July in the Caribbean.
5. You will draw a red card from a standard deck of 52 cards.

Discuss briefly. No math yet -- just intuition.

Say: "Today we are going to replace vague words like 'likely' and 'unlikely' with precise numbers. Instead of saying 'maybe,' we will say exactly how likely something is on a scale from 0 to 1."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary

- **Probability** -- a number between 0 and 1 (inclusive) that measures the likelihood of an event occurring. Can also be expressed as a percent (0% to 100%).
- **Event** -- a specific outcome or group of outcomes that we are asking about.
- **Impossible event** -- probability = 0. The event cannot happen.
- **Certain event** -- probability = 1 (or 100%). The event will definitely happen.
- **Equally likely outcomes** -- all possible outcomes that have the same probability of occurring.
- **Sample space** -- the complete list of all possible outcomes.
- **Favorable outcomes** -- the outcomes within the sample space that correspond to the event we care about.
- **Theoretical probability** -- the probability computed from the structure of the problem, assuming equally likely outcomes.
- **Experimental probability** -- the probability estimated from actually performing an experiment and counting results.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 -- The probability scale.**

Draw a number line from 0 to 1 on the board.

```
0 --------- 0.25 --------- 0.5 --------- 0.75 --------- 1
|              |               |               |           |
Impossible  Unlikely     As likely      Likely        Certain
                          as not
```

Say: "Every probability lives on this scale. A probability of 0 means the event absolutely cannot happen. A probability of 1 means it will definitely happen. A probability of 0.5 means the event is equally likely to happen or not happen -- like a fair coin flip."

Practice: Ask the student to estimate a probability (as a decimal between 0 and 1) for each item from the warm-up:
- Sun will rise tomorrow: ≈ 1 (essentially certain)
- Roll a 7 on a 6-sided cube: = 0 (impossible -- no face shows 7)
- Fair coin lands heads: = 0.5
- Snow in July in the Caribbean: ≈ 0 (extremely unlikely, essentially impossible in most contexts)
- Draw a red card from 52-card deck: = 26/52 = 0.5

**Step 2 -- Theoretical probability formula.**

Say: "When all outcomes are equally likely, we can calculate the theoretical probability using a simple formula."

Write: **P(event) = number of favorable outcomes / total number of outcomes in the sample space**

Say: "Think of it as: the part I want, out of the whole."

Example: A bag contains 3 red chips and 2 blue chips. If you draw one chip at random, what is the probability of drawing a red chip?

- Sample space: {red, red, red, blue, blue} → 5 outcomes total
- Favorable outcomes (red): 3
- P(red) = 3/5 = 0.6 = 60%

**Step 3 -- Theoretical vs. experimental probability.**

Demonstrate with a coin:

Say: "The theoretical probability of flipping heads on a fair coin is 1/2 = 0.5. That's the math. But what happens when I actually flip it?"

Flip the coin 10 times. Record results. Calculate: (number of heads) / 10.

Say: "This is the experimental probability -- what actually happened in our experiment. Did it equal exactly 0.5? Probably not. That's okay. If we flip 1,000 times, the experimental probability gets much closer to 0.5. This is the Law of Large Numbers, and we'll explore it more in Lesson 7."

**Step 4 -- Sample spaces.**

Say: "To calculate theoretical probability, we need the sample space -- the complete list of all possible outcomes. Let's practice listing sample spaces."

Examples:
- Rolling a number cube: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} -- 6 outcomes
- Flipping a coin: {Heads, Tails} -- 2 outcomes
- Spinning a 4-color spinner (equal sections): {Red, Blue, Green, Yellow} -- 4 outcomes

### Worked Examples

**Example A:**

A spinner has 8 equal sections numbered 1 through 8. What is the probability of landing on a prime number?

- Sample space: {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8}
- Prime numbers in the sample space: {2, 3, 5, 7} -- 4 favorable outcomes
- P(prime) = 4/8 = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%

**Example B:**

A jar contains 6 green marbles, 4 yellow marbles, and 2 purple marbles. A marble is drawn at random. Find:
(a) P(green)
(b) P(purple)
(c) P(not yellow)
(d) P(orange)

- Total: 6 + 4 + 2 = 12 marbles
- (a) P(green) = 6/12 = 1/2
- (b) P(purple) = 2/12 = 1/6
- (c) P(not yellow) = (6+2)/12 = 8/12 = 2/3
- (d) P(orange) = 0/12 = 0 (no orange marbles -- impossible event)

**Example C:**

From a standard deck of 52 cards, a card is drawn at random. What is the probability of drawing a heart?

- Total: 52 cards
- Favorable: 13 hearts
- P(heart) = 13/52 = 1/4 = 0.25 = 25%

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity:** Students work with a bag containing 10 colored tokens: 4 red, 3 blue, 2 green, 1 yellow.

1. What is the sample space? How many total outcomes are there?
   - All 10 tokens. 10 total outcomes.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Each individual token is one possible outcome."

2. What is P(blue)?
   - P(blue) = 3/10 = 0.3 = 30%

3. What is P(not red)?
   - P(not red) = (10-4)/10 = 6/10 = 3/5 = 60%
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Not red means anything that isn't red: blue, green, or yellow."

4. Which color is least likely to be drawn?
   - Yellow. P(yellow) = 1/10 = 10%, the smallest probability.

5. What is P(orange)?
   - P(orange) = 0. There are no orange tokens. This is an impossible event.

6. What is P(red OR blue)?
   - P(red or blue) = (4+3)/10 = 7/10 = 70%
   - **Facilitator tip:** "'Or' means we count both favorable groups together."

7. Add up all four probabilities: P(red) + P(blue) + P(green) + P(yellow). What do you notice?
   - 4/10 + 3/10 + 2/10 + 1/10 = 10/10 = 1. All probabilities in a complete sample space sum to 1.

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. A standard 6-sided number cube is rolled. Find each probability. Express as a fraction, decimal, and percent.
   - (a) P(rolling a 4)
   - (b) P(rolling an even number)
   - (c) P(rolling a number greater than 4)
   - (d) P(rolling an 8)

2. A class has 30 students: 12 boys and 18 girls. One student is chosen at random to present. Find:
   - (a) P(girl is chosen)
   - (b) P(boy is chosen)
   - (c) Do P(girl) + P(boy) = 1? Why does that make sense?

3. A spinner has 6 equal sections: 2 sections are red, 2 are blue, 1 is green, 1 is yellow. Find:
   - (a) P(red)
   - (b) P(green or yellow)
   - (c) P(not blue)
   - (d) Place P(red), P(green), and P(blue) on a probability scale from 0 to 1.

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

4. A bag contains some marbles. P(drawing a red marble) = 3/8. There are 16 marbles total.
   - (a) How many red marbles are in the bag?
   - (b) How many marbles are NOT red?

5. Two students make predictions about the same experiment (rolling a number cube):
   - Student A: "The probability of rolling an odd number is 1/2."
   - Student B: "The probability of rolling a number less than 3 is 1/3."
   - Are both students correct? Verify each claim by listing favorable outcomes.

6. Interleaved review (Unit 2 -- Rational Numbers): A probability is 3/8. Express this as a decimal and a percent. If a different event has a probability of 0.625, is it more or less likely than the 3/8 event? Show your reasoning.

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

7. A bag contains red and blue chips. The probability of drawing a red chip is 2/5. If you add 4 more red chips to the bag, the probability of drawing a red chip becomes 1/2. How many chips were in the bag originally?

8. A school schedule has 6 class periods. A student has: 2 math periods, 1 science period, 1 English period, 1 history period, and 1 art period. If a period is chosen at random for a school visit, find P(math). If the probability of visiting during math or science is 1/2, verify this using the formula.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A jar has 5 red, 3 white, and 2 blue marbles. A marble is drawn at random. What is P(white)? Express as a fraction, decimal, and percent.

**Problem 2:** A student says the probability of rolling a number less than 7 on a standard 6-sided cube is 6/6 = 1. Is this student correct? What does a probability of 1 mean?

**Problem 3:** Place three events on a 0-to-1 probability scale: rolling a 3 on a number cube; drawing a heart from a deck of 52 cards; rolling a number greater than 0 on a 6-sided cube.

**Mastery:** All three problems correct with fractions accurate = Mastery. Probability formula applied correctly but conversions wrong = Review fraction-decimal-percent from Unit 2 before Lesson 6. Cannot apply the formula = Reteach Step 2 with more physical examples.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. Total = 6 outcomes. (a) P(4) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167 ≈ 16.7%. (b) Even numbers: {2,4,6}, P = 3/6 = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%. (c) Greater than 4: {5,6}, P = 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.333 ≈ 33.3%. (d) P(8) = 0/6 = 0 = 0%.

2. Total = 30. (a) P(girl) = 18/30 = 3/5 = 0.6 = 60%. (b) P(boy) = 12/30 = 2/5 = 0.4 = 40%. (c) Yes: 3/5 + 2/5 = 5/5 = 1. Every student chosen must be either a boy or a girl, so these two events cover the entire sample space and their probabilities sum to 1.

3. Total = 6 sections. (a) P(red) = 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 33.3%. (b) P(green or yellow) = (1+1)/6 = 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 33.3%. (c) P(not blue) = (2+1+1)/6 = 4/6 = 2/3 ≈ 66.7%. (d) On the scale: P(green) = 1/6 ≈ 0.17 (closer to 0); P(red) = 1/3 ≈ 0.33 (between 0 and 0.5); P(blue) = 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.33 (same as red).

4. (a) P(red) = 3/8 with 16 total. Favorable = (3/8) * 16 = **6 red marbles**. (b) Not red = 16-6 = **10 marbles**.

5. Student A: Odd numbers on a cube: {1,3,5} = 3 favorable out of 6. P = 3/6 = 1/2. **Correct.** Student B: Numbers less than 3: {1,2} = 2 favorable out of 6. P = 2/6 = 1/3. **Correct.** Both students are correct.

6. 3/8 = 0.375 = 37.5%. 0.625 > 0.375, so the 0.625 event is more likely. (Note: 0.625 = 5/8, which is greater than 3/8.)

7. Let original number of chips = n. Red chips = (2/5)n. After adding 4 red: (2n/5 + 4)/(n + 4) = 1/2. Cross-multiply: 2(2n/5 + 4) = n + 4. 4n/5 + 8 = n + 4. 8 - 4 = n - 4n/5. 4 = n/5. n = **20 chips originally**. Check: 2/5 of 20 = 8 red chips. Add 4: 12 red chips out of 24 total = 12/24 = 1/2. Correct.

8. P(math) = 2/6 = 1/3. Math or science: 2 math + 1 science = 3 periods. P(math or science) = 3/6 = 1/2. Yes, 1/2 is verified.

### Exit Ticket

1. Total = 5+3+2 = 10. P(white) = 3/10 = 0.3 = 30%.

2. Yes, the student is correct. Numbers less than 7 on a 6-sided cube: {1,2,3,4,5,6} = all 6 outcomes. P = 6/6 = 1. A probability of 1 means the event is certain -- it will always happen. Rolling a number less than 7 on a standard 6-sided cube is guaranteed.

3. P(rolling a 3) = 1/6 ≈ 0.17. P(drawing a heart) = 13/52 = 1/4 = 0.25. P(rolling a number > 0) = 6/6 = 1. Positions on scale: P(rolling a 3) at approximately 0.17; P(heart) at 0.25; P(rolling > 0) at exactly 1 (at the right end, "Certain").

## Teacher Notes

- This lesson is the foundation for everything in the probability strand. Spend time ensuring the student is comfortable with: (1) the 0-to-1 scale, (2) the formula P = favorable/total, and (3) the difference between theoretical and experimental probability.
- Many students confuse "impossible" (P=0) with "unlikely." Emphasize: impossible means the event CANNOT happen, not just that it's unusual. Similarly, "certain" means it MUST happen.
- The complement rule (P(not A) = 1 - P(A)) is used in Guided Practice Problems 3 and 6. Make it explicit: "If P(blue) = 1/3, then P(not blue) = 1 - 1/3 = 2/3."
- For the deck of cards context, ensure the student knows: 52 cards, 4 suits (hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades), 13 cards per suit, 26 red (hearts + diamonds), 26 black (clubs + spades).
- Tier 3, Problem 7 is an algebraic probability problem requiring equation-solving from Unit 3. It is an excellent interleaved review challenge.
