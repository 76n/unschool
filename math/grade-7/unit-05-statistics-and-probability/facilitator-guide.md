# Facilitator Guide -- Grade 7 Math, Unit 5: Statistics and Probability

**Grade:** 7
**Subject:** Mathematics
**Unit:** 5 -- Statistics and Probability
**Recommended Duration:** 10 lessons (approximately 3-4 weeks at 3 sessions/week)
**Facilitation Mode:** Guided independence (facilitator leads instruction; student works independently for practice)

---

## Unit Overview

This unit develops two interconnected strands of Grade 7 mathematics: statistical reasoning and probability. In the statistics strand, students build on their Grade 6 foundation of measures of center and variability to tackle a new question: what can a sample tell us about a population? They learn what makes a sample representative, how to draw inferences from sample data, and how to compare two populations using both numerical summaries and visual displays (dot plots and box plots). In the probability strand, students move from informal language about chance to precise numerical probability on the 0 to 1 scale. They calculate theoretical probability, conduct experiments to gather experimental probability, and discover that as the number of trials grows, experimental results approach theoretical predictions. The unit culminates with compound events -- probability when two or more things happen together -- using organized lists, tree diagrams, and tables. Students also design and run simulations to model real-world probability situations.

By the end of this unit, the student will be able to:

- Distinguish between a population and a sample, and explain why random sampling produces representative samples
- Use data from a random sample to draw inferences about a population
- Compare two populations using measures of center (mean, median) and measures of variability (MAD, IQR, range)
- Interpret the overlap and separation of two data distributions using visual displays
- Express the probability of an event as a number from 0 to 1, and explain what probabilities of 0, 0.5, and 1 represent
- Calculate theoretical probability for simple events using sample spaces
- Collect experimental data and calculate relative frequency (experimental probability)
- Explain why experimental probability approaches theoretical probability as the number of trials increases
- Find the probability of compound events using organized lists, tree diagrams, and two-way tables
- Design and run simulations to estimate probability

**Why this matters:** Statistics and probability are among the most practical branches of mathematics. Every survey, poll, medical study, and scientific experiment involves sampling and inference. Probability governs everything from weather forecasting to insurance rates to sports analytics. Students who understand these ideas can be informed consumers of data -- they can evaluate whether a survey result is trustworthy, assess whether a game is fair, and interpret news reports about scientific findings. This unit also directly prepares students for high school statistics and data science coursework.

## Pacing Guidance

| Lesson | Topic | Sessions |
|--------|-------|----------|
| 1 | Random Sampling | 1 |
| 2 | Making Inferences from Samples | 1-2 |
| 3 | Comparing Two Populations | 1-2 |
| 4 | Visual Comparisons of Populations | 1-2 |
| 5 | Introduction to Probability | 1 |
| 6 | Theoretical Probability | 1-2 |
| 7 | Experimental Probability and Relative Frequency | 1-2 |
| 8 | Compound Events | 1-2 |
| 9 | Simulations | 1-2 |
| 10 | Statistics and Probability Review | 1-2 |
| -- | Unit Assessment | Separate session |

**Adjusting the pace:**
- Lesson 1 (random sampling) introduces the unit's central statistical question. It is conceptual and discussion-heavy. Budget the full session for the sampling activity and debrief.
- Lesson 2 (making inferences) requires students to reason from sample data to population claims. This is where many students first encounter the idea that a statistic from a sample is an estimate, not a fact. Allow extra time if the student struggles with the concept of sampling variability.
- Lessons 3 and 4 cover comparing two populations. Lesson 3 focuses on numerical summaries (mean, median, IQR, MAD); Lesson 4 focuses on visual displays (dot plots, box plots). These lessons pair well and can share a session if the student is fluent with the tools from Grade 6. If the student needs to rebuild fluency with IQR or MAD, budget two sessions for Lesson 3.
- Lesson 5 (introduction to probability) is foundational. The 0 to 1 scale and the distinction between experimental and theoretical probability must be solid before Lessons 6 and 7.
- Lesson 6 (theoretical probability) involves sample space construction. Students often undercount or overcount outcomes. Budget extra time for the sample space work with organized lists.
- Lesson 7 (experimental probability) includes a hands-on experiment. Do not skip the experiment -- the experience of watching relative frequency converge toward theoretical probability is one of the most important ideas in the unit.
- Lesson 8 (compound events) is the most technically demanding lesson. Tree diagrams and tables take time to construct and interpret. Budget two sessions if the student is working carefully. Do not rush compound events -- this content reappears in high school.
- Lesson 9 (simulations) is open-ended. Allow the student to design and run their own simulation if possible. This is a creative application of all probability ideas and is highly engaging.
- Lesson 10 (review) should integrate statistics and probability. Use mixed problems that require the student to draw on any lesson from Units 1-5.

## Lesson Summaries

| Lesson | Summary |
|--------|---------|
| 1 | Students distinguish between populations and samples, explore what makes a sample random and representative, and identify sources of bias in non-random samples. They conduct a simple sampling activity using a class of fictional students. |
| 2 | Students use data from random samples to draw inferences about a population. They practice making predictions, express uncertainty in their claims ("I estimate that approximately..."), and recognize that different random samples produce different estimates. |
| 3 | Students compare two populations by computing and comparing measures of center (mean, median) and variability (range, IQR, MAD). They determine whether differences between groups are meaningful by examining the degree of overlap. |
| 4 | Students construct dot plots and box plots for two data sets on the same number line and use visual overlap and separation to draw conclusions about the populations. They connect visual comparisons to the numerical summaries from Lesson 3. |
| 5 | Students are introduced to probability as a number from 0 to 1. They place events on a likelihood scale, distinguish between experimental and theoretical probability, and begin to use the classical probability formula P(event) = favorable outcomes / total outcomes. |
| 6 | Students calculate theoretical probability for simple events by listing all outcomes in the sample space (organized lists, tables). They verify that probabilities in a complete sample space sum to 1. |
| 7 | Students conduct probability experiments, calculate relative frequency (experimental probability), and compare their results to the theoretical probability. They explore how more trials leads to results closer to the theoretical value. |
| 8 | Students find the probability of compound events (two or more things happening) using organized lists, tree diagrams, and two-way tables. They apply the counting principle to determine the size of a sample space. |
| 9 | Students design and run simulations using simple tools (coins, number cubes, spinners) to estimate the probability of events that would be difficult to calculate exactly. They connect simulation results to theoretical probability. |
| 10 | Students complete a comprehensive review integrating sampling and inference, population comparisons, and all probability models through mixed practice, error analysis, and multi-step problems. |

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Population** | The entire group being studied. |
| **Sample** | A subset of the population selected for study. |
| **Random sample** | A sample in which every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. |
| **Biased sample** | A sample that does not represent the population because certain members were more likely to be selected. |
| **Inference** | A conclusion about a population drawn from sample data. |
| **Sampling variability** | The natural variation that occurs when different samples from the same population produce different statistics. |
| **Measures of center** | Mean and median; values that describe the "middle" of a data set. |
| **Measures of variability** | Range, IQR, and MAD; values that describe how spread out a data set is. |
| **Probability** | A number from 0 to 1 (or 0% to 100%) that describes the likelihood of an event. |
| **Theoretical probability** | The expected probability based on mathematical reasoning and equally likely outcomes. P(event) = favorable / total. |
| **Experimental probability** | The probability estimated from actual experimental results. P(event) = number of times event occurred / total trials. |
| **Relative frequency** | Another name for experimental probability; the fraction of trials in which an event occurred. |
| **Sample space** | The complete set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. |
| **Event** | Any specific outcome or set of outcomes from an experiment. |
| **Compound event** | An event made up of two or more simple events occurring together (e.g., flipping a coin AND rolling a number cube). |
| **Tree diagram** | A branching diagram that shows all possible outcomes of a compound event. |
| **Simulation** | A model that uses a simple random process (coin, die, spinner) to represent and estimate the probability of a real-world event. |

## Facilitation Notes

### Building from Grade 6 Statistics

Grade 6 statistics focused on describing a single data set: what is the center? what is the spread? Students know how to compute mean, median, range, IQR, and MAD, and how to read dot plots, histograms, and box plots. This unit extends that work in two ways: (1) using sample data to make claims about populations (not just describing the data in hand), and (2) comparing two data sets to determine which population has a higher or lower value. Explicitly activate Grade 6 vocabulary at the start of Lessons 3 and 4.

### The Big Statistical Idea: Inference Under Uncertainty

The central insight of the statistics strand is that data from a sample is never certain -- it is an estimate with variability. Help the student develop language for this: "Based on my sample, I estimate that..." is better than "The answer is..." Reinforce this epistemic humility throughout Lessons 1-4.

### Hands-On Probability

The methodology framework's CRA progression applies here: start with physical experiments (coins, dice, bags of colored chips) before moving to numerical calculations. Students who "see" probability through repeated trials develop a much deeper understanding of why the formula P = favorable/total works. Do not skip the hands-on experiments in Lessons 7 and 9.

### Connecting Probability to Fractions, Decimals, and Percents

All three forms of expressing probability (fraction, decimal, percent) are valid. When students express 3/4 as 0.75 or 75%, they are applying Unit 2 skills. Encourage fluency in all three forms and require students to label which form they are using. This unit offers excellent organic review of fraction/decimal/percent conversions.

### Common Stumbling Block: Confusing Theoretical and Experimental Probability

Students often expect experimental results to match theoretical predictions exactly. Lesson 7 directly addresses this with an activity that shows variability across trials. Help the student understand that with a fair coin, each flip has a 1/2 probability of heads -- but flipping the coin 10 times does not guarantee exactly 5 heads. The law of large numbers says that as you flip more and more times, the relative frequency gets closer and closer to 1/2.

### Compound Events: The Two-Step Process

For compound events, students must (1) build the complete sample space and (2) count favorable outcomes. Errors almost always come from step 1 -- students miss some outcomes or double-count. Teach the structured approaches (organized list, tree diagram, table) as tools for ensuring completeness, not as arbitrary procedures.

## Differentiation Strategies

**For the student who needs more support:**
- For sampling, use concrete, physical examples: drawing names from a hat (random) versus picking your friends (biased). Connect to real experiences like school surveys or sports drafts.
- Provide a formula reference card: P(event) = favorable/total; P(A and B) use tree diagram; relative frequency = successes/trials.
- For comparing two populations, provide a structured table: "Population 1 mean: ___, Population 2 mean: ___, Difference: ___. Population 1 IQR: ___, Population 2 IQR: ___. What does this tell us?"
- For compound events, always start with a tree diagram before attempting a table or list. The visual branching structure is more intuitive than a table.
- Reduce the number of problems in Independent Practice if needed, but maintain both tiers and ensure the core concept problems are completed.

**For the student who needs more challenge:**
- Introduce the concept of margin of error: if a sample proportion is p, a simple estimate of the margin of error is approximately 1/sqrt(n). What does this mean for sample size choices?
- Explore conditional probability informally: "Given that the first card drawn is red, what is the probability the second card is also red?"
- Introduce geometric probability: if a dart lands at a random point on a target, what is the probability it lands in the bull's-eye? (Area of bull's-eye / Area of target.)
- Challenge the student to design a fair game: construct a game where each player has an equal probability of winning. Verify the fairness mathematically.
- Extend simulations: run 100+ trials and graph the running relative frequency. Observe the convergence to theoretical probability.

## Common Errors

| What You Might See | What It Means | What to Do |
|---|---|---|
| Student treats sample statistics as exact population values | Does not understand sampling variability | Emphasize: "A sample statistic is an estimate, not a fact. Different samples give different estimates." Run a sampling activity with multiple samples and compare results. |
| Student identifies a convenience sample as random | Confuses accessibility with randomness | Ask: "Did every person in the population have an equal chance of being in this sample?" If no, the sample is not random. Use the "name in a hat" model for random sampling. |
| Student computes P = total / favorable (inverts the fraction) | Reverses numerator and denominator | Reinforce: "Probability is the part we want out of the whole. Part / Whole." Use physical setups (3 red chips, 5 total chips: P(red) = 3/5). |
| Student lists outcomes for compound events incompletely | Does not use a systematic method | Require the student to use a tree diagram or organized table before writing the sample space. Emphasize checking: "Did I cover all possible combinations?" |
| Student expects experimental probability to exactly match theoretical | Does not understand the role of chance in short runs | Run 10 trials and compare to 100 trials. Show that the relative frequency is closer to the theoretical probability with 100 trials. Discuss: "Why does it not match exactly with 10 trials?" |
| Student adds probabilities for compound events instead of multiplying | Confuses "and" (multiply) with "or" (add, with care) | Focus on tree diagrams: each branch is a multiplication step. Use the term "and" to trigger multiplication. ("What is P(heads AND 4)?": multiply P(heads) * P(4).) |
| Student compares population means without considering variability | Ignores spread when making comparisons | Ask: "Even if Group A has a higher mean, does that mean every person in Group A scored higher than every person in Group B? Why does the spread matter?" |

## Assessment Guidance

The unit assessment is a 60-point summative assessment with five sections:

| Section | Skill | Points |
|---------|-------|--------|
| 1 | Sampling and Inference | 12 |
| 2 | Comparing Two Populations | 14 |
| 3 | Simple Probability | 12 |
| 4 | Compound Events | 14 |
| 5 | Problem Solving and Reasoning | 8 |
| -- | **Total** | **60** |

**Mastery threshold:** 70% (42 of 60 points) AND at least 50% in each section.

Note: The mastery threshold for this unit is set at 70% (lower than some prior units) because the content is conceptually dense and some of the reasoning questions require interpretation skills that develop over time. A student who demonstrates solid procedural competence AND reasonable conceptual understanding at 70% is well-prepared for Grade 8 probability and statistics.

If the student does not meet mastery:
- Section 1 (Sampling and Inference): Return to Lessons 1-2. Redo the sampling activity. Focus on the vocabulary: population, sample, random, biased, inference.
- Section 2 (Comparing Two Populations): Return to Lessons 3-4. Rebuild fluency with measures of center and variability from Grade 6. Practice the structured comparison format (mean, median, IQR side by side).
- Section 3 (Simple Probability): Return to Lessons 5-6. Use physical probability models (bags of chips) to build intuition before moving to symbolic calculation.
- Section 4 (Compound Events): Return to Lesson 8. Emphasize tree diagrams as the primary tool. Practice listing the complete sample space before asking for any probabilities.
- Section 5 (Problem Solving): Identify which underlying skills are weak and reteach those specific lessons. Multi-step reasoning improves when the component skills are solid.

## Conversation Starters

- "If I want to know what the average 7th grader in the country eats for lunch, should I just ask the students in your school? Why or why not? What would a better approach be?"
- "If I roll a fair die 6 times, do I have to get exactly one 1, one 2, one 3, one 4, one 5, and one 6? Why or why not?"
- "A news report says '6 out of 10 teenagers prefer streaming over TV.' How many people do you think they asked? Does the sample size matter?"
- "In basketball, some players shoot 80% from the free-throw line. What does that mean? If a player takes 10 free throws in a game, will they make exactly 8?"
- "If I flip two coins, how many ways can I get exactly one head? List all the possibilities."
- "What is the probability that it will rain tomorrow? Is that an experimental probability or a theoretical probability?"

## When to Seek Additional Help

Consider seeking additional support if:

- The student cannot compute mean and median from a data set after review (prerequisite gap from Grade 6; required for Lessons 3-4)
- The student cannot compute IQR or interpret a box plot after review (prerequisite gap from Grade 6; required for Lesson 4)
- The student cannot convert fractions to decimals and percents fluently (prerequisite gap from Unit 2; required throughout the probability lessons)
- The student struggles consistently to identify favorable vs. total outcomes, even with physical models (foundational probability concept; indicates a need for more concrete experience)
- The student cannot distinguish between a sample and a population after multiple explanations and examples (core conceptual prerequisite for the entire statistics strand)

## Standards Alignment

This unit addresses the following standards:

- **7.SP.1** -- Understand that statistics can be used to gain information about a population by examining a sample of the population; understand that random sampling tends to produce representative samples and support valid inferences.
- **7.SP.2** -- Use data from a random sample to draw inferences about a population with an unknown characteristic of interest. Generate multiple samples (or simulated samples) of the same size to gauge the variation in estimates or predictions.
- **7.SP.3** -- Informally assess the degree of visual overlap of two numerical data distributions with similar variabilities, measuring the difference between the centers by expressing it as a multiple of a measure of variability.
- **7.SP.4** -- Use measures of center and measures of variability for numerical data from random samples to draw informal comparative inferences about two populations.
- **7.SP.5** -- Understand that the probability of a chance event is a number between 0 and 1 that expresses the likelihood of the event occurring. Larger numbers indicate greater likelihood.
- **7.SP.6** -- Approximate the probability of a chance event by collecting data on the chance process that produces it and observing its long-run relative frequency, and predict the approximate relative frequency given the probability.
- **7.SP.7** -- Develop a probability model and use it to find probabilities of events. Compare probabilities from a model to observed frequencies; if the agreement is not good, explain possible sources of the discrepancy.
- **7.SP.8** -- Find probabilities of compound events using organized lists, tables, tree diagrams, and simulation.

## What Comes Next

After this unit, Grade 7 mathematics is complete. In **Grade 8**, students will encounter:
- **Statistics:** Scatter plots, lines of best fit, and two-way tables (extending the comparison work from this unit)
- **Probability:** The Grade 8 curriculum does not introduce new probability topics, but the compound event reasoning from this unit supports Grade 8 rational number work and algebraic thinking
- **High School Statistics and Probability:** This unit is a direct foundation for Algebra 2 and Statistics courses covering probability distributions, expected value, normal distributions, and hypothesis testing
