# Math -- Grade 7 -- Unit 5, Lesson 1

## Topic
Random Sampling

## Learning Objectives
- Distinguish between a population and a sample.
- Explain why random sampling tends to produce representative samples.
- Identify sources of bias in non-random (biased) sampling methods.
- Evaluate whether a described sample is likely to be representative of a given population.

## Prerequisites
- Familiarity with data sets and summary statistics from Grade 6 (mean, median, mode).
- Understanding of ratios and proportions (Unit 1).
- No prior formal probability background required.

## Materials Needed
- Paper slips or index cards for the sampling activity (30 cards numbered 1-30, or use a list of fictional student names)
- A bag or container for drawing random samples
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice)
- Index cards for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 7.SP.1 -- Understand that statistics can be used to gain information about a population by examining a sample; understand that random sampling tends to produce representative samples and support valid inferences.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Activate prior knowledge of data and statistics from Grade 6.

Ask the student to answer these questions without calculating:

1. "If I want to know the average height of all 200 students at a school, do I need to measure all 200 students? What is another approach?"
   - Elicit: You could measure a smaller group and use it to estimate the average for everyone.

2. "If I ask the five tallest students on the basketball team, will that give me a good estimate of the average height for all students? Why or why not?"
   - Elicit: No -- basketball players are selected partly for being tall. That group is not typical of all students.

Say: "Today we are going to learn exactly why some samples give us accurate information about a population and others mislead us."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary

- **Population** -- the entire group being studied. Example: all 7th graders in the United States.
- **Sample** -- a smaller group selected from the population to be studied. Example: 200 randomly chosen 7th graders.
- **Random sample** -- a sample in which every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. Like drawing names from a hat.
- **Biased sample** -- a sample in which some members of the population are more likely to be selected than others. The sample does not represent the population fairly.
- **Representative sample** -- a sample whose characteristics closely match the characteristics of the population.
- **Inference** -- a conclusion about a population based on data from a sample.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 -- Why we use samples.**

Say: "Imagine a company wants to know the average number of hours per day that American teenagers spend on social media. There are about 42 million teenagers in the United States. It would be impossible to survey every single one. Instead, researchers survey a sample -- maybe 1,000 teenagers -- and use that data to make an inference about all 42 million."

Say: "The key question is: will the sample give us accurate information about the whole population? The answer depends on how the sample is chosen."

**Step 2 -- Random sampling.**

Say: "A random sample is one where every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected. Think of putting all 42 million names in a hat and drawing 1,000 at random. With a random sample, we expect the sample to look like the population -- it will include people from all regions, all income levels, all backgrounds, proportionally."

Say: "Random samples are not perfect, but they are fair. The variation we get from different random samples is called sampling variability, and it is predictable and manageable."

**Step 3 -- Biased sampling.**

Say: "A biased sample systematically favors certain members of the population over others. Let's look at three common types of bias:"

Write and explain:

1. **Convenience sampling:** Surveying people who are easy to reach. Example: posting an online survey and only people who see and respond to the post participate. This overrepresents people who spend time online.

2. **Voluntary response sampling:** Only people who choose to respond participate. Example: a TV news channel asks viewers to call in and vote on a question. People with strong opinions are more likely to call in.

3. **Self-selected sampling:** Similar to voluntary response. Example: a restaurant hands out comment cards. Customers who had a very good or very bad experience are most likely to fill them out.

Say: "Biased samples tend to overrepresent one part of the population, which means inferences drawn from biased samples can be misleading or flat-out wrong."

### Worked Examples

**Example A:** A school wants to know how students feel about the new lunch menu. They survey the 30 students who are first in line at lunch each day for one week.

Is this a random sample? Is it likely biased? Explain.

- This is NOT a random sample. Students who arrive first in the lunch line may systematically differ from other students (perhaps they have shorter class periods before lunch, or they are more enthusiastic about the menu). The sample overrepresents fast-arriving students.

**Example B:** A survey asks: "What is your favorite sport?" Researchers stand outside a football stadium and ask 100 people leaving after a game.

Is this sample biased? What population would this sample represent accurately?

- This sample is biased for the general population -- people leaving a football game are far more likely to say football is their favorite sport than the general population. However, it might accurately represent people who attend football games.

**Example C:** A researcher wants to know the average number of books read per year by students at a school of 400. She assigns each student a number from 1 to 400 and uses a random number generator to select 40 students to survey.

Is this a random sample? Why is it likely to be representative?

- Yes, this is a random sample. Every student had an equal probability of being selected (40/400 = 1/10). The sample is likely to be representative because the selection process was fair and unbiased.

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity: Population or Sample? Random or Biased?**

For each scenario, the student identifies (a) what the population is, (b) what the sample is, (c) whether the sampling method is random or biased, and (d) if biased, what the bias is.

**Scenario 1:** The city government wants to know how residents feel about a new park. They set up a survey booth at the park entrance and collect responses from 150 park visitors.

- (a) Population: all city residents
- (b) Sample: 150 park visitors who stop at the booth
- (c) Biased: only people who visit the park are included; people who never go to the park are excluded, but they are part of the population and may have different opinions
- **Facilitator tip:** "Who is NOT represented in this sample?"

**Scenario 2:** A teacher wants to know the average time her students spend studying each week. She writes each of her 28 students' names on a slip of paper, puts them in a bowl, and draws 8 names.

- (a) Population: the teacher's 28 students
- (b) Sample: 8 randomly drawn students
- (c) Random: every student had an equal chance of being selected
- **Facilitator tip:** "Could any student have been more likely to be chosen than another student?"

**Scenario 3:** A magazine publishes an online poll: "Do you think teenagers use too much social media?" Readers can vote yes or no.

- (a) Population: teenagers (or adults with opinions about teenagers -- clarify with the student)
- (b) Sample: magazine readers who choose to vote
- (c) Biased: voluntary response bias. People who feel strongly about the issue are more likely to vote. People who think the question is unimportant are less likely to engage.

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. A researcher wants to know the average number of hours per night that 7th graders in a school sleep. She posts a survey on the school's social media page and gets 45 responses.
   - (a) What is the population?
   - (b) What is the sample?
   - (c) Is this a random sample? Explain.

2. A company surveys 500 customers to learn about their satisfaction with a product. The survey is sent by email to 500 randomly chosen people from their complete customer database.
   - (a) Is this a random sample? Why or why not?
   - (b) If the survey finds that 72% of respondents are satisfied, what inference can be drawn about all customers?

3. Identify the bias in each scenario and suggest a better sampling method:
   - (a) To find out the most popular music genre among students at a school, a reporter asks 20 students outside the school's band room.
   - (b) To estimate the average number of siblings of students at a school, a teacher surveys her own homeroom class of 30.

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

4. A political candidate wants to know how voters in a city feel about a new policy. Her campaign team calls 200 people from a list of registered voters, selected randomly by a computer.
   - (a) Is this sample likely to be representative of all city residents? Explain why or why not.
   - (b) What group of city residents would be excluded from this sample?

5. Two students survey the same school of 500 students to find the average number of extracurricular activities each student participates in. Student A stands by the gym and surveys 40 students after school. Student B uses a list of all 500 student names and randomly selects 40 names. Both collect their data and compute averages. Student A gets a mean of 2.8 activities; Student B gets a mean of 1.4 activities. Which result is more likely to accurately represent the school? Why is there such a large difference?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

6. You want to find the average amount of money 7th graders spend per week. You have access to a complete roster of all 7th graders in your school district (1,200 students). Design a random sampling plan. Describe: (a) the method for selecting names, (b) the sample size you would choose and why, (c) one source of potential bias that could still exist even in a random sample, and (d) how you would report your findings to acknowledge that your result is an estimate, not a certainty.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A wildlife biologist wants to estimate the average weight of fish in a lake. She catches 40 fish at random from the lake. What is the population? What is the sample?

**Problem 2:** A news website reports that 80% of people are worried about climate change. The data came from an online poll on the news website's homepage, with 4,000 responses. Is this a reliable estimate for the general population? Explain.

**Mastery:** Both questions answered correctly with adequate reasoning = Mastery demonstrated. Correct identification without explanation = Partial credit; discuss before next lesson. Incorrect on either = Review definitions and bias concepts before proceeding.

---

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. (a) Population: all 7th graders at the school. (b) Sample: 45 students who responded to the social media survey. (c) Not a random sample -- only students who follow the school page and choose to respond are included. Students who are not on social media, or who saw the post but didn't respond, are excluded. This is voluntary response bias.

2. (a) Yes, this is a random sample -- the 500 were randomly chosen from the complete customer database. Every customer had a chance to be selected. (b) Inference: approximately 72% of all the company's customers are satisfied with the product.

3. (a) Bias: students outside the band room are likely musicians, who may have different music preferences than non-musicians. Better method: randomly select students from the full school roster. (b) Bias: the teacher's homeroom is a convenience sample -- this single class may differ from the school as a whole. Better method: randomly select students from the full roster.

4. (a) This is likely representative of registered voters, but not necessarily all city residents -- only registered voters are on the list. (b) Excluded: non-registered voters, residents who are not eligible to vote, residents below voting age.

5. Student B's result (1.4) is more likely to be accurate. Student A's sample was collected outside the gym after school -- students who stay after school for activities are more likely to participate in more extracurriculars, which inflates the average. The large difference (2.8 vs. 1.4) is a result of this selection bias.

6. Sample answer: (a) Use the district's complete roster of 1,200 7th graders. Number each student. Use a random number generator (or a number cube rolled repeatedly, or a computer's random number function) to select 60-120 students. (b) A sample of about 60-100 students gives a reasonable estimate; larger samples give more reliable estimates but require more work. (c) Even with a random sample, there could be non-response bias -- some selected students may not respond, and those who don't respond might differ systematically (e.g., students from lower-income families may have less disposable spending money and be less likely to participate in surveys). (d) "Based on a random sample of [n] 7th graders, the estimated average weekly spending is $___. Because this is based on a sample, not the entire population, the actual average may differ by a few dollars."

### Exit Ticket

1. Population: all fish in the lake. Sample: the 40 fish caught and weighed.

2. Not reliable for the general population. This is a voluntary response sample from one website -- only people who visit that site and choose to click on the poll are included. People who are very concerned about climate change, or who already agree with the site's editorial slant, may be disproportionately represented. A random sample of the general population would be more reliable.

## Teacher Notes

- The sampling activity from the warm-up can be extended into a full class simulation: write 30 fictional student names and heights (or test scores) on paper slips, place them in a bag, and have the student draw random samples of 5 repeatedly. Compare sample means across draws to illustrate sampling variability.
- Students often struggle with the idea that a large biased sample can be worse than a small random sample. The classic example is the 1936 Literary Digest poll that surveyed 2 million people and incorrectly predicted the presidential election outcome because the sample was drawn from telephone directories and car registrations, over-representing wealthy voters.
- Connect to real-world media literacy: train the student to ask "How was this data collected?" whenever they see a statistic in a news article or social media post.
- This lesson sets the conceptual foundation for Lesson 2. Do not rush it -- the idea that sampling method matters more than sample size is genuinely surprising to most students.
