## Direct Instruction (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Remind the student of the unit's structure and key tools. No new content.

Display or write the Unit 5 "toolkit" on the board:

**Statistics strand:**
- Inference from sample: sample proportion → population estimate (scale up using proportions)
- Comparing populations: compute mean, median, range, IQR, MAD; then write a comparison
- Visual comparison: dot plots and box plots on the same scale; describe overlap

**Probability strand:**
- Simple probability: P = favorable / total; complement P(not A) = 1 - P(A)
- Experimental probability: relative frequency = events / trials; Law of Large Numbers
- Compound events: organized list, tree diagram, or two-way table; then P = favorable / total
- Simulations: assign tool outcomes to real-world outcomes; run trials; compute relative frequency

Say: "Every problem we do today uses one of these tools. When you see a problem, your first job is to identify which tool to use."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Problem Set: Mixed Applications**

Work through these problems together, modeling the "identify the tool first" approach:

**Problem 1 (Inference):** A random sample of 40 students from a school of 800 showed that 24 students have a pet at home. Estimate the number of students in the whole school with pets.

- Tool: sample proportion → scale up
- 24/40 = 60%. 60% of 800 = **480 students**.
- **Facilitator tip:** "What fraction of the sample has a pet? Apply that fraction to the whole school."

**Problem 2 (Comparing populations):** Two groups of students reported daily steps. Group A (athletes) mean = 9,400 steps, median = 9,200, IQR = 1,800. Group B (non-athletes) mean = 6,100 steps, median = 5,900, IQR = 2,200.

Write two sentences comparing these populations.

- Sample: "Athletes walk significantly more steps per day on average (mean 9,400 vs. 6,100; median 9,200 vs. 5,900). Athletes are also more consistent in their step counts -- the IQR for athletes (1,800) is smaller than for non-athletes (2,200)."
- **Facilitator tip:** "Compare both center AND variability."

**Problem 3 (Theoretical probability):** A jar has 5 red, 3 blue, 4 green, and 8 yellow chips. A chip is drawn at random.
- (a) P(yellow) = 8/20 = **2/5 = 40%**
- (b) P(not red) = 1 - 5/20 = 15/20 = **3/4 = 75%**
- (c) P(red or blue) = (5+3)/20 = 8/20 = **2/5 = 40%**

**Problem 4 (Compound event):** A coin is flipped and a spinner with sections labeled 1, 2, 3, 4 is spun.
- How many total outcomes? 2 * 4 = **8**
- P(heads AND an even number) = ? Favorable: H-2, H-4 = 2 outcomes. P = 2/8 = **1/4**.
- **Facilitator tip:** "List the sample space first; then identify favorable outcomes."

## Independent Practice (15 minutes)

### Section A: Statistics

**Problem 1 (Sampling).** A social media influencer asks her followers to vote in a poll: "Is homework helpful?" 82% of the 5,000 respondents say "no." She concludes that 82% of all teenagers think homework is not helpful. Identify two problems with her reasoning.

**Problem 2 (Inference).** A random sample of 30 students from a school of 450 found that 12 students participate in after-school clubs. Estimate the total number of club participants in the school.

**Problem 3 (Comparing populations).** Two classes took the same test. Compute the five-number summary for each class and write a comparison of their performance.

Class A scores: 62, 70, 75, 78, 80, 83, 84, 85, 90, 93

Class B scores: 45, 55, 68, 72, 80, 82, 87, 90, 95, 98

**Problem 4 (Visual comparison).** Using the five-number summaries you computed in Problem 3, describe (or sketch) what two box plots of these data sets would look like side by side. What would the overlap look like? Which class performed more consistently?

### Section B: Probability

**Problem 5 (Theoretical probability).** A standard number cube is rolled. Find:
- (a) P(a factor of 12)
- (b) P(a number greater than 2 AND less than 6)
- (c) P(a perfect square)

**Problem 6 (Experimental probability).** A student rolls a number cube 60 times. She gets a 5 exactly 12 times.
- (a) What is the experimental probability of rolling a 5?
- (b) What is the theoretical probability?
- (c) How many 5s would she expect in 60 rolls, based on theoretical probability?
- (d) The actual count (12) is higher than expected. Does this mean the die is unfair? Explain.

**Problem 7 (Compound events).** A bag has 3 green chips and 2 yellow chips. Two chips are drawn WITH replacement (the first chip is put back before the second is drawn).
- (a) Create a tree diagram or table showing all possible outcomes (use G and Y).
- (b) P(both chips are green)
- (c) P(at least one yellow chip)

### Section C: Interleaved Review (Units 1-4)

**Problem 8 (Unit 1 -- Proportional Relationships).** A survey found that 5 out of every 8 students prefer texting to calling. If there are 240 students in a grade, how many prefer texting?

**Problem 9 (Unit 2 -- Rational Numbers).** A probability is given as 7/15. Express as a decimal (rounded to nearest hundredth) and as a percent (rounded to nearest tenth). Is this event more likely or less likely than a coin flip?

**Problem 10 (Unit 3 -- Equations).** A bag has red and blue chips. P(red) = 3/7. There are 28 chips total. How many red chips are in the bag? Set up and solve an equation.

**Problem 11 (Unit 4 -- Geometry).** A circular spinner has a total radius of 8 cm. The bullseye region is a circle with radius 2 cm. What fraction of the spinner's area is the bullseye? What is the theoretical probability of a dart landing in the bullseye if it lands at a random point on the spinner?

### Section D: Error Analysis

**Problem 12.** A student writes: "I flipped a coin 10 times and got 7 heads. The probability of heads must be 7/10, not 1/2." Evaluate this claim. Is the student correct or incorrect? What should they have said?

**Problem 13.** A student tries to list all outcomes for rolling two number cubes. She writes: {(1,1), (1,2), (1,3), (1,4), (1,5), (1,6), (2,3), (2,4), (2,5), (2,6), (3,4), (3,5), (3,6), (4,5), (4,6), (5,6)} -- 16 outcomes. She says "there are 16 outcomes." What is her error? How many outcomes are there?

## Answer Key

### Section A

1. Two problems: (1) This is a voluntary response sample (self-selected) -- the influencer's followers who choose to vote may not represent all teenagers. People who follow this particular influencer, and those who feel strongly enough to vote, are overrepresented. (2) Even if the sample were random, you cannot generalize from followers of one social media account to "all teenagers" -- this population (her followers) may not match the general teenage population in terms of homework attitudes.

2. Sample proportion: 12/30 = 40%. Estimated club participants: 40% of 450 = **180 students**.

3. Class A ordered: 62, 70, 75, 78, 80, 83, 84, 85, 90, 93. Five-number summary: Min=62, Q1=(75+78)/2=76.5, Median=(80+83)/2=81.5, Q3=(84+85)/2=84.5, Max=93. IQR=84.5-76.5=8.

Class B ordered: 45, 55, 68, 72, 80, 82, 87, 90, 95, 98. Five-number summary: Min=45, Q1=(68+72)/2=70, Median=(80+82)/2=81, Q3=(87+90)/2=88.5, Max=98. IQR=88.5-70=18.5.

Comparison: "Both classes have very similar medians (Class A: 81.5, Class B: 81). However, Class A is much more consistent -- their IQR is only 8 points compared to Class B's IQR of 18.5. Class B has more spread: their scores range from 45 to 98 (range = 53) while Class A ranges from 62 to 93 (range = 31). Some Class B students scored much lower and some much higher than typical Class A students."

4. Box plot description: Both medians are similar (≈81). Class A box spans roughly 76.5-84.5 (IQR = 8); Class B box spans roughly 70-88.5 (IQR = 18.5). The boxes substantially overlap in the 76-84.5 region. Class A's box is noticeably narrower, indicating more consistent scores. Class A's minimum (62) is higher than Class B's minimum (45). Class A is more consistent.

### Section B

5. Number cube sample space: {1,2,3,4,5,6}. (a) Factors of 12 on a cube: {1,2,3,4,6} = 5 values. P = 5/6. (b) Greater than 2 AND less than 6: {3,4,5} = 3 values. P = 3/6 = 1/2. (c) Perfect squares: {1,4} = 2 values. P = 2/6 = 1/3.

6. (a) Experimental P(5) = 12/60 = 1/5 = 0.2 = 20%. (b) Theoretical P(5) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167 ≈ 16.7%. (c) Expected 5s in 60 rolls = (1/6) * 60 = **10 fives**. (d) No -- getting 12 instead of 10 is a small difference. With 60 trials, variation of ±2-3 from the expected count is completely normal. This single experiment cannot determine if the die is unfair; many more trials (hundreds) would be needed to draw a reliable conclusion.

7. (a) Sample space with replacement: GG, GY, YG, YY. (If treating individual chips: 5 * 5 = 25 outcomes; but treating by color: 2-color * 2-color = 4 patterns. Use individual chip model for accuracy.) Individual chips: 3G and 2Y = 5 chips. With replacement, first draw has 5 options, second draw has 5 options: 25 total outcomes. G₁G₁, G₁G₂, G₁G₃, G₁Y₁, G₁Y₂, G₂G₁, ... etc. By color pattern: GG = 3*3=9 outcomes; GY = 3*2=6 outcomes; YG = 2*3=6 outcomes; YY = 2*2=4 outcomes. Total = 25. (b) P(both green) = 9/25. Or using probability: P(G) = 3/5; P(GG) = 3/5 * 3/5 = 9/25. (c) P(at least one yellow) = 1 - P(both green) = 1 - 9/25 = **16/25**. Or count: GY + YG + YY = 6+6+4 = 16. P = 16/25.

### Section C

8. 5/8 prefer texting. 240 * 5/8 = **150 students** prefer texting.

9. 7/15 ÷ 7÷15: 7 ÷ 15 ≈ **0.47** (rounded). As percent: **46.7%**. A coin flip has probability 0.5 = 50%. Since 0.47 < 0.50, this event is **less likely** than a coin flip (but not by much).

10. P(red) = 3/7; total chips = 28. Number of red chips = (3/7) * 28 = **12 red chips**. Equation: r/28 = 3/7. Cross-multiply: 7r = 84. r = 12.

11. Area of bullseye = 3.14 * 2^2 = 3.14 * 4 = 12.56 sq cm. Area of full spinner = 3.14 * 8^2 = 3.14 * 64 = 200.96 sq cm. Fraction = 12.56/200.96 = 4/64 = **1/16**. P(bullseye) = 1/16 = 0.0625 = 6.25%. (Alternatively: since area scales with radius^2, the fraction = (2/8)^2 = (1/4)^2 = 1/16.)

### Section D

12. The student is incorrect. 7/10 is the experimental probability -- the relative frequency from this specific experiment of 10 flips. The theoretical probability of a fair coin is 1/2, which is based on the structure of the coin (equally likely outcomes). With only 10 flips, getting 7 heads is not unusual (sampling variability). The student should have said: "My experimental probability from 10 flips was 7/10, which is an estimate. The theoretical probability of a fair coin is 1/2. With more flips, the experimental probability would approach 1/2."

13. The student listed only combinations where the first die's number is less than or equal to the second die's number -- she missed outcomes where the first die is higher than the second, like (2,1), (3,1), (3,2), (4,1), etc. The correct sample space has 6 * 6 = **36 outcomes** (every combination of Die 1 and Die 2, including (2,1), (3,1), etc.). A two-way table would have shown all 36 outcomes clearly.

## Teacher Notes

- The self-assessment exit ticket is an important diagnostic tool. Use it to guide which review sections to prioritize before the assessment.
- This lesson should feel like a confidence-builder, not a stressful test. Frame the problems as "practice" rather than "evaluation."
- Problem 13 (error analysis) is particularly important. The error of counting unordered pairs instead of ordered pairs is extremely common in compound event problems. Students who can identify and correct this error demonstrate deep understanding.
- Section C (interleaved review) is important for maintaining mastery of Units 1-4 while learning new content. Prioritize these problems if the student needs to reinforce earlier material before the assessment.
- Allow the student to use notes or formula cards during this review lesson if desired. The assessment should be closed-note, but the review is a learning activity where scaffolding supports is appropriate.
