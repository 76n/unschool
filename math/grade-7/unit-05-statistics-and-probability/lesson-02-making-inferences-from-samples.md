# Math -- Grade 7 -- Unit 5, Lesson 2

## Topic
Making Inferences from Samples

## Learning Objectives
- Use data from a random sample to draw an inference about a population characteristic.
- Express inferences with appropriate uncertainty (e.g., "I estimate approximately...").
- Recognize that different random samples from the same population produce different estimates.
- Predict population characteristics from sample proportions, applying proportional reasoning.

## Prerequisites
- Understanding of population vs. sample and random sampling (Lesson 1).
- Ability to compute mean and median (Grade 6).
- Ability to work with fractions, decimals, and proportions (Units 1-2).

## Materials Needed
- Paper slips or cards for the sampling simulation (see Warm-Up)
- Calculator (for mean calculations)
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice)
- Index cards for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 7.SP.2 -- Use data from a random sample to draw inferences about a population with an unknown characteristic of interest. Generate multiple samples (or simulated samples) of the same size to gauge the variation in estimates or predictions.

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Demonstrate sampling variability through a physical simulation.

**Setup:** Before the lesson, prepare 20 paper slips each with a number representing a fictional student's screen time in minutes per day:

45, 60, 90, 120, 75, 30, 150, 80, 95, 110, 55, 70, 100, 85, 65, 125, 40, 105, 115, 50

(Population mean = 82 minutes.)

**Activity:** Place slips in a bag. Have the student draw 5 slips at random (without looking), record the values, and calculate the mean. Replace the slips and draw 5 more. Calculate the mean again. Repeat a third time.

Ask: "Did your three samples give the same mean each time? Which sample mean is closest to the true population mean of 82 minutes?"

Say: "This is sampling variability -- different samples give different estimates. This does not mean something went wrong. It just means all estimates have some uncertainty. That's why scientists and researchers use the word 'approximately' or 'estimate.'"

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary

- **Inference** -- a conclusion about a population drawn from sample data. An inference is always an estimate, not an exact value.
- **Sampling variability** -- the natural variation that occurs because different samples from the same population produce different statistics.
- **Proportion (as a fraction)** -- a ratio that describes part of a group. "3 out of 5 students preferred X" = 3/5.

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 -- Making an inference from a sample.**

Say: "When we have data from a random sample, we can make an inference about the population. The key idea is: if our sample is random, we expect it to look like the population. So the sample proportion should be approximately equal to the population proportion, and the sample mean should be approximately equal to the population mean."

Write the inference template on the board:
> "Based on my sample of [n], I estimate that approximately [result] of [population]."

**Step 2 -- Inferring proportions.**

Say: "Sometimes the data is a proportion -- a fraction of the sample that has some characteristic. We use the sample proportion to estimate the population proportion, then scale up."

Example: In a random sample of 50 students from a school of 600, 30 say they play a sport. What is the estimated number of sport players in the whole school?

- Sample proportion: 30/50 = 60%
- Apply to population: 60% of 600 = 360 students

**Step 3 -- Inferring means.**

Say: "When the data is numerical, we compute the sample mean and use it as an estimate for the population mean."

Example: A random sample of 10 students from a school reports these weekly homework hours: 5, 8, 7, 4, 9, 6, 5, 7, 8, 6. Estimate the mean weekly homework hours for all students in the school.

- Sample mean = (5+8+7+4+9+6+5+7+8+6)/10 = 65/10 = 6.5 hours per week
- Inference: "Based on this sample, I estimate that the average student spends approximately 6.5 hours per week on homework."

**Step 4 -- Acknowledging variability.**

Say: "A good statistician always acknowledges that the estimate could be off. Different samples would give different estimates. With small samples, the estimate can vary quite a bit. With larger samples, estimates tend to be more stable. But even with large random samples, the estimate is still an approximation."

### Worked Examples

**Example A: Using a sample proportion to estimate a population quantity**

A wildlife manager tags 80 fish from a random catch in a lake and releases them. One week later, she catches 60 fish. Of those 60, 12 have tags.

Estimate the total number of fish in the lake. (This is called the mark-recapture method.)

Reasoning:
- In the second catch, 12/60 = 1/5 of fish are tagged.
- We expect the proportion of tagged fish in the lake to be approximately the same: 1/5.
- 80 tagged fish represent 1/5 of the total. So: total fish = 80 / (1/5) = 80 * 5 = **400 fish**.

**Example B: Comparing sample-based inferences across two samples**

Two students each take a random sample of 20 students from the same school of 400 to estimate how many students have a pet.

- Student A's sample: 14 out of 20 have a pet. Estimate: 14/20 * 400 = **280 students**.
- Student B's sample: 11 out of 20 have a pet. Estimate: 11/20 * 400 = **220 students**.

Both are valid estimates. The difference shows sampling variability. A reasonable inference might be: "I estimate between 220 and 280 students in the school have a pet, most likely around 250."

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity: Drawing Inferences from Sample Data**

**Scenario:** A middle school has 800 students. A researcher randomly surveys 40 students and collects the following data.

| Question | Sample Result |
|----------|--------------|
| How many students walk to school? | 16 out of 40 walk |
| Average daily screen time (hours) | 3.5 hours |
| Favorite app: Video games | 8 out of 40 |
| Favorite app: Social media | 18 out of 40 |
| Favorite app: Streaming | 14 out of 40 |

For each question, write a complete inference about the full population of 800 students.

1. How many of the 800 students probably walk to school?
   - Sample proportion: 16/40 = 40%. Estimate: 40% of 800 = **320 students walk to school**.
   - "Based on the sample, I estimate approximately 320 of the 800 students walk to school."
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Did you use the fraction from the sample and apply it to the whole school?"

2. What is the estimated average daily screen time for all 800 students?
   - The sample mean is 3.5 hours. Inference: "I estimate the average student in this school spends approximately **3.5 hours** per day on screens."
   - **Facilitator tip:** "The sample mean is our best estimate for the population mean."

3. About how many of the 800 students prefer video games?
   - 8/40 = 20%. Estimate: 20% of 800 = **160 students**.

4. If the estimate for students who prefer social media is 18/40, could it also be reasonable for another random sample to find 14/40 students preferring social media? Explain.
   - Yes. Sampling variability means different samples produce different proportions. 14/40 (35%) is not far from 18/40 (45%), and both are plausible results from the same population.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Does a different result necessarily mean one sample was wrong?"

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

Use the following scenario for Problems 1-3:

A random sample of 25 students from a school of 500 was asked about their reading habits. Results:

| Variable | Sample Finding |
|----------|---------------|
| Read at least one book per month | 15 out of 25 |
| Average books read per year | 8 books |
| Prefer e-books over print | 10 out of 25 |

1. Based on the sample, estimate how many of the 500 students read at least one book per month. Show your work.

2. Estimate the average number of books read per year by students in the school.

3. Estimate how many of the 500 students prefer e-books. What percentage is that?

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

4. A biologist wants to estimate the deer population in a forest. She captures and tags 60 deer, then releases them. One month later, she captures a random sample of 75 deer. Of these, 9 have tags.
   - (a) What fraction of deer in the second catch are tagged?
   - (b) Using the mark-recapture method, estimate the total deer population in the forest. Show your proportional reasoning.

5. Two students each survey a random sample of 30 students from a school of 450 about their after-school snack choices.
   - Student A: 18 out of 30 students prefer a salty snack.
   - Student B: 12 out of 30 students prefer a salty snack.
   - (a) What is each student's estimate for the number of students in the whole school who prefer salty snacks?
   - (b) Why do they get different estimates even though both sampled randomly?
   - (c) What is a reasonable range for the estimated number of "salty snack" students in the school?

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

6. Interleaved review (Unit 1 -- Proportional Relationships): A recipe calls for flour and sugar in a ratio of 5:2. A sample of 20 students at a baking club each made the recipe. The sample's average yield was 24 cookies per batch. If the school plans to make 15 batches for a bake sale:
   - (a) How much total flour will be needed? (Use the proportion; assume 1 batch uses 5 cups flour.)
   - (b) The school wants to estimate how many cookies all 80 baking club members could make if each person made 1 batch. Based on the sample mean of 24 cookies/batch, how many total cookies is that?

7. A student claims: "I asked 100 of my classmates and found that 60% prefer action movies. So exactly 60% of all teenagers in the country prefer action movies." Write a response explaining two flaws in this claim, using the vocabulary from this lesson.

## Exit Ticket

**Problem 1:** A random sample of 30 students from a school of 600 finds that 18 have a school library card. Estimate how many students in the school have a library card.

**Problem 2:** A second random sample of 30 students from the same school finds that 12 have a library card. Is this a contradiction? What does it tell us?

**Mastery:** Both parts correct and well-reasoned = Mastery. Problem 1 correct but Problem 2 unexplained = Review sampling variability. Both incorrect = Return to Lesson 1 and rebuild the concept of random sampling before revisiting inference.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. Sample proportion: 15/25 = 60%. Estimate: 60% of 500 = **300 students**.

2. Sample mean = 8 books per year. Estimate: "The average student in the school reads approximately **8 books per year**."

3. Sample proportion: 10/25 = 40%. Estimate: 40% of 500 = **200 students** prefer e-books. That is 40%.

4. (a) 9/75 = 12% = 3/25 of deer in the second catch are tagged. (b) If 9/75 of the population are tagged, and 60 deer were tagged, then: 9/75 = 60/N. Cross-multiply: 9N = 75 * 60 = 4,500. N = 4,500/9 = **500 deer**.

5. (a) Student A: 18/30 = 60%. 60% of 450 = **270 students**. Student B: 12/30 = 40%. 40% of 450 = **180 students**. (b) Different estimates arise because of sampling variability -- by chance, Student A's random sample happened to include more students who prefer salty snacks. Neither student made an error; this is normal variation. (c) A reasonable range: approximately 180 to 270 students prefer salty snacks, or roughly 200-250 (around the midpoint of the two estimates).

6. (a) 15 batches * 5 cups flour = **75 cups of flour**. (b) 80 members * 24 cookies = **1,920 cookies**.

7. Sample answer: Flaw 1 -- The sample is not random. Surveying "my classmates" is a convenience sample and may not represent teenagers nationwide; the student's school/community may have atypical preferences. Flaw 2 -- Even if the sample were random, the result is an estimate, not a certainty. The correct inference would be "approximately 60% of teenagers nationally may prefer action movies," not "exactly 60%." Sampling variability means different samples would give different percentages.

### Exit Ticket

1. Sample proportion: 18/30 = 60%. 60% of 600 = **360 students**.

2. Not a contradiction -- this illustrates sampling variability. The first sample estimated 60% and the second estimated 40%. Both are random samples from the same school; by chance they selected different students. The true proportion of students with library cards is somewhere in between (possibly around 50%), and both estimates are plausible given normal sampling variation.

## Teacher Notes

- The mark-recapture method in Tier 2, Problem 4 is a real wildlife biology technique. It is a wonderful real-world application of proportional reasoning that many students find compelling.
- Reinforce the language of inference: "I estimate approximately ___" rather than "The answer is ___." This epistemic precision is a key learning goal.
- If the student gets frustrated by the fact that different samples give different answers, that frustration is productive. Lean into it: "Yes, different samples give different answers. That's exactly why we need to be careful about how we sample, and why we say 'estimate' rather than 'fact.'"
- Interleaved review (Problem 6) connects to Unit 1. This is a good reminder that proportional reasoning underlies much of statistics.
- Do not skip the sampling simulation in the Warm-Up. The physical experience of watching means vary across samples is one of the most effective ways to build intuition for sampling variability.
