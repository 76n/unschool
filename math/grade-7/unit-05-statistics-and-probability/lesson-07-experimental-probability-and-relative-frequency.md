# Math -- Grade 7 -- Unit 5, Lesson 7

## Topic
Experimental Probability and Relative Frequency

## Learning Objectives
- Calculate experimental probability (relative frequency) from experimental data.
- Compare experimental probability to theoretical probability for the same event.
- Explain why experimental probability approaches theoretical probability as the number of trials increases (Law of Large Numbers).
- Predict approximate frequency of events using theoretical probability.

## Prerequisites
- Theoretical probability formula and sample spaces (Lessons 5-6).
- Fraction and decimal operations (Unit 2).
- Understanding that probability is a long-run relative frequency.

## Materials Needed
- A fair coin (each student)
- A standard 6-sided number cube
- A bag with 4 red and 6 blue chips (for the class experiment)
- Tally sheet (for recording results -- draw this before the lesson)
- Calculator
- Student worksheet (Guided Practice and Independent Practice)
- Index cards for Exit Ticket

**Standards:** 7.SP.6 -- Approximate the probability of a chance event by collecting data on the chance process and observing its long-run relative frequency, and predict the approximate relative frequency given the probability.

---

## Warm-Up (5 minutes)

**Purpose:** Demonstrate the core idea of this lesson through immediate experience.

Have the student flip a coin 10 times and record the results (H or T).

Ask:
1. "How many heads did you get? What is the relative frequency of heads? (heads / 10)"
2. "The theoretical probability of heads is 1/2 = 0.5. Did your experiment match exactly? Is that surprising?"
3. "What do you think would happen if you flipped the coin 100 times? 1,000 times?"

Say: "You just experienced experimental probability. The fraction of your trials that produced heads is your experimental estimate of how often heads occurs. Today we investigate how experimental probability relates to theoretical probability -- and why you need many trials to get a reliable estimate."

---

## Direct Instruction (12 minutes)

### Key Vocabulary

- **Experimental probability** -- the probability estimated from actual experiment results. Formula: P(event) = number of times the event occurred / total number of trials.
- **Relative frequency** -- another name for experimental probability; the fraction (or percent) of trials in which an event occurred.
- **Trial** -- one instance of performing the experiment (one flip, one roll, one draw).
- **Law of Large Numbers** -- as the number of trials increases, experimental probability gets closer to the theoretical probability.
- **Frequency** -- how many times an event occurred in a set of trials. (Not divided by total -- just the count.)

### Instruction Notes

**Step 1 -- Experimental vs. theoretical probability.**

Say: "Theoretical probability is what we expect based on the mathematical model (fair coin, fair die). Experimental probability is what we actually observe when we run the experiment. They are almost never exactly equal -- especially with a small number of trials."

Write on board:
- Theoretical probability = computed from the model
- Experimental probability = observed from data = (event count) / (total trials)

Example: You roll a number cube 30 times and roll a 3 exactly 7 times.
- Theoretical P(3) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167 (≈ 16.7%)
- Experimental P(3) = 7/30 ≈ 0.233 (≈ 23.3%)
- These differ. That's normal. 7/30 is our experimental estimate.

**Step 2 -- Why more trials improve estimates.**

Draw or describe this table (what might happen if a student keeps rolling a number cube and tracking how often they roll a 6):

| Trials completed | Sixes rolled | Experimental P(6) | Theoretical P(6) |
|-----------------|-------------|-------------------|------------------|
| 10 | 3 | 3/10 = 0.30 | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| 50 | 10 | 10/50 = 0.20 | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| 100 | 18 | 18/100 = 0.18 | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| 500 | 84 | 84/500 = 0.168 | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |

Say: "Notice how the experimental probability starts far from 1/6 with few trials, but gets closer and closer as we add trials. This is the Law of Large Numbers. It does not say the 100th trial is more likely to be a 6 just because we haven't rolled one recently -- each roll is independent. It says that over many, many trials, the proportion balances out."

**Step 3 -- Predicting frequency from probability.**

Say: "We can also use theoretical probability to predict how often an event will occur in a large number of trials."

Formula: **Expected frequency = probability * number of trials**

Example: If P(rolling a 4) = 1/6, and you roll the die 120 times, how many 4s do you expect?
- Expected frequency = (1/6) * 120 = 20 fours

Say: "This is a prediction, not a guarantee. You might get 17 or 23 fours. But 20 is your best estimate."

### Worked Example

**Class experiment: drawing from a bag**

A bag contains 4 red chips and 6 blue chips (total 10). Theoretical probabilities:
- P(red) = 4/10 = 0.4
- P(blue) = 6/10 = 0.6

The student draws a chip, records the color, replaces it (sampling with replacement), and repeats 20 times. Suppose the results are:

| Draw | Red or Blue? |
|------|-------------|
| 1-20 | R, B, B, R, B, R, B, B, B, R, B, B, R, B, R, B, B, B, R, B |

Count: Red appears 7 times; Blue appears 13 times.

Experimental probabilities:
- Experimental P(red) = 7/20 = 0.35
- Experimental P(blue) = 13/20 = 0.65

Compare to theoretical:
- P(red) diff: |0.40 - 0.35| = 0.05 (5% off)
- P(blue) diff: |0.60 - 0.65| = 0.05 (5% off)

Say: "Our experiment estimates are close but not exactly equal. If we repeated this 200 times, we'd expect even closer results."

Predict: "If we draw 100 times (with replacement), how many reds do we expect?"
- Expected reds = 0.4 * 100 = **40 reds**

## Guided Practice (10 minutes)

**Activity:** The student conducts an experiment with a number cube.

Have the student roll a number cube 30 times. For each roll, record the result in this table:

| Outcome | Tally | Count | Experimental P | Theoretical P |
|---------|-------|-------|----------------|---------------|
| 1 | | | /30 = | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| 2 | | | /30 = | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| 3 | | | /30 = | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| 4 | | | /30 = | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| 5 | | | /30 = | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| 6 | | | /30 = | 1/6 ≈ 0.167 |
| **Total** | | **30** | Should sum to 1 | 6/6 = 1 |

After completing the experiment:

1. Which outcome occurred most often? Which occurred least often? Are these significant differences?
   - Answers vary by experiment.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "Does the most common outcome tell us the die is unfair, or is it just normal variation?"

2. Compute the experimental probability for each outcome. Do any of them exactly equal 1/6?
   - Answers vary. Likely none exactly equal 1/6 (e.g., exactly 5 rolls out of 30).

3. If you rolled the die 600 times, how many times would you expect to roll each number?
   - Expected = (1/6) * 600 = **100 times each**.

4. If in a different experiment you got 4 out of 30 rolls as "6" (experimental P = 4/30 ≈ 0.133), and the theoretical P(6) = 1/6 ≈ 0.167, does this mean the die is unfair? Explain.
   - Not necessarily. With only 30 trials, variation of this size is normal. We would need many more trials (hundreds or thousands) to draw a reliable conclusion about fairness.
   - **Facilitator tip:** "What does the Law of Large Numbers say about conclusions from small experiments?"

## Independent Practice (12 minutes)

### Tier 1 (On-level)

1. A student flips a coin 40 times and gets 22 heads.
   - (a) What is the experimental probability of heads?
   - (b) What is the theoretical probability of heads?
   - (c) By how much do the experimental and theoretical probabilities differ?
   - (d) If the coin is flipped 200 more times, approximately how many heads should the student expect?

2. A bag has 3 red, 5 blue, and 2 yellow marbles. A marble is drawn and replaced 50 times. The results: red appeared 14 times, blue appeared 28 times, yellow appeared 8 times.
   - (a) Find the experimental probability for each color.
   - (b) Find the theoretical probability for each color.
   - (c) Which color's experimental probability is farthest from its theoretical probability?

3. Using the theoretical probability of drawing a blue marble from Problem 2, predict: in 100 draws (with replacement), how many blue marbles would you expect?

### Tier 2 (Stretch)

4. A student rolls two number cubes and records whether the sum is even or odd. Out of 60 rolls, the sum is even 34 times.
   - (a) What is the experimental probability that the sum is even?
   - (b) The theoretical probability that the sum of two fair dice is even is 1/2. (Even + Even = Even; Odd + Odd = Even; Even + Odd = Odd.) Does the experimental result agree reasonably well with theory?
   - (c) If the student rolled 600 times, what would be the expected number of even sums?

5. A school cafeteria records students' lunch choices for one week (5 days):

| Lunch Choice | Monday | Tuesday | Wednesday | Thursday | Friday |
|-------------|--------|---------|-----------|----------|--------|
| Pizza | 48 | 52 | 50 | 47 | 53 |
| Salad | 32 | 28 | 31 | 33 | 29 |
| Sandwich | 20 | 20 | 19 | 20 | 18 |

Total students per day = 100.

   - (a) Find the relative frequency (experimental probability) of each lunch choice using the 5-day totals.
   - (b) Based on this data, predict how many students would choose pizza in a school year of 180 days.

### Tier 3 (Challenge)

6. Interleaved review (Unit 1 -- Proportional Relationships): If the probability of an event is 0.3 and the event occurs 45 times in an experiment, how many total trials were conducted? Set up and solve a proportion.

7. A spinner has 4 equal sections. A student spins it 80 times. She records: Red=24, Blue=18, Green=22, Yellow=16.
   - (a) Find the experimental probability for each color.
   - (b) What is the theoretical probability for each color?
   - (c) Could the differences between experimental and theoretical probabilities indicate that the spinner is unfair? What would you need to do to investigate further?

## Exit Ticket

**Scenario:** A bag contains 2 green chips and 8 orange chips (total 10). A student draws a chip with replacement 25 times and gets green 8 times.

**Problem 1:** What is the experimental probability of drawing green?

**Problem 2:** What is the theoretical probability of drawing green?

**Problem 3:** The student concludes "the bag is weighted to give more green chips than expected." Do you agree? Explain using the Law of Large Numbers.

**Mastery:** All three correct with explanation = Mastery. Probabilities correct but explanation missing or incorrect = Review Law of Large Numbers. Probabilities incorrect = Review the experimental and theoretical probability formulas.

## Answer Key

### Independent Practice

1. (a) Experimental P(heads) = 22/40 = 0.55 = 55%. (b) Theoretical P(heads) = 1/2 = 0.5 = 50%. (c) |0.55 - 0.50| = 0.05 (5% difference). (d) Expected heads in 200 flips = 0.5 * 200 = **100 heads**.

2. Total draws = 50. (a) Experimental: P(red) = 14/50 = 0.28; P(blue) = 28/50 = 0.56; P(yellow) = 8/50 = 0.16. (b) Theoretical: P(red) = 3/10 = 0.30; P(blue) = 5/10 = 0.50; P(yellow) = 2/10 = 0.20. (c) Blue: |0.56 - 0.50| = 0.06 is the largest difference. (Red: |0.28-0.30|=0.02; Yellow: |0.16-0.20|=0.04.) Blue is farthest off.

3. Theoretical P(blue) = 5/10 = 0.5. Expected blue in 100 draws = 0.5 * 100 = **50 blue marbles**.

4. (a) Experimental P(even sum) = 34/60 ≈ 0.567 ≈ 56.7%. (b) Theoretical = 1/2 = 0.50. The experimental result (0.567) is reasonably close to the theoretical (0.50) -- a difference of about 6.7% with only 60 trials is not surprising. (c) Expected even sums in 600 rolls = 0.5 * 600 = **300**.

5. (a) 5-day totals: Pizza = 48+52+50+47+53 = 250; Salad = 32+28+31+33+29 = 153; Sandwich = 20+20+19+20+18 = 97. Total = 500. Relative frequencies: P(pizza) = 250/500 = 0.50 = 50%; P(salad) = 153/500 = 0.306 = 30.6%; P(sandwich) = 97/500 = 0.194 = 19.4%. (b) Expected pizza days in 180 days: 0.50 * 180 * 100 students = 9,000 students choose pizza, or 0.50 * 100 = 50 students per day. Over 180 days: **50 * 180 = 9,000 students** total, or about 50 per day.

6. P(event) = successes / total trials. 0.3 = 45 / total. Total = 45 / 0.3 = **150 trials**.

7. (a) Total = 80 spins. Experimental: P(red) = 24/80 = 0.30; P(blue) = 18/80 = 0.225; P(green) = 22/80 = 0.275; P(yellow) = 16/80 = 0.20. (b) Theoretical (4 equal sections): P(each color) = 1/4 = 0.25. (c) With 80 trials, some variation from 0.25 is expected -- differences of 0.025 to 0.05 are within normal range. To investigate whether the spinner is truly unfair, you would need many more trials (perhaps 500 or 1,000). If the deviations from 0.25 persist with hundreds of trials, that would be stronger evidence of unfairness. You cannot conclude unfairness from 80 trials alone.

### Exit Ticket

1. Experimental P(green) = 8/25 = 0.32 = 32%.

2. Theoretical P(green) = 2/10 = 0.2 = 20%.

3. No. 25 trials is a small number of trials. With only 25 draws, getting 8 green chips (32%) instead of the expected 5 (20%) is plausible due to normal sampling variation. The Law of Large Numbers says that with many more trials (hundreds or thousands), the experimental probability would get much closer to 20%. Drawing 8 greens in 25 tries does not provide sufficient evidence that the bag is weighted -- we need far more data before making that conclusion.

## Teacher Notes

- The hands-on experiment is essential for this lesson. Students who physically conduct the experiment and watch variation emerge have a much stronger intuition for the Law of Large Numbers than students who only read about it.
- Consider pooling data across multiple runs of the experiment (the student can run 30 trials, then another 30, then another 30) to observe how the cumulative relative frequency approaches the theoretical value.
- If you want to make the Law of Large Numbers more vivid, tally results on a running graph: after each 10 trials, plot the cumulative relative frequency. Students can see the line stabilize as trials increase.
- The prediction formula (Expected frequency = P * n) is a direct application of proportional reasoning from Unit 1, making this a natural interleaved review moment.
- Common error: students confuse "frequency" (count) with "relative frequency" (proportion). Require both in the table to reinforce the distinction.
